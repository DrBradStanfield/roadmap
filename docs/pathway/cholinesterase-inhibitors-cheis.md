# Cholinesterase Inhibitors (CHEIs)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers cholinesterase inhibitor (CHEI) medications used in dementia — who they are suitable for, what to expect, and how they are monitored and reviewed over time.

---

## Background

Cholinesterase inhibitors (CHEIs) are medicines that may help improve symptoms in certain types of dementia by supporting chemical signalling in the brain. They do not cure dementia or slow the underlying disease process, but they can improve quality of life and functioning in some people.

Benefits are usually modest and temporary — lasting months to a few years. Stabilisation or symptomatic improvement occurs in up to 50% of patients. Improvements are more likely to be subtle than dramatic, though marked benefit can occur.

Symptoms that may improve include:
- Apathy and lack of spontaneity
- Delusions or hallucinations (if present)
- Cognitive symptoms
- Some behavioural and psychological symptoms

---

## Who CHEIs Are Suitable For

Your doctor may consider a CHEI trial for:
- Mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's dementia
- Parkinson's dementia
- Dementia with Lewy bodies
- Some vascular dementias

CHEIs are **not** used for:
- Frontotemporal dementia
- Alcohol-related dementias (where other pathologies are not likely)
- Dementia secondary to large single infarctions
- Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) — no evidence of benefit

---

## Before Starting a CHEI

Your doctor will:

- Confirm the dementia diagnosis and type
- Discuss treatment goals with you and your family — including that any benefits are likely temporary, and that the medication will usually be stopped once benefits have faded
- Check your heart health — take a cardiovascular history, check your pulse and heart rate, and consider an ECG if there are any abnormalities or known cardiovascular disease (CHEIs can worsen pre-existing bradycardia or heart block)
- Assess your falls risk (CHEIs may occasionally increase falls risk through postural syncope, though they are generally associated with a decreased falls risk overall)
- Record your baseline weight

Your doctor will also check for contraindications and precautions:
- CHEIs should **not** be prescribed alongside anticholinergic medications (e.g. oxybutynin), as they work against each other. Medications with anticholinergic effects include some antidepressants, antihistamines, antipsychotics, and antispasmodics
- The risk of bradycardia is increased when CHEIs are used with beta-blockers, digoxin, amiodarone, or calcium-channel blockers

Other precautions include:
- History of gastric or duodenal ulcer disease
- Chronic nausea or diarrhoea
- Sick sinus syndrome
- Recent heart attack
- Urinary outflow obstruction
- Asthma
- Frequent muscle cramps

## The Three CHEI Medicines

### Donepezil (first choice)

Your doctor will usually start with donepezil (tablet form only).

- **Starting dose:** 5 mg once daily. Nausea is common early on — taking it with food or just before bedtime can help
- **Dose increase:** After one month, increased to 10 mg daily if side effects are tolerated
- If there was no improvement at 5 mg and significant side effects at 10 mg, your doctor may reduce back to 5 mg for 4 weeks, then try 10 mg again

Common side effects include:
- Nausea, loss of appetite, diarrhoea
- Fatigue
- Vivid dreams or nightmares
- Increased urinary frequency
- Dizziness or syncope

Less common but significant cardiac side effects: arrhythmia, bradycardia, heart block.

### Rivastigmine (if donepezil not tolerated or ineffective)

Available as capsules or transdermal (skin) patches. The patch is as effective as capsules but better tolerated.

- **Patch dosing:** 4.6 mg/day for one month → 9.5 mg/day after 4 weeks → up to 13.3 mg/day after another 4 weeks
- **Capsule dosing:** 1.5 mg twice daily with food, increasing by 1.5 mg every 2–4 weeks, up to 6 mg twice daily

Rivastigmine has a higher incidence of gastrointestinal side effects than donepezil, but a better profile for cardiac and bladder side effects. The patch may occasionally cause a localised skin rash.

### Galantamine (if donepezil not tolerated or ineffective)

- **Dosing:** 8 mg once daily with food for 4 weeks → 16 mg/day → maintenance 16–24 mg/day as tolerated
- Common side effects: nausea and vomiting

## Monitoring

Your doctor will review you at 1, 3, and 6 months, then every 6–12 months. At each review:

- Check pulse and heart rate (ECG if any arrhythmia, bradycardia, dizziness, or syncope)
- Check body weight
- Assess response with input from family or carers
- Ask about sleep, appetite, neuropsychiatric symptoms, and carer impressions of change

A clinically meaningful response includes improvements in:
- Quality of life and level of independence
- Cognitive function (attention, recognition, interest in environment)
- Behavioural symptoms (hallucinations, delusions, anxiety, agitation)

---

## Switching CHEIs

If switching from one CHEI to another:
- Stop the current medication one day and start the new one the next
- Dose titration of the new medication can often be quicker (1–2 week intervals), but slow down if side effects develop

It can be worthwhile trying a different CHEI if the first was stopped due to side effects, as each medication has variable effects and individual responses differ.

---

## If Symptoms Worsen on a CHEI

If cognition worsens after starting a CHEI, your doctor will consider other causes first — including delirium, depression, medication effects (especially anticholinergics, sedatives, opioids), metabolic causes (B12 deficiency, hypothyroidism, electrolyte abnormalities), and neurological conditions.

If no other cause is found, cognitive worsening may be a side effect of the medication — your doctor may trial a withdrawal. If cognition improves on stopping, CHEIs should not be continued.

---

## When CHEIs Are Stopped (Deprescribing)

At least 50% of people who trial a CHEI do not experience a clinically meaningful response. Your doctor will regularly review whether the medication is still beneficial.

CHEIs are usually stopped when:
- There is no significant benefit, and switching drugs has not helped
- There are significant side effects or poor adherence
- A significant change in general health occurs (e.g. metastatic cancer, stroke)
- Particular treatment end-points are reached (e.g. advanced dementia, transition to residential care)
- You, your family, or your legal representative (enduring power of attorney) wishes to stop

How stopping is managed:
- Dose is halved every 4 weeks to the lowest available dose, then discontinued — reviewed at least every 4 weeks
- Do not stop abruptly unless there are significant adverse effects
- If cognition worsens significantly on withdrawal, the previous effective dose can be restarted
- Family and carers should be warned to report any unexpected deterioration during dose reduction
