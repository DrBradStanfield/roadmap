# Before Entering Aged Residential Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to assessing whether an older adult needs aged residential care (ARC), what level of care may be appropriate, and what steps should happen first.

---

## Key Principle

Most older people prefer to stay in their own home. Aged residential care placement should only be considered when disabilities and available supports mean needs cannot be met at home. Entry into ARC is not automatic — there are often alternatives.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider:

1. **Whether you can be supported at home** — home-based supports, equipment, and rehabilitation may prevent or delay ARC.
2. **What you want** — your wishes are central to this decision. Family concerns are taken into account, but are not the sole consideration.
3. **The trigger that has caused this situation**, for example:
   - An abrupt decline in function (which may be reversible)
   - Carer stress, or family can no longer provide support
   - A recent hospital admission
   - An event that makes home unsafe
4. **Whether any recent decline may be reversible** — many patients can make functional improvements through:
   - Reversing deconditioning
   - Improving cardiovascular fitness and muscle strength
   - Aids or equipment (e.g., walking frame, grab rails, improved lighting)
5. **Whether you have reached your functional potential** — if not, a trial of treatment, rehabilitation, or other intervention may be appropriate first.

---

## Levels of Aged Residential Care

Your doctor will consider which level of care — if any — matches your needs.

### Rest Home Level Care

May be considered if you:
- Require overnight care that cannot be provided by family, carers, or home-based supports
- Make consistently poor or unsafe decisions
- Need some assistance with at least 2 of: eating, bed mobility, transfers, and toileting
- Wander and require supervision
- Can be easily redirected, and live alone
- Have treatment-resistant depression, anxiety, or psychotic symptoms
- Have a documented gap in available services that prevents continued community living

Or if you have at least 3 of the following functional impairments:

**Physical function:**
- Requires assistance with toileting and to maintain continence
- Little or no involvement with meal preparation, housework, or phone use
- Visual impairment that significantly impacts function
- Requires essential treatment more than twice a day
- Impaired mobility requiring another person's oversight or cueing to move around

**Psychological and cognitive function:**
- Nocturnal anxiety and lives alone
- Cognitive impairment requiring 24-hour supervision

**Social function and supports:**
- Significant carer stress that cannot be relieved with support
- Brittle social support that cannot be provided in the community
- Signs of neglect or abuse from others

### Hospital Level Care

May be required if rest home criteria are met, plus at least one of:

**Physical function:**
- Requires help with most activities of daily living (ADLs)
- Requires 2 carers to transfer, mobilise, and toilet
- Requires frequent care at night
- Requires registered nursing assessment and monitoring over 24 hours to maintain health
- Frequent faecal incontinence
- Dependent on essential medical and nursing treatment more than twice a day

**Psychological and cognitive function:**
- Requires prompting for all ADLs, including eating and toileting
- Has psychological symptoms requiring frequent carer input during the day or night
- Requires registered nursing management and support to meet psychosocial needs

### Dementia Rest Home Care

May be required if rest home criteria are met, and significant dementia-related behaviours are present, such as:
- Regular wandering, or attempts to wander, out of the facility
- Persistent troubling intrusion into other residents' rooms or personal space, including persistent loud repetitive vocalisation
- Verbal or physical abuse of staff, residents, or visitors
- Disinhibited behaviours (including sexual) causing concern
- Significant resistance to essential daily living tasks (e.g., hygiene, feeding) due to dementia
- Dementia-related apathy causing the person to miss activities, support, or ADL assistance

**Dementia hospital care** may be required if both general hospital care criteria and dementia rest home care criteria are met.

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider:

1. **Identifying the need early** and putting supports or treatments in place to prevent or delay ARC.
2. **Home-based support services** — if needs can be met at home, your doctor will consider rehabilitative and home-based support services (e.g., Acute Community Support).
3. **A non-acute older person's health assessment** — if assistance is needed, your doctor can request this assessment. A Needs Assessment and Service Coordination (NASC) assessment will also be required for ARC placement.
4. **A trial of treatment, rehabilitation, or intervention** — if there has been a potentially reversible recent decline, or functional potential has not yet been reached.
5. **ARC placement** — only if your disabilities and available social supports mean your needs cannot be met at home, or you or your family feel you are not coping.

## Advance Care Planning and Legal Documents

When considering ARC, your doctor will also discuss:

### Enduring Power of Attorney (EPA)

An EPA is a legal document where a competent person (the donor) authorises another trusted person (the attorney) to make decisions on their behalf if they later become incapacitated.

- **EPA Welfare** — covers decisions about personal care and welfare
- **EPA Property** — covers decisions about property and finances

Key points:
- An EPA is different from a general power of attorney, which loses effect when the donor loses capacity
- An EPA Welfare is activated only after the donor has become mentally incapable, through a formal assessment by a relevant health practitioner
- The attorney for an activated EPA Welfare cannot refuse standard medical treatment required to save life or prevent serious damage to health
- It is legally important to obtain a copy of any EPA document before assuming it exists

Useful resources for patients:
- [Healthify — Enduring Power of Attorney](https://healthify.nz/health-a-z/e/enduring-power-of-attorney) (includes forms)
- [Office for Seniors — Protect Your Future](https://www.officeforseniors.govt.nz/our-work/promoting-enduring-power-of-attorney)
- [Public Trust — What is an EPA?](https://www.publictrust.co.nz/personal/enduring-power-of-attorney)

### Advance Care Plan (ACP)

An advance care plan records your wishes about future care and treatment. It does not replace an EPA, but complements it. If you do not have an EPA, the ACP provides a space to name others you would like involved in decisions — though being named in an ACP does not give legal authority.

### Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (CPR)

Your doctor will explain CPR and discuss your preferences. Important points:

- CPR involves chest compressions and rescue breaths to move blood around the body while the cause of cardiac or respiratory arrest is being treated
- CPR does not restart the heart — it supports the body while treatment is attempted
- CPR works best when the heart stops suddenly and unexpectedly (e.g., heart attack)
- When the circulation stops as part of the natural dying process, CPR is generally not effective
