# Headaches in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of headaches in children and adolescents, including how to tell common headaches from those that need urgent attention.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if your child has a headache with any of the following:

- Seizures
- Stiff neck (meningism)
- High blood pressure
- Neurological signs — including swelling at the back of the eye (papilloedema), weakness, or visual changes
- Confusion or impaired consciousness

---

## Background

- Headaches are one of the three most common pain syndromes in childhood, alongside recurrent abdominal pain and leg pains.
- Parents often worry that headaches signal something serious like a brain tumour — but this is rare. The two most common causes in children are the same as in adults: migraines and tension headaches.
- Using painkillers every day can convert an occasional headache into a chronic daily headache. Your doctor will ask about painkiller patterns carefully.
- Brain tumour is an important diagnosis to rule out. In New Zealand, the average time from first symptoms to diagnosis is 8.6 weeks — longer than in comparable countries. For Māori and Pasifika children, this delay is significantly longer.

---

## Concerning Features

The following features increase the likelihood that a headache has a serious underlying cause. Your doctor will look for these:

- Headaches persisting for more than 4 weeks
- Headache always in exactly the same spot (constant unifocal headache)
- Headaches getting more frequent or more severe over time
- Waking from sleep with a headache
- Headache brought on by coughing, laughing, sneezing, toileting, or physical effort
- Vomiting for more than 2 weeks, eye symptoms, or motor signs

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a full history including:

- What the headache feels like — location, any warning signs or aura, how quickly it builds, whether it is always in the same place
- Associated symptoms — nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, pallor, any neurological symptoms before, during, or after
- Whether symptoms persist between headaches
- Factors that make it better or worse
- Impact on daily activities and school attendance

**Triggers your doctor may ask about:**
- Tiredness or poor sleep
- Missed meals or not drinking enough fluids
- Specific foods (chocolate, aged cheese, citrus fruits, processed meats, MSG)
- Caffeine consumption
- Excessive screen time
- Stress — school, home, bullying, family situation
- Menstruation (in older girls)
- Medication overuse

**Other history:**
- Current medications (including herbal remedies, vitamins, caffeine, oral contraceptive)
- How often painkillers are used and how soon after headache onset
- Family history of headaches
- Any features of depression or anxiety
- Any developmental concerns

**For adolescents,** your doctor may use a structured psychosocial assessment (HEEADSSS) covering home, education, eating, activities, drugs, sexuality, and mental health. For younger children, a similar tool called HEARTS may be used.

**Physical examination includes:**
- Vital signs and blood pressure (compared against age-appropriate normal ranges)
- Skin — looking for café-au-lait spots or depigmented patches
- Signs of fever, ear/nose/throat problems
- Full neurological examination, including looking at the back of the eye (fundoscopy) for raised intracranial pressure
- Head circumference in children 2 years and under

**Investigations** are not usually required in the community. Your doctor may suggest keeping a headache diary or log to track frequency, severity, and medication use — this helps with diagnosis and monitoring treatment response.

---

## Management

> **Important:** Avoid giving regular painkillers for headaches more than 2–3 times per week. Using them more often can transform occasional headaches into chronic daily headaches (medication overuse headache).

Your doctor will consider the following based on what type of headache your child has.

---

### If Red Flags Are Present

Your doctor will arrange urgent paediatric assessment. This is not something to manage at home.

---

### Non-Medication Management

Once serious causes have been ruled out, your doctor may suggest these approaches first:

- **Sleep and routine:** Regular sleep schedule and meals, avoiding missed meals
- **Screen time:** Reducing excessive screen time
- **Stress:** Identifying and addressing sources of stress at school or home — it is better to manage the cause than rely on medication
- **Food triggers:** If a specific food seems to trigger headaches, removing it may help — but there is no strong evidence for eliminating multiple foods at once
- **Acupuncture:** Some evidence supports acupuncture for preventing chronic tension-type headaches and migraines in children, when provided by a trained practitioner
- **Vitamin B2 (Riboflavin):** Some evidence suggests 200 mg/day for 3 months may reduce headache frequency and duration (but not intensity) in children with migraines. Available from pharmacies. Note: it turns urine bright yellow. B-complex supplements do not contain enough riboflavin to be useful here.
- **Headache diary:** Tracking headaches can help identify triggers and assess treatment response

---

### Migraine — Acute Management

Your doctor may consider:

**Analgesia — take as early as possible, at the first warning signs:**
- Paracetamol 15 mg/kg per dose (max 4 doses/day; do not exceed adult dose of 1 g/dose in larger children)
- Ibuprofen 6 mg/kg every 4 hours (max 4 doses/day; do not exceed adult dose of 800 mg/dose or 2.4 g/day)
- Do not give opioids
- Rest in a dark, quiet room can be helpful

**If vomiting is a significant problem:**
- Ondansetron (orally disintegrating tablet) 0.1 mg/kg — your doctor can advise on preparation

**Note:** Triptans are not licensed for children under 18 years in New Zealand, though they are occasionally used by paediatric specialists.

---

### Migraine — Preventive (Prophylactic) Medication

Your doctor may consider preventive medication if your child has more than 3–4 migraines per month that significantly affect daily life. No medication is 100% effective and all have possible side effects.

Options your doctor may consider:

- **Riboflavin (Vitamin B2):** 100 mg/day. Takes at least 2–3 months to show effect. No major side effects (urine may turn orange). Available from pharmacies.

- **Pizotifen:** Commonly used in children. Start at 0.5 mg in the evening, increase as needed to a maximum of 1.5 mg.

- **Beta-blockers** (contraindicated in asthma):
  - Nadolol: start at 1 mg/kg/day, increase by 1 mg/kg every 1–2 weeks, maximum 3 mg/kg/day
  - Propranolol: aged 2–11 years, start 10 mg 2–3 times/day (max 20 mg 3 times/day); aged 12+, start 20 mg 2–3 times/day (max 40 mg 3 times/day)

- **Topiramate:** Start at 25 mg once daily, increase as needed to 50 mg twice daily. Important precautions:
  - Must be initiated and supervised by a clinician experienced in migraine management
  - Associated with increased risk of birth defects and learning or behavioural problems if taken during pregnancy — effective contraception is essential for girls of childbearing potential
  - Reduces effectiveness of hormonal contraceptives — discuss contraception options with your doctor
  - If pregnancy occurs while on topiramate, stop immediately and seek specialist advice

- **Sodium valproate:** Used at epilepsy doses. In girls from menarche onwards, only used with specialist recommendation and effective contraception due to significant teratogenicity risk.

- **Amitriptyline:** Useful for both migraines and chronic daily headache. Start at 10 mg at night, increase as needed to maximum 50 mg at night. Generally recommended for children aged 12 and older.

Your doctor will continue prophylactic treatment until your child has been migraine-free for 6–9 months, then slowly reduce the dose. Do not stop medications suddenly.

---

### Tension Headache — Management

Your doctor may consider:

1. Checking whether medication overuse is contributing (especially if your child has more than 15 headaches per month). If so, painkillers may need to be stopped — headaches may worsen briefly before improving.
2. Identifying and addressing environmental or psychological triggers.
3. Limiting analgesia use to no more than 2–3 times per week:
   - Paracetamol 15 mg/kg per dose (max 4 doses/day)
   - Ibuprofen 6 mg/kg every 4 hours (max 4 doses/day)
   - Do not give opioids
4. If headaches are frequent, your doctor may consider preventive medication:
   - Amitriptyline (as above)
   - Topiramate (as above — with same precautions)
