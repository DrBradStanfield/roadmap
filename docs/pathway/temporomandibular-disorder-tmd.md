# Temporomandibular Disorder (TMD)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of temporomandibular disorder — a condition affecting the jaw joint, chewing muscles, and surrounding structures.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- **Pain** — facial pain that may be one-sided or both sides; pain in front of the ear, especially when moving the jaw
- **Joint noise** — clicking, grating, or popping sounds from the jaw joint
- **Reduced jaw movement** — difficulty opening the mouth fully
- **Muscle overactivity** — teeth grinding (bruxism) or jaw clenching
- **Trauma** — any significant blow to the head, face, or neck; or prolonged mouth-opening during dental treatment
- **Other symptoms** — ear symptoms (pain, fullness, tinnitus), headaches, neck pain, jaw locking

Physical examination typically includes:

- Range of movement in the jaw joint (temporomandibular joint), comparing both sides
- Facial muscle tenderness
- Examination of the mouth, ears, and neck

Your doctor will also consider other causes of facial or jaw pain, including:

- Temporal arteritis (giant cell arteritis)
- Migraine or other headache disorders
- Trigeminal neuralgia
- Dental problems
- Shingles or postherpetic neuralgia
- ENT conditions (e.g. head and neck cancers, salivary gland disorders)

Tests are not usually needed unless there is concern about another cause. A full dental check may be arranged to rule out a dental cause.

---

## Management

Most patients are managed with conservative treatments in general practice. A collaborative approach involving your dentist and/or physiotherapist is often most useful.

### Initial treatments

Your doctor may consider the following depending on the most likely cause:

- **Patient education** — self-care strategies and avoiding triggers that aggravate symptoms
- **Physiotherapy** — particularly helpful when jaw muscle pain (myofascial pain) is the main feature. May include jaw mobilisation, home exercise programmes (stretching, self-massage, stability exercises), posture education, stress management, and biofeedback to reduce night clenching or bruxism
- **Analgesia** — paracetamol and NSAIDs (e.g. ibuprofen, naproxen) are first-line pain relief
- **Occlusal splint** — a dental device (such as a Michigan-type splint) fitted by a dentist. May help reduce load on the jaw joint and protect the teeth in patients with grinding or clenching; evidence is mixed but it is commonly used

### If symptoms are more severe or not improving

Your doctor may consider:

- **Chronic pain management** — including tricyclic antidepressants (e.g. amitriptyline) for persistent pain
- **Cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT)** — via psychologist referral or self-directed e-therapy tools
- **Botox injections** — for severe muscular jaw pain; evidence is limited
- **Specialist referral** — to oral and maxillofacial surgery (private) if there is no improvement with conservative measures, or for specific conditions including chronic jaw locking (closed lock), recurrent dislocation, ankylosis, or other degenerative jaw joint diseases
