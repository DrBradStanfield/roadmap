# Endometriosis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the recognition and primary care management of endometriosis — a common condition affecting women of reproductive age that is often significantly delayed in diagnosis.

---

## Background

Endometriosis is defined as the presence of endometrial tissue outside the endometrial cavity.

Key facts:
- Affects an estimated 5–10% of women of reproductive age
- Affects up to 50% of women with reduced fertility
- Affects 30–50% of women with chronic pelvic pain
- Peak incidence is between ages 25 and 30 years
- Approximately one third of women with endometriosis have no symptoms
- The average delay in diagnosis is 8 to 10 years — during which the disease frequently progresses

Genetic and familial factors play a role. Retrograde menstruation is believed to be central to its development.

---

## Symptoms That May Suggest Endometriosis

Symptoms more predictive of endometriosis:

- **Dysmenorrhoea** (period pain) that limits daily activities and quality of life — pain often starts several days before menstruation, may accumulate over time, and can become constant
- Non-cyclical pelvic pain
- Sub-fertility that is otherwise unexplained
- **Deep dyspareunia** — pain felt abdominally with deep penetration, often persisting 2–3 days after intercourse
- Fatigue alongside any of the above symptoms

Other symptoms that are suggestive but less predictive:

- Abdominal bloating
- Gastrointestinal symptoms (painful defecation/dyschezia, tenesmus, constipation, blood in stool) — especially if cyclical
- Urinary symptoms (painful bladder, frequency, blood in urine) — especially if cyclical
- Lower back or leg pain
- Headache or lethargy

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history including:

- Pattern and severity of pelvic pain — how symptoms have progressed over time
- Pain relief already tried
- Impact on quality of life
- Desire for fertility and duration of trying to conceive (if relevant)
- Family history of endometriosis and gynaecological cancers
- Gynaecological and obstetric history (age of first period, cycle regularity, previous pregnancies, hormonal contraception use)
- Psychosocial factors that increase the risk of chronic pain syndromes (mental health history, history of sexual trauma, personal or family history of chronic pain)

Physical examination will be performed as appropriate and may include:

- General examination
- Pelvic examination (speculum and bimanual), unless the patient has not been sexually active — clinical examination is frequently normal in endometriosis

Signs that may be found on examination:
- Pelvic tenderness
- Fixed retroverted uterus
- Tight or tender pelvic floor muscles
- Enlarged ovaries (may indicate ovarian endometriomas)
- Palpable nodules on the utero-sacral ligaments or in the pouch of Douglas
- Endometriotic lesions visible in the vagina or on the cervix

Your doctor may consider investigations including:

- Full blood count (FBC)
- STI swabs
- Urinalysis
- Pelvic ultrasound — particularly if there are abnormal findings on examination or specialist referral is being considered

Note: Endometriosis itself cannot be seen on ultrasound as its appearance is the same as surrounding tissue, but its effects (adhesions, thickening of ligaments) and endometriomas (ovarian cysts) may be visible.

## Management

The priorities in managing endometriosis are symptom control and managing sub-fertility. Your doctor may start medical management even before specialist review.

### Pain Relief (Analgesia)

Your doctor may consider:

- **NSAIDs** (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs) such as ibuprofen, or paracetamol if NSAIDs are contraindicated, or a combination of both
  - Starting analgesia the day before expected onset of pain and continuing regularly for the likely duration (often 3–4 days) is recommended
  - There is no evidence to support one NSAID over another
- Regular opioids are **not** recommended for suspected endometriosis pain

### Hormonal Treatment

The aim of hormonal treatment is to suppress ovulation and menstruation. This is not appropriate if you are currently trying to become pregnant.

Your doctor may consider:

**First-line progestin-dominant therapy** (offers the best chance of stopping progression and controlling symptoms):
- Norethisterone (e.g., Primolut) 5 mg once daily
- Medroxyprogesterone acetate (e.g., Provera) 2.5–10 mg once daily
- Cyproterone acetate 50 mg once daily (if also needed for hirsutism or acne)
- Norethisterone (Noriday) 350 mcg once daily
- Levonorgestrel IUCD (Mirena)
- Medroxyprogesterone acetate (Depo-Provera) 150 mg every 3 months
- Desogestrel (Cerazette) 75 mcg once daily
- Levonorgestrel implant (Jadelle)

**Alternative — Combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP):**
- Continuous use is recommended (omitting placebo tablets) to reduce withdrawal bleeds
- The 30 microgram pill is recommended for adolescents

Medications may be used in combination — for example, Mirena with norethisterone, or Mirena with the COCP. The aim is to achieve anovulation and amenorrhoea for 6 months.

Hormonal treatment can be effective in controlling symptoms but may not control disease progression.

If using Depo-Provera for more than 2 years, your doctor will consider monitoring bone mineral density.

### Neuromodulators (for pain not controlled by the above)

These are used as part of a multidisciplinary approach and usually require specialist input. Your doctor may consider:

- **Tricyclic antidepressants:**
  - Nortriptyline — started at 10–25 mg as a single early evening dose; typical effective analgesic dose up to 50 mg
  - Amitriptyline — more sedating; may be useful where sleep is also affected
- **Gabapentinoids:**
  - Gabapentin — started at 300 mg at night, gradually increased; maximum 1800–3600 mg/day in divided doses
  - Pregabalin — started at 75 mg at night, increased as needed; maximum 300 mg twice daily

Special caution applies when gabapentinoids are used alongside opioids or in those with a history of substance use — your doctor will discuss the risks.

### Managing Associated Conditions

Endometriosis is commonly associated with:
- Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) — present in approximately 80% of cases
- Bladder pain syndrome (interstitial cystitis)
- Pelvic floor spasm — pelvic floor physiotherapy may be recommended
- Headache or migraine

Your doctor will address these alongside endometriosis.

### Self-Management

Knowledge about endometriosis empowers patients and leads to better outcomes. Self-management strategies may include:

- Mindfulness (e.g., apps such as Headspace)
- Regular exercise
- Good sleep habits — consistent sleep/wake times, relaxing pre-sleep routine, avoiding caffeine and alcohol before bed
- Yoga (note: exercises that work heavily on core muscles can aggravate pelvic floor spasm)
- Keeping a menstrual diary app (e.g., Period Tracker Lite, Flo) to record symptoms and patterns

## When Specialist Referral Is Considered

Your doctor may refer you to a gynaecologist if:

- Deep dyspareunia is present
- Frequent presentations to acute services for pelvic pain
- Signs suggesting deep endometriosis (e.g., fixed retroverted uterus, nodularity on examination)
- Endometrioma found on ultrasound
- You are trying to conceive and have not conceived after 6 months of regular intercourse (fertility treatment is more effective when initiated earlier)
- At risk of developing chronic pain (for consideration of multidisciplinary management)
- Neuromodulators are needed for pain control
- Failure to respond adequately to 6 months of optimised medical management

Specialist management may include:
- Optimisation of hormonal treatment
- Consideration of GnRH (gonadotropin-releasing hormone) therapy to induce medical menopause
- Surgery (usually laparoscopic) to make a definitive diagnosis and to excise or ablate visible lesions — this can improve pain short-term (6–18 months) and improve natural fertility
- Multidisciplinary team involvement (gynaecologist, surgeon, physiotherapist, psychologist)

---

## Post-Surgical Follow-Up

If you have been discharged after surgery and symptoms have not improved adequately, your doctor may:

- Seek further gynaecology advice (ideally from the treating surgeon)
- Optimise medical management
- Refer to pain services, clinical psychology, or women's health physiotherapy
- Arrange re-referral to gynaecology if symptoms persist
