# Prescribing Naloxone

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers prescribing naloxone for patients at risk of acute opioid overdose. Naloxone is an opioid antagonist that temporarily reverses the effects of opioids during an overdose.

---

## Background

- Opioid overdose can slow or stop breathing, leading to unconsciousness and cardiac arrest. It is one of the leading causes of morbidity and mortality among people who use opioids.
- Opioids — whether prescribed, illicit, or diverted — are responsible for the largest number of overdoses in New Zealand. Overdose can occur at any dose of prescribed or non-prescribed opioid, depending on a person's tolerance and risk factors. Risk increases when a person also takes another depressant drug.
- The most commonly represented opioids in opioid fatalities are methadone, codeine, oxycodone, fentanyl, morphine, and nitazenes (highly potent novel synthetic opioids).
- Access to naloxone is recommended by the Australian and New Zealand Committee on Resuscitation (ANZCOR). It is available in single-dose ampoules of 400 micrograms on prescription and through needle exchange services.

---

## Assessing Overdose Risk

All patients who take opioids — including prescribed opioids — are at risk of overdose. The majority of accidental overdoses occur in patients with chronic pain.

Your doctor will assess background and current risk factors.

**Background risk factors:**

- A history of non-medical opioid use
- A history of opioid use with other substance use, including cannabis, cocaine, or methamphetamine
- A previous history of overdose of any substance
- Recent discharge from a substance withdrawal unit or residential rehabilitation programme (including where past polysubstance use has not included opioids)
- Recent induction onto methadone or buprenorphine (for addiction or pain)
- Recent release from prison, even if there was no substance use before imprisonment

**Current risk factors:**

- A legitimate medical need for analgesia with a suspected or confirmed history of substance use disorder, non-medical use of opioids, or alcohol use
- Taking high-dose opioids for long-term management of chronic pain (high dose is 40 mg or more per day morphine equivalence; the threshold is lower if there is co-prescription of benzodiazepines or gabapentinoids)
- Opioid prescription with known or suspected:
  - Smoking history
  - Chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), emphysema, asthma, sleep apnoea, or other respiratory disease
  - Renal or hepatic disease
  - Concurrent use of benzodiazepines, zopiclone, gabapentinoids, antipsychotics, or other sedatives
  - Alcohol use

## Management

### Discussing Naloxone with Patients

Your doctor may explain that naloxone:

- Could save the life of a person experiencing an opioid overdose
- Is a standard safety measure — much like wearing a seatbelt. Being prescribed naloxone does not imply increased risk-taking; it simply ensures protection in the event of an unexpected opioid-related emergency
- Is routinely offered when opioids are prescribed at high doses (equivalent to 40 mg morphine or more) or when other overdose risk factors are present
- Temporarily reverses the effects of opioids, allowing time for emergency services to arrive
- Is safe and effective — it will not cause harm if given to someone who is not actually experiencing an opioid overdose

### Overdose Response — What to Do

If you suspect someone is having an opioid overdose:

1. Make sure it is safe to intervene.
2. Check that the person cannot be woken up.
3. Ensure the airway is clear and place the person in the recovery position.
4. Call for help from people nearby, and call an ambulance on **111**.
5. Look for signs of opioid overdose:
   - Not responding to voice, touch, or gentle shaking
   - Not breathing, or slow/shallow breathing or snoring
   - Skin pale or clammy
   - Blue lips
   - Very small "pinpoint" pupils
6. Give naloxone — even if you are not entirely sure opioids are the cause, it will not cause harm:
   - 1 ampoule (0.4 mg in 1 mL) via intramuscular injection into the outer thigh or upper arm
7. If the person has stopped breathing, apply rescue breathing: roll them onto their back and give two breaths every 5 seconds. Return to recovery position once breathing resumes.
8. If there is no response after 3 to 5 minutes, give a second naloxone dose at a different site.
9. Stay with the person until the ambulance arrives — naloxone is temporary (effects last 20 to 90 minutes) and the person could lose consciousness again.

### Naloxone Prescription and Kit

Your doctor may consider:

- Providing a naloxone prescription to all patients who wish to have access to naloxone. A naloxone kit typically includes:
  - 5 × 1 mL naloxone ampoules 400 micrograms/mL
  - 5 × 3 mL syringes
  - 5 needles (23G or 25G)
  - 10 × alcohol swabs
  - An opioid overdose pamphlet
- Patients can also obtain a free naloxone kit from a local needle exchange service.
- Encouraging patients to request additional naloxone if their supply is used, damaged, or near expiration.

### Potential Risks of Naloxone

Your doctor will advise that:

- Life-threatening withdrawal reactions can occur in approximately 1% of cases following naloxone administration, with the potential to cause pulmonary oedema and ventricular arrhythmia.
- Other risks include aggression and refusal to accept further treatment.

### If Opioid Dependence or Addiction Is Present

Your doctor may consider referral to a Community Alcohol and Drug Service (CADS) for additional harm reduction advice, case management, counselling, and support.

Features of **opioid dependence** may include:
- Feeling unable to cope without the medication
- Anxiety, insomnia, or feeling unwell after reducing the dose
- Feeling the medication has less effect over time
- Taking extra doses during stressful times or increasing the dose
- Making sure opioids are always accessible

Features of **opioid addiction** include a problematic pattern of use causing significant impairment or distress, with at least 2 of the following in the past year:
- Using larger amounts of opioids, or using for longer than intended (opioid tolerance)
- Using multiple sources to maintain supply (e.g. different doctors or illicit supply)
- A persistent desire to cut down
- Spending a great deal of time obtaining or recovering from use
- Strong cravings to use opioids
- Failing to fulfil major work, home, or educational roles
- Continuing use despite interpersonal problems
- Giving up or reducing important social, occupational, or recreational activities
- Continuing use despite persistent physical or psychological problems caused or worsened by opioids
