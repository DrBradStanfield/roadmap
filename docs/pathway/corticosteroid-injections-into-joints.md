# Corticosteroid Injections into Joints

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers when corticosteroid injections into joints and soft tissue may be appropriate, what to expect, and how they are managed. Your doctor will assess whether this treatment is suitable for you.

---

## Background

Corticosteroids mimic the anti-inflammatory action of cortisol. Clinically this results in decreased swelling, redness, heat, and tenderness of the inflamed joint.

For most conditions, evidence shows that a corticosteroid injection may provide short-term pain relief but makes little difference to longer-term outcomes. Intra-articular corticosteroid injections are considered an adjunct to core treatments — not a first-line standalone therapy.

---

## When Your Doctor May Consider an Injection

Your doctor may consider a corticosteroid injection if you have:

- Moderate to severe pain that cannot be controlled effectively with other means, including oral anti-inflammatory medications (NSAIDs) or other analgesia
- A condition suitable for injection (see below)

---

## Conditions and Typical Doses

### Shoulder and Elbow

**Adhesive capsulitis (frozen shoulder)**
- Injections may be effective for short-term symptom relief, especially when combined with a supervised exercise programme initiated after the painful phase.
- A better outcome is achieved if corticosteroid volume is increased with local anaesthetic.
- Typical dose: 40 mg triamcinolone or methylprednisolone acetate (Depo-Medrol), with 4 mL of 1% lidocaine.
- Some specialists recommend 3 injections at 4 to 6 week intervals, starting as soon as possible after diagnosis.
- If 3 injections are not successful, your doctor will reassess and consider alternative diagnoses.

**Rotator cuff impingement**
- Injections may be effective for short-term symptom relief in impingement and partial thickness tears.
- May be considered for full thickness tears where surgery is not planned, but should be avoided if surgery is a possibility.
- Typical dose: 40 mg triamcinolone or methylprednisolone acetate (Depo-Medrol), using the posterior approach, with 2 to 3 mL of 1% lidocaine.
- If 3 injections (minimum 4 to 6 weeks apart) are not successful, your doctor will reassess and consider alternative diagnoses.

**Medial and lateral epicondylitis (tennis and golfer's elbow)**
- Injections may be effective for short-term symptom relief of 6 to 12 weeks.
- Patients with lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow) may be worse off after 1 year with corticosteroid injections, despite physiotherapy — exercise alone may result in better long-term outcomes.
- Current evidence questions the efficacy of ongoing injections in these conditions.
- Both conditions are self-limiting and about 80% of patients recover within 1 year.
- Typical dose: 10 mg corticosteroid with 1 mL of 1% lidocaine, injected around the epicondyle.
- A rehabilitation, exercise, and physiotherapy programme is critical to management. Tennis elbow supports can also be helpful.

---

### Hand and Wrist

**Carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS)**
- Injections may be effective for short-term symptom relief of up to 3 months.
- Can be as effective as surgery for mild to moderate symptoms lasting less than 6 months.
- Less likely to be effective if symptoms are severe or have lasted longer than 6 months, but may provide some temporary relief.
- Injections are limited to no more than 3 per year.
- Typical dose: 20 to 40 mg triamcinolone (Kenacort) or methylprednisolone acetate (Depo-Medrol) plus lignocaine 1%, to a total volume of approximately 3 mL, injected into the carpal tunnel from the palmar aspect of the wrist.

**De Quervain's tenosynovitis**
- Corticosteroid injection is effective in approximately 50% of people after the first injection, and a further 40 to 45% after a second injection.
- X-ray or imaging is not a prerequisite for a trial of steroid injection when a reasonable clinical diagnosis has been made and red flags excluded.

**Trigger finger**
- Radiology is not a prerequisite for a trial of steroid injection when a reasonable clinical diagnosis has been made and there are no signs of flexor sheath infection.
- Typical dose: 10 mg triamcinolone with 0.5 mL plain lignocaine, injected into the palmar surface of the hand towards the A1 pulley at the base of the affected finger.
- A finger splint may help rest the finger for a few days after injection.
- This treatment works in up to 75% of cases.
- Pain relief may be effective for up to 3 months.
- If symptoms are severe and do not respond to treatment, your doctor may refer for orthopaedic assessment.

**First carpometacarpal joint or thumb osteoarthritis**
- Steroid injections are well-tolerated and effective at 1 month, but pain relief does not tend to persist beyond this.
- Recommended for patients who have not benefited from other options.
- Imaging is not a prerequisite when a reasonable clinical diagnosis has been made and red flags excluded.

### Hip and Knee

**Greater trochanteric pain syndrome (lateral hip pain syndrome)**
- Typical symptoms: lateral hip pain with localised tenderness over the greater trochanter. Often diagnosed as trochanteric bursitis, but bursitis is actually uncommon.
- May be caused by a gait abnormality associated with gluteal muscle weakness, causing tendinopathy of the gluteal insertions.
- NSAIDs and surgery are of limited benefit. Local heat and weight loss may help.
- Physiotherapy including a supervised exercise programme is important for long-term management.
- Corticosteroid injections alone are only effective long-term if the underlying cause is corrected; however, they can provide enough pain relief to allow gluteal muscle activation.
- Typical dose: 20 to 40 mg triamcinolone or methylprednisolone acetate (Depo-Medrol) with 3 to 4 mL of 1% lidocaine, injected to the area of maximum tenderness down to the periosteum of the greater trochanter.

**Knee osteoarthritis**
- Injections provide short-term pain relief of up to 3 months.
- It appears safe to repeat injections every 3 months for up to 2 years.
- Useful for short-term pain relief in acute knee osteoarthritis flares — may be considered for patients waiting for knee replacement, as long as surgery is not likely within the next 12 months.
- There is no evidence that corticosteroid injections cause disease progression.
- Typical dose: methylprednisolone (Depo-Medrol, 40 mg/mL), 1 mL, or triamcinolone acetonide (Kenacort, 40 mg/mL), 1 mL — either mixed with 3 to 5 mL of 1% lidocaine.

---

### Plantar Fasciitis (Heel Pain)

- Multi-factorial cause with limited evidence of benefit for any single treatment modality.
- Initial management should always involve physiotherapy and/or podiatry: stretches, footwear supports, activity modification, and pain relief.
- Corticosteroid injections are only considered for short-term relief (3 to 6 weeks) if initial management has failed.
- Triamcinolone dose is limited to 20 mg per injection, with 2 to 3 injections per year maximum, due to the risk of plantar fascia rupture and fat pad atrophy.
- If an injection is ineffective, your doctor may consider repeating it under ultrasound guidance, as needle placement is important.

---

### Inflammatory Arthritis (Gout, CPPD, Synovitis)

For acute single-joint flares of chronic inflammatory arthritis, synovitis, gout, or acute calcium pyrophosphate deposition (CPPD) arthritis, your doctor will seek advice from a rheumatologist before proceeding with a corticosteroid injection.

---

### Trigger Points and Spine-Adjacent Soft Tissue

When other treatment options have failed, a corticosteroid injection may provide pain relief for tender and trigger points around the spine and in soft tissue. Dry needling, acupuncture, or local anaesthetic injection could be tried first.

---

## Contraindications

Your doctor will check for contraindications before proceeding.

**Absolute contraindications (injection will not be given):**
- Local infection or inflammation, including infectious arthritis, adjacent cellulitis, or osteomyelitis
- Prosthetic joint
- Joint fracture
- Blood in the joint (haemarthrosis)
- Active systemic infection, including tuberculosis (TB)

**Relative contraindications (your doctor will weigh up risks and benefits):**
- Upcoming surgery on the injected joint within the next 12 months
- Localised osteoporosis or joint instability
- Anticoagulation or bleeding disorder
- Severe immunosuppression
- Failure of previous injections to provide significant relief, or injection more than 3 times per year or within the past 6 weeks

---

## Adverse Effects

Most patients do not experience any side-effects. Possible effects include:

**Common/expected:**
- Post-injection pain or flare once the local anaesthetic wears off — can last up to 3 days
- Facial flushing
- Temporary mood changes
- Temporary rise in blood glucose (important for people with diabetes — monitoring is recommended for up to 7 days)

**Less common:**
- Infection, bleeding, or bruising at the injection site
- Tendon weakening or rupture
- Nerve infiltration or injury
- Local fat or skin atrophy
- Skin hypopigmentation at the injection site
- Vasovagal reaction (feeling faint)

**Signs of infection after injection** — seek medical attention if you develop a hot, swollen joint, feel unwell, have a fever, or have pain that does not improve after 48 hours or starts 48 or more hours after the injection.

---

## After the Injection

Your doctor will advise:

- Cold compresses and paracetamol or anti-inflammatories for post-procedural pain once the local anaesthetic wears off
- A pressure bandage (e.g., Tubigrip) may be applied
- Rest the joint and reduce weight-bearing activity for the first 1 to 2 days
- Resume exercise 1 to 2 days after the procedure, once the acute painful phase has settled
- People with diabetes should monitor blood glucose more closely — elevated levels have been observed for up to 7 days after injection

---

## Follow-up and Further Treatment

- If there is no improvement from the first injection, your doctor will re-evaluate the joint and reconsider the diagnosis.
- A single injection is often sufficient, but several may be needed for adequate symptom control.
- Your doctor will aim to keep the interval between injections to more than 3 months.
- If you fail to respond, have refractory symptoms after approximately 6 months, or have severe symptoms, your doctor may refer you for orthopaedic or rheumatology assessment.
