# Cellulitis in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and community management of cellulitis in adults — identifying severity, choosing the right antibiotic, and knowing when hospital assessment is needed.

For children younger than 15 years, a separate pathway applies.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if:

- Severe pain or signs of sepsis (see below)
- Significant co‑morbidities making community management unsafe
- Cellulitis due to a diabetic foot ulcer
- Orbital cellulitis (eye involvement with deep pain, proptosis, or vision changes)

**Signs of sepsis** include: blood pressure drop, heart rate ≥ 130 bpm, temperature over 38°C or under 36°C, respiratory rate ≥ 25 breaths/min, new oxygen requirement, or acute confusion. If sepsis is suspected, this is a medical emergency.

---

## Background

Cellulitis is a bacterial infection of the skin and soft tissues. Key points:

- It is almost always **unilateral** — bilateral red legs are extremely unlikely to be cellulitis.
- **Erysipelas** is a form of cellulitis with rapid onset and sharply demarcated margins, almost exclusively caused by group A streptococci.
- **Necrotising fasciitis** is a life‑threatening emergency requiring urgent surgical assessment — suspect if there is marked pain disproportionate to appearance, rapidly advancing involvement, sepsis, or necrotic bullae.
- **IV antibiotics are not recommended** for community-managed cellulitis. There is no evidence they have better outcomes than appropriately dosed oral antibiotics, and there are increasing recognised harms. For moderate infection, oral probenecid added to flucloxacillin or cefalexin achieves similar drug levels to IV therapy.

**Predisposing factors:** obesity, venous insufficiency, oedema, lymphatic obstruction, previous cellulitis, diabetes, alcohol dependency, skin break or trauma.

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

**Take a history asking about:**
- Onset, location, and progression — acute erythematous, warm, painful, swollen skin; fever, malaise, or nausea may be present
- Systemic symptoms: fever, vomiting, chills, rigors
- Marked pain or rapidly advancing involvement (raises concern for necrotising fasciitis)
- Possible exposure to unusual organisms: sea/freshwater, animal or human bite, heavy environmental contamination, diabetic foot wounds
- Previous episodes and co‑morbidities (diabetes, heart failure, immunocompromise, peripheral vascular disease, organ dysfunction, systemic steroids)
- Risk factors for MRSA: frequent antibiotic courses, previous MRSA, crowded living, healthcare exposure, open skin
- Social circumstances and supports

**Examine:**
- Skin for erythema, warmth, tenderness, swelling
- Vital signs for signs of sepsis
- Weight (relevant to antibiotic dosing)
- Eyes if periorbital — to distinguish preseptal cellulitis (mild, anterior eyelid only, rarely serious) from **orbital cellulitis** (involves orbit, deep eye pain, pain with eye movements, possible proptosis or vision change — always requires hospital admission)
- Predisposing causes: oedema, obesity, fungal infections, leg ulcers, eczema, psoriasis

**Consider differential diagnoses:**
- Purulent skin infection (abscess, boil, furuncle) — incision and drainage usually sufficient
- Necrotising fasciitis — surgical emergency
- Deep vein thrombosis or superficial thrombophlebitis
- Septic arthritis (if overlying a joint with reduced movement)
- Osteomyelitis, bursitis, gout, mastitis
- Chronic venous changes (lipodermatosclerosis, venous eczema)
- Atopic eczema, urticaria, drug/insect bite reaction, erythema nodosum

**Classify severity:**
- **Mild** — typical features, no systemic features, no significant co‑morbidities
- **Moderate** — typical features with systemic features (fever, tachycardia) or stable co‑morbidities (including BMI > 40, severe venous insufficiency, symptomatic peripheral vascular disease)
- **Severe/life-threatening** — significant sepsis or severe infection (e.g. necrotising fasciitis) → requires hospital

**Investigations:** Usually not needed. Wound swabs only if not responding to treatment or MRSA risk. Blood tests only if the diagnosis is uncertain. Blood cultures are not done in primary care.

## Management

**Important:** The natural history of cellulitis shows **increasing redness and swelling for the first 48 hours**. Your doctor will not automatically change treatment in this window unless you develop fever, fast heart rate, or other signs of deterioration.

### Antibiotic treatment — mild infection

For uncomplicated cellulitis (not caused by bite, water exposure, or MRSA):

| Allergy status | Antibiotic | Dose |
|---|---|---|
| No penicillin allergy | Flucloxacillin | 1 g orally four times a day, 5 days |
| Non-severe penicillin allergy (no anaphylaxis) | Cefalexin | 1 g orally three times a day, 5 days |
| Severe penicillin allergy (anaphylaxis) | Trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole | 960 mg twice a day (three times a day if > 90 kg), 5 days |

Alternatives for severe allergy: doxycycline 200 mg once daily, or clindamycin 450 mg three times daily, for 5 days.

**Special causes — mild infection:**
- **Animal/human bite:** amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 625 mg three times a day for 5 days. If penicillin allergic: doxycycline 100 mg twice daily + metronidazole 400 mg twice daily.
- **Sea/freshwater or heavy contamination:** flucloxacillin 1 g four times a day + ciprofloxacin 750 mg twice a day for 5 days (if not penicillin allergic). If penicillin allergic: ciprofloxacin + cefalexin or trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole.
- **Known MRSA:** antibiotic choice guided by susceptibilities; trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole 960 mg twice a day is preferred.

### Antibiotic treatment — moderate infection

For uncomplicated moderate cellulitis, **probenecid is added** to boost antibiotic levels to near-IV levels:

| Allergy status | Antibiotic | Dose |
|---|---|---|
| No penicillin allergy | Flucloxacillin + probenecid | Flucloxacillin 1 g four times a day + probenecid 500 mg four times a day with food, 5 days |
| Non-severe penicillin allergy | Cefalexin + probenecid | Cefalexin 1 g three times a day + probenecid 500 mg three times a day with food, 5 days |
| Severe penicillin allergy | Trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole | 960 mg twice a day (three times a day if > 90 kg), 5 days |

**Probenecid cautions:** avoid if history of blood dyscrasia, urate renal stones, or recent gout. Use with caution in peptic ulcer disease or pregnancy. Many drug interactions — important ones: avoid with methotrexate; reduce paracetamol dose to 1 g three times a day; use half the maximum NSAID dose. Renal dose adjustments apply (your doctor will calculate).

**Special causes — moderate infection:** Same antibiotics as for mild infection above.

### General measures

- Start the first dose of antibiotics as soon as possible.
- **Limb elevation is essential** — elevate for at least the first 48 hours. For lower leg cellulitis, limit walking and keep the ankle higher than the hip when resting. This may require time off work.
- Use regular pain relief and stay well hydrated.
- Redness and swelling normally increase for the first 48–72 hours of treatment — this is expected and does not mean the treatment is failing.
- Wound care for any broken skin; tetanus prophylaxis for traumatic wounds.
- Seek advice if you have a fever lasting more than 48 hours, begin vomiting, or cannot tolerate the antibiotics.

## Follow‑up

Your doctor will review you **48–72 hours after starting treatment** if there are any concerns about severity, co‑morbidities, or factors that could delay healing.

**Co‑morbidities that increase recurrence risk:** venous insufficiency, oedema, obesity, lymphatic obstruction, previous cellulitis, diabetes, alcohol dependency, vascular disease, immunocompromise, advanced age.

**If you are improving after 48 hours:** your doctor may mark the edge of the redness with a pen to track progress. Continue the full antibiotic course. Return promptly if redness spreads beyond the marked line. Discolouration can persist for weeks to months even after successful treatment.

**If you are not improving or deteriorate** (beyond the expected first 48-hour increase), your doctor will reassess — they may maximise antibiotic therapy, increase leg elevation, check for resistant organisms, or arrange hospital assessment.

### Reducing recurrence

Cellulitis has a **50% recurrence rate**. Your doctor will address predisposing causes:

- **Reduce oedema** — treat underlying causes (heart failure, venous insufficiency, lymphoedema); compression stockings if not contraindicated.
- **Treat skin conditions** — eczema, psoriasis, fungal skin and nail infections.
- **Maintain a healthy skin barrier:** moisturise at least twice daily with an emollient; avoid harsh soaps; clean and cover cuts; keep fingernails short; wear gloves when working outdoors.
- **Weight loss** if overweight.

**For recurrent cellulitis (more than one episode in 6 months):**
- *Prophylaxis (preferred if severe episodes):* phenoxymethylpenicillin 250 mg twice daily for up to 12 months. Stop if cellulitis recurs during prophylaxis. If penicillin allergic, your doctor will seek specialist advice.
- *Self‑initiated antibiotics:* your doctor may provide a standing prescription to start at first signs of a new episode, with a follow-up appointment within 5 days to confirm the diagnosis and review.

## When to See a Doctor or Go to Hospital

Always discuss with your doctor. Seek urgent/emergency care if:

- Signs of **sepsis** — high heart rate, low blood pressure, fever, confusion, breathing fast
- **Orbital cellulitis** — deep eye pain, pain moving the eye, blurred vision, or the eye is bulging
- **Necrotising fasciitis** suspected — severe pain out of proportion to visible changes, rapidly spreading, sepsis, or skin blistering/blackening
- **Diabetic foot infection** spreading or severe
- **Significant bite injury**
- Cellulitis not responding after 48 hours on antibiotics, or you are getting worse
- Unable to take oral antibiotics (vomiting)
- You have major co‑morbidities making home management unsafe

Your doctor will determine what specialist assessment is needed based on the location and severity of your cellulitis.
