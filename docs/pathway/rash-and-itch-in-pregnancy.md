# Rash and Itch in Pregnancy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to rash and itch during pregnancy — identifying the cause and determining urgency.

> **Important clinical note (May 2025):** Aciclovir and valaciclovir doses must be reduced in patients with renal impairment. Aciclovir is 80% renally cleared; accumulation can cause neurotoxicity. Ensure adequate hydration and monitor for lethargy, confusion, agitation, and hallucination. Always discuss dosing with your doctor.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

The following conditions require urgent specialist assessment and should not be managed in the community without prompt escalation:

- **Pemphigoid gestationis** — rare autoimmune blistering disease in mid-to-late pregnancy
- **Generalised pustular psoriasis** — can be life-threatening in pregnancy
- **Cholestasis of pregnancy** — especially with elevated liver function tests or bile acids above 100 umol/L

---

## Background

- Pregnancy can increase the prevalence or severity of some common skin conditions.
- Skin conditions of particular concern in pregnancy include:
  - **Cholestasis of pregnancy** — occurs in around 0.7% of pregnancies. Typically presents as intense itch with no visible rash.
  - **Pemphigoid gestationis** — rare autoimmune blistering condition.
  - **Pustular psoriasis** — can be life-threatening.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history asking about:

- Current illnesses, associated itch, nausea, fever, sore throat
- Symptoms of cholestasis: jaundice, pale stools, dark urine
- Contact with someone who has had a rash or been unwell
- Medical history including liver disease, previous cholestasis
- History of skin disease
- Rash in a previous pregnancy
- Family history
- Obstetric history
- Medications
- Vaccination history

Physical examination focuses on the distribution and morphology of any lesions.

## Conditions by Presentation

### Itchy Rash

Your doctor will consider the following causes:

**Exacerbation of an existing skin condition** (e.g., eczema)

**Scabies**

**Varicella zoster (chickenpox or shingles)**
- Primary varicella (chickenpox) can have implications for the fetus in non-immune pregnant women. In pregnancy, the diagnosis is confirmed with VZV PCR (swab of a lesion) rather than serology.
- Herpes zoster (shingles) is more common in pregnancy but does not carry additional fetal risk. Your doctor may consider valaciclovir or aciclovir, as for non-pregnant patients.

**Atopic eruption of pregnancy**
- Usually in women with a history of eczema.
- Often occurs in the first trimester.

**Pruritic urticarial papules and plaques of pregnancy (PUPPP)** *(also called polymorphic eruption of pregnancy)*
- An itchy, bumpy rash that starts in the stretch marks of the abdomen.
- Usually occurs in the third trimester and resolves with delivery.
- Most likely in a first pregnancy (when abdominal stretching is tightest).
- Small papules coalesce into large, raised red patches that can spread to the buttocks, thighs, arms, and legs.
- The rash is very itchy and can disrupt sleep.

**Prurigo of pregnancy**
- Itchy papules, often excoriated (scratched).
- Papules typically occur on extensor surfaces or the torso.
- Liver function tests may be checked to exclude cholestasis.

**Pemphigoid gestationis** *(red flag — see above)*
- A rare autoimmune blistering disease with onset in mid-to-late pregnancy.
- Starts with itchy red bumps around the belly button, spreading to other parts of the body (usually sparing the face, scalp, palms, soles, and mucous membranes).
- Over 2–4 weeks, large fluid-filled blisters form (some patients develop plaques instead).
- Skin biopsy with direct immunofluorescence is needed to confirm the diagnosis.

**Generalised pustular psoriasis** *(red flag — see above)*
- Can be life-threatening. Presents initially with red, tender skin that develops small pustules within hours. Pustules coalesce over days, dry out to form a glazed surface, and further pustules may appear every few days or weeks.
- Systemic symptoms include fever, chills, headache, fast heart rate, poor appetite, nausea, and muscle weakness.

### Rash Without Itch

Your doctor will consider viral and bacterial causes:

**Viral rash**
- Many viral infections in pregnancy have no negative consequences for the fetus. However, varicella, parvovirus B19, rubella, measles, Zika virus, and HIV can have implications for the fetus in non-immune pregnant women.
- Testing (PCR or antibody testing) and specialist involvement guide management.

**Bacterial rash**
- Conditions such as cellulitis and meningococcal disease also occur in pregnancy and are managed with disease-specific pathways.

---

### Intense Itch Without a Rash

**Cholestasis of pregnancy**
- Occurs in approximately 0.7% of pregnancies.
- A diagnosis of exclusion — typically presents as intense, persistent itch, often worse at night, especially on the hands and feet, in the absence of a skin rash.
- May be associated with jaundice, pale stools, or dark urine.
- Can rarely cause premature delivery, meconium staining, or late fetal death.
- Risk is increased when bile acids exceed 100 umol/L.
- The itch resolves after delivery.
- Your doctor will check liver function tests (LFTs) and bile acids; if normal and symptoms persist, these are repeated at 10–14 day intervals.

---

### Asymptomatic Exposure to a Potentially Infectious Rash

If you have been exposed to a potentially infectious viral rash but have no symptoms yourself, your doctor will:

- Establish the diagnosis in the source person
- Arrange relevant antibody testing if you may not be immune to varicella zoster, rubella, measles, or parvovirus B19
- For varicella zoster and measles, testing is done urgently — immunoglobulin (e.g., VZIG) may need to be given within 72 hours of contact

---

## Management

Your doctor will manage based on the identified or suspected cause:

- **Suspected pemphigoid gestationis or generalised pustular psoriasis:** Your doctor will arrange urgent specialist assessment (acute general medicine or obstetric medicine).

- **Cholestasis of pregnancy with abnormally elevated LFTs, or with bile acids > 100 umol/L:** Your doctor will arrange urgent obstetric assessment.

- **Cholestasis of pregnancy with normal LFTs and bile acids < 100 umol/L:** Your doctor may consider:
  - Antenatal clinic referral
  - Liver and gallbladder ultrasound
  - Oral antihistamines and emollients as needed
  - Low-fat diet and increased water intake
  - Weekly monitoring of LFTs and bile acids
  - Ursodeoxycholic acid 10–15 mg/kg/day (pruritus relief usually occurs within 1–2 weeks)

- **Ongoing intense itch without rash and normal results:** Your doctor will repeat LFTs and bile acids at 10–14 day intervals.

- **Confirmed viral infection (varicella, rubella, measles, parvovirus B19, Zika):** Your doctor will discuss with the Maternal Fetal Medicine Unit.

- **Asymptomatic exposure with confirmed non-immunity:**
  - Varicella zoster, rubella, or measles: infectious diseases advice regarding immunoglobulin administration
  - Parvovirus B19: repeat testing in one month; obstetric advice if IgM becomes positive

- **PUPPP:** Your doctor may consider:
  - Emollients as needed
  - Topical steroids applied twice daily to itchy papules
  - Daily oral antihistamines (e.g., loratadine, cetirizine); a sedating antihistamine at night (e.g., promethazine) if sleep is affected

- **Atopic eruption or prurigo of pregnancy:** Your doctor may consider:
  - Emollients and topical steroids
  - Oral antihistamines

- **Exacerbation of an existing skin condition (e.g., eczema):** Managed according to the specific condition.
