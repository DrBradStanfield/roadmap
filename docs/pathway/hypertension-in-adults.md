# Hypertension in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: July 2021.*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of high blood pressure in adults, from diagnosis through to long-term treatment.

> This is general information from a clinical pathway written for doctors. It is not medical advice. Always discuss your own blood pressure and treatment with your doctor.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

**Hypertensive emergency** is high BP (often > 180/110 mmHg) with acute target organ damage or dysfunction, such as chest pain, stroke symptoms, breathlessness from fluid on the lungs, or confusion. This is an emergency: call 111 or go to the emergency department. Signs of organ damage include:

- Heart failure or acute pulmonary oedema
- Acute myocardial infarction
- Aortic aneurysm
- Acute renal failure (proteinuria or haematuria)
- Major neurological changes or hypertensive encephalopathy
- Flame-shaped haemorrhages on fundoscopy
- Cerebral infarction or haemorrhagic stroke

**Symptomatic hypertension** (headache or visual disturbance): see your doctor the same day.

---

## Background

Hypertension is an important contributor to cardiovascular disease. Blood pressure category is defined by the highest level, whether systolic or diastolic. End-organ damage is more closely associated with ambulatory blood pressure than in-practice measurements.

Diagnosis is important because treatment is typically life-long. Treatment decisions are individualised based on overall cardiovascular risk.

Apart from the red flags above, high blood pressure is usually asymptomatic. Your doctor may consider whether blood pressure medicine is causing symptoms you put down to high blood pressure.

## Assessment

### Blood Pressure Measurement

Before starting antihypertensive medication:

- Take an average of 2 seated readings after you have rested for at least 5 minutes.
- Repeat on 3 separate occasions.
- Consider an automated machine with you alone in a quiet room, taking serial readings over 5 minutes. This can be more informative than supervised manual measurement.
- Check BP in both arms during the initial diagnostic phase; use the arm with the higher reading for subsequent measurements.
- Use the correct cuff size.

**Adult cuff size guide** (arm circumference):

| Arm circumference | Cuff |
|---|---|
| 22–26 cm | Small adult (12 × 22 cm) |
| 27–34 cm | Adult (16 × 30 cm) |
| 35–44 cm | Large adult (16 × 36 cm) |
| 45–52 cm | Adult thigh (16 × 42 cm) |

Automated devices should be validated, maintained, and regularly calibrated. They may at times over- or under-estimate BP. A manual device may be used where appropriate.

**Diagnostic thresholds:**

- Diagnose hypertension if > 140/90 mmHg.
- Consider hypertensive urgency if BP > 180/110 mmHg without organ damage.
- Consider hypertensive emergency if there is acute target organ damage or dysfunction (see Red Flags above).

### Hypertensive Urgency

BP > 180/110 mmHg that is not immediately life-threatening. It is sometimes associated with headache or visual disturbance, but without target organ damage. Your doctor will treat with oral antihypertensives and review daily.

- They may start 2 complementary medicines (ACEi or ARB plus a dihydropyridine CCB) at adequate (non-minimum) doses.
- Diuretics are avoided, because people with hypertensive urgency are often low in potassium (hypokalaemic).
- Daily follow-up checks BP, signs of a hypertensive emergency, and medicines. The gap between visits may slowly lengthen once BP is < 170/105.

### Ambulatory and Home Blood Pressure Monitoring

Ambulatory blood pressure monitoring (ABPM) can be considered the gold standard for diagnosing hypertension. Hypertension can be misdiagnosed in up to 20% of patients if the diagnosis is based only on office readings.

Your doctor may recommend ABPM or home blood pressure monitoring (HBPM) if:

- Suspected white coat hypertension.
- Suspected nocturnal hypertension (in sleep apnoea, diabetes, or chronic kidney disease).
- Marked variability between clinic and home readings.
- Autonomic, postprandial, postural, or drug-induced hypotension.

**Home monitoring protocol:**

- Use a validated automatic monitor, preferably with memory storage.
- Rest seated for 5 minutes before each reading; measure before medication, food, tea, coffee, or vigorous exercise.
- Record morning and evening (2 readings each time) for 7 days.
- Calculate the average over the full 7 days, excluding day 1.
- An average of ≥ 135/85 mmHg indicates hypertension.

**Ambulatory monitoring thresholds for hypertension:**

| Period | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Awake | ≥ 135/85 mmHg |
| Asleep | ≥ 120/70 mmHg |
| 24-hour average | ≥ 130/80 mmHg |

Use validated machines, not wrist monitors.

Night systolic should be at least 10% lower than daytime. Patients who do not show this dipping pattern are at higher cardiovascular risk.

**Approximate equivalence between office and ambulatory/home readings:**

| Office BP | Ambulatory or Home BP |
|---|---|
| 130/80 | 125/75 |
| 140/90 | 135/85 |
| 160/100 | 150/96 |

### Checking for Secondary Causes

Your doctor will look for symptoms suggesting an underlying cause for the hypertension:

| Possible cause | Features |
|---|---|
| Obstructive sleep apnoea | Obesity, snoring, daytime sleepiness, Epworth score ≥ 10 |
| Hypokalaemia | Muscle weakness, cramps, hypotonia |
| Hypothyroidism | Tiredness, weight gain, bradycardia |
| Hyperthyroidism | Heat sensitivity, weight loss, tachycardia |
| Cushing syndrome | Central obesity, skin pigmentation, round flushed face |
| Phaeochromocytoma | Frequent headache, sweating, palpitations |

**Medications and substances that can raise blood pressure:**

- NSAIDs
- Stimulants (dexamphetamine, modafinil)
- Oestrogen-containing contraceptives and menopausal hormone therapy
- Corticosteroids
- SNRIs and MAOIs
- Clozapine, bupropion, decongestants, diet pills
- Excessive alcohol, liquorice, caffeine pills and products, energy drinks and guarana, cocaine, amphetamines

### Risk Factors

- Male sex; increasing age
- Lifestyle factors: smoking, obesity, alcohol intake, recreational drug use
- Family history of kidney disease, hypertension, diabetes, dyslipidaemia, stroke, or early-onset heart disease (men aged < 55 years, women aged < 65 years)
- Dyslipidaemia, diabetes, or prediabetes
- Depression, social isolation, or other psychosocial factors

### Cardiovascular and Renal Disease

Your doctor will check for existing:

- Stroke or transient ischaemic attack (TIA)
- Coronary heart disease (angina, previous CABG or PCI)
- Heart failure (including heart failure with preserved ejection fraction)
- Peripheral vascular disease
- Chronic kidney disease (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²)
- Macroalbuminuria (urine ACR > 25 in males or > 35 in females)
- Advanced retinopathy

### Examination

Your doctor will typically measure pulse rate, rhythm and character; jugular venous pressure; waist circumference; and BMI. They will listen to the heart and lungs, assess for arterial disease, check for kidney enlargement, and look for signs of endocrine or retinal abnormalities and any focal neurological signs.

### Investigations

**Initial investigations** (to assess end-organ damage and associated conditions):

- Urine: urinalysis for blood and protein, microalbuminuria and albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR)
- Blood: sodium, potassium, creatinine, eGFR, TSH, FBC, HbA1c, lipid profile, liver function tests
- ECG: check for left ventricular hypertrophy, previous MI

**Further investigations** (if clinically indicated):

- Plasma aldosterone-renin ratio, for primary aldosteronism (ideally before starting beta blockers, diuretics, ACEi, or ARBs; patient should have breakfast and be ambulatory for at least 1 hour before the test)
- Plasma-free metanephrine or 24-hour urinary metanephrine, if phaeochromocytoma is suspected
- 24-hour urinary cortisol or 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test, for Cushing syndrome
- Echocardiogram, for left ventricular hypertrophy

### Secondary Hypertension

This is uncommon. Your doctor may consider it, especially in a younger person, if:

- Hypertension is severe or resistant, persisting despite 3 agents from different classes taken together.
- Acute rise in BP over a previously stable value.
- Age < 40 years with no family history and no obesity.
- Malignant hypertension (severe hypertension with signs of end-organ damage).

Causes include renal disease (e.g., polycystic kidney disease, renal artery stenosis), coarctation of the aorta, endocrine disorders (hyperaldosteronism, Cushing syndrome, phaeochromocytoma), obstructive sleep apnoea, medications (steroids, NSAIDs, amphetamine-type drugs), and baroreflex failure.

### Renovascular Hypertension

Your doctor may consider renovascular causes (e.g., renal artery stenosis) if:

- BP is resistant to maximally tolerated doses of 3 antihypertensive agents.
- The patient is younger than 40 or older than 50 with risk factors for atherosclerotic vascular disease.
- Serum creatinine rises by more than 20% after starting an ACE inhibitor or ARB.

## Management

Hypertension is mostly managed in primary care.

If a secondary cause is suspected, your doctor will investigate and manage it according to the cause. This may involve specialist referral.

### When Are Medications Indicated?

Your doctor will base this on your 5-year cardiovascular disease (CVD) risk:

- **Office BP ≥ 160 mmHg systolic or ≥ 100 mmHg diastolic** (or ≥ 150/95 on ambulatory/home monitoring) after lifestyle modification: treat with medication regardless of CVD risk.
- **5-year CVD risk ≥ 15% (high)** with persistent BP ≥ 130/80 (or ≥ 125/75 on monitoring): lifestyle changes and medication strongly recommended.
- **5-year CVD risk 5–15% (moderate)** with persistent BP ≥ 140/90 (or ≥ 135/85 on monitoring): lifestyle changes, and discuss risks and benefits of medication.

The current New Zealand guidelines are more conservative than European and NICE guidelines. They recommend treatment only at higher CVD risk levels.

For each 10 mmHg reduction in systolic BP, there is approximately a 20% relative reduction in cardiovascular events over 5 years.

Where a calculator is available, your doctor may work out your own absolute risk reduction to help you decide together.

**Very high-risk groups** (treated as > 15% risk):

- Previous CVD event (angina, MI, PCI, CABG, TIA, ischaemic stroke, peripheral vascular disease)
- Congestive heart failure
- Familial hypercholesterolaemia
- Chronic kidney disease (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²)
- Diabetes with overt nephropathy or other renal disease (eGFR < 45 mL/min/1.73 m²)

### Lifestyle Interventions

**Physical activity:** some form of activity is encouraged regardless of age, ability, or other conditions.

- Ages 18–64: aim for 150–300 minutes of moderate-intensity activity or 75–150 minutes of vigorous activity per week, plus muscle-strengthening on at least 2 days.
- Ages > 65: aim for at least 30 minutes of moderate-intensity activity on most days.

**Smoking cessation:** your doctor can refer you to [Quitline](https://quit.org.nz/). They may consider nicotine replacement therapy, or bupropion or varenicline, if you smoke > 10 cigarettes a day and have no contraindications.

**Weight management:** If waist circumference is > 94 cm (men) or > 80 cm (women) and BMI > 25 kg/m², reducing kilojoule intake and increasing activity is advised.

**Dietary changes:**

- Eat more vegetables, fruits, and grains, including low-fat dairy (DASH diet approach).
- Restrict salt to ≤ 4 g/day (65 mmol sodium/day).
- Limit alcohol: no more than 2 standard drinks in one sitting; ≤ 15 standard drinks/week for men, ≤ 10 for women; at least 2 alcohol-free days per week.

### Antihypertensive Medications

Your doctor will select the most appropriate medication based on your age, other medical conditions, and any contraindications.

**Drug selection by age (first-line, absent specific indications):**

- **Aged < 55 years:** ACE inhibitor (ACEi) or angiotensin-II receptor blocker (ARB).
- **Aged ≥ 55 years:** Calcium channel blocker (CCB) or thiazide diuretic.

If systolic BP is > 160 mmHg, your doctor may start a combination of 2 complementary drugs (ACEi or ARB plus a dihydropyridine CCB or a thiazide diuretic) at low doses.

Medicines are usually started at non-minimum doses, except in elderly (aged > 75 years) or frail patients.

Beta blockers are reserved for inadequate BP control with the other 3 main classes, or if specific indications exist (e.g., heart disease, increased sympathetic drive, ACEi intolerance).

**Preferred drugs for common co-conditions:**

| Condition | Preferred agents |
|---|---|
| Angina | Beta blocker, CCB, or ACEi |
| Atrial fibrillation/flutter | Rate-controlling beta blocker or diltiazem |
| Heart failure with reduced ejection fraction | ACEi or ARB, cardioselective beta blocker, or aldosterone antagonist |
| Diabetes | ACEi or ARB, or CCB (avoid beta blockers or thiazide diuretics) |
| Chronic kidney disease | ACEi or ARB (especially with albuminuria) |
| Benign prostatic hypertrophy | Alpha blocker (watch for postural hypotension, especially in older patients) |
| Asthma or COPD | Beta blockers are generally contraindicated in asthma; cardioselective beta blockers (e.g., metoprolol) may be used cautiously in stable COPD, especially if specifically indicated (e.g., heart failure) |

**Effective drug combinations:**

- ACEi or ARB + CCB, particularly in diabetes or dyslipidaemia
- ACEi or ARB + thiazide diuretic, particularly in heart failure or post-stroke
- ACEi or ARB + beta blocker, after MI or in heart failure
- Beta blocker + dihydropyridine CCB, in heart disease

**Combinations to avoid:**

- Beta blocker + verapamil or diltiazem: risk of heart block (unless specifically indicated for AF rate control)
- ACEi + ARB: increased risk of hypotension, syncope, and renal dysfunction
- Thiazide + beta blocker: not recommended in glucose intolerance, metabolic syndrome, or diabetes

**Monitoring:**

- Check electrolytes and creatinine 2 weeks after starting or increasing the dose of an ACEi, ARB, or diuretic.
- Review BP monthly until target is reached.
- If not at target, add a second agent from a different class at low dose. If still not at target, titrate up one agent, then add a third if needed.

**Perindopril (Coversyl) note, August 2026:** the salt in Coversyl is changing. This needs a switch to a slightly different dose and a new prescription. See Pharmac: [Perindopril (Coversyl): Your Medicine is Updating](https://www.pharmac.govt.nz/medicine-funding-and-supply/medicine-notices/coversyl).

### Blood Pressure Targets

Research on optimal BP targets continues. Your doctor will set an individual target, but general guidance is:

- **General minimum target once medication is started:** ≤ 140/90 mmHg.
- **Younger patients and those with high CVD risk, diabetes, CKD, coronary artery disease, or left ventricular hypertrophy:** ideally < 130/80 mmHg.
- **Elderly (> 75 years) or frail patients:** < 130/80 mmHg if tolerable, otherwise ≤ 140/90 mmHg; monitor for orthostatic hypotension.

## Special Groups

### Hypertension in the Elderly

Clinical trials consistently show benefit from antihypertensive treatment, including in those aged > 80 years. However, your doctor will watch closely for:

- Hypotension and syncope
- Electrolyte abnormalities
- Acute kidney injury

In frail elderly patients, the risks of treatment (orthostatic hypotension, falls, polypharmacy interactions) must be weighed against the benefits.

### Isolated Systolic Hypertension

Predominantly a problem in older patients. First-line agents are diuretics or calcium channel blockers.

### Hypertension with Proteinuria

Hypertension is a driver of progressive kidney failure in chronic kidney disease.

- Use an ACEi or ARB, titrated to maximum tolerated dose.
- Consider a systolic target of < 130 mmHg if macroalbuminuria is present (urine ACR > 25 in males or > 35 in females).
- Monitor eGFR.

### Resistant Hypertension

Defined as failure to reach target BP despite appropriate lifestyle measures and 3 drugs from different classes (including a diuretic) at adequate doses.

Your doctor will:

- Exclude non-adherence, white coat hypertension, and incorrectly sized BP cuffs.
- Confirm with ambulatory or home BP measurement.
- Review lifestyle factors and exclude a missed secondary cause, particularly underlying kidney disease.
- If resistant hypertension is confirmed, seek a specialist assessment and consider adding spironolactone 12.5–25 mg daily (approximately twice as effective as adding a beta blocker or long-acting alpha blocker in this setting, with ~8 mmHg vs ~4 mmHg reductions). This requires monitoring of electrolytes and creatinine; watch for hyperkalaemia.

Invasive treatments (e.g., renal denervation) are only considered in specialist centres.

### Pregnancy and Postpartum

Hypertension in pregnancy and the postpartum period is managed under a separate clinical pathway. If you are pregnant or recently gave birth, your doctor will follow specific guidelines for this situation.

## Ongoing Review

Once BP is satisfactory, your doctor will typically review every 6 months:

- Physical examination including heart sounds, BP, weight, BMI, and waist circumference.
- Screening for dyslipidaemia, diabetes, and organ damage: electrolytes, creatinine, eGFR, urine ACR.
- Cardiovascular risk reassessment as appropriate.
- Medication side-effects and adherence check.
- For long-standing hypertension: consider ECG or chest X-ray for end-organ damage.

Your doctor may also seek a specialist assessment if hypertension is resistant (BP > 140/90 despite good adherence to maximally tolerated triple therapy), if you are aged < 30 years, if a secondary cause is suspected, or if it is uncertain whether to start medication.
