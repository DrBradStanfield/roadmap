# NSAIDs and Dyspepsia / Heartburn

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways (last reviewed October 2022)*

This pathway guides doctors on how to safely prescribe NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac) while minimising the risk of stomach and gastrointestinal (GI) side effects.

---

## Important Warning

**NSAIDs and stomach-protecting medications (PPIs) can mask early warning signs.** If someone is taking both an NSAID and a PPI together, serious stomach damage can develop without the usual early symptoms of pain or discomfort — meaning problems may only be noticed when they become more serious.

Your doctor needs to know about all medications you are taking.

---

## When NSAIDs Should Not Be Used (Contraindications)

Your doctor will check whether NSAIDs are safe for you. NSAIDs are generally not suitable if you have:

- Active GI bleeding or an active stomach/gut ulcer
- A past history of:
  - GI bleeding or perforation caused by NSAIDs
  - Two or more episodes of GI bleeding or ulceration
  - Severe allergic reaction to any NSAID (including aspirin)
- Severe heart failure
- Severe liver disease
- Severe kidney impairment (eGFR < 30)
- Current attempts to become pregnant (NSAIDs may impair fertility)

---

## GI Risk Assessment

NSAIDs can cause three levels of GI harm:
1. **Dyspepsia** (indigestion, stomach discomfort)
2. **Mucosal damage** such as ulcers
3. **Ulcer complications** such as bleeding or perforation

### Which NSAIDs carry the most GI risk?

From lowest to highest GI toxicity:
1. Ibuprofen and COX-2 inhibitors (e.g. celecoxib) — lowest risk
2. Aspirin
3. Diclofenac
4. Naproxen
5. Indometacin
6. Piroxicam — highest risk

Note: NSAIDs cause GI harm through a body-wide (systemic) effect — not just local irritation. This applies even when given by injection or suppository, not just by mouth.

### Factors that increase GI risk

Your doctor will categorise your risk based on:

**High risk** — history of a complicated ulcer, OR more than 2 of the following risk factors:

**Risk factors include:**
- Age over 65
- Previous bad reaction to an NSAID
- History of peptic ulcers or GI bleeding
- Taking other medicines that increase GI risk, such as:
  - Anticoagulants (blood thinners like warfarin)
  - Antiplatelet drugs (e.g. clopidogrel)
  - SSRIs (antidepressants)
  - Corticosteroids (e.g. prednisone)
- Serious other health conditions such as liver disease, chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular disease, diabetes, or dehydration
- Heavy smoking
- Excessive alcohol use
- Needing NSAIDs long-term or at high doses
- *Helicobacter pylori* infection

**Moderate risk** — 1 to 2 risk factors present
**Low risk** — no risk factors

---

## Management — What Your Doctor Will Do

Your doctor's decisions will be based on your risk level:

1. **If an NSAID is needed, ibuprofen is usually tried first** — it has the lowest GI toxicity of the standard NSAIDs.

2. **The lowest effective dose for the shortest time possible** is always the goal.

3. **If you have one or more risk factors:**
   - Ideally, NSAIDs should be avoided — your doctor will consider alternatives for pain relief.
   - If NSAIDs are unavoidable, your doctor will either:
     - Add a **PPI (proton pump inhibitor)** — typically omeprazole 20 mg daily or pantoprazole 20 mg daily to protect the stomach lining, or
     - Consider switching to a **COX-2 inhibitor** (e.g. celecoxib) which has less GI toxicity — but note this protection is lost if you are also taking aspirin.
   - If you have a high risk of GI complications (e.g. previous NSAID-related GI bleeding), both a COX-2 inhibitor AND a PPI should be used together.

4. **If you have no risk factors:**
   - A standard NSAID can be used with monitoring for any side effects.

5. **If you develop dyspepsia (indigestion/stomach pain) while on an NSAID:**
   - Your doctor will either stop the NSAID and try a different option or COX-2 inhibitor, or
   - Continue on the lowest effective NSAID dose while adding a PPI.

6. **If stomach symptoms persist**, your doctor will follow the Dyspepsia and Heartburn / GORD pathway for further assessment.

---

## When Referral to a Specialist Is Needed

Your doctor may request a non-urgent gastroenterology (stomach specialist) assessment if:
- You have **severe symptoms** combined with **risk factors** for GI complications

This is a decision for your doctor based on the full clinical picture.
