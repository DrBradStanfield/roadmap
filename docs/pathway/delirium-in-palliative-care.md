# Delirium in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers management of delirium in adults receiving palliative care for a life-limiting illness. See also the general [Delirium](delirium.md) pathway.

---

## Background

Delirium is a global disorder of cognition and attention with disorientation and often visual hallucinations. It is common towards the end of life and can be distressing for both patients and their carers.

---

## Recognising Delirium

Delirium typically involves:
- Disorganised thinking
- Inattention
- Altered levels of consciousness
- Rapid onset
- Fluctuating course across the day

Your doctor may use the **Confusion Assessment Method (CAM)** to assess this.

There are three types:
- **Hyperactive** — increased arousal and agitation (this is what people usually picture)
- **Hypoactive** — quiet, withdrawn, and inactive. This type is more common but is often missed or misdiagnosed as depression.
- **Mixed pattern** — features of both

Your doctor will distinguish delirium from dementia and restlessness, and will talk with caregivers to understand what changes have occurred.

---

## Common Causes

Your doctor will consider reversible causes:
- Unfamiliar environment or excessive stimuli
- Pain, fatigue, or pressure areas
- Anxiety or depression
- Organ failure (hepatic, renal)
- Cerebral tumours, brain metastases, or leptomeningeal disease
- Hypercalcaemia, dehydration, or biochemical abnormalities
- Low oxygen levels (hypoxia)
- Infection or sepsis
- Drug toxicity (e.g. amitriptyline, opioids, steroids)
- Urinary retention, faecal impaction, or constipation
- Drug or alcohol withdrawal

---

## Management

### Treating Underlying Causes

Where appropriate, your doctor may:
- Stop or reduce medications with CNS effects
- Ensure adequate pain relief
- Give oxygen if the patient is hypoxic
- Give high-dose dexamethasone (e.g. 16 mg) if a cerebral tumour is the cause

### Supportive Care

- Acknowledging the distress delirium causes to patients and families
- Minimising staff changes
- Encouraging the presence of familiar family members
- Maintaining a calm environment with frequent gentle re-orientation

### Medications

Medications are used when the patient has troublesome symptoms such as nocturnal confusion, agitation, aggression, hallucinations, or paranoia.

**Haloperidol** is the first-line medication (not used if the patient has Parkinson disease):
- Starting dose: 0.5 to 1 mg subcutaneously, repeated after 30 minutes if needed
- Maximum daily dose: 5 mg (3 mg in elderly patients)
- Given as a regular twice-daily dose (subcutaneously or orally) or continuous subcutaneous infusion
- If higher doses or other medications are needed, your doctor will seek specialist palliative care advice

**Note on benzodiazepines:** These can worsen delirium but may be used in a dying patient when sedation or anxiolysis is needed alongside an antipsychotic.

### Intractable Delirium in a Dying Patient

When haloperidol alone is insufficient (≥ 5 mg per 24 hours required), your doctor may add **midazolam**:
- Oral (buccal or sublingual): 2.5 mg from a 15 mg/3 mL ampoule
- Subcutaneous (crisis): 2.5 to 5 mg up to every 15–30 minutes as needed
- Continuous infusion: starting at 10 to 20 mg over 24 hours; higher doses require specialist palliative care guidance

Midazolam has a short half-life, making it preferable to clonazepam in acute situations. It is fully funded in the community.

If the patient does not settle with 5 mg haloperidol per 24 hours plus midazolam, your doctor may switch to **levomepromazine** — this requires specialist advice.

In palliative care, doses, routes, and indications may be outside standard licensed use. Your doctor will explain the rationale.

---

## When to Involve a Specialist

Your doctor will seek adult palliative care support and advice for:
- Higher doses of haloperidol or other medications needed
- Intractable delirium in a dying patient
- Anticipatory planning and acute management
- Arranging a joint specialist visit
- Considering hospice admission

---

*This information is intended to support conversations with your doctor. Management of palliative symptoms requires individualised clinical judgement — always discuss your situation with your treating team.*
