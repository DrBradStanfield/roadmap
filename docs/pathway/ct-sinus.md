# CT Sinus

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway explains when a CT sinus scan may be considered — including serious warning signs, the criteria your doctor uses to decide if a scan is appropriate, and what to do if concerning symptoms develop.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

If you have sinusitis symptoms **plus any of the following**, this may indicate a serious complication (such as spread to the eye socket or brain). Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance:

**Signs of orbital (eye socket) involvement:**
- Significant eye pain
- Swelling and redness of the eyelid
- Pain when moving the eyes
- Double vision
- Decreased vision
- Proptosis (eye appearing to protrude)

**Signs of intracranial (brain) involvement:**
- Severe frontal headache
- Swelling over the forehead
- Neck stiffness
- Photophobia (sensitivity to light)
- Focal neurological deficit (weakness, numbness, speech changes)
- Decreased level of consciousness

**Other emergency features:**
- Severe systemic symptoms (high fever, appearing very unwell)

---

## Red Flags for Sinonasal Malignancy — See Your Doctor Urgently

If you have sinus symptoms with any of the following, see your doctor promptly — these require urgent specialist assessment rather than a CT scan:

- Unilateral (one-sided) nasal obstruction with bloodstained discharge
- New onset of Horner syndrome, double vision, or changes to facial sensation (cheek, upper gum, or hard palate)
- Numbness or tingling in the skin of the cheek, maxillary gums, or hard palate

---

## When a CT Sinus May Be Considered

Your doctor may consider a CT sinus scan in the following situations:

**Chronic rhinosinusitis not responding to treatment:**
- Symptoms lasting 12 weeks or more (nasal blockage, congestion, or discharge, plus facial pain/pressure, reduced smell, or persistent cough)
- Moderate to severe symptoms despite daily use of both a nasal corticosteroid spray *and* saline nasal irrigation for at least 12 weeks
- Good treatment technique and compliance confirmed
- No previous CT sinus scan done

**Specialist recommendation:**
- An ORL (Ear, Nose and Throat) specialist, radiologist, or primary care radiology liaison has recommended a CT sinus.

---

## Radiation Considerations

CT scans use ionising radiation. Your doctor will weigh the benefits against a small potential risk:

- CT scanning is avoided in pregnancy unless essential
- For patients under 40, your doctor may consider alternatives to minimise cumulative radiation exposure over time
- IV contrast (dye) is **not commonly required** for CT sinus scans

---

## What Happens After a CT Sinus

Depending on results, your doctor may:

- Continue or adjust treatment for chronic rhinosinusitis
- Refer urgently to an ENT (ORL) specialist if a unilateral polyp or suspected malignancy is found
- Seek specialist advice if you are immunocompromised with severe sinusitis (e.g., suspected invasive fungal sinusitis)

Publicly funded follow-up CT scans to check treatment response are not available — this would need to be done privately if required.
