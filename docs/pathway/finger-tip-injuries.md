# Finger Tip Injuries

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and initial management of fingertip injuries, including crush injuries, lacerations, fractures, nail injuries, and amputations.

---

## Background

Fingertip injuries are common and often result from crush or sharp injuries — for example, from knives, glass, or doors — affecting the terminal phalanx (the tip of the finger).

Types of injury include:

- Subungual haematoma (blood under the nail)
- Nail root avulsions
- Fractures or dislocations
- Tendon injury
- Amputation

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will examine:

- The nail and nail bed for involvement
- Whether bone is involved
- Any deformity
- Viability of the fingertip — including neurovascular function (circulation and sensation)
- Any foreign body (e.g., glass)
- Any tendon injury
- Whether any amputated tissue is present
- Skin damage

Your doctor will also test resisted flexion and extension and stress-test the collateral ligaments. All findings — especially sensation — are documented before any treatment begins.

An **X-ray** is arranged for injuries where a fracture or foreign body is possible.

---

## Management

Management depends on the type of injury. Your doctor will assess which approach is appropriate.

### When Urgent Hand Surgery Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will seek an urgent hand surgery assessment if:

- Amputated tissue is present and replantation may be possible
- Pulp (soft tissue at the fingertip) is missing
- Bone is exposed
- The nail bed is injured with incongruity, or the nail bed is missing
- The fracture of the terminal phalanx involves the joint, is significantly displaced, or is a compound fracture

### Phalangeal Tuft Fracture

A fracture of the fingertip bone — often comminuted (multiple fragments) but usually stable and not displaced. Commonly associated with a subungual haematoma.

Your doctor may consider:

- If there is a laceration: treat as a compound fracture and refer for hand surgery assessment
- If the nail plate is intact: leave it in place and apply a mallet splint (padded aluminium or plastic)
- If the nail plate is damaged: refer for hand surgery assessment for removal or repair
- If there is a painful subungual haematoma not involving the nail bed: draining the haematoma using electrocautery, a heated paper clip, or an 18G needle

### Transverse Fracture of the Distal Phalanx

- If stable: your doctor will apply a splint
- If displaced and cannot be reduced: hand surgery assessment to check whether the nail bed is caught in the fracture

### Mallet Deformity

A mallet deformity occurs when forced flexion of an extended fingertip avulses (tears) the extensor tendon attachment, causing a drooping fingertip. There may also be a small bone fragment (avulsion fracture) at the back of the joint.

Your doctor will suspect mallet deformity if:

- The injury involved forced flexion of the extended distal interphalangeal (DIP) joint
- Examination shows loss of extension at the DIP joint and tenderness over the back of the joint

An X-ray is taken for all mallet deformities, as management depends on whether there is an avulsion fracture.

**If no avulsion fracture:**

- The fingertip is hyperextended and held in place with Mefix tape, then placed in a mallet splint for 6 weeks
- Hand therapy is arranged for a custom-made mallet splint
- At 6 weeks, the splint is weaned over the following 6 weeks

**If avulsion fracture is present:**

- A true lateral X-ray is arranged to check for volar subluxation (often missed)
- If volar subluxation or incongruent joint surface: hand surgery assessment
- Otherwise: the joint is fixed in neutral or slight flexion with a mallet splint for 6 weeks — the fingertip is not hyperextended, as this risks displacing the fracture fragment
- X-ray at 6 weeks to confirm fracture healing

**In children with mallet deformity:** the nail bed is checked for involvement before treatment. If there is a nail bed laceration, contusion, or widening of the growth plate on lateral X-ray, hand surgery assessment is arranged.

### Subungual Haematoma

Blood under the nail may be drained if:

- It involves more than 50% of the nail area, or
- It is painful and does not involve the nail bed

Drainage can be performed using electrocautery, a heated paper clip, or an 18G needle. If there are concerns about nail bed incongruity, hand surgery advice is sought.

### General Measures

Your doctor may consider:

- A **digital nerve block** for adequate pain relief before assessment or minor procedures
- **Tetanus vaccine** (ADT Booster) if the wound is tetanus-prone
- **Prophylactic antibiotics**, particularly if bone is involved or the wound is dirty
- **Minor suturing** in primary care using 5.0 or 6.0 nylon
- **Wound care**: clean and cover with a non-adherent dressing
- **Elevation** in a high sling with early mobilisation
- **Monitoring for infection**

### Allied Health Support

Your doctor may refer you to:

- **Hand therapy** (physiotherapy): if there is a functional deficit, associated hypersensitivity, or a splint is required
- **Occupational therapy**: if you are having difficulty with everyday activities such as self-care, household tasks, driving, work, or study
- **Work assessment and rehabilitation**: if you are having difficulty returning to or sustaining work
