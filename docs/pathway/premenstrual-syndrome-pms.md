# Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers premenstrual syndrome (PMS), premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD), and premenstrual exacerbation (PME).

---

## Background

- **PMS** is defined as psychological and physical symptoms that occur in the luteal phase of the menstrual cycle, cease when menstruation starts, and are followed by a symptom-free week.
- Up to 40% of people who menstruate may have PMS symptoms. Symptoms can begin at any age from menarche to menopause.
- The proposed mechanism involves ovulation triggering changes in neurotransmitters, including a luteal phase reduction in serotonin.
- People with premenstrual disorders appear to be at higher risk of mood disorders during the menopausal transition.
- Diagnosis is made by tracking symptoms over two cycles — retrospective recall is unreliable.

- **PMDD** (Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder) is the DSM-5 definition of severe PMS. Symptoms cause significant distress and interfere with work, study, social interactions, and relationships. Around 3–8% of menstruating people have symptoms severe enough to meet PMDD criteria. PMDD symptoms can mimic depression, anxiety, panic disorder, bipolar disorder, or borderline personality disorder.

- **PME** (Premenstrual Exacerbation) refers to worsening of existing symptoms in the luteal phase. Standard PMS/PMDD treatments are not evidence-based for PME. People with PME are often sensitive to medications (including hormonal treatments). Mental health support is central to management.

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed menstrual and symptom history to establish the relationship between symptoms and cycle phase.

**Affective or behavioural symptoms your doctor will ask about:**
- Mood swings
- Irritability or anger
- Anxiety
- Depression
- Reduced interest in usual activities
- Difficulty concentrating
- Loss of confidence
- Food cravings
- Sleep disturbance
- Changes to libido
- Reduced cognitive ability

**Physical symptoms your doctor will ask about:**
- Breast pain
- Joint or muscle pain
- Abdominal bloating
- Fatigue
- Headache
- Weight gain or fluid retention
- Hot flushes (in people who are not postpartum or peri/postmenopausal)

Your doctor will assess whether symptoms:
- Occur repetitively in the second half of the menstrual cycle
- Disappear by end of menstruation (with a symptom-free follicular phase)
- Impair daily activity

Note: more than 150 symptoms have been associated with PMS and not all presentations follow a classical cyclical pattern.

**Severity grading:**
- **Mild** — does not interfere with personal, social, or professional life
- **Moderate** — some impact on personal, social, or professional life
- **Severe** — significant impact on relationships, work, and social functioning; associated with risk of self-harm

**Your doctor will also consider underlying conditions that may mimic or worsen PMS/PMDD:**
- Depression or anxiety disorders
- Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) or other androgen disorders
- Thyroid disorders
- Substance use
- Early perimenopause (can cause PMS to appear for the first time between ages 30–40)
- History of domestic violence, physical or emotional trauma

**Investigations:**
- Routine ovarian hormone blood tests are not recommended — they are often normal in PMS.
- Your doctor may consider thyroid function tests, testosterone, calcium, iron studies, FBC, liver function, and creatinine to screen for other causes.

## Management

Your doctor will discuss the following approaches with you.

### Symptom Tracking

Keeping a symptom diary over at least two cycles is important — it helps confirm the diagnosis and track whether treatment is working. Options include:

- **Daily Record of Severity of Problems (DRSP)** — a printable symptom tracker
- **Me v PMDD** app (free, from IAMPD)
- **Clue** period tracker (free version available)
- **Flo** period tracker (free)

Tracking is especially important if menstruation is absent (e.g., after hysterectomy with ovarian conservation, or with a Mirena IUD).

### Lifestyle Measures

All patients can benefit from lifestyle changes, regardless of symptom severity:

- **Regular physical activity**
- **Healthy diet** — eating regular complex carbohydrates throughout the day may help reduce premenstrual cravings
- **Limiting excess alcohol**
- **Psychological support** — understanding that functioning differently at different cycle phases is normal and okay. Planning demanding activities for better days and lower-intensity tasks on harder days can help.
- **Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)** — recommended for moderate to severe symptoms. Studies show better long-term treatment outcomes with CBT.

### Supplements

Evidence is limited but the following have some support:

- **Calcium** 1000 mg/day and **magnesium** ≥ 300 mg/day have shown benefit in studies.
- **Vitamin B6** — limited evidence; systematic reviews of lower-quality studies suggest doses up to 100 mg/day may be more effective than placebo. High dietary (not supplement) intake has been associated with lower PMS risk.
- **Evening primrose oil** — inconclusive evidence overall; some patients find ≥ 3000 mg/day helps with cyclical breast pain (mastalgia), though this may be a placebo effect. Four months of continuous use is needed to assess effectiveness.
- **Chaste berry (Vitex agnus-castus)** and **St John's wort** may help with mild premenstrual symptoms (less evidence for PMDD). Further studies are needed.

### Medications

Your doctor may consider medication — either hormonal therapy or an SSRI — as first-line treatment, depending on your symptoms and whether contraception is desired. Severe symptoms may require both.

**Hormonal therapy:**

- **Combined oral contraceptives (COCs)** — first-line hormonal option. Drospirenone-containing COCs are preferred (though not currently funded in New Zealand). Continuous use (skipping hormone-free breaks) may prevent symptom recurrence. Some people find the pill worsens symptoms.
- **Topical estradiol patches + progestogen** — considered for severe symptoms when other options have failed or are unsuitable (in premenopausal patients only, after specialist advice). Estradiol 100 microgram patches are used with either micronised progesterone (e.g., Utrogestan) for 12 days of the cycle, or a levonorgestrel-containing IUD for endometrial protection. Barrier contraception is needed if pregnancy is not desired, as ovulation may not be reliably suppressed.

**SSRIs (Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors):**

SSRIs are considered first-line treatment for severe PMS and PMDD. They can be taken continuously or only during the luteal phase (day 14 of the cycle until bleeding begins). Benefit is typically seen within 3 cycles.

Your doctor may consider:
- **Escitalopram** 10 mg/day
- **Citalopram** 20 mg/day
- **Sertraline** 50–100 mg/day (approved indication; lowest transfer into breast milk)
- **Fluoxetine** 20 mg/day (approved indication)

Luteal-phase-only dosing is less likely to cause dependence and benefits are more immediate. Continuous dosing may be more effective for some people.

Common side effects include nausea, insomnia, drowsiness, fatigue, and reduced libido. If also taking NSAIDs, there is an increased risk of gastrointestinal bleeding.

Your doctor will review you 1 week after starting. A 3-month trial is typical; if beneficial, treatment continues for 6–12 months.

30–40% of people do not respond to an initial SSRI. If there is no response after several cycles, switching to a different SSRI or changing from luteal-phase to daily dosing may help.

**Spironolactone** 25–100 mg/day during the luteal phase may be considered if fluid retention is a prominent symptom. It has a negative interaction with NSAIDs and may have an anti-androgen effect if pregnancy occurs.

### If Symptoms Do Not Improve

If severe symptoms persist after adequate primary care management, your doctor may refer to a gynaecologist or reproductive endocrinologist for specialist options, including:

- Gonadotrophin-releasing hormone (GnRH) agonists with estrogen-progestogen add-back therapy
- Hysterectomy and bilateral salpingo-oophorectomy (only considered for severe, disabling symptoms that have not responded to GnRH agonist therapy for at least 6 months)
