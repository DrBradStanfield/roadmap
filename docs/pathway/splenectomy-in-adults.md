# Splenectomy in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the long-term management of adults who have had a splenectomy or who have hyposplenism — including infection prevention, antibiotic prophylaxis, vaccinations, and special situations such as animal bites and travel.

---

## Key Risk

Patients who have had a splenectomy or have hyposplenism are at **increased risk of life-threatening infection**, particularly from encapsulated bacteria. This risk persists lifelong. Your doctor will explain this to you and put preventive measures in place.

---

## Recognising Hyposplenism

If hyposplenism is found unexpectedly (rather than after planned surgery), your doctor will:

- Look at a blood film for **Howell-Jolly bodies** in red cells (a sign of absent splenic function). Other findings may include target cells, irregular contracted red blood cells, and mild thrombocytosis or leukocytosis.
- Screen for associated conditions such as inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease, or autoimmune disease.

---

## Management After Splenectomy or with Hyposplenism

Patients with hyposplenism are managed the same way as those who have had a surgical splenectomy.

### 1. Infection Awareness and Education

Your doctor will provide education and written patient information about the infection risk. Key points you should know:

- Wear a **medical alert bracelet** stating "post-splenectomy" or "hyposplenism".
- **Seek prompt medical review when unwell.** Do not delay.

**Symptoms that require immediate GP review:**
- Cough with purulent sputum (green, brown, or yellow)
- Moderate to severe sore throat
- Flu-like illness with tiredness, aching, headache, vomiting, diarrhoea, or abdominal pain

**If you develop rigors (shaking chills), fever, or low blood pressure:** this is a medical emergency requiring IV antibiotics — go to an emergency department immediately. Take blood cultures before starting antibiotics if this does not cause delay.

### 2. Emergency Antibiotic Supply

Your doctor will prescribe an emergency supply of antibiotics to carry at all times and use if you cannot get prompt medical attention (e.g., at onset of fever or rigors):

- **Standard:** Amoxicillin 3 g as a starting dose, then 1 g every 8 hours
- **Mild penicillin allergy:** Cephalexin 500 mg three times a day
- **Immediate penicillin allergy (e.g., anaphylaxis):** Roxithromycin 300 mg once a day

Always carry these antibiotics, including on holiday. Check the expiry date regularly. Your doctor will explain when to start them with each new prescription.

### 3. Prophylactic Antibiotics

Your doctor will prescribe daily preventive antibiotics:

- **Standard:** Phenoxymethylpenicillin 250 mg twice daily, or amoxicillin 250 mg once daily
- **Penicillin allergy:** Roxithromycin 150 mg once daily, or erythromycin ethyl succinate 400 mg once daily

**Duration:**
- Continue for a minimum of **2 years**.
- After 2 years, your doctor will discuss whether to stop or continue long-term.
- **Lifelong prophylaxis is recommended** if you:
  - Are immunosuppressed or need ongoing immunosuppression
  - Have had an episode of severe sepsis
  - Had a splenectomy for a haematological disorder or malignancy
- The Australasian guidelines recommend at least 3 years. Some guidelines recommend lifelong antibiotics given that sepsis risk continues throughout life — discuss this with your doctor.

### 4. Vaccinations

Your doctor will ensure you are vaccinated against common encapsulated organisms (as per the Te Whatu Ora funded vaccines for special groups, section on Asplenia):

- Ideally given before elective splenectomy, or as soon as possible after recovery from surgery.
- All patients with hyposplenism need these vaccinations.
- Your doctor will advise on the exact vaccines and timing — there are specific requirements around spacing some vaccines (e.g., pneumococcal and meningococcal).

### 5. Animal or Human Bites

Bites carry a greatly increased risk of serious infection in splenectomy patients. Your doctor will prescribe:

- **Standard:** Amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 625 mg three times a day for 5 days
- **Penicillin allergy:** Clindamycin 450 mg three times a day plus either cotrimoxazole 160/800 mg twice daily or ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily (specialist endorsement required for clindamycin)

Seek medical advice promptly after any bite. See also the Cellulitis in Adults pathway.

### 6. Travel Advice

- **Malaria-endemic areas:** Travel is discouraged. If unavoidable, strict adherence to anti-malarial prophylaxis and anti-mosquito measures is essential — splenectomy patients are at high risk of severe falciparum malaria.
- **Meningococcal risk areas** (e.g., sub-Saharan Africa): Consider a booster of meningococcal ACYW135 vaccine before travel.
- Specialist travel advice may be needed before trips to high-risk areas.

---

## When to Seek Emergency Care

Go to an emergency department or call 111 if you have:

- Rigors (uncontrollable shaking chills), fever, or low blood pressure
- Rapid deterioration with any infection-like illness

Take your emergency antibiotics on the way if there may be any delay in being seen, but do not delay going to hospital.

---

## When Your Doctor Will Refer

Your doctor will refer to or seek advice from specialists if:

- You develop rigors, fever, or hypotension — **emergency department assessment** required
- You have an animal or human bite without serious symptoms — **infectious diseases advice** as needed
