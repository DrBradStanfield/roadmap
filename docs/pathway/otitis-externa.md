# Otitis Externa

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of otitis externa — inflammation of the outer ear canal.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

See a doctor urgently or go to the emergency department if you have:

- Vesicles (blisters) and scabs in or around the ear
- Facial nerve palsy (weakness or drooping of the face)
- Ear pain that seems out of proportion to what the doctor can see
- Fever, feeling systemically unwell, and headache, swollen lymph nodes, or difficulty opening the mouth (trismus)
- Tenderness and swelling over the mastoid region (the bone behind the ear)

---

## Background

Otitis externa is a common condition involving inflammation of the ear canal.

**Acute otitis externa:**
- Is primarily caused by bacterial or fungal infection. *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* and *Staphylococcus aureus* are the most common bacteria. In 10% of cases there is a fungal cause such as *Aspergillus* or *Candida*. Mixed infection is common.
- Often occurs due to repeated water exposure and contamination, and is more common in summer.

**Chronic otitis externa** (lasting more than 3 months) is often secondary to an underlying inflammatory skin condition and can be worsened by excessive cleaning or scratching.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history and ask about symptoms and risk factors.

**Symptoms to report include:**
- Ear pain (otalgia)
- Discharge from the ear (sometimes foul-smelling)
- Itching (pruritus)
- Pain when moving the tragus (the small flap at the front of the ear opening) or pinna, or when chewing
- Swelling of the pinna
- Swollen lymph nodes
- Blocked ear or impaired hearing
- Vertigo or dizziness
- Facial weakness
- Fever (temperature above 38.5°C suggests infection has spread beyond the ear canal)
- Recent upper respiratory tract infection
- Symptoms suggestive of shingles (herpes zoster prodrome)

**Risk factors your doctor will consider:**
- Excessive moisture — from bathing or swimming, especially in fresh water
- Abnormal ear canal (e.g., too narrow, bony growths called exostoses)
- Trauma — ear picking, cotton buds, ear syringing, or recent ear surgery
- Foreign body in the ear
- Skin conditions such as eczema, psoriasis, or seborrhoeic dermatitis
- Chronic ear discharge (e.g., from glue ear)
- Hearing aids or frequent ear plug use
- Diabetes or immunocompromise

**Examination:** Your doctor will look inside the ear canal (otoscopy), checking for rashes or vesicles in the ear canal opening, severity of inflammation, discharge, and whether the eardrum is intact. They will also check whether moving the ear causes pain, and look for redness, swelling, or lymph node enlargement around the ear.

---

## Possible Diagnoses

### Bacterial Otitis Externa
- Ear pain (can be mild to severe)
- Canal swelling, possibly extending to the pinna
- White-to-yellow discharge; green discharge may indicate *Pseudomonas*
- Trismus or lymphadenopathy may be present

### Fungal Otitis Externa
- Severe itching; ear pain is usually mild
- Feeling of fullness in the ear
- Fungal material (off-white, grey, or black) visible in the canal
- Can be associated with a perforated eardrum

### Other Causes of Ear Discharge
- **Ramsay Hunt syndrome** (herpes zoster oticus) — painful rash with fluid-filled blisters in or around the ear, and possibly facial weakness or hearing loss
- **Dermatitis or eczema** — usually chronic, very itchy, may involve skin elsewhere
- **Acute otitis media with perforated eardrum** — severe ear pain before the perforation, then whitish-to-yellow discharge
- **Chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM)** — history of ear infections, perforated eardrum with recurrent discharge lasting more than 6 weeks, usually not painful
- **Cholesteatoma** — destructive growth in the middle ear
- **Foreign body**
- **Lateral skull base osteomyelitis (malignant otitis externa)** — uncommon; occurs mainly in older patients with diabetes or immunocompromise

## Management

> Your doctor will guide your treatment. The information below reflects the type of care typically considered for otitis externa.

### Ear Canal Cleaning

Cleaning the ear canal is one of the most important steps in management — it exposes the canal surfaces to topical treatment. Your doctor may arrange microsuction to clear debris. Do not syringe the ear with water.

To help at home, your doctor may advise dry mopping every 6 hours using tissue spears (twisted tissue paper).

### Ear Drops

Studies have not shown a clear difference between different types of ear drops. Your doctor may consider:

**Mild otitis externa:**
- Acetic acid with isopropyl alcohol and glycerine (e.g., Audisol Dry Ears spray) — typically 5 drops three times a day until symptoms resolve
- Review after 1 week, or sooner if worsening

**Moderate to severe bacterial otitis externa:**
- Your doctor may consider an antibiotic and steroid combination drop such as dexamethasone + framycetin + gramicidin (Otodex or Sofradex) — typically 2–3 drops three to four times a day
- Used for a further 3 days after symptoms resolve; more severe infections may need 10–14 days
- If *Pseudomonas* is suspected, ciprofloxacin + hydrocortisone (Ciproxin HC) may be used — 3 drops twice a day for 5–7 days

**Moderate to severe fungal otitis externa:**
- Your doctor may consider flumetasone pivalate + clioquinol (Locacorten Vioform or Locorten Vioform) — typically 2–3 drops twice a day for 7–10 days (do not exceed 10 days). Note: this drop can stain hair, clothing, and bedding.
- Second-line: triamcinolone + neomycin + gramicidin + nystatin (Kenacomb) — 2–3 drops two to four times a day for 5–10 days

**Underlying skin condition (e.g., eczema, psoriasis):**
- Your doctor may consider a topical steroid such as mometasone furoate (Elocon lotion 0.1%) or framycetin sulfate (Soframycin)

**Using ear drops correctly:**
- Mop any discharge from the ear with twisted tissue paper before inserting drops
- Lie with the affected ear pointing upwards
- Instil drops without the dropper touching the ear canal
- Press gently on the tragus several times to help the drops penetrate
- Remain in position for 3 minutes
- Do not plug the ear canal after drops

### Oral Antibiotics

Oral antibiotics are not required for most cases of otitis externa. Your doctor may consider them if:
- Otitis externa is severe or persistent
- You have systemic symptoms (fever, feeling unwell)
- There is early cellulitis of the pinna (perichondritis) or facial cellulitis
- You have diabetes or are immunocompromised

If oral antibiotics are needed, your doctor may consider:
- **Flucloxacillin** (first choice) — adults: 1 g four times a day for 5 days; children: 12.5–25 mg/kg four times a day for 5 days
- **Cefalexin** — if non-severe beta-lactam allergy; adults: 1 g three to four times a day for 5 days
- **Trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole (co-trimoxazole)** — if severe beta-lactam allergy; adults: 960 mg twice a day for 5 days

### Pain Relief

Your doctor may recommend paracetamol or anti-inflammatory medication (NSAIDs) for pain relief if needed and not contraindicated.

### Keeping the Ear Dry

Your doctor will advise keeping the ear canal dry for at least 14 days:
- Wear ear plugs or a shower cap in the shower (may be painful if inflammation is severe; a cotton ball coated in Vaseline is a cheap, effective alternative)
- Dry mop the ear every 6 hours with tissue spears
- Avoid submerging your head in water (no swimming)

### Swab for Microscopy and Culture

If the diagnosis is uncertain, the infection is not responding to first-line treatment, or there is recurrent discharge, your doctor may take a swab to identify the specific organism and guide treatment.

### Prevention of Recurrence

If you are prone to repeat infections, your doctor may advise:
- Wearing a good swimming cap or ear plugs while swimming
- Drying the ear thoroughly after swimming or showering (towel or hairdryer)
- Using 2% acetic acid in isopropyl alcohol after swimming (e.g., Audisol Dry Ears Spray) as a preventive measure
- Avoiding putting anything into the ear canal (including cotton buds)
- Note: long-term topical antibiotics are not used for prevention, as they can cause fungal superinfection

---

## When Your Doctor May Refer

Your doctor may refer you to an ear, nose, and throat (ORL/ENT) specialist if:
- There are signs of complicated infection (Ramsay Hunt syndrome, mastoiditis, malignant otitis externa, intracranial infection, or cellulitis spreading beyond the ear canal)
- Canal swelling is so severe it prevents treatment
- Repeat infections are not responding to culture-directed treatment
