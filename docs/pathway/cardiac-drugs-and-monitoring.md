# Cardiac Drugs and Monitoring

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: September 2026.*

This page provides an overview of common cardiac medications. Cardiologists often start these drugs, and general practice then monitors them. Your doctor manages and reviews these medications. Do not make changes without consulting them first.

Full prescribing information is not included here. Your doctor will check the New Zealand Formulary or their prescribing software for complete details.

> This page is general information, not medical advice. Always talk to your doctor or cardiologist before starting, stopping, or changing a cardiac medicine.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Help

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department** if, while taking one of these medicines, you have:

- Fainting (syncope). Amiodarone and sotalol can cause dangerous heart rhythms.
- Severe or abnormal shortness of breath
- Blurred vision, eye pain, or reduced vision while taking amiodarone (possible optic neuritis)
- Signs of a severe infection of the genitals or the skin around the genitals or anus while taking an SGLT2 inhibitor. This may be Fournier's gangrene, which is rare but can be life-threatening.

**See your doctor or go to hospital the same day to have your blood ketones checked** if you take an SGLT2 inhibitor and develop:

- Nausea, vomiting, or abdominal pain
- A fast heart rate, excessive thirst, shortness of breath, loss of appetite, dizziness, or confusion

Your blood glucose may be normal or only slightly raised, even with ketoacidosis.

If you are confused, very drowsy, or struggling to breathe, call 111 or go to the emergency department.

If a finger-prick test shows blood ketones over 1.5 mmol/L while you take an SGLT2 inhibitor, call 111 or go to the emergency department.

**Contact your doctor** if you notice:

- Palpitations or feeling faint. Your doctor may repeat an ECG.
- Signs of bleeding, such as from the gut, while taking clopidogrel or ticagrelor. Do not stop the medicine without advice.
- A slower change in vision while taking amiodarone. This needs an eye assessment within 1 to 2 weeks.

## Amiodarone

Amiodarone is mainly a Class III anti-arrhythmic drug. It prolongs the heart's refractory period and slows conduction through the AV node. It is very effective and does not weaken the heart's pumping, making it useful for people with heart failure who also have rhythm disturbances.

**Amiodarone is usually started by a hospital specialist (such as a cardiologist, general physician, or cardiac surgeon), or under their supervision.** If your GP starts it, they will seek specialist advice for an individual plan and discuss the possible side effects with you first. Your doctor will manage ongoing monitoring.

### Indications

- Atrial fibrillation (AF)
- Ventricular tachycardias

### How Your Doctor Will Use It

Amiodarone needs a high starting dose because it takes a long time to reach a stable level in the body. Dosing varies with circumstances. A common regime is:

- Week 1: 200 mg three times daily
- Week 2: 200 mg twice daily
- Week 3 onward: 200 mg or 100 mg daily

The minimum effective dose is used, usually 200 mg daily.

If your pulse is slow or you report palpitations or pre-syncope at a review, your doctor will repeat an ECG. If second or third degree heart block is detected, the drug will be stopped. No tapering is needed when stopping, due to the drug's very long half-life (up to 150 days).

### Adverse Effects

Most side effects take time to develop, but some can occur early and may be severe. Side effects during the loading phase may improve with a lower dose or slower loading. Because of the long half-life, side effects can persist for up to 3 to 6 months after stopping the drug. Your doctor will watch for adverse effects for up to 18 months after cessation.

**Skin**
- Photosensitivity is common. Protect yourself with sunscreen and covering clothing.
- Rarely, a persistent grey or blue skin discolouration may develop.

**Thyroid**
- Both overactive (hyperthyroidism) and underactive (hypothyroidism) thyroid can occur.
- More common with higher doses and in people with pre-existing thyroid conditions.
- Thyroid dysfunction can be difficult to treat. Your doctor may seek cardiology or endocrinology advice.

**Eyes**
- Optic neuritis (rare but serious): presents with blurred vision, eye pain, and reduced visual acuity. Call 111 or go to the emergency department.
- Less acute visual disturbance: needs an urgent eye assessment within 1 to 2 weeks.
- Corneal deposits: common and benign.

**Heart**
- Prolonged QT interval, torsades de pointes (a dangerous heart rhythm)
- Sinus bradycardia or AV block
- These may present as palpitations, fainting, or abnormal shortness of breath.

**Lungs**
- Pneumonitis or pulmonary fibrosis: typically dry cough and shortness of breath, sometimes with fever, weight loss, or pleuritic pain. Usually develops months to years after starting. This can be severe and life-threatening, and amiodarone is stopped if it occurs. Your doctor will investigate any unexplained breathlessness. If breathlessness is severe, call 111 or go to the emergency department.

**Liver**
- A temporary rise in liver enzymes after starting is common.
- The drug should be stopped if enzymes show a larger than 2-fold increase.
- Symptomatic hepatitis is rare, but 3 to 6 monthly electrolyte and liver function tests are recommended.

**Nervous system (less common, especially in older people)**
- Fatigue, insomnia, sleep disturbance, dizziness, balance and coordination problems, tremor.

### Drug Interactions

Amiodarone interacts with many medicines. Your doctor will check the full list before prescribing. Interactions can persist after amiodarone is stopped, due to its long and variable half-life.

Key interactions include:
- **Simvastatin:** If combined with amiodarone, the simvastatin dose must not exceed 20 mg daily (increased risk of muscle breakdown, called rhabdomyolysis). An alternative statin may be considered for people on long-term amiodarone.
- **Erythromycin, warfarin, dabigatran, digoxin, verapamil, colchicine:** commonly used drugs that interact with amiodarone.
- **Other drugs that prolong the QT interval** (see list below).
- Amiodarone and sotalol should not be used together.

**Drugs that can prolong the QT interval** (relevant across multiple cardiac medications):
- Antihistamines
- Other anti-arrhythmic drugs (only under cardiology or other specialist direction)
- Psychoactive drugs: lithium, tricyclic antidepressants, haloperidol, phenothiazines, citalopram, escitalopram
- Domperidone
- Methadone
- Antibiotics: erythromycin, clarithromycin
- Fluconazole, macrolide antibiotics, SSRIs, and diuretics (through lowering potassium) can raise the levels of these drugs and add to QT prolongation.

### Monitoring

Blood levels of amiodarone are not used for routine monitoring, to guide dosing, or to predict toxicity.

Your doctor will arrange:
- ECG, chest X-ray, potassium, liver function tests (LFT), and thyroid function tests (TFT) before starting
- Pulmonary function tests before starting if you have breathing problems
- TFT, potassium, LFT, and a symptom review every 6 months, or earlier if there are concerns
- Annual ECG
- An annual cardiology review to check amiodarone is still the right treatment
- Chest X-ray and pulmonary function tests if suspicious symptoms develop. If the X-ray is normal but concerns remain, your doctor may seek respiratory advice about a CT scan of the chest.
- Slit-lamp eye examination if suspicious eye symptoms develop

## Clopidogrel

Clopidogrel is a potent antiplatelet drug. It is used to prevent further events caused by blocked blood flow, and after angioplasty or stenting. The hospital team decides how long you take it and records the plan.

**Do not stop clopidogrel without consulting your doctor or cardiologist first.** Stopping early is linked to a higher risk of cardiac events. Your doctor will consult your cardiologist before making changes, especially within the first 12 months after an acute coronary syndrome (ACS) or a coronary stent procedure (PCI).

### Indications

Clopidogrel is also used after TIA and stroke. For heart conditions, your cardiologist will advise how long to use it. Common durations are:

- Second-line antiplatelet after myocardial infarction (MI) or unstable angina needing hospital admission (when ticagrelor cannot be tolerated): 12 months, with aspirin
- After PCI for chronic stable angina: 6 months of dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin
- In-stent thrombosis: used indefinitely
- Following a vascular event after recent cessation of clopidogrel: used indefinitely

### How Your Doctor Will Use It

- Usually started with a 300 mg loading dose, then 75 mg daily

### Contraindications and Cautions

**Contraindications:**
- Severe liver impairment
- Active bleeding (e.g., peptic ulcer)
- Breastfeeding

**Cautions:**
- Avoid premature discontinuation. Your doctor will consult your cardiologist before any stop (for a procedure, surgery, bleeding, or adverse effects).
- There is a theoretical concern that omeprazole may reduce clopidogrel's effect, but this is not supported by the evidence to date. If long-term clopidogrel with a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) is planned, your doctor may use pantoprazole or lansoprazole instead.
- Stopped 5 days before elective surgery, or if there is a risk of gastrointestinal bleeding, but only after consulting your cardiologist
- Use with caution alongside NSAIDs or aspirin (increased bleeding risk)

### Adverse Effects

- Prolongs bleeding time
- Gastrointestinal bleeding risk

### Monitoring

Your doctor will:
- Monitor clinically for gastrointestinal bleeding
- Assess before any surgical procedure whether temporary cessation is needed (after cardiology consultation)

## Flecainide

Flecainide is a Class 1C anti-arrhythmic that acts on sodium channels in the heart. It has a narrow range of uses because its adverse effects can be severe, and it is only used on cardiologist advice.

**Flecainide is initiated and managed under cardiologist advice.**

### Indications

- Supraventricular tachycardias (SVT)
- Atrial fibrillation
- Prevention of ventricular tachycardia in a structurally normal heart

### How Your Doctor Will Use It

Two formulations exist: standard and controlled release (CR). Dose increases are made slowly, together with a cardiologist, because of the long half-life. The lowest dose that controls the arrhythmia is used:
- Standard formulation: starting dose 50 mg twice a day, or as your specialist recommends. Wait 3 to 4 days or longer between increases.
- CR formulation: starting dose 100 mg a day, or as your specialist recommends. Wait at least 7 days between increases.
- Maximum dose: 300 mg (400 mg for ventricular arrhythmias). Once the arrhythmia is controlled, a lower dose may be possible to reduce side effects.

### Contraindications and Cautions

**Contraindications:**
- Heart failure (usually)
- After myocardial infarction
- Conduction disease

**Cautions:**
- Electrolyte disturbances (low potassium, low magnesium, high potassium) increase arrhythmia risk
- Renal impairment: plasma level measurement may be needed

### Monitoring

Your doctor will:
- Measure trough flecainide blood levels and perform an ECG 2 weeks after starting or after each dose increase, checking for bradyarrhythmia, QRS prolongation, bundle branch block, or heart block. A QRS increase of less than 25 % from baseline is generally acceptable.
- Arrange annual ECG and annual trough flecainide level

**Your doctor will seek urgent cardiology advice about stopping flecainide if:**
- You develop ischaemic heart disease (proven or suspected)
- You have cardiac conduction delay (second or third degree heart block, QRS prolongation, bundle branch block)
- Your left ventricular ejection fraction drops below 35%, or heart failure is suspected

## Perhexiline

Perhexiline is an antianginal drug that can relieve refractory angina by improving how efficiently the heart muscle works. It needs monitoring because people break it down at very different rates, and because of the risk of side effects such as liver and nerve toxicity.

**Perhexiline is usually reserved for intractable angina not responding to other treatments, and is started after cardiology review.**

### Indications and Dosing

- Rarely used alone
- Reserved for angina that has not responded to other treatments
- Starting dose: 50 mg to 100 mg daily, slowly adjusted up or down at 2 to 4 weekly intervals
- Maximum dose: 300 mg daily
- The minimum tolerated effective dose is used, based on blood levels and clinical response

### Contraindications, Cautions, and Adverse Effects

**Contraindications:**
- Severe kidney impairment (eGFR less than 30 mL/min)
- Severe liver impairment

**Cautions:**
- Approximately 5 to 10% of the population are slow metabolisers of perhexiline
- Multiple drug interactions
- Use with caution in diabetes (especially with insulin or sulfonylureas), as it can cause hypoglycaemia

**Adverse effects** (occur in 65% of users; 8% need to stop):
- Hepatitis
- Peripheral neuropathy
- Extrapyramidal dysfunction (movement problems)
- Muscle weakness
- Ataxia (balance problems)
- These may be dose-dependent.

### Monitoring

Your doctor will measure plasma perhexiline levels:
- 1 week after starting
- 4 weeks after starting
- 4 weeks after any dose change
- Annually thereafter

The blood test is time sensitive. Blood should be taken 6 to 12 hours after a dose.

Target level: 0.15–0.60 microgram/mL

Liver function tests (LFTs) are checked:
- At initiation
- Monthly until a stable dose is reached
- Then annually

## Sotalol

Sotalol is a Class III anti-arrhythmic drug. It prolongs action potential duration without affecting cardiac conduction. It is used to treat ventricular tachyarrhythmias and to maintain sinus rhythm in atrial fibrillation or flutter.

**Sotalol is initiated by cardiologists.**

### Indications

- Treatment and prevention of ventricular tachycardia (VT), in place of or alongside an implantable cardiac defibrillator
- Maintenance of sinus rhythm in paroxysmal atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter

### How Your Doctor Will Use It

- Before starting, your doctor will check an ECG for the QT interval, and your electrolytes and magnesium, correcting any problem first.
- The usual starting dose is 80 mg once daily or 40 mg twice a day. If needed, it is increased gradually every 2 to 3 days to 80 to 160 mg twice a day.
- Doses up to 640 mg a day may be used under specialist supervision for life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias.

### Contraindications and Cautions

**Contraindications:**
- Bronchospasm
- Chronic atrial fibrillation (higher risk of life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias including torsades de pointes)
- Severe renal impairment (eGFR less than 30 mL/min)
- Second or third degree heart block
- Prolonged QTc interval (particularly a QTc greater than 500 msec)

**Cautions:**
- Heart failure: your doctor will seek cardiology advice about stopping if this develops
- Renal impairment: sotalol is renally excreted, so it is used with caution at a reduced dose
- Do not stop suddenly, especially in patients with coronary artery disease
- Significant bradycardia (heart rate less than 50 bpm, or less than 60 bpm with symptoms): your doctor will discuss stopping and alternatives with a cardiologist
- Use care with other QT-prolonging drugs (see list under Amiodarone above)
- Amiodarone and sotalol should not be used together

### Monitoring

Your doctor will:
- Arrange an ECG before starting, then 2 to 4 weeks after starting, after any dose increase, or after a significant change in kidney function
- Arrange annual ECG thereafter
- Watch for QTc prolongation, which may cause ventricular arrhythmias. The dose is reduced, or the drug stopped if QTc exceeds 0.5 seconds.
- Check kidney function and magnesium before starting and annually
- Recheck if diarrhoea or other illness causes risk of low potassium or magnesium (which can worsen QT prolongation)

## Ticagrelor

Ticagrelor is an oral antiplatelet medication similar to clopidogrel but slightly more potent. It is used with aspirin after an acute coronary syndrome (ACS). The hospital team decides how long you take it and records the plan.

**Ticagrelor is initiated by a cardiologist. Do not stop without consulting your doctor or cardiologist.** Your doctor will consult your cardiologist before making changes, especially within the first 12 months after ACS or PCI.

### Indications

- Recommended antiplatelet therapy (with aspirin) after ACS, whether managed medically or by coronary intervention
- Used for 12 months, or as long as your cardiologist specifies
- Funded

### How Your Doctor Will Use It

- Loading dose: 180 mg
- Usual regular dose: 90 mg twice daily

### Contraindications, Cautions, and Adverse Effects

**Contraindications:**
- Elective surgery: stop 5 days before (consult your cardiologist first)
- Active bleeding or high risk of bleeding
- Moderate to severe liver impairment

**Cautions:**
- Avoid premature discontinuation, which is associated with increased risk of cardiac events. Your doctor will seek cardiology advice before any early stop.
- Use with NSAIDs or aspirin
- Cardiac conduction problems on ECG (second or third degree heart block)
- Interacts with CYP 3A4 inhibitors (e.g., ketoconazole, verapamil) and can increase digoxin and ciclosporin levels
- Elderly patients or those weighing less than 60 kg

**Adverse effects:**
- Dyspnoea (breathlessness): common, predominantly at rest, usually mild and short-lived (1 to 2 weeks). Other causes should be excluded before attributing this to ticagrelor. Tell your doctor if this occurs; they may consult your cardiologist.
- Headache
- Bleeding
- Rises in creatinine and uric acid levels

### Monitoring

Your doctor will:
- Monitor clinically for gastrointestinal bleeding, and for situations where stopping may become necessary, such as around surgery
- Check kidney function 1 month after starting

### Drug Interactions

- Ticagrelor is a mild CYP 3A4 inhibitor, and interacts with CYP 3A4 inhibitors and inducers
- Ticagrelor is a p-glycoprotein inhibitor

## Bumetanide

Bumetanide is a loop diuretic. It is usually reserved for people who do not respond to furosemide.

### Indications

- It is absorbed more reliably and less variably than furosemide.
- 1 mg of bumetanide is equivalent to 40 mg of furosemide.
- Usually started second line, if furosemide is not having the desired effect.

### How Your Doctor Will Use It

- 1 mg once daily in the morning, or the equivalent dose if switching from furosemide. It may be repeated after 6 to 8 hours if needed.
- 500 micrograms daily may be enough for older people.

### Contraindications, Cautions, and Adverse Effects

- Not used if you have severe low potassium (hypokalaemia) or low sodium (hyponatraemia).
- Low blood volume and low blood pressure are corrected before starting.
- Adverse effects include mild gut upset, symptoms of low blood pressure, and gout flares.

### Monitoring

Your doctor will check kidney function and electrolytes 1 to 2 weeks after starting and after dose changes. On a stable dose, these are checked:
- At least annually
- More often if needed, such as with kidney impairment, a high risk of electrolyte imbalance, or high doses
- 1 to 2 weeks after a potentially interacting drug is added
- With any illness that may affect the kidneys, especially diarrhoea and vomiting

## Eplerenone

Eplerenone is an aldosterone antagonist (mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist). It helps the kidneys remove sodium and water while keeping potassium.

### Indications

- Added in stable heart failure with an ejection fraction under 40%, for people who cannot tolerate an optimal dose of spironolactone or had a clinically significant adverse effect on it

### How Your Doctor Will Use It

- Initially 25 mg once daily, increased within 4 weeks to 50 mg once daily as tolerated

### Contraindications, Cautions, and Adverse Effects

- Not used with potassium-sparing diuretics or potassium supplements.
- Used with caution in older people and those taking amiodarone. For them, the recommended maximum dose is 25 mg daily.
- Adverse effects include gut upset, symptoms of low blood pressure, muscle cramps, or weakness.

### Monitoring

Eplerenone increases the risk of high potassium (hyperkalaemia), so close monitoring is needed. Your doctor will:
- Measure potassium before starting, within the first week, and 1 month after starting or any dose change
- Monitor periodically after that
- On a stable dose, check kidney function and electrolytes every 3 months, with extra tests during any illness that may affect the kidneys, especially diarrhoea and vomiting

## Metolazone

Metolazone helps the kidneys remove sodium, chloride, and water.

### Indications

- Used in flare-ups of heart failure and in hard-to-treat fluid build-up (refractory oedema), for people who cannot tolerate or do not respond to other diuretics

### How Your Doctor Will Use It

- Kidney function and electrolytes are checked before starting, and any imbalance is corrected.
- After the first dose, your response is reassessed the next day, and kidney function and electrolytes are checked within 2 to 3 days.
- The usual maintenance dose is 2.5 to 5 mg once daily in the morning. The maximum dose is 20 mg daily.
- The lowest effective dose and frequency is used, which can include alternate-day or weekly dosing.

### Contraindications, Cautions, and Adverse Effects

- Not used in refractory low potassium, low sodium, or high calcium.
- Adverse effects include mild gut upset, symptoms of low blood pressure, and electrolyte disturbances.

### Monitoring

Your doctor will check kidney function, electrolytes, and fluid status often when starting, when it is used with loop diuretics, and if you have kidney impairment.

## Spironolactone

Spironolactone reduces illness and death in symptomatic heart failure with reduced ejection fraction (HFrEF).

### Indications

It is started after an ACE inhibitor or ARB and a beta blocker for HFrEF if:
- eGFR is 30 mL/min/m2 or more, and
- potassium is 5.0 mmol/L or less, and
- systolic blood pressure is over 90 mmHg

### How Your Doctor Will Use It

- The standard starting dose is 12.5 to 25 mg daily, titrated up to 25 to 50 mg daily

### Contraindications, Cautions, and Adverse Effects

- Not used with high potassium, low sodium, or Addison's disease.
- Adverse effects include gut upset, liver toxicity, confusion and drowsiness, breast pain and breast enlargement in men (gynaecomastia), excess hair growth, and electrolyte disturbances.
- Combined with ACE inhibitors, there is a risk of serious high potassium, low blood pressure, and falls.

### Monitoring

Your doctor will check creatinine and potassium at 1, 4, 8, and 12 weeks. After that:
- Kidney function and electrolytes every 3 months
- Extra tests with any illness that may affect the kidneys, especially diarrhoea and vomiting

After dose changes, your doctor will check for a significant drop in blood pressure on standing, especially in older people. This is more common on doses over 25 mg.

## Sacubitril + Valsartan (ARNI)

Sacubitril + valsartan (Entresto) is the currently available angiotensin receptor neprilysin inhibitor (ARNI). It combines an angiotensin II receptor blocker with a neprilysin inhibitor. It is proven to reduce death and hospital admission. International guidelines now recommend an ARNI as first-line treatment for heart failure with reduced or mildly reduced ejection fraction. Any relevant practitioner can start it.

### Contraindications and Cautions

**Contraindications:**
- End-stage kidney failure
- Severe liver impairment
- Past angioedema with an ACE inhibitor, or hereditary angioedema
- Pregnancy
- Current ACE inhibitor treatment

**Cautions:**
- High potassium and sodium depletion are corrected before starting.
- Avoided if systolic blood pressure is less than 100 mmHg. A lower starting dose is used if systolic blood pressure is 100 to 110 mmHg or there are other risk factors for low blood pressure.
- Other medicines that raise potassium are avoided, except those specifically for heart failure, or potassium is monitored closely.
- The dose is reduced if eGFR is less than 60 mL/min/1.73 m2, and it is used cautiously if eGFR is less than 30 mL/min/1.73 m2.
- Moderate liver impairment, or AST/ALT more than twice the upper limit of normal

### How Your Doctor Will Use It

- Your ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker is stopped first. After the last ACE inhibitor dose, you must wait at least 36 hours before starting, to avoid potentially serious side effects. Your doctor will give you written advice about this. No wait is needed when switching from an angiotensin receptor blocker.
- The starting dose is 49 mg/51 mg twice daily, increased at 2 to 4 weeks to 97 mg/103 mg twice daily if well tolerated.
- A lower starting dose, such as 24 mg/26 mg twice a day, may be used if systolic blood pressure is less than 110 mmHg, you are aged 75 years or older, you were not previously on an ACE inhibitor or angiotensin receptor blocker (or were on a low dose), you have severe kidney impairment, or you have moderate liver impairment.

### Monitoring

- Potassium, creatinine, and blood pressure are checked weekly when starting and while the dose is adjusted, then every 6 to 12 months once stable.
- Contraception must be used by females of reproductive age.

## SGLT2 Inhibitors

Sodium glucose co-transporter 2 (SGLT2) inhibitors are one of the pillars of treatment for heart failure with reduced ejection fraction.

- Dapagliflozin is registered for heart failure but not funded.
- Empagliflozin is funded.
- The dose of both is 10 mg per day.
- Creatinine may rise (and eGFR may fall) within the first 6 to 8 weeks. Unless dialysis or other renal replacement therapy is needed, this does not require stopping or a dose change.

### Contraindications, Cautions, and Adverse Effects

**Contraindications:**
- Previous severe genitourinary infection
- Ketogenic diets, because of the increased risk of ketoacidosis
- Starting when eGFR is less than 20 mL/min (once started, it does not have to be stopped until renal replacement therapy begins)

**Cautions:**
- Pregnancy and breastfeeding
- Reduced kidney function: empagliflozin can be started safely when eGFR is greater than 20 mL/min. Where eGFR is less than 30 mL/min, the maximum dose of empagliflozin is 10 mg daily.
- Type 1 diabetes, diabetes due to loss of pancreatic function, a history of ketoacidosis, or excessive alcohol use: not started without specialist approval
- Other situations with a higher risk of ketoacidosis: insulin dose reduction (such as insulin pump failure), malnutrition or low food intake, low-carbohydrate diets or fasting, acute illness or infection, surgery or bowel preparation, and severe dehydration

**Adverse effects:**
- Ketoacidosis, including in people without diabetes (see below)
- Passing more urine. Your doctor may reduce diuretics before starting.
- Genitourinary infections, such as urine infections, vaginal thrush, and balanitis. Rarely, Fournier's gangrene, a severe infection of the genital, perineal, or perianal area that can be life-threatening.
- Low blood pressure. Your doctor may reduce blood pressure medicines before starting.
- Raised haemoglobin and haematocrit. Your doctor will exclude other causes. A rise due to empagliflozin alone is not linked to more blood clots, so it is not a reason to stop.

### Ketoacidosis Risk

Ketoacidosis may occur with near-normal or only slightly raised glucose. Finger-prick blood ketones over 1.5 mmol/L on an SGLT2 inhibitor need urgent medical advice. Your doctor may advise you to:
- Stop the SGLT2 inhibitor while you have an illness or severe dehydration
- Stop it for at least 3 days, including the day of surgery, before any major elective procedure, or any elective procedure with reduced eating or drinking for more than 12 hours, including dental surgery
- Stop it at least 3 days before any bowel preparation (such as for a colonoscopy) or a low-carbohydrate diet before a procedure (such as bariatric surgery)
- Restart only when you are well and eating and drinking normally
- Go to your general practice or hospital to have your ketones checked if you have nausea, vomiting, or abdominal pain

### Monitoring

Your doctor will check kidney function:
- Before starting
- At least annually
- Before starting other medicines that may reduce kidney function
- With any illness that may affect the kidneys, especially diarrhoea and vomiting
