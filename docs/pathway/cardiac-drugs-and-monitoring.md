# Cardiac Drugs and Monitoring

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This page provides an overview of common cardiac medications. These drugs are typically started by a cardiologist or specialist but may need ongoing monitoring in general practice. Your doctor manages and reviews these medications — do not make changes without consulting them first.

Full prescribing information is not included here. Your doctor will refer to the New Zealand Formulary or equivalent prescribing reference for complete details.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Help

Contact your doctor or seek emergency care immediately if you experience any of the following while on a cardiac medication:

- Palpitations, fainting, or pre-syncope (feeling like you are about to faint)
- Sudden significant shortness of breath or difficulty breathing
- Chest pain
- Sudden or worsening visual disturbance, eye pain, or reduced vision
- Unusual bleeding that does not stop
- Signs of a stroke (sudden weakness, numbness, speech difficulty, confusion)
- Severe fatigue, jaundice, or abdominal pain (possible liver toxicity)
- New or worsening swelling of the legs or fluid retention

## Amiodarone

Amiodarone is a Class III anti-arrhythmic drug. It prolongs the heart's refractory period and slows conduction through the AV node. It has no significant negative effect on heart pumping function, making it useful for people with heart failure who also have rhythm disturbances.

**Amiodarone must be initiated by a cardiologist or specialist.** Your doctor will manage ongoing monitoring.

### Indications

- Atrial fibrillation (AF)
- Ventricular tachycardias

### How Your Doctor Will Use It

Amiodarone requires a loading phase because of its slow absorption into the body:

- Week 1: 200 mg three times daily
- Week 2: 200 mg twice daily
- Week 3 onward: 200 mg or 100 mg daily (minimum effective dose)

If your pulse is slow or you report palpitations or pre-syncope at a review, your doctor will repeat an ECG. If second or third degree heart block is detected, the drug will be stopped. No tapering is needed when stopping, due to the drug's very long half-life (up to 150 days).

### Adverse Effects

Amiodarone has a long half-life, so side effects can persist for up to 3–6 months after stopping the drug. Your doctor will watch for adverse effects for up to 18 months after cessation.

**Skin**
- Photosensitivity is common. Protect yourself with sunscreen and covering clothing.
- Rarely, a persistent grey or blue skin discolouration may develop.

**Thyroid**
- Both overactive (hyperthyroidism) and underactive (hypothyroidism) thyroid can occur.
- More common with higher doses and in people with pre-existing thyroid conditions.
- Your doctor will arrange specialist advice if thyroid dysfunction develops.

**Eyes**
- Optic neuritis (rare but serious): presents with blurred vision, eye pain, and reduced visual acuity. Requires urgent assessment.
- Less acute visual disturbance: requires assessment within 1–2 weeks.
- Corneal deposits: common and benign.

**Heart**
- Prolonged QT interval, torsades de pointes (a dangerous heart rhythm)
- Sinus bradycardia or AV block
- These may present as palpitations, fainting, or unusual shortness of breath.

**Lungs**
- Pneumonitis or pulmonary fibrosis: typically dry cough and shortness of breath, sometimes with fever, weight loss, or pleuritic pain. Usually develops months to years after starting. Your doctor will investigate any unexplained breathlessness.

**Liver**
- A temporary rise in liver enzymes after starting is common.
- The drug should be stopped if enzymes rise more than twice the upper limit of normal.
- Symptomatic hepatitis is rare, but liver function tests are monitored regularly.

**Nervous system (especially in older people)**
- Fatigue, insomnia, sleep disturbance, dizziness, balance and coordination problems.

### Drug Interactions

Amiodarone has many important interactions. Your doctor will review all your medications before prescribing.

Key interactions include:
- **Simvastatin:** If combined with amiodarone, the simvastatin dose must not exceed 20 mg/day (increased risk of muscle breakdown — rhabdomyolysis). An alternative statin may be preferred.
- **Warfarin, dabigatran, digoxin, verapamil, colchicine, erythromycin:** Increased concentrations or toxicity risk.
- **Other QT-prolonging drugs** (see list below): Additive risk of dangerous arrhythmias.
- Amiodarone and sotalol should not be used together.
- Drug interactions can persist after amiodarone is stopped, due to its long half-life.

**Drugs that can prolong the QT interval** (relevant across multiple cardiac medications):
- Antihistamines
- Other anti-arrhythmic drugs (specialist supervision required)
- Psychoactive drugs: lithium, tricyclic antidepressants, haloperidol, phenothiazines, citalopram, escitalopram
- Domperidone
- Methadone
- Antibiotics: erythromycin, clarithromycin
- Certain antifungals, SSRIs, and diuretics (through lowering potassium) can increase QT risk.

### Monitoring

Your doctor will arrange:
- Liver function tests (LFT) and thyroid function tests (TFT) before starting
- TFT and LFT every 6 months, or earlier if there are concerns
- Annual ECG
- Chest X-ray (annually per Medsafe recommendation; respiratory specialists emphasise monitoring symptoms and early referral)
- Pulmonary function tests if respiratory symptoms develop
- Eye examination if visual symptoms develop

## Clopidogrel

Clopidogrel is a potent antiplatelet drug that reduces the risk of blood clots forming in arteries and inside stents. It is commonly prescribed after a heart attack, unstable angina, or coronary stenting.

**Do not stop clopidogrel without consulting your doctor or cardiologist first.** Stopping prematurely significantly increases the risk of a cardiac event or stent thrombosis.

### Indications

- Second-line antiplatelet after myocardial infarction (MI) or unstable angina (when ticagrelor cannot be tolerated) — used for 12 months in combination with aspirin
- After coronary stenting for stable angina — 6 months dual antiplatelet therapy with aspirin
- In-stent thrombosis — used indefinitely
- Following a vascular event after recent cessation of clopidogrel — used indefinitely

### Contraindications and Cautions

**Contraindications:**
- Severe liver impairment
- Active bleeding (e.g., peptic ulcer)
- Breastfeeding

**Cautions:**
- Avoid premature discontinuation — consult your cardiologist before stopping for any reason (procedure, surgery, bleeding, adverse effects)
- If long-term clopidogrel use with a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) is planned, your doctor may prefer pantoprazole or lansoprazole over omeprazole (theoretical interaction reducing clopidogrel's effectiveness)
- Stop 5 days before elective surgery — only after consulting your cardiologist
- Use with caution alongside NSAIDs or aspirin (increased bleeding risk)

### Adverse Effects

- Prolongs bleeding time
- Gastrointestinal bleeding risk

### Monitoring

Your doctor will:
- Monitor clinically for gastrointestinal bleeding
- Monitor for allergy
- Assess before any surgical procedure whether temporary cessation is needed (after cardiology consultation)

## Flecainide

Flecainide is a Class 1C anti-arrhythmic. It is effective for certain rhythm disturbances but carries a risk of serious adverse effects. It has a narrow range of safe use and is only prescribed under specialist advice.

**Flecainide is initiated and managed under cardiologist advice.**

### Indications

- Supraventricular tachycardias (SVT)
- Atrial fibrillation
- Prevention of ventricular tachycardia in a structurally normal heart
- Commonly co-prescribed with beta-blockers or calcium-channel blockers

### How Your Doctor Will Use It

Two formulations exist: standard and controlled release (CR). Dose increases are made slowly:
- Standard formulation: wait at least 3–4 days between increases
- CR formulation: wait at least 7 days between increases

### Contraindications and Cautions

**Contraindications:**
- Heart failure (flecainide has negative inotropic effects)
- After myocardial infarction
- Conduction disease (heart block)

**Cautions:**
- Electrolyte disturbances (low potassium, low magnesium, high potassium) increase arrhythmia risk
- Renal impairment — dose adjustment and plasma level measurement may be needed

### Monitoring

Your doctor will:
- Measure trough flecainide blood levels and perform an ECG 2 weeks after starting or after each dose increase — checking for bradyarrhythmia, prolonged QTc, or heart block
- Arrange annual ECG and annual trough flecainide level

**Seek urgent cardiology advice if:**
- You develop ischaemic heart disease (proven or suspected)
- You have cardiac conduction delay (second or third degree heart block, bundle branch block, long QTc)
- Your left ventricular ejection fraction drops below 35%, or heart failure develops

## Perhexiline

Perhexiline is an antianginal drug used for severe, refractory angina. It improves myocardial efficiency by altering the heart muscle's energy source. It requires careful monitoring due to its variable metabolism and risk of serious side effects.

**Perhexiline is reserved for intractable angina not responding to other treatments, and is initiated after specialist review.**

### Indications and Dosing

- Rarely used alone
- Reserved for angina that has not responded to other treatments
- Starting dose: 50–100 mg daily, slowly adjusted every 2–4 weeks based on blood levels and response
- Maximum dose: 300 mg daily
- The minimum effective dose is used based on plasma monitoring

### Contraindications, Cautions, and Adverse Effects

**Contraindications:**
- Severe kidney impairment (eGFR less than 30 mL/min)
- Severe liver impairment

**Cautions:**
- Approximately 5–10% of the population are slow metabolisers of perhexiline, increasing toxicity risk
- Multiple drug interactions
- Use with caution in diabetes (especially with insulin or sulfonylureas) — can cause hypoglycaemia

**Adverse effects** (occur in up to 65% of users; 8% need to stop):
- Hepatitis
- Peripheral neuropathy
- Extrapyramidal dysfunction (movement problems)
- Muscle weakness
- Ataxia (balance problems)
- These may be dose-dependent and monitored through blood levels.

### Monitoring

Your doctor will measure plasma perhexiline levels:
- 1 week after starting
- 4 weeks after starting
- 4 weeks after any dose change
- Annually thereafter

Target level: 0.15–0.60 microgram/mL

Liver function tests (LFTs) are checked:
- At initiation
- Monthly until a stable dose is reached
- Then annually

## Prasugrel

Prasugrel is an antiplatelet drug similar to clopidogrel but more potent. It reduces the risk of stent thrombosis after coronary artery stenting. It is used when clopidogrel cannot be tolerated.

**Prasugrel must be initiated by a cardiologist.**

### Indications

- Clopidogrel-allergic patients requiring dual antiplatelet therapy after ACS managed by angioplasty or stenting
- Used in combination with aspirin for up to 12 months (or longer for drug-eluting stents)

### Contraindications, Cautions, and Adverse Effects

**Contraindications:**
- Previous stroke or TIA (transient ischaemic attack)
- Elective surgery — stop at least 7 days before
- Not proven to benefit patients over 75 years old or weighing less than 60 kg

**Cautions:**
- Increased bleeding risk compared to clopidogrel
- Use with NSAIDs, aspirin, or other drugs affecting bleeding increases risk further

**Adverse effects:**
- Increased risk of bleeding (more than with clopidogrel)

### Monitoring

Your doctor will monitor for signs of bleeding. No specific blood test monitoring is required beyond this.

## Sotalol

Sotalol is a Class III anti-arrhythmic drug. It prolongs action potential duration without significantly affecting cardiac conduction speed. It is used to treat ventricular tachyarrhythmias and to maintain sinus rhythm in atrial fibrillation or flutter.

**Sotalol is initiated by cardiologists.**

### Indications

- Treatment and prevention of ventricular tachycardia (VT), sometimes alongside an implantable cardiac defibrillator
- Maintenance of sinus rhythm in paroxysmal atrial fibrillation or atrial flutter

### Contraindications and Cautions

**Contraindications:**
- Bronchospasm or asthma
- Chronic atrial fibrillation (higher risk of life-threatening ventricular arrhythmias including torsades de pointes)
- Severe renal impairment (eGFR less than 30 mL/min)
- Second or third degree heart block
- Prolonged QTc interval

**Cautions:**
- Heart failure — your doctor will seek specialist advice about stopping if this develops
- Renal impairment — sotalol is renally excreted; dose reduction is needed
- Do not stop suddenly, especially in patients with coronary artery disease
- Significant bradycardia (heart rate less than 50 bpm, or less than 60 bpm with symptoms) — your doctor will discuss stopping with a cardiologist
- Use care with other QT-prolonging drugs (see list under Amiodarone above)
- Amiodarone and sotalol must not be used together

### Monitoring

Your doctor will:
- Arrange ECG 2–4 weeks after starting, after any dose increase, or after a significant change in kidney function
- Arrange annual ECG thereafter
- Watch for QTc prolongation — reduce or stop if QTc exceeds 0.5 seconds
- Check kidney function (and electrolytes) before starting and annually
- Recheck electrolytes if diarrhoea or other illness causes risk of low potassium or magnesium (which can worsen QT prolongation)

## Ticagrelor

Ticagrelor is an oral antiplatelet medication similar to clopidogrel but slightly more potent. It is generally used in combination with aspirin for up to 12 months after an acute coronary syndrome (ACS).

**Ticagrelor is initiated by a cardiologist. Do not stop without consulting your doctor or cardiologist.**

### Indications

- Recommended antiplatelet therapy (with aspirin) after ACS — whether managed medically or by coronary intervention
- Used for 12 months

### How Your Doctor Will Use It

- Standard dose: 90 mg twice daily

### Contraindications, Cautions, and Adverse Effects

**Contraindications:**
- Elective surgery — stop 5 days before (consult your cardiologist first)
- Active bleeding or high risk of bleeding
- Moderate to severe liver impairment

**Cautions:**
- Avoid premature discontinuation — associated with increased risk of cardiac events; consult your cardiologist before stopping
- Use with NSAIDs or aspirin increases bleeding risk
- Cardiac conduction problems on ECG (second or third degree heart block)
- Interacts with CYP P450 3A4 inhibitors (e.g., ketoconazole, verapamil) and can increase digoxin and ciclosporin levels
- Elderly patients or those weighing less than 60 kg are at higher bleeding risk

**Adverse effects:**
- Dyspnoea (breathlessness) — common, predominantly at rest, usually mild and resolving within 1–2 weeks. Other causes should be excluded before attributing this to ticagrelor. Inform your doctor if this occurs.
- Headache
- Bleeding
- Rises in creatinine and uric acid levels

### Drug Interactions

- Ticagrelor is a mild CYP 3A4 inhibitor — important interactions with CYP 3A4 inhibitors and inducers
- Ticagrelor is a p-glycoprotein inhibitor
