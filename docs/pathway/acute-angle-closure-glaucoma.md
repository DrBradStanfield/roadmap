# Acute Angle-Closure Glaucoma

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Acute angle-closure glaucoma is an **ophthalmic emergency**. Vision loss and blindness can occur quickly. This pathway covers recognition and initial management — always seek emergency care immediately if this is suspected.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if you have:

- Sudden severe eye pain or severe headache
- Rapidly reduced or blurred vision in one eye
- A red eye with a cloudy or hazy cornea
- Nausea and vomiting alongside eye symptoms
- A mid-dilated pupil that is not responding to light

---

## Background

Acute angle-closure glaucoma occurs when the anterior chamber angle of the eye narrows or closes, causing a rapid rise in intraocular pressure.

**Risk factors include:**

- Family history of angle-closure glaucoma
- Increasing age
- Female sex
- Long-sightedness (hyperopia)
- Certain medications, particularly anticholinergics
- Asian ethnicity (higher incidence)

Symptoms often begin in the evening, when lower light levels cause pupil dilation — the peripheral iris then blocks the narrow angle. The condition is usually in one eye only, unless both pupils have been pharmacologically dilated.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will look for the following:

**History:**
- Reduced vision
- Red eye
- Severe pain — may be described as a severe headache or pain behind the eye
- Nausea and vomiting (common)
- In older patients: non-specific "feeling unwell" with a red eye that may be overlooked
- Rainbow-coloured haloes around lights in the evening (with hazy vision and headache) — this can warn of an upcoming acute episode even if not yet acute

**Examination findings:**
- Patient appears very uncomfortable and unwell
- Markedly decreased visual acuity
- Cloudy cornea (iris details indistinct or absent)
- Marked redness
- Mid-dilated, unresponsive pupil

---

## Management

This is an emergency — specialist ophthalmology advice should be sought immediately.

**Your doctor may consider:**

1. Pain relief: opioid analgesia and an antiemetic for nausea and vomiting
2. If more than 1 hour from specialist ophthalmic care, the acute eye team may recommend starting treatment while awaiting transfer, which can include:
   - **Acetazolamide** 500 mg orally or IV — to reduce intraocular pressure
   - **Mannitol** 15% IV 500 mL over 45 minutes — to reduce intraocular pressure
   - **Timolol** eye drops — a beta-blocker to lower intraocular pressure
   - **Pilocarpine nitrate** eye drops — to constrict the pupil and open the drainage angle

Definitive treatment is performed by ophthalmology.
