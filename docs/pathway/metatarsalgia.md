# Metatarsalgia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of pain in the metatarsal head region (the ball of the foot).

---

## Background

Metatarsalgia refers to pain in the metatarsal head region. Common causes include:

- **Overuse** — runners, athletes, or those on their feet for long periods
- **Poor footwear** — high heels or narrow toe boxes
- **Being overweight** — increased load on the forefoot
- **Collapse of the transverse arch** — the arch formed by the metatarsal heads
- **Morton's neuroma** — interdigital neuroma causing burning pain that radiates between toes, most commonly between the third and fourth metatarsals. Often caused by tight-fitting shoes. Pressure into the web space, combined with squeezing the metatarsal heads together, may reproduce a palpable click and pain (Mulder's click) — this is diagnostic.
- **Stress fracture** — should be considered, particularly in athletes

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- **Site of pain** — midfoot, forefoot, or specific metatarsal or MTP joints; medial, central, lateral, or sole
- **Duration and onset** — including any significant past injury and mechanism
- **Night or rest pain** — this suggests increased inflammation, severity, or neurological pain
- **Exacerbating factors** — obesity, excessive exercise, footwear restrictions (e.g., work footwear)
- **Past history** — previous surgeries or foot conditions

Physical examination typically includes:

- **Standing assessment** — hindfoot alignment, heel raise test (checking if the heel swings into varus, suggesting normal tibialis posterior function; inability to single heel raise may indicate tibialis posterior deficiency or hindfoot stiffness)
- **Gait assessment** — looking for limp, calcaneal varus or valgus, marked pronation or supination, forefoot valgus or varus
- **Lying examination** — checking for wasting, swelling, tenderness, erythema, sensory changes, numbness, or ulcers over the metatarsal joints and intermetatarsal spaces; range of motion, stability, and tenderness in the hindfoot, ankle, midfoot, hallux, and lesser toes; callosities, nail condition, and peripheral pulses; testing for Morton's neuroma (Mulder's click)
- **Footwear inspection** — pattern of wear on the sole

Investigations your doctor may arrange:

- Blood tests to check for arthritis, gout, or diabetes
- X-ray (weight-bearing AP and lateral foot) — to assess toe alignment and look for stress fractures
- Bone scan — if a stress fracture is suspected
- Ultrasound — may be considered if the diagnosis of Morton's neuroma is uncertain

---

## Management

### Self-Management

Your doctor will likely recommend starting with:

- Limiting activities that aggravate the pain
- Weight loss if overweight
- Changing to shoes with lower heels, a supportive sole, and a wide toe area
- Using cushioning under the forefoot — gel or foam metatarsal pads are available from pharmacies
- Calf and anterior leg muscle stretches

### Pain Relief

Your doctor may consider:

- Paracetamol
- NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen, naproxen) if tolerated

### If Self-Management Is Not Enough

Your doctor may consider:

- **Podiatry assessment** — for a full assessment including gait analysis, activity modification advice, footwear guidance, stretching and strengthening, and biomechanical interventions such as strapping, in-shoe wedging or padding, orthotics (simple or custom), heel cushions, arch supports, or night splints
- **Steroid injection** — for bursitis or Morton's neuroma, using a dorsal or ultrasound-guided approach

### If Not Improving After 4–6 Months of Conservative Treatment

Your doctor may consider:

- Referral to a private sports physician or musculoskeletal specialist (public system referral is unlikely to be accepted)
- Community podiatry assessment and/or orthopaedic foot and ankle assessment if Morton's neuroma is suspected
