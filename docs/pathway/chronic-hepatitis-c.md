# Chronic Hepatitis C

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment, treatment, and long-term management of chronic hepatitis C (HCV) infection in adults and adolescents aged 12 and older.

---

## Red Flags — Requires Specialist Input

Your doctor will refer to a specialist if:

- Concurrent hepatitis B and/or HIV infection
- Cirrhosis
- Hepatocellular carcinoma
- Concurrent substance use — notably high alcohol intake or injecting (may require additional specialist support before or during treatment)

---

## Background

- HCV is transmitted by blood-to-blood transfer. Sexual and perinatal transmission are uncommon.
- An estimated 35,000 New Zealanders are living with HCV; around 40% do not know they are infected. Untreated, many develop cirrhosis, which can lead to liver cancer and liver failure.
- Over 80% of existing HCV infections are among people with a history of injecting drug use.
- HCV can now be **cured** using direct-acting antivirals (DAAs). Successful treatment prevents all liver-related complications, although patients with established cirrhosis still require ongoing surveillance for liver cancer.
- Māori have a higher prevalence of HCV and higher rates of long-term complications due to under-diagnosis and inequitable access to treatment.
- HCV is a highly stigmatised condition — your doctor will approach this with care and without judgment.

---

## Who Should Be Tested

Your doctor will consider testing if you:

- Have a **history of injecting drug use** (even once), including performance- and image-enhancing drugs
- Have had **tattoos or body piercings** in circumstances where infection control may have been suboptimal
- Have **shared items** like needles, syringes, spoons, scissors, razors, or toothbrushes with someone known to have HCV
- Were **born to a mother with HCV** (5% transmission risk)
- Are a **sexual partner of someone with HCV**, particularly men who have sex with men (MSM)
- Were **born in a region with high HCV prevalence:** Southeast Asia, Middle East (especially Egypt), Eastern Europe, the Mediterranean, Africa, the Indian subcontinent, or Russia
- Have **been imprisoned**
- Had **occupational blood exposure** (e.g., needle-stick injury)
- Are in the **highest-risk age group: 50–70 years**
- Are a **sex worker**
- **Received blood products or organ donation** in New Zealand before 1992, or at any time overseas in a high-prevalence region
- Had **medical or dental treatment** in a country where equipment may not have been adequately sterilised
- Are **living with HIV** (particularly MSM with HIV) or hepatitis B
- Request testing
- Have **evidence of liver disease**

If you have ongoing risk factors, your doctor will recommend annual screening.

---

## Testing

Your doctor will arrange blood tests:

- **HCV antibody (HCV Ab) test** if you have not previously tested positive
  - If you are already known to be HCV Ab positive, this test is not repeated — you remain HCV Ab positive for life, even if cured
- **HCV RNA (PCR) test** if you are already known to be HCV Ab positive, or if your HCV Ab test is positive
- HCV genotyping is not routinely required (Maviret treats all genotypes); it is only needed if you have had prior failed interferon-based treatment

**Interpreting your results:**

| Result | What it means |
|---|---|
| HCV Ab negative | Most likely no prior exposure. Repeat in 3 months if recent possible exposure. |
| HCV Ab positive | Prior exposure confirmed. An HCV RNA test is needed to check for active infection. |
| HCV RNA negative | HCV has been cleared (naturally or with prior treatment). No active infection. |
| HCV RNA positive | Active HCV infection confirmed. Treatment should be considered promptly. |

A positive HCV antibody result lasts for life — it does not mean you are immune to reinfection.

---

## Assessment Before Treatment

If HCV infection is confirmed, your doctor will assess:

- **Ongoing risk factors** for reinfection (injecting drug use, etc.)
- **Factors that affect liver disease progression:**
  - Heavy alcohol intake (>4 standard drinks per day), current and historic
  - Co-infection with HIV or hepatitis B
  - Diabetes, obesity, fatty liver disease, or metabolic syndrome
  - Age >40 years at time of HCV infection
  - Duration of infection
  - Daily heavy cannabis use
- **Readiness for treatment** and any barriers (practical, financial, social, cultural). Injecting drug use is not a contraindication to treatment.
- **Previous HCV treatment** — date, drug regimen, outcome
- **All medications** including over-the-counter and supplements (some interact with Maviret)
- **Pregnancy, breastfeeding, and contraception**

**Physical signs** your doctor will check for include: enlarged or firm liver, enlarged spleen, spider naevi, palmar erythema, jaundice, fluid retention.

**Blood tests** your doctor will arrange (for adults aged ≥30 years):
- Liver function tests (LFT) including AST
- Full blood count (FBC)
- Hepatitis B serology
- INR (if liver decompensation suspected)
- HIV test (if risk factors present)
- Pregnancy test (women aged ≤55 years)
- Alpha-fetoprotein (if cirrhosis suspected)

### Liver Fibrosis Assessment

Your doctor will calculate an **APRI score** (AST to platelet ratio) to assess liver fibrosis:
- **APRI < 1.0 with no clinical concern:** proceed to treatment
- **APRI ≥ 1.0 or clinical concern about cirrhosis:** liver elastography (FibroScan or shear wave) is arranged to assess liver stiffness

**FibroScan result interpretation:**
- < 10.0 kPa: cirrhosis excluded
- 10.0–12.5 kPa: likely severe fibrosis
- > 12.5 kPa: indicates cirrhosis

## Treatment

Most people aged ≥12 years with HCV can be treated in primary care. Treatment is **free** in New Zealand.

The standard treatment is **Maviret (glecaprevir + pibrentasvir):**
- **3 tablets once daily with food**
- **8 weeks** (or 16 weeks if you previously had failed interferon-based treatment with genotype 3)
- Cure rate: approximately **98%** in general practice

Treatment is deferred in pregnancy — your doctor will advise.

### Drug Interactions

Your doctor will check for drug interactions before starting Maviret. Important interactions include:
- Ethinylestradiol (high-dose combined oral contraceptives — see below)
- Atorvastatin and simvastatin
- St John's wort, rifampicin (contraindicated), carbamazepine, phenobarbital, phenytoin, primidone
- Dabigatran, rivaroxaban
- Some HIV medications (e.g., atazanavir — contraindicated; patients on HIV treatment are managed in secondary care)

If a medication needs to be paused during treatment, it is stopped 1–2 days before starting and restarted 1–2 days after finishing.

### Contraception and Pregnancy

- If you take an ethinylestradiol-containing contraceptive at a dose **>20 micrograms/day**, you will need to switch to an alternative contraception method during treatment. Progesterone-only contraception has no contraindication.
- **Pregnancy:** Maviret has not been studied in pregnancy — treatment is delayed until after delivery.
- **Breastfeeding:** Seek specialist advice if benefits may outweigh risks.

### Side Effects

Maviret has far fewer side effects than older HCV treatments. In clinical trials, side-effect rates were similar to placebo. Most people start feeling better within 4 weeks as the virus clears.

Common side effects (if they occur):
- Headache (13.2%)
- Fatigue (11.4%)
- Nausea (7.6%)

Side effects are usually minor and transient, and rarely require stopping treatment.

### Alcohol and Drug Use During Treatment

- If no cirrhosis, low-level alcohol use is not prohibited during treatment
- If cirrhosis, abstinence from alcohol is advised
- Injecting drug use is not a contraindication to treatment
- If ongoing substance use, your doctor will discuss strategies to support medication adherence

### Adherence

It is important to complete the full course:
- A missed dose can be taken with food **within 18 hours**. After 18 hours, skip that dose and resume normal schedule.
- Partial treatment risks treatment failure and limits future options
- Strategies: daily alarm, involvement of support people

### Monitoring During Treatment

**No routine blood tests are needed** during treatment for most patients without cirrhosis.

If you have cirrhosis, your doctor will check liver function monthly and seek specialist input if certain values rise above thresholds.

If you have concurrent hepatitis B, additional monitoring is needed — your specialist will advise.

Report significant side effects to your doctor.

---

## After Treatment

**At least 4 weeks after completing treatment**, your doctor will arrange:
- HCV RNA (PCR) test
- Liver function tests

**Results:**
- **HCV RNA negative:** You are cured. This is called a sustained viral response (SVR). If liver function tests are abnormal, your doctor will investigate other possible causes.
- **HCV RNA positive:** Treatment was unsuccessful. Specialist assessment will be arranged.

Even after being cured, you remain HCV antibody positive for life — this does not mean you are immune to reinfection.

---

## Long-Term Follow-Up

### If Cured and No Severe Fibrosis

- No specific follow-up required, apart from repeat testing if you have ongoing risk factors
- If ongoing risk factors, annual HCV RNA testing to check for re-infection
- If no cirrhosis, an APRI score will be repeated every 3 years

### If You Have Severe Fibrosis or Cirrhosis

Your doctor will refer you to a specialist for regular surveillance for liver cancer. If surveillance is done in primary care (in consultation with a liver specialist):
- Every 6 months: liver ultrasound and serum alpha-fetoprotein
- Lifestyle advice: no alcohol, weight reduction, care with medications, good nutrition
- Consider bone density assessment if you have cirrhosis and jaundice

### If You Decline Treatment

Your doctor will:
- Explore and document your reasons
- Offer referral to additional support services
- Recommend treatment at each visit
- Monitor for signs of progressive liver disease with annual blood tests (LFT, INR, albumin, FBC, alpha-fetoprotein)
- Provide lifestyle advice to slow liver damage

### Lifestyle Advice to Protect Your Liver

| Factor | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Alcohol (no or mild fibrosis) | <15 drinks/week (men), <10 drinks/week (women) |
| Alcohol (moderate-severe fibrosis or cirrhosis) | No alcohol |
| Cannabis | ≤2 joints per day |
| Weight | Lose weight if overweight |
| Coffee | Increasing coffee intake by 2 cups/day has a protective effect against cirrhosis |
| Nutrition | Good nutrition is important |

## Preventing Transmission

Your doctor will discuss how to reduce the risk of transmitting HCV to others or being re-infected:

**HCV is NOT transmitted through:** hugging, kissing, sharing food or drink, sneezing, coughing, or sharing toilet and bathroom facilities.

**Steps to reduce transmission:**
- Do not share injecting equipment (needles, syringes, spoons)
- Do not share personal items that may have blood on them (razors, toothbrushes, nail clippers)
- Keep wounds clean and covered
- Clean up blood spills with undiluted bleach or soap
- Dispose of blood-contaminated items in sealed plastic bags
- Use condoms, gloves, or other barriers during sexual practices involving blood

**Risk by activity:**
- **High risk:** Sharing drug injecting equipment; tattooing or piercing with unsterilised equipment
- **Moderate risk:** Pregnancy/breastfeeding (5% risk mother to baby); occupational needlestick (3%); sharing drug-snorting equipment
- **Low risk:** Sexual activity involving blood contact; sharing personal items with possible blood contamination

**Pregnancy:** The risk of HCV transmission from mother to baby is only 5%. Mode of delivery makes no difference. Breastfeeding is safe unless nipples are cracked or bleeding.

---

## Vaccination

If you are not immune to hepatitis B, your doctor will arrange hepatitis B vaccination. This is usually funded for people with HCV. Opportunistic immunisation against other conditions may also be offered.

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will refer to a specialist (gastroenterologist/hepatologist) if:

- You have cirrhosis (specialist advice needed before treating in primary care)
- You are aged under 12 years
- You have had previous unsuccessful DAA treatment for HCV
- You have severe fibrosis or cirrhosis (for ongoing cancer surveillance)

Your doctor will seek specialist advice (without necessarily referring) if:

- Co-infection with HBV or HIV
- You are aged ≥30 years and liver staging has not yet occurred
- You are breastfeeding
- Concerns about ongoing recreational drug or alcohol use affecting treatment
- Any other clinical uncertainty
