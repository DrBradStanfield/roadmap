# Bone Pain

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to bone pain — assessing the likely cause and determining urgency.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Prompt Medical Assessment

The following require urgent evaluation. If you have any of these features, see your doctor promptly or go to the emergency department:

- Suspected neoplastic disease — benign, primary, or metastatic
- Suspected infection (osteomyelitis, spinal infection)
- Fracture — traumatic or non-traumatic
- Unexplained bruising or suspected inflicted injury (especially in children and older persons)

---

## Background

Bone pain can present a diagnostic challenge because there are many serious potential causes, including:

- Metastatic cancer to bone
- Primary bone cancer (e.g. myeloma, lymphoma, sarcoma)
- Osteomyelitis (bone infection)
- Stress fractures
- Osteoporotic fractures

Each of these conditions requires laboratory tests, imaging, and sometimes specialist assessment to diagnose. Early assessment is important to avoid delayed diagnosis.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a careful history and examination. Key questions include:

**About the pain:**
- Is it a deep, dull, poorly localised ache? (This pattern is typical of bone pain, as distinct from joint or soft tissue pain)
- Is it worse with activity or relieved by rest? (May suggest impending fracture)
- Does it occur at night?

**General symptoms (constitutional symptoms):**
- Unexplained weight loss
- Night sweats or fever
- Symptoms suggesting possible malignancy

**Relevant history:**
- Previous cancers (including melanoma that was previously removed)
- Family history of malignancy
- Recent infections
- Ancestry, country of origin, travel history (some conditions such as Lyme disease and sickle cell anaemia can cause bone pain)

**Physical examination typically includes:**
- Breast or prostate examination if malignancy is suspected
- Lymph nodes and skin assessment
- Bone tenderness on gentle pressure
- Skin changes overlying the tender area
- Rash, fever, or deformity
- Unexplained bruising in unrelated areas (may raise concern for inflicted injury)

**Investigations your doctor may arrange:**
- Blood tests: full blood count (FBC), CRP, liver function tests (especially alkaline phosphatase, gammaGT, and AST), serum protein electrophoresis, prostate-specific antigen (PSA), calcium, and phosphate
- Plain X-ray if neoplastic disease, infection, or fracture is suspected
- Bone isotope scan and/or SPECT CT if plain X-ray is negative but clinical suspicion remains

> **Important:** Suspected bone tumours should not be biopsied in the community — biopsy is arranged by the specialist team if needed.

## Conditions to Consider

### Sarcoma (Bone Cancer)

Your doctor will consider sarcoma if there is an unexplained bony mass **plus** one or more of:
- A palpable mass fixed to bone
- Increasing in size
- Radiology suspicious for malignancy
- A recurrence after previous excision
- Suspected spontaneous fracture
- Increasing or persistent bone pain, especially at rest
- Night pain
- Limp (in children)

### Osteoporotic Fractures

- Typically occur without significant injury, commonly in the spine (sacral, lumbar, thoracic), wrist, or hip after a low-energy fall
- Your doctor will have a high index of suspicion in an older person with continuing pain and a history of repeated investigations

### Osteomyelitis (Bone Infection)

- Can occur at any age, with acute or gradual onset of local bone pain, loss of use of a limb, inconsistent fever, or malaise
- May occur in association with chronic, non-healing lower leg ulcers, particularly in people with diabetes
- CRP is nearly always elevated; white blood cell count and plain X-ray may initially be normal

### Spinal Infection

- Consider when back pain is unrelenting, both day and night — often more severe than other causes of back pain
- Fever is not always present
- Prior trauma is not always present

### Stress Fractures

- Most common in the metatarsal bones or tibias — in athletes, those new to exercise, or in people with obesity
- Also occur in specific sports (e.g. spinal fractures in cricket bowlers and gymnasts)
- Fractures can result from abnormal stress on normal bone, or normal stress on abnormal bone (e.g. rickets, malunions — called insufficiency fractures)
- If X-ray is normal but a stress fracture is still suspected, a nuclear medicine bone scan can show increased bone activity at the fracture site

### Paget Disease

- Causes gradual onset of bone pain in older adults; usually not severe; may be associated with arthritis, deformity, and fracture
- Alkaline phosphatase is frequently elevated with otherwise normal liver function tests
- Diagnosed with plain X-ray and bone isotope scan

### Growing Pains (Children)

- Typically bilateral, occurring in an otherwise well child, more often at night
- If the pain is unilateral, associated with a limp, or any other symptoms are present, your doctor will investigate for other causes such as tumour or infection
- Your doctor will also have a high index of suspicion for inflicted injury

## Management

Management depends on the suspected cause. Your doctor will direct the approach based on assessment findings:

- **Suspected primary bone cancer:** Your doctor will refer to orthopaedic specialist assessment.
- **Children with bone pain:** If non-traumatic, any red flags, or unexplained injury, your doctor will arrange acute paediatric specialist assessment before investigations.
- **Suspected sarcoma (children or adults):** Referral to orthopaedic specialist without biopsy or fine needle aspiration. If criteria for high suspicion of cancer are met, your doctor will flag this on the referral and may refer you to Cancer Support Services.
- **Suspected metastatic bone disease:** Your doctor will assess for the likely primary source (lung, breast, prostate, thyroid, kidney, bowel, melanoma, myeloma, lymphoma) and arrange oncology, haematology, or orthopaedic specialist referral accordingly.
- **Impending fracture in the lower limb:** Your doctor may restrict weight bearing (crutches or wheelchair) or apply a shoulder immobiliser (for shoulder/humeral disease) and arrange urgent orthopaedic assessment.
- **Suspected spinal infection:** Your doctor will discuss with the on-call emergency physician or arrange acute orthopaedic assessment, as MRI and hospital admission are likely to be required.
- **Suspected spinal stress fracture:** Your doctor may request musculoskeletal specialist assessment for consideration of MRI.
- **Suspected osteomyelitis:** Your doctor will refer for acute orthopaedic assessment.
- **Paget disease confirmed on X-ray with pain:** Your doctor will refer to a bone clinic (Endocrinology/Bone Health Service) without further investigation.
- **Severe pain requiring narcotics with no diagnosis established:** Your doctor may arrange acute emergency assessment.
