# Multi-Drug Resistant Organisms (MDRO)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA), extended spectrum beta lactamase-producing bacteria (ESBL), vancomycin-resistant enterococcus (VRE), and carbapenem-resistant organisms (CRO).

---

## Background

Multi-drug resistant organisms (MDROs) have emerged and spread in response to antibiotic misuse and overuse in humans, animals, and agricultural production.

The most important preventive strategies are:

- **Antibiotic stewardship** — follow prescribing guidelines and avoid just-in-case antibiotic use
- **Standard infection control** — routine hand hygiene before and after patient contact, environmental hygiene, and asepsis

The four main MDROs are:

- **MRSA** — Methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*
- **ESBL** — Extended spectrum beta lactamase-producing bacteria (e.g., *E. coli*, *Klebsiella*, *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*)
- **VRE** — Vancomycin-resistant enterococcus
- **CRO** — Carbapenem-resistant organism (also called CRE or CPE)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider MDRO if you have a clinical infection and any of the following risk factors:

- Previously tested positive for an MDRO
- Admitted to, or had an invasive procedure (endoscopy, minor surgery, intravenous procedure) in, a New Zealand hospital or long-term care facility in the last 12 months
- Admitted to an overseas hospital in the last 12 months
- Travelled to an endemic country (e.g., India) in the last 12 months (particularly relevant for CRO)

### Risk factors for ESBL

- Living in a residential care home
- Extended hospital stay
- Urinary catheter in place
- Recurrent urinary tract infections
- Frequent or long-term antibiotic use, including extended-spectrum cephalosporins

### Risk factors for MRSA wound infection

- Frequent antibiotic courses
- Infection not responding to standard oral antibiotic treatment
- Family history of MRSA
- Recent stay in a high-risk healthcare setting (e.g., a North Island hospital or overseas hospital)
- Crowded living conditions
- Open or broken skin

### Swab collection

Your doctor will take a sample appropriate to your presentation — for example, a wound swab or faecal sample. For MRSA screening, swabs are taken from the nostrils (moistened swab for both nostrils) and any areas of dermatitis or eczema.

---

## Management

### Infection control

Hand hygiene is the single most important practice to prevent the spread of MDROs. Your doctor or practice team will:

- Wear gloves for patient contact
- Use alcohol-based hand rub before and after contact
- Wear a plastic apron for procedures involving body fluids from MDRO-positive patients
- Decontaminate all shared equipment

Patients are advised to practice careful hand hygiene at home to avoid spreading the infection to others.

---

### ESBL

1. **If you have a positive swab but no symptoms:** Your doctor may consider no antibiotic treatment. Patients can spontaneously lose ESBL colonisation over time (around 75% within 6–12 months). You will need to inform any treatment providers that you are ESBL-positive and seek treatment promptly if you develop an infection.

2. **If you have symptoms and antibiotics are being considered:** Your doctor may consider contacting a microbiologist or infectious diseases specialist before prescribing, as ESBL infections often require carbapenem antibiotics (not available in the community) and some strains may not be treatable with standard antibiotics.

3. **Preventing spread:**
   - If you have a wound, UTI, or collection device, take care with waste disposal
   - Restriction of activities or visitors at home is not necessary
   - If you are in a rest home, your doctor may consider arranging a single room with a private bathroom

---

### MRSA

1. **If you are MRSA-positive but have no symptoms** (and are not a healthcare worker): Your doctor may consider no antibiotic treatment. You will need to inform any treatment providers that you are MRSA-positive and seek treatment if you develop an infection.

2. **Treating infections:**

   **Skin or soft tissue infections:**
   - Small abscesses may be managed with incision and drainage alone
   - Your doctor may consider antiseptics as first-line over topical antibiotics:
     - Silver sulfadiazine cream or povidone-iodine 10% ointment
     - Hydrogen peroxide cream
   - If antibiotics are required, your doctor will base treatment on sensitivity results. Options may include:
     - Co-trimoxazole — 2 tablets twice daily for 5–7 days
     - Doxycycline — 100 mg once daily for 5–7 days (not in children under 12)
     - Erythromycin — 800 mg twice daily for 5–7 days
     - Topical chloramphenicol for ear or eye infections
   - Ciprofloxacin is avoided for MRSA
   - Mupirocin ointment is reserved for treating nasal carriage, not wound infections

   **Other bacterial infections in MRSA-positive patients:**
   - Antibiotics are avoided unless necessary, to reduce the risk of a MRSA flare
   - Your doctor will compare antibiotic sensitivities for both the MRSA and the new infection before prescribing

3. **Decolonisation treatment** — your doctor may consider this on hospital discharge, before elective surgery, or based on infectious diseases advice:

   **Washing (do both washing and ointment):**
   - Shower with chlorhexidine 4% aqueous solution once daily for 1 week (allow 2 minutes contact time before rinsing; moisturise afterwards as it may dry the skin), or
   - Diluted bleach bath (sodium hypochlorite) 2–3 times over 1 week — do not use if there are extensive areas of broken skin

   **Ointment (applied to the nostrils three times daily for 5–7 days):**
   - Mupirocin ointment (funded for MRSA-positive patients) — apply to the inside of each nostril using a clean cotton bud, inserting about 1 cm
   - Fusidic acid ointment (if the strain is susceptible)

   If asymptomatic household members cannot be screened, your doctor may consider empirical treatment for all household members. Household members may need assistance completing decolonisation at home.

---

### VRE and CRO

VRE and CRO infections are very difficult to treat. Your doctor will seek specialist advice in all cases and will not attempt to manage these infections without input from infectious diseases or microbiology services.
