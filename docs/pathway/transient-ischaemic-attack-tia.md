# Transient Ischaemic Attack (TIA)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

A TIA is a medical emergency. This pathway covers initial assessment and management of a suspected TIA in adults. Always discuss your situation with your doctor.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately if:

- Neurological symptoms have **not fully resolved** — if signs and symptoms are still present when seen, this must be managed as a stroke, not a TIA

---

## Background

A TIA is a transient episode of neurological dysfunction caused by focal ischaemia, with no lasting infarction. By definition it must fully resolve within 24 hours — most true TIAs resolve within 1 hour and show no abnormality on brain imaging. TIA is therefore usually a clinical diagnosis based on history and symptoms.

**A TIA is a medical emergency.** The risk of stroke is highest in the first 48 hours after a TIA, and up to 10% in the first 7 days. Immediate treatment is required.

---

## Assessment

### FAST Screening Tool

Your doctor will use the Face, Arm, Speech Test (FAST) to screen for TIA or stroke. Because TIA symptoms may have resolved by the time of presentation, this is often applied retrospectively based on what the patient experienced:

- **Face** — Was one side of the face droopy when asked to smile?
- **Arm** — Did one arm drift down or fall rapidly when both arms were raised to 90 degrees?
- **Speech** — Were there new disturbances such as slurring, difficulty finding words, or naming objects?
- **Take action** — If any of the above were present, emergency assessment is needed.

### Features Typical of TIA

TIA is more likely if symptoms included:

- Unilateral weakness or paralysis in the face, arm, or leg
- Unilateral loss of sensation
- Dysphasia (difficulty with language)
- Monocular blindness (one eye only — often called amaurosis fugax)
- Binocular diplopia or homonymous hemianopia
- Gait or limb ataxia
- Vertigo occurring alongside any of the above
- Dysarthria (slurred speech) where other causes have been excluded

TIA is also more likely if:

- Symptoms came on rapidly and fully recovered within 30 to 60 minutes
- The neurological deficit was maximal at onset
- Symptoms were consistent with a single vascular territory
- Hypoglycaemia has been excluded

### Features Not Typical of TIA

The following symptoms alone are less suggestive of TIA:

- Headache
- Confusion (unless dysphasia is present)
- Impaired consciousness or syncope
- Dizziness or light-headedness on its own
- Isolated positional vertigo
- Generalised (not one-sided) weakness or sensory symptoms
- Bilateral blurred vision or scintillating scotoma (visual aura)
- Incontinence — bladder or bowel
- Amnesia alone

*Note: Ataxia, vertigo, dysphagia, dysarthria, and sensory symptoms affecting part of one limb or the face may be consistent with TIA if they occur together with other typical features.*

### Vascular Territory

Your doctor may determine whether the TIA was in the carotid or vertebrobasilar territory, as this influences management:

**Carotid artery territory:**
- Monocular blindness (amaurosis fugax) — usually rapid onset, brief (under 10 minutes)
- Dysphasia
- Unilateral motor and/or sensory symptoms affecting face and limbs

**Vertebrobasilar artery territory:**
- Cortical blindness
- Diplopia
- Isolated homonymous hemianopia or quadrantanopia
- Bilateral motor and/or sensory symptoms affecting face and/or limbs
- Ataxia

### Differential Diagnoses

Your doctor will also consider other causes that can mimic TIA:

- Hypoglycaemia
- Migraine aura
- Hypotension with or without syncope
- Confusion
- Isolated vertigo
- Partial epileptic seizure
- Anxiety
- Transient global amnesia
- Drop attacks

## Management

### Acute Management

Your doctor will determine the appropriate level of urgency. Urgent specialist (TIA or stroke service) assessment is typically sought if it is 7 days or fewer since the TIA episode and any of the following apply:

- Crescendo TIAs (two or more TIAs within the past week)
- New or known atrial fibrillation (an urgent CT head is needed before starting anticoagulants)
- The patient is already on anticoagulant medications
- Known or newly diagnosed carotid stenosis

For all other TIA presentations, your doctor will seek TIA/stroke service advice to determine whether admission or specific investigations are needed.

### Secondary Prevention

Your doctor will review your medical history and begin appropriate secondary prevention treatment promptly, without necessarily waiting for all investigation results.

**Antiplatelet therapy**

Your doctor may consider antiplatelet medication to reduce the risk of stroke:

- **Clopidogrel** monotherapy is generally considered the best first-line antiplatelet for stroke secondary prevention. A typical approach is 300 mg immediately, then 75 mg daily.
- **Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT)** — clopidogrel plus aspirin — is recommended for 3 weeks in patients with TIA or minor stroke, then aspirin is stopped and clopidogrel is continued alone. DAPT is not used long-term or after moderate or severe stroke.
- **Aspirin** monotherapy (100 mg daily) may be used in patients who do not tolerate clopidogrel.
- If atrial fibrillation is present, **anticoagulation** (not antiplatelet therapy) is required for secondary stroke prevention — your doctor will advise on this separately.
- *Note:* Dipyridamole (Pytazen SR) has been discontinued. Anyone previously on this should be switched — typically to clopidogrel or aspirin monotherapy.

**Blood pressure lowering**

Your doctor may consider starting or intensifying blood pressure treatment:

- Target blood pressure is ≤ 130/80 mmHg. Even if blood pressure is normal, your doctor may aim to reduce systolic blood pressure by around 10 mmHg.
- A low-dose ACE inhibitor is a common starting point, or existing medications may be increased.
- Dose adjustments are made gradually to minimise the risk of falls or symptomatic hypotension.
- Lying and standing blood pressure will be checked.

**Statin therapy**

Your doctor may consider statin therapy for all patients who have had an ischaemic TIA:

- **Atorvastatin 80 mg daily** has been shown to reduce the risk of recurrent stroke or vascular disease.
- In patients who have had a stroke or TIA, the aim is a fasting LDL below 1.8 mmol/L.
- A lower dose of atorvastatin or an alternative such as simvastatin may be used in elderly or frail patients.

**Other secondary prevention measures**

- Aggressively manage diabetes, hypertension, lipids, and atrial fibrillation
- Address lifestyle factors: smoking cessation, weight loss, exercise, reducing alcohol intake
- Rehabilitation support as appropriate

### Investigations

Your doctor will arrange investigations to confirm the diagnosis and assess risk factors. These typically include:

- Blood tests: FBC, electrolytes, creatinine, CRP, random glucose, HbA1c. ESR is added if monocular blindness suggests temporal arteritis. INR if on warfarin.
- ECG — to look for atrial fibrillation or recent myocardial infarction
- Imaging as advised by the TIA/stroke service:

**CT head scan** — routinely performed for TIA, ideally within 24 hours for high-risk patients, to exclude haemorrhage and other pathology.

**Carotid ultrasound** — used to identify patients who may need carotid endarterectomy (surgery). This applies to anterior circulation events in patients who are surgical candidates. The benefit of surgery reduces rapidly over time, so surgery — if indicated — must occur within 1 to 2 weeks of the TIA. If stenosis greater than 50% is found, your doctor will discuss with a TIA/stroke specialist immediately.

### Driving

Your doctor will advise you not to drive following a TIA. New Zealand guidelines (NZTA — Medical Aspects of Fitness to Drive) specify:

- **Car or motorcycle licence:**
  - Single TIA — 1-month stand-down
  - Multiple TIAs — 3-month stand-down (subject to being on secondary prevention treatment)
- **Commercial licence:**
  - Single TIA — minimum 6-month stand-down; specialist review required to return
  - Multiple TIAs — minimum 12-month stand-down; application to NZTA with specialist review required

An occupational therapy driving assessment may be arranged if there is any concern about safety or need for driver retraining.

### Long-Term Management

Your doctor will typically schedule follow-up at 1 week, 1 month, and 3 months after a TIA, then 3 to 6 monthly as appropriate.

Ongoing monitoring includes:

- Continued management of diabetes, hypertension, lipids, and atrial fibrillation
- Repeat ECG to look for paroxysmal atrial fibrillation (PAF) — episodes that stop spontaneously, often asymptomatic, which can progress to permanent AF
- Medication review if multiple comorbidities are present
- Lifestyle support: smoking, weight, exercise, alcohol
