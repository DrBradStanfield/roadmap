# Female Genital Mutilation (FGM)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of patients who have experienced female genital mutilation (FGM), including complications, deinfibulation, and child protection considerations.

---

## Background

Female genital mutilation (FGM) is the term for all procedures involving the partial or total removal of external genitalia or other injury to the female genital organs for non-medical reasons. According to the World Health Organisation, FGM is a global health issue, with over 200 million women and girls affected.

FGM in New Zealand:

- There is no documented evidence of FGM taking place in New Zealand, but there are a growing number of refugees and migrants from countries that practice FGM. Some of these women and children have already undergone FGM and may require specialised health care or support.
- The main ethnic groups affected by FGM in New Zealand are Ethiopian, Somali, Egyptian, Eritrean, Sudanese, Kurdish, and some Muslim Indonesian groups.
- FGM became a criminal offence in New Zealand in 1996. Not only can the person who conducts the procedure be charged, but anyone who makes arrangements for a child to undergo the procedure is liable for a criminal charge.

Identification of FGM:

- Health professionals have an opportunity to sensitively enquire about FGM. Once identified, they can respond to any health needs and refer as needed.
- Identification of FGM may occur in different settings including maternity services, general practitioner clinics, sexual health clinics, gynaecology clinics, and family planning clinics.
- It can be difficult to identify the milder forms of FGM and they may not need specific treatment.

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider any challenges and barriers when assessing you:

- Many women with FGM come from refugee backgrounds and may have received little or no prior health screening, or have limited access to knowledge about sexual or reproductive health.
- Some women may be uncomfortable discussing sexual or reproductive health issues — a female clinician or female interpreter may be preferred.
- Some women may not know the type of FGM they have had, or may not perceive FGM as unusual or harmful.
- Some women may not be aware that symptoms they are experiencing — such as urinary retention or pain passing urine — are associated with FGM.

Your doctor will:

1. Discuss FGM in an appropriate medical context and in a non-judgemental way, building trust before raising sexual health issues. The terms "female circumcision" or "cutting" may be preferred by some patients.
2. Consider the risk to any female children in the family if they come from a culture and family that supports the practice.
3. Record, if possible: country of origin, age when FGM was performed, any previous interventions or referrals.
4. Record the type of FGM if an examination has been performed (see below).
5. Perform cervical screening if indicated, noting that speculum examination is generally not possible in women with Type III FGM who have not had intercourse.
6. Discuss any anxieties or concerns relating to FGM, sexual intercourse, or childbirth.

### Types of FGM

- **Type I (Clitoridectomy):** Partial or total removal of the clitoris and/or the prepuce.
  - Type Ia: removal of the clitoral hood or prepuce only.
  - Type Ib: removal of the clitoris with the prepuce.
- **Type II (Excision):** Partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora, with or without excision of the labia majora.
  - Type IIa: removal of the labia minora only.
  - Type IIb: partial or total removal of the clitoris and the labia minora.
  - Type IIc: partial or total removal of the clitoris, the labia minora and the labia majora.
- **Type III (Infibulation):** Narrowing of the vaginal opening through the creation of a covering seal by cutting and repositioning the inner or outer labia, with or without removal of the clitoris.
  - Type IIIa: removal and apposition of the labia minora.
  - Type IIIb: removal and apposition of the labia majora.
- **Type IV:** All other harmful procedures to the female genitalia for non-medical purposes, including pricking, piercing, incising, stretching, cauterisation, scraping, or introduction of corrosive substances into the vagina.

## Possible Complications

### Short-term Complications

- Haemorrhage
- Shock
- Urinary retention
- Injury to adjacent tissue and/or the urethra
- Infection
- Failure to heal

### Long-term Complications

- Dyspareunia (painful intercourse)
- Dysmenorrhoea (painful periods)
- Haematocolpos (retained menstrual blood)
- Chronic pelvic infections
- Vulval adhesions
- Abscesses
- Calculus formation
- Keloid scars
- Dermoid cysts
- Neuromas

### Sexual Complications

- Fear associated with initial intercourse
- Painful intercourse
- Vaginismus
- Sexual dysfunction

### Pregnancy, Labour, and Delivery Complications

- Difficulties with assessing pelvic size, performing induction of labour with prostaglandins, assessing cervical dilation and fetal presentation, monitoring the baby, and/or inserting a catheter.
- Prolonged labour which can lead to uterine inertia, rupture or prolapse, tearing to the perineum, haemorrhage, and/or birth asphyxia.

### Psychosocial Complications

- Children may report fear, loss of trust, betrayal, and anxiety related to the initial FGM procedure.
- Adults may experience anxiety, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and feelings of incompleteness. They may experience stigma, alienation, shame and fear of being outcast and ineligible for marriage without FGM.

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following:

- **Deinfibulation** — If you have had Type III FGM, your doctor may discuss referral for deinfibulation. Deinfibulation is a surgical procedure that reverses more extensive forms of FGM (Type III) by opening up closed scar tissue. It may be required before sexual intercourse or childbirth.
  - If aged 15 years or younger: your doctor will refer to paediatric surgery.
  - If aged 16 years or older: your doctor will refer to gynaecology.
- **Cervical screening difficulties** — If a speculum examination cannot be performed because of FGM, your doctor may refer you to gynaecology.
- **Child protection** — If there are concerns about imminent or recent FGM of a child, or if a child is considered to be at risk, your doctor is required to contact Oranga Tamariki: Ministry for Children immediately (phone **0508‑FAMILY** / **0508‑326‑459**).
- **Mental health support** — If you require mental health and well-being support and are from a refugee background, your doctor may refer you to Refugees as Survivors New Zealand.
- **Community education** — If community education is required, the FGM Education Programme is available.
