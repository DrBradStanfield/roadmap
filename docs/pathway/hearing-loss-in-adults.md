# Hearing Loss in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to hearing loss in adults — assessing the type, urgency, and likely cause to guide investigation and management.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department if hearing loss is:

- Associated with neurological symptoms, dizziness, or cerebellar signs or symptoms
- Associated with significant head injury
- Idiopathic sudden onset sensorineural hearing loss (an otologic emergency)

---

## Background

Hearing loss is common, affecting:

- 20% of people aged over 15 years
- 40% of people aged over 65 years

It is often under-diagnosed and can affect social interactions, employment, and psychological well-being.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess hearing loss by considering:

### 1. History

- Whether hearing loss is **sudden** (72 hours or less) or **long-standing** (more than 72 hours)
- Whether it is **unilateral** (one ear) or **bilateral** (both ears)

Associated symptoms your doctor will ask about:

- Fever, ear discharge, ear pain, or recurrent ear infections with foul-smelling discharge
- **Non-specific symptoms** (do not help distinguish type): tinnitus, dizziness or vertigo
- **Neurological symptoms:** facial paralysis, ataxia, altered sensation, blurred vision, nausea or vomiting

Any history of intermittent hearing loss — your doctor may consider:

- Ménière's disease
- Autoimmune inner ear disease
- Perilymphatic fistula (fluctuating hearing loss with vertigo provoked by sneezing, lifting, or straining)

### 2. Risk Factors

**Environmental and occupational:**

- Sudden loud noise (e.g., explosion, air bag deployment)
- Hobbies such as loud music or shooting
- Diving or activities with air pressure changes
- Occupational noise exposure (current and past jobs, use of hearing protection)

**Medical, surgical, and family history:**

- Head injury
- Medical conditions: diabetes, hypertension, autoimmune disease, vascular disease, neurofibromatosis type 2, sarcoidosis
- Previous ear surgery
- Family history of hearing loss or otosclerosis

**Ototoxic medications** — drugs that can damage hearing include:

- Salicylates (e.g., aspirin)
- NSAIDs (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs)
- Antibiotics: gentamicin, vancomycin, macrolides, minocycline, chloramphenicol
- Diuretics
- Quinine and antimalarials
- PDE5 inhibitors: sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil
- Platinum-based chemotherapy
- Certain ear drops (e.g., Sofradex, Soframycin, Kenacomb)

### 3. Examination

Your doctor will:

- Perform **otoscopy** (look in the ear) to assess the ear canal and eardrum for wax, foreign bodies, discharge, perforation, retraction, or cholesteatoma (a destructive skin growth in the middle ear)
- Use **tuning fork tests** (Weber and Rinne) to help distinguish between conductive and sensorineural hearing loss — always interpreted alongside examination findings and audiological assessment
- Assess regional lymph nodes
- Perform a **cranial nerve examination** looking for facial weakness, loss of corneal reflex, nystagmus, or cerebellar signs — these may suggest stroke, vestibular schwannoma, malignancy, or multiple sclerosis

### 4. Type and Likely Cause

Your doctor will determine the type of hearing loss:

**Sudden onset sensorineural hearing loss (SSNHL)**

- Defined as sensorineural hearing loss of at least 30 dB across three or more consecutive frequencies, occurring within 72 hours
- An otologic emergency — best outcomes when treated within 72 hours of onset
- Almost always affects one ear; patients may wake with hearing loss or a blocked feeling, with or without tinnitus or vertigo
- 85–90% of cases are idiopathic; other causes include infection (HIV, CMV, herpes, mumps, syphilis), ototoxic drugs, barotrauma, neurological conditions (MS, TIA, stroke), Ménière's disease, and rarely vestibular schwannoma

**Sudden conductive hearing loss**

Potential causes include: ear wax, foreign body, otitis externa, otitis media, tympanic membrane perforation (from barotrauma, injury, or infection), temporal bone fracture, or Eustachian tube dysfunction

**Long-standing sensorineural hearing loss**

Potential causes include:

- **Presbyacusis:** progressive age-related high-frequency hearing loss; reduced speech discrimination in noisy environments
- **Noise-induced hearing loss (NIHL):** from occupational or recreational noise above 85 dB over prolonged periods
- **Vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma):** a benign tumour of cranial nerve VIII, occurring in about 3% with asymmetrical unilateral sensorineural hearing loss; typically slow-growing and may be asymptomatic; associated symptoms (from pressure on nearby structures) can include altered facial sensation, facial weakness, ataxia, and — if very large — blurred vision, nausea, or headaches
- Hereditary or congenital hearing loss (rare in adults)

**Long-standing conductive hearing loss**

Potential causes include:

- **Otosclerosis:** bony overgrowth fixing the stapes; more common in women aged 30–50; may be familial
- Cholesteatoma
- Benign bony growths (exostosis, osteoma)
- Tumour of the external auditory canal (squamous cell carcinoma, basal cell carcinoma, melanoma)
- Ear wax, otitis media with effusion, eardrum perforation, anaemia

## Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

**For sudden onset hearing loss:**

- **Urgent audiometry** (pure tone audiometry with bone conduction and tympanometry) as soon as possible, ideally within 72 hours, to confirm sensorineural vs conductive hearing loss and establish a baseline
- If sudden bilateral sensorineural hearing loss, **blood tests** depending on clinical suspicion: HIV, CMV, syphilis serology, TSH, ANCA, ANA

**For long-standing hearing loss:**

- **Audiometry:** pure tone audiogram with bone conduction, tympanometry, and speech discrimination test

---

## Management

### Sudden Onset Sensorineural Hearing Loss

This is an otologic emergency. Your doctor will:

- Manage any identified reversible cause
- Arrange urgent audiometry (within 72 hours)
- Consider **prednisone 1 mg/kg (up to 60 mg) for 7–14 days** — evidence is uncertain but treatment is considered standard given the potential for permanent hearing loss; best started as soon as possible
- If prednisone is contraindicated, outside the optimal timeframe, or ineffective, your doctor may consider intratympanic steroids or hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT)
- Refer for specialist assessment, including MRI to exclude acoustic neuroma

### Sudden Conductive Hearing Loss

Your doctor will manage the underlying cause:

- Ear wax impaction, otitis externa, otitis media with effusion, foreign body
- Eardrum perforation generally heals spontaneously within days to weeks; your doctor will advise keeping water out of the ear while healing. If the perforation persists for 6 weeks or longer, specialist assessment is arranged.

### Long-Standing Sensorineural Hearing Loss

Your doctor will:

- If tumour is suspected, arrange urgent specialist assessment
- If audiology shows asymmetric or unilateral hearing loss, consider specialist referral for MRI to exclude vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma)
  - MRI is the investigation of choice if aged under 70 years; in older or frail patients, neurological monitoring and referral if indicated
- Advise **preventing further hearing loss:**
  - Avoid ototoxic medications where possible
  - Use well-fitting hearing protection in loud environments
- Consider **hearing aids** in conjunction with an audiologist (for presbyacusis, noise-induced, or hereditary hearing loss)
  - Hearing aids restore hearing to approximately half the loss; they do not restore normal hearing
  - Users will still have difficulty in background noise
  - Styles include in-the-ear and behind-the-ear; prices range from approximately $500 to $10,000 per pair
  - Funding and subsidies may be available — your audiologist can advise
- If no benefit from hearing aids, your doctor may consider referral for other options: cochlear implants (for moderate-to-severe bilateral sensorineural hearing loss), bone-anchored hearing aids, or middle ear implants
- **Assistive listening devices** can also help, with or without hearing aids, using visual or tactile signals to improve communication

### Long-Standing Conductive Hearing Loss

Your doctor will:

- Arrange urgent specialist assessment if cholesteatoma with facial palsy or vertigo, or suspected tumour
- Arrange non-urgent specialist assessment for benign bony growths, otosclerosis, cholesteatoma without facial palsy or vertigo, or persistent eardrum perforation
- Advise avoiding ototoxic medications and using hearing protection in loud environments
- Manage ear wax impaction and otitis media with effusion

### Fitness to Drive and Work

Your doctor will consider the implications of hearing loss on driving and employment, particularly for commercial drivers.
