# First Carpometacarpal Joint Osteoarthritis (CMCJ OA)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of osteoarthritis at the base of the thumb (first carpometacarpal joint).

---

## Background

The first carpometacarpal joint (CMCJ) is a very mobile joint, and this mobility predisposes it to degeneration over time. The condition becomes more common with age and is particularly common in postmenopausal women — the female to male ratio is approximately 6:1. There appears to be an association with previous ligamentous laxity.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about your symptoms, including:

- Pain at the base of the thumb, usually present for months
- Pain that is activity-related — particularly with sustained pinching, such as:
  - gripping
  - unscrewing lids, taps, or door knobs
  - hand sewing
  - turning keys
- Weakness in the thumb
- How the pain affects your daily activities and lifestyle

**Physical examination** typically includes:

- **Inspection** — looking for a square-shaped deformity or prominence at the base of the thumb, reduced web space, or hyperextension of the thumb joint
- **Palpation** — feeling for tenderness at the first CMC joint, and checking for crepitation or subluxation
- **Movement** — assessing range of motion in all planes, with and without resistance, and testing pinch strength
- **Grind test** — your doctor grasps the thumb metacarpal, moves the thumb in a circular motion, and applies axial compression to reproduce symptoms

**Investigations** your doctor may consider:

- X-ray — only if there is severe deformity, another cause such as gout or infection needs to be excluded, or surgery is being considered. Note: there is poor correlation between symptoms and X-ray findings.
- Blood tests (CRP, uric acid, anti-CCP, rheumatoid factor) if inflammatory arthritis is a possibility

---

## Management

Your doctor will usually manage this condition conservatively first, regardless of how severe it appears.

**Education and activity modification**

- Your doctor may provide information about thumb arthritis and advise rest and activity modification.

**Pain relief — your doctor may consider:**

- **Paracetamol** — historically used as a first-line analgesic for mild to moderate osteoarthritis pain, though recent evidence suggests it may be less effective than previously thought and carries some gastrointestinal risk
- **Topical NSAIDs** (anti-inflammatory gels applied to the skin) — most beneficial for superficial joints; applied gently to the skin
- **Capsaicin cream** — works by depleting substance P in the nerves (not directly on inflammation); recommended at 0.025% strength for hand OA. Takes one week for initial effect, 4–6 weeks for full effect. May cause a slight burning sensation initially, which fades with regular use (3–4 times daily). Avoid broken skin; wash hands after application.
- **Oral NSAIDs** (anti-inflammatory tablets) — your doctor will weigh risks and benefits
- Note: there is insufficient evidence to recommend glucosamine or chondroitin for this condition

**Hand therapy**

Your doctor may refer you for physiotherapy or hand therapy if:

- you are experiencing barriers to recovery
- there is loss of strength, range of movement, or function
- pain is a significant feature

**Corticosteroid (steroid) injection**

Your doctor may offer an injection into the joint if other measures have not provided sufficient relief. A typical dose is triamcinolone 10 mg with lignocaine 0.5 mL. Evidence suggests steroid injections are well tolerated and effective at one month, though pain relief may not persist beyond this. If the first injection is ineffective, it would not be repeated without a full clinical re-evaluation.

**Surgery**

Your doctor may consider referring you for surgical assessment if:

- conservative management is no longer providing relief
- there is severe pain or significant reduction in function despite the above measures

Surgical options include removing the trapezium bone or fusing the thumb metacarpal with the trapezium, usually under general anaesthetic. Surgery is generally effective for pain relief, though it does not restore pinch strength. Osteotomy and joint replacement may also be options.
