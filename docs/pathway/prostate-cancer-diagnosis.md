# Prostate Cancer Diagnosis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment of symptomatic men for possible prostate cancer, including when to investigate and when referral may be needed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Prompt Medical Attention

Tell your doctor promptly if you have any of the following:

- Visible blood in the urine (macroscopic haematuria)
- Signs of kidney failure (nausea, tiredness, leg swelling)
- New, severe, or progressive bone pain — especially if localised or associated with a fracture not caused by injury
- Neurological symptoms suggesting spinal cord compression (leg weakness, numbness, loss of bladder or bowel control)

---

## Background

- Prostate cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in New Zealand men, with approximately 3,500 new diagnoses and 600 deaths each year.
- The mortality rate from prostate cancer is around 3%.
- Māori men are much less likely to be diagnosed but are 37% more likely to die from prostate cancer — partly due to inequitable access to investigations.
- There are ongoing debates about the accuracy of screening tools and inconsistent approaches to treatment. Your doctor will help you weigh the benefits and risks of testing.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess whether prostate cancer should be investigated. This includes:

### Risk Factors Your Doctor Will Ask About

- **Family history:**
  - One first-degree relative (father or brother) diagnosed with prostate cancer under 70 doubles your risk
  - Two or more first-degree relatives increases risk by 4 to 5 times
  - Familial cancer syndromes such as Lynch syndrome or the BRCA gene also increase risk
- Visible blood in the urine
- Lower urinary tract symptoms (see below)
- Systemic symptoms such as bone pain, unexplained tiredness, weight loss, or neurological symptoms

### Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms

**Obstructive symptoms:**
- Poor urine flow
- Hesitancy before urination begins
- Dribbling after urination
- Overflow incontinence

**Irritative symptoms:**
- Frequent urination
- Pain when urinating (dysuria)
- Waking at night to urinate (nocturia)
- Sudden urgency to urinate
- Blood in the urine
- Recurrent urinary tract infections

### Physical Examination

Your doctor may examine:
- External genitalia (testes, foreskin, urethral opening)
- The prostate via a **digital rectal examination (DRE)** — this checks prostate size, texture, and shape:
  - A smooth, firm, symmetrical enlargement usually suggests benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)
  - Asymmetrical hard areas or nodules raise suspicion for prostate cancer
  - Importantly, not all prostate cancers cause an elevated PSA — a suspicious DRE should be investigated even if PSA is normal
- Abdomen — for a palpable bladder, masses, or swelling
- Neurological — lower limb power, reflexes, and sensation

### Investigations

Your doctor may order:

- **Urine dipstick** and midstream urine (MSU) if abnormal
- **PSA (prostate-specific antigen)** blood test — not routinely recommended in men with less than 10 years' life expectancy
  - A PSA above 4 nanograms/mL may be abnormal in any age group and has about a 30% positive predictive rate for cancer
  - Non-cancerous causes of elevated PSA include: urinary tract infection, prostatitis, benign prostatic hyperplasia, recent prostate biopsy or cystoscopy, ejaculation within 48 hours, and intense exercise (including cycling) within 48 hours
  - If the PSA is abnormal, your doctor may repeat it — typically after 12 weeks if there is a reversible cause being treated, or after 6 weeks if there is no reversible cause
  - Do **not** repeat the PSA if it is ≥ 50 nanograms/mL, or if ≥ 10 nanograms/mL with severe back pain and acute neurological symptoms — these require urgent management
- **Creatinine** (kidney function)
- **Renal ultrasound** if there is a palpably enlarged bladder, recurrent urinary tract infections, or recurrent loin pain

Note: Ultrasound of the prostate alone should not be arranged in primary care — a urologist will arrange this if needed.

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following approaches depending on your results:

**If PSA is below 4 nanograms/mL, with no red flags and no suspicious DRE:**
- Prostate cancer is considered highly unlikely
- Your doctor may manage any lower urinary tract symptoms as benign prostatic hyperplasia
- Follow-up will depend on your family history and your own informed preference:
  - Strong family history (first-degree relative with prostate cancer under 65): your doctor may recommend checking PSA and DRE every 12 months if aged 40–70
  - No significant family history: there is no clear evidence that regular testing reduces mortality; if you choose to continue, testing every 1–4 years may be offered

**If PSA is 4 nanograms/mL or above, with no red flags and no suspicious DRE:**
- If PSA is ≥ 50 nanograms/mL, your doctor will refer you to urology without waiting for a repeat PSA
- If there is high clinical concern after a single abnormal result, your doctor may seek urology advice — an MRI or biopsy may be considered
- Otherwise, your doctor will repeat the PSA in 6–12 weeks after addressing any reversible causes
- If the repeat PSA remains abnormal for your age (see thresholds below), referral to urology is recommended

**PSA thresholds for urology referral (Ministry of Health guidelines):**
- Men aged 70 or under with normal DRE: PSA ≥ 4 nanograms/mL
- Men aged 71–75 with normal DRE: PSA ≥ 10 nanograms/mL
- Men aged 76 or over with normal DRE: PSA ≥ 20 nanograms/mL
- Men with a palpable abnormality on DRE: refer regardless of PSA level
- A significant PSA rise of more than 1.5 nanograms/mL/year from a previously low value may also warrant referral

**If PSA is above 100 nanograms/mL on a single reading with bone pain:**
- Your doctor will arrange urgent urology assessment, taking into account your other health conditions
- If life expectancy is limited, palliative care support may also be considered

**Urgent oncology input is sought if all three of the following apply:**
- PSA ≥ 10 micrograms/L
- Severe back pain
- Acute neurological symptoms consistent with spinal cord compression or cauda equina syndrome

**For older patients:**
- The benefits of curative treatment in men over 70 are unproven
- Your doctor may discuss other management options including active surveillance, androgen deprivation therapy, or watchful waiting, as decided by a multidisciplinary team
