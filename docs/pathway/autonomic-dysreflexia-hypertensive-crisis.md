# Autonomic Dysreflexia (Hypertensive Crisis)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the recognition and emergency management of autonomic dysreflexia (AD) — a potentially life-threatening hypertensive crisis that can occur in people with spinal cord injury (SCI) at or above the T6 level. Always discuss management with your doctor.

---

## What Is Autonomic Dysreflexia?

Autonomic dysreflexia is a medical emergency. It can occur in anyone with a spinal cord injury at or above the T6 level and rarely occurs with injury below T6.

If not recognised and treated **promptly**, blood pressure can escalate to dangerous levels, causing intracranial haemorrhage, seizures, cardiac arrhythmia, or death.

It is triggered by any irritating or painful stimulus below the level of injury — most commonly urinary retention or faecal impaction, which can often be managed at home or in the community.

People with SCI at or above T6 should carry an autonomic dysreflexia emergency card describing first aid treatment.

---

## Symptoms

About 85% of cases are caused by urinary tract or catheter problems. Symptoms include:

- Severe, pounding headache
- Sudden significant rise in blood pressure
- Bradycardia (slow pulse), or occasionally fast pulse or irregular rhythm
- Blurred vision or spots in the visual field
- Nasal congestion
- Profuse sweating and/or skin flushing above the level of injury (especially face, neck, shoulders)
- Goosebumps above the level of injury
- Feelings of apprehension or anxiety
- Sometimes no symptoms at all despite significantly elevated blood pressure ("silent" AD)

**Important:** People with SCI often have a baseline systolic BP of 90–110 mmHg. A rise of 20 mmHg or more above their normal baseline is clinically significant — even a BP of 130/90 may represent AD in this population.

## Emergency Response — Do Not Leave the Patient Alone

Your doctor or emergency team will manage this systematically. The steps below reflect established clinical guidance.

### If Blood Pressure Is Not Elevated

If symptoms are present but BP is not raised and no cause is found, your doctor will seek specialist advice and monitor closely at frequent intervals.

### If Blood Pressure Is Elevated — Step-by-Step Management

1. **Sit the patient upright** — immediately sit up or elevate the head of the bed.

2. **Loosen clothing and constrictive devices** — including compression stockings and abdominal binders.

3. **Monitor BP and pulse every 2–5 minutes** — watching for further rises or hypotension.

4. **Check the urinary catheter (if one is in place):**
   - Check entire system for kinks, folds, obstructions, and correct placement
   - Check for bladder distension
   - Ensure drainage bag is not full
   - If catheter is not draining and BP remains elevated: remove catheter, instil lignocaine 2% lubricating jelly into the urethra, wait 3–5 minutes, insert new catheter
   - Drain bladder gradually: 500 mL first, then 250 mL every 10–15 minutes (avoid sudden large drainage especially if BP medication has been given)
   - Continue monitoring BP for at least 4 hours after bladder is emptied

5. **If no catheter and bladder/abdomen appears distended (urinary retention likely):**
   - Instil lignocaine 2% jelly into the urethra, wait 3–5 minutes
   - Insert an indwelling catheter and drain gradually as above

6. **Check BP before and after each intervention.** Medications are indicated if systolic BP reaches 170 mmHg or above.

7. **If systolic BP is ≥ 170 mmHg — give glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) spray** (unless contraindicated):
   - **Do not use GTN** if the patient has taken sildenafil or vardenafil within 24 hours, or tadalafil within 4 days
   - 1 puff under the tongue — response begins in 2–3 minutes and lasts up to 30 minutes
   - Repeat every 5–10 minutes up to a maximum of 4 puffs if BP remains high
   - Use only short-acting agents — there is risk of significant hypotension once the trigger is resolved

8. **If catheter is draining but BP remains elevated — check for faecal impaction:**
   - Use copious 2% lignocaine jelly on a gloved finger to examine the rectum
   - If rectum is full and BP < 170 mmHg: perform gentle manual disimpaction
   - If rectum is full and BP ≥ 170 mmHg: give 1 puff GTN sublingual first, then apply lignocaine jelly, wait 2 minutes, gently remove faecal mass
   - If symptoms worsen during this: stop immediately, instil more topical anaesthetic, recheck after 20 minutes
   - If BP does not fall within 1 minute after evacuation, repeat the sequence

9. **Check for less common triggers** (if cause still not found): pressure sores, UTI, ingrown toenail, or any source of irritation below the injury level. Ensure adequate analgesia (e.g. morphine) if a persistent noxious stimulus is identified.

10. **Pre-procedure caution:** If a patient has a history of AD, your doctor may pre-treat with a glyceryl trinitrate or clonidine patch before urological or other procedures, allowing immediate removal once the stimulus is resolved.

## When to Call an Ambulance

If no cause can be found and BP cannot be adequately reduced with treatment:

- Call a Priority 1 ambulance
- Go to the **nearest** emergency department — this is a medical emergency
- Keep the patient upright and continue monitoring while waiting

---

## When to Seek Specialist Advice

Your doctor will contact a spinal cord impairment specialist or general medicine for advice if:

- Symptoms are present but BP is not raised and no cause is identified
- AD episodes are recurrent or difficult to manage
