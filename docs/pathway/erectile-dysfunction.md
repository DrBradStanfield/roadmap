# Erectile Dysfunction

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of erectile dysfunction (ED) in men.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department immediately if:

- Pharmacologically induced erection lasting more than 4 hours (priapism) — this requires urgent treatment to prevent permanent damage

---

## Background

Erectile dysfunction is very common:

- Approximately 50% of men aged 50 years or older have some degree of ED
- Approximately 80% of men aged 80 years or older have some degree of ED
- The incidence is even higher in men with cardiovascular disease or diabetes

If ED does not bother you or your partner, no treatment is necessary.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will use erectile difficulty as an opportunity to assess your general health. ED can be an early warning sign of cardiovascular disease.

### Risk Factors Your Doctor Will Ask About

- Smoking
- Obesity
- Cardiovascular disease or hypertension
- High cholesterol (dyslipidaemia)
- Diabetes
- Age

### Causes Your Doctor Will Consider

- **Psychogenic** — regardless of the underlying cause, there is often a psychological component (anxiety about whether it will happen again)
- **Cardiovascular disease** — ED may be an early sign of future cardiovascular events
- **Diabetes** — via blood vessel or nerve damage
- **Hormonal** — testosterone deficiency, thyroid disorder, or high prolactin levels
- **Medications, recreational drugs, or alcohol**
- **Smoking**
- **Neurological** — e.g. stroke, spinal cord injury, Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis
- **Post-surgery or radiotherapy** — e.g. after radical prostatectomy
- **Local penile factors** — e.g. Peyronie's disease

### History Your Doctor Will Take

- Details about erections: ability to achieve an erection, presence of nocturnal or morning erections, quality and duration, whether it is situational or global
- Presence of pain or curvature during erection (may suggest Peyronie's disease)
- Premature ejaculation
- Stress, anxiety, or depression and their effect on libido
- Medications that may affect erectile function:
  - Thiazide diuretics, spironolactone, beta-blockers
  - Antipsychotics and antidepressants (especially SSRIs)
  - Anti-androgens (e.g. cyproterone)
- Lifestyle: physical activity, smoking, alcohol, recreational drugs
- Previous sexually transmitted infections, urological surgery, or genital trauma
- Cardiovascular risk factors

### Distinguishing Psychogenic vs Organic ED

| Feature | Mainly Psychogenic | Mainly Organic |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Sudden | Gradual |
| Pattern | Situational | Consistent |
| Course | Intermittent | Constant |
| Morning erections | Normal | Reduced or absent |
| Nocturnal erections | Present | Reduced |
| Anxiety | Primary cause | Secondary to ED |

### Examination

Your doctor will typically:

- Assess cardiovascular health (blood pressure, pulse, BMI, lower limb pulses)
- Examine the genitalia for signs of Peyronie's disease (palpable plaques) and assess testicular size
- Look for signs of androgen deficiency (reduced body hair, gynaecomastia, reduced muscle mass)
- Assess for neurological disease if indicated

### Investigations

Your doctor may organise:

- Full blood count, electrolytes, kidney function
- HbA1c and lipids
- Morning serum testosterone — particularly if libido is reduced, or if you have signs of androgen deficiency, or are younger than 50
- Liver function tests (if high alcohol intake)
- Vitamin B12 (very low levels are associated with ED)
- Thyroid function tests
- Prolactin (if low testosterone or signs of hypogonadism)

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following approaches, depending on the cause and severity.

### 1. Treat Contributing Medical Conditions

- Optimise management of cardiovascular disease risk factors, diabetes, thyroid disorders, and high prolactin
- Review and reduce contributing medications if possible

### 2. Lifestyle Changes

The following can have a meaningful positive impact:

- Stopping smoking
- Reducing or stopping recreational drugs
- Reducing alcohol intake
- Increasing regular exercise

### 3. Testosterone Deficiency

If testosterone deficiency is identified, your doctor will discuss treatment options with you.

### 4. Phosphodiesterase 5 (PDE5) Inhibitors

Your doctor may consider a trial of PDE5 inhibitors. These are not subsidised for ED in New Zealand and are available privately.

**Important safety note:** PDE5 inhibitors are absolutely contraindicated if you are taking any form of nitrates (oral, sublingual, transdermal, or spray) — the combination can cause life-threatening drops in blood pressure.

Other contraindications include severe cardiovascular disease and severe liver disease.

**How they work:**
- They improve erection quality but not libido directly (though confidence gained from better erections can improve libido indirectly)
- Sexual stimulation is still required for them to work
- About 70–80% of men have a worthwhile improvement in erection quality
- Low doses are often very effective in psychogenic ED and may only be needed short term
- If one PDE5 inhibitor does not work well, there is a good chance a different one will

**Sildenafil (e.g. Viagra):**
- Generic versions are available and can be inexpensive
- Onset approximately 60 minutes; less effective if taken after a meal
- Starting dose 25 mg, increasing in 25 mg steps up to 100 mg
- Each dose should be tried at least 5 times before concluding it is ineffective
- Also available through community pharmacy (for men aged 35–70) without a prescription

**Tadalafil (e.g. Cialis):**
- Onset approximately 30 minutes; not affected by food
- Can be taken as 10–20 mg on demand, or 5 mg once daily
- Remains effective for 24–36 hours

**Side effects** are usually mild: headache, facial flushing, indigestion, blue-tinged vision, and raised blood pressure.

**If priapism occurs:** If a pharmacologically induced erection lasts more than 1 hour, try a warm shower then gentle walking. If it remains firm after 4 hours, go to the emergency department immediately.

A penis ring applied at the base once erection starts can help maintain partial erections (remove after sexual activity).

### 5. Psychological Therapies

Your doctor may refer you to:

- A health improvement practitioner (HIP) or health coach
- A psychologist or counsellor
- A sex therapist (private referral)

### 6. Other Treatment Options (Specialist)

If you are considering self-funded treatments, your doctor may refer you to a urologist to discuss:

- **Injected vasodilators** — injection into the penis causes erection within 10 minutes without requiring arousal. Requires training and prescription. Side effects include discomfort, scarring, and risk of prolonged erection.
- **Penile prosthesis** — a surgical implant, suitable for carefully selected patients with irreversible organic ED who have not responded to other treatments.
- **Vacuum pump devices** — can be suitable for partial ED or if PDE5 inhibitors have not worked. Side effects include penile pain, numbness, coldness, and difficulty ejaculating.

### 7. Associated Conditions

**Premature ejaculation:**
- Behavioural techniques: the "squeeze method" or "start-stop" technique (both require a cooperative partner)
- Wearing a condom may reduce sensation and help
- Medications: daily SSRI or topical local anaesthetic cream (e.g. Emla)

**Peyronie's disease (penile curvature):**
- For mild curvature without symptoms or impact on sexual function, observation is safe
- No medications have been shown to effectively limit pain or deviation
- Daily vacuum pump use for 6 months can be tried but is unlikely to be effective; traction devices are not recommended
- If the curvature has stabilised, is beyond 45 degrees, and is preventing intercourse, your doctor may refer you to a urologist to discuss surgery
