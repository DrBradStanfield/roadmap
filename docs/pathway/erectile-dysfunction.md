# Erectile Dysfunction

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: May 2025.*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of erectile dysfunction (ED) in men.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call 111 or go to the emergency department if:

- An erection caused by medicine lasts more than 4 hours (priapism). This needs urgent emergency treatment.

---

## Background

Erectile dysfunction is common:

- Approximately 50% of men aged 50 years or older have some degree of ED
- Approximately 80% of men aged 80 years or older have some degree of ED
- The incidence is even higher in men with cardiovascular disease or diabetes

If ED does not bother you or your partner, no treatment is necessary.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will use erectile difficulty as an opportunity to assess your general health. ED can be an early warning sign of cardiovascular disease.

### Risk Factors Your Doctor Will Ask About

- Smoking
- Obesity
- Cardiovascular disease
- High blood pressure (hypertension)
- High cholesterol (dyslipidaemia)
- Diabetes
- Older age

### Causes Your Doctor Will Consider

- **Psychogenic:** whatever the cause, ED is likely to have a psychological part (for example, wondering whether it will happen again)
- **Cardiovascular disease:** ED may be an early warning sign of future cardiovascular events
- **Diabetes:** through blood vessel or nerve damage
- **Hormonal:** testosterone deficiency, thyroid disorder, or high prolactin levels
- **Medications, recreational drugs, or alcohol**
- **Smoking**
- **Neurological:** for example, stroke, spinal cord or brain injury, dementia, Parkinson's disease, or multiple sclerosis
- **After major surgery or radiotherapy:** for example, after radical prostatectomy
- **Local penile factors:** for example, Peyronie's disease or scarring of the erectile tissue (cavernous fibrosis)

### History Your Doctor Will Take

- Details about erections: whether they happen on their own or need direct stimulation, whether you have night-time or morning erections, whether they are absent altogether or fail some of the time, and their quality, duration and circumstances
- Pain, bend or curvature during erection (may suggest Peyronie's disease)
- Premature ejaculation
- Stress, anxiety, or depression and their effect on libido and relationships
- Previous use of PDE5 inhibitors, and medications that may affect erectile function:
  - Blood pressure medicines: thiazide diuretics (the most likely to affect erections), spironolactone, methyldopa, clonidine, and beta-blockers
  - Antipsychotics and antidepressants (especially SSRIs)
  - Anti-androgens (for example, cyproterone)
- Lifestyle: physical activity, smoking, alcohol, recreational drugs
- Previous sexually transmitted infections, urological surgery, hernia surgery, or genital trauma
- Cardiovascular risk factors

### Distinguishing Psychogenic vs Organic ED

| Feature | Mainly Psychogenic | Mainly Organic |
|---|---|---|
| Onset | Sudden | Gradual |
| Circumstances | Situational | Global (all situations) |
| Course | Intermittent | Constant |
| Erections outside intercourse | Rigid | Poor |
| Night-time or early morning erections | Normal | Inconsistent |
| Psychosexual problems | Long history | Secondary to ED |
| Partner problems | At onset | Secondary to ED |
| Anxiety or fear | Primary cause | Secondary to ED |

### Examination

Your doctor will typically:

- Assess cardiovascular health (weight, BMI, blood pressure, pulse, lower limb pulses)
- Examine the genitalia for signs of Peyronie's disease (firm plaques in the erectile tissue) and check testicular size and consistency.
- Look for signs of androgen deficiency (loss of body hair, gynaecomastia, reduced muscle mass)
- Assess for neurological disease if indicated

### Investigations

Your doctor may organise:

- Full blood count, electrolytes, kidney function
- HbA1c and lipids
- Morning serum testosterone, especially if libido is reduced, if you have symptoms or signs of androgen deficiency, or if you are younger than 50 years
- Liver function tests (especially if high alcohol intake is suspected)
- Vitamin B12 (very low levels are associated with ED)
- Thyroid function tests
- Prolactin (if low testosterone and signs of hypogonadism suggest high prolactin)

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following approaches, depending on the cause and severity.

### 1. Treat Contributing Medical Conditions

- Optimise management of cardiovascular disease risk factors, diabetes, thyroid disorders, and high prolactin
- Review contributing medications, and reduce or stop them if possible

### 2. Lifestyle Changes

These changes may have a positive impact:

- Stopping smoking
- Stopping recreational drugs
- Reducing alcohol intake
- Increasing exercise

### 3. Testosterone Deficiency

If testosterone deficiency is a possibility, your doctor may look into it further and discuss treatment options with you.

### 4. Phosphodiesterase 5 (PDE5) Inhibitors

Your doctor may consider a trial of PDE5 inhibitors. They are not subsidised for ED.

**Important safety note:** PDE5 inhibitors are absolutely contraindicated if you use any form of nitrates (oral, sublingual, transdermal, or spray). PDE5 inhibitors may react severely with nitrates and cause fatal widening of the blood vessels (vasodilatation).

Other contraindications include severe cardiovascular disease, severe liver disease, and medicines that block liver enzymes. These medicines may raise PDE5 inhibitor levels, so your doctor or pharmacist will check your current medicines for interactions.

**How they work:**
- They improve erections but not libido directly. Libido can improve indirectly, through the confidence that more reliable erections bring and a placebo effect.
- Sexual stimulation is still needed for them to work
- About 70 to 80% of men have a worthwhile improvement in erection quality
- Men with more severe ED tend to respond poorly
- Low doses are very effective in psychogenic ED and are often only needed short term
- If one PDE5 inhibitor does not work well, there is a good chance a different one will

**Sildenafil (e.g. Viagra):**
- Generic versions can cost as little as $1 to $2 per tablet
- Onset 60 minutes; less effective if you have eaten recently
- Start low at 25 mg and increase in steps of 25 mg, to a maximum of 100 mg
- Each dose should be tried at least 5 times before deciding it is ineffective
- Also available from a trained community pharmacist for men aged 35 to 70 years. Exclusions and consultation costs apply.

**Tadalafil (e.g. Cialis):**
- Onset 30 minutes; not affected by food
- Either 10 to 20 mg on demand, or 5 mg once a day
- Generally remains effective for 24 to 36 hours

**Side effects** are usually mild: headache, raised blood pressure, facial flushing, indigestion, and blue-tinged vision.

**If priapism occurs:** If an erection caused by medicine lasts longer than 1 hour, try a warm shower, then go for a walk. A firm erection after 4 hours needs urgent emergency treatment: call 111 or go to the emergency department.

If a PDE5 inhibitor works only partly, a penis ring placed around the base of the penis once erection starts can help (remove after sexual activity).

### 5. Psychological Therapies

Your doctor may refer you to:

- A health improvement practitioner (HIP) or health coach, if available
- A psychologist or counsellor
- A sex therapist (private referral)

### 6. Other Treatment Options (Specialist)

If you are considering self-funded treatments, your doctor may refer you privately to a urologist to discuss:

- **Injected vasodilators:** an injection into the penis causes an erection within 10 minutes without needing arousal. It works for most men but may not work with severe blood vessel disease. It needs a prescription and training from experienced clinicians, and the dose starts low and is adjusted. Side effects may include discomfort, scarring at the injection site, and a prolonged erection, which is painful and after 4 hours causes permanent damage to the erectile tissue.
- **Penile prosthesis:** a surgical implant for carefully selected men with irreversible organic ED who have not responded to less invasive treatments. The main complications are infection, which usually means removal, and mechanical failure.
- **Vacuum pump devices:** these have a limited role. Response varies and may be unreliable. They can be suitable for partial ED or if PDE5 inhibitors have not worked. Side effects include penile pain, numbness, coldness, and difficulty ejaculating.

### 7. Associated Conditions

**Premature ejaculation:**
- Ejaculation that keeps happening with minimal stimulation before, on, or within 1 minute of penetration, with a lack of control and sexual dissatisfaction
- Behavioural techniques: the "squeeze method" (the head of the penis is withdrawn and squeezed just before ejaculation) or the "start-stop" technique (stimulation stops to hold back the urge). Both work, but both need a cooperative partner.
- Wearing a condom reduces sensation, so it may help
- Medications: a daily SSRI, or topical local anaesthetic cream (Emla cream)

**Peyronie's disease (penile curvature):**
- For mild curvature without symptoms or impact on sexual function, observation is safe
- No medications effectively limit pain or deviation
- Daily vacuum pump use for 6 months can be tried but is unlikely to be effective. Traction devices are not recommended: they do not work and can cause nerve damage.
- Surgery may improve the bend. If after 6 months the curvature has stabilised, is beyond 45 degrees, and is preventing intercourse, your doctor may refer you privately to a urologist to discuss surgery.
