# Long QT Syndrome

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of long QT syndrome — both acquired (usually medication-related) and congenital (higher risk of sudden cardiac death).

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

The following require immediate emergency assessment:

- **Torsades de pointes** — a serious ventricular arrhythmia
- **Suspected inherited long QT syndrome**

---

## Background

### Two Types of Long QT Syndrome

- **Acquired** — usually caused by medications or low potassium (hypokalaemia). More common and often reversible.
- **Congenital (inherited)** — this group has a higher risk of sudden cardiac death. May be associated with congenital deafness.

### Medications That Prolong the QT Interval

The following drug classes can prolong the QT interval:

- **Antihistamines**
- **Anti-arrhythmics** — amiodarone and sotalol should not be used together. Amiodarone should only be combined with other anti-arrhythmics under specialist direction.
- **Psychoactive drugs** — lithium, tricyclics, haloperidol, phenothiazines, citalopram, escitalopram
- **Domperidone** (see Medsafe safety warning)
- **Methadone** — cardiac vigilance recommended (Medsafe Prescriber Update)
- **Antibiotics** — erythromycin, clarithromycin

The QT-prolonging effect of these drugs may be worsened by drugs that increase their concentration — particularly fluconazole, macrolides, SSRIs, and diuretics (the latter via hypokalaemia).

**Note:** Some drugs can cause long QT but rarely cause torsades de pointes (e.g., amiodarone).

## Assessment

### When to Assess for Long QT Syndrome

Assess if the patient has a past history of:

- An ECG suggesting QT may be prolonged
- Unexplained syncope
- Family history of sudden death (which may indicate inherited long QT syndrome)

### Indications of Inherited Long QT Syndrome

Your doctor will consider inherited long QT syndrome in any patient:

- With a family history of definite long QT syndrome, or sudden cardiac death in someone younger than 40 years
- Younger than 40 years with unexplained syncope — particularly syncope that:
  - Has no warning
  - Occurs on exercise
  - Is triggered by sudden noise or fright
  - Occurs when diving into water
  - Occurs when lying flat (supine)

**Multiple ECGs are required.** A single normal ECG does not exclude long QT syndrome. Your doctor will typically arrange 2–3 ECGs over 4–5 days.

### Measuring the QT Interval

QTc is the QT interval (from the beginning of the QRS complex to the end of the T wave), corrected for heart rate. It is best measured in lead II, V5, or V6.

**Determining the end of the T wave — tangent method:**

1. Draw a line from the peak of the T wave through the steepest part of the descending slope.
2. The end of the T wave is where this line intersects the isoelectric line.
3. If the U wave amplitude is more than 50% of the T wave amplitude, include the U wave in the measurement.

For drug-induced QT prolongation, the QTc is ideally calculated as the median of measurements across multiple leads: I, II, aVF, V2, V4, V6.

**Calculating QTc — Bazett formula:**

> QTc = QT ÷ √RR

Free online tools are available for this calculation (e.g., MDCalc Corrected QT Interval).

**Tip:** If the QT interval is more than 50% of the R-R interval, this is a reliable marker that QT is prolonged.

ECG machine estimates of QTc may be unreliable if the patient has:
- Atrial fibrillation
- Marked sinus arrhythmia
- Frequent ectopic heartbeats
- Bundle branch block

In patients with clinical suspicion of long QT and toxicological overdose, QT should be calculated manually.

### QTc Thresholds — Defining Long QT

| Group | Long QT defined as QTc greater than |
|---|---|
| Females (non-neonatal) | 0.47 seconds |
| Males | 0.45 seconds |
| Neonates | 0.45 seconds |

**Note on neonates:** Neonatal ECGs are difficult to interpret. If there is a family history concern, your doctor will seek acute paediatric advice.

QTc can vary within individuals, so several ECGs are often required before a diagnosis is confirmed or excluded.

### Causes to Investigate

Your doctor will look for a reversible cause, including:

- Medications or medication interactions (see list above)
- Myocardial ischaemia
- Bradycardia
- Electrolyte disturbances — low potassium, low magnesium, low calcium, low phosphate
- Thyroid dysfunction

Investigations typically include: potassium, calcium, magnesium, phosphate, and thyroid function tests (TFTs).

## Management

### Emergency Situations

- **Torsades de pointes:** Emergency care is required immediately. Your doctor will activate emergency services and arrange acute cardiology assessment (or acute paediatric medicine assessment for patients under 15 years).
- **Syncope:** Your doctor will arrange acute cardiology assessment (or acute paediatric medicine assessment for patients under 15 years).

### Non-Urgent Specialist Referral

Your doctor will arrange a non-acute cardiology assessment (or paediatric assessment for patients under 15 years) if:

- You are asymptomatic and no reversible cause has been identified
- Your ECG is suspicious but not clearly diagnostic
- You have a family history of long QT in a first-degree relative, even if your own ECG is normal
- Inherited long QT syndrome is suspected (this group has a higher risk of sudden cardiac death)

If your doctor is uncertain about admission or follow-up, they will seek specialist advice from cardiology or paediatric medicine.

### Addressing Reversible Causes

Reversible causes contributing to QT prolongation should be corrected. Avoid QT-prolonging medications where possible.

**Correcting electrolyte abnormalities:**
- Replace potassium (treat underlying hypokalaemia)
- Stop potassium-depleting medications
- Start magnesium supplementation (treat underlying hypomagnesaemia)
- Manage thyroid dysfunction and arrange follow-up if appropriate

**Important:** Adrenaline-containing local anaesthetic is not recommended in patients with long QT syndrome.

### QT Thresholds for Medication Decisions

If a medication is suspected as the cause, your doctor will use these QTc thresholds to guide dose decisions:

| QTc (males) | QTc (females) | Action |
|---|---|---|
| 0.46–0.50 seconds | 0.48–0.50 seconds | Reduce or discontinue the medication if clinically appropriate; repeat ECG |
| Greater than 0.50 seconds | Greater than 0.50 seconds | Withdraw the medication — significant risk of torsades de pointes |

**Note:** Torsades de pointes is twice as common in females as in males.

If there is significant risk from stopping the medication, your doctor will consider whether a lower dose is appropriate, and may discuss with the original prescriber.

If the QT returns to normal after a medication change, specialist cardiology referral is generally not required.
