# Wound Infections

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of wound infections — from localised skin wound infections to spreading or systemic infections.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department immediately if:

- Suspected **necrotising fasciitis** (severe pain disproportionate to appearance, rapidly spreading soft tissue involvement, signs of sepsis, necrotic or blistering skin changes)
- Suspected **sepsis** (confirmed or suspected infection plus looking very unwell, with features of severe or moderate abnormal observations such as rapid heart rate or breathing)

---

## Assessment

### Diagnosing a Wound Infection

Wound infection is diagnosed clinically. Signs of a **localised wound infection** include:

- Wound breakdown or dehiscence
- Increase in wound size or delayed healing
- Malodour
- New or increased pain in the wound
- Increased, altered, or purulent (pus-like) discharge
- Wound bed discolouration (e.g., deep red or grey)
- Pocketing or bridging in the wound bed
- Redness (erythema), swelling, warmth, or hardness within 2 cm of the wound edge

### Signs of Spreading or Systemic Infection

Your healthcare provider will also assess for features suggesting infection has spread beyond the wound, including:

- Severe, excessive, or rapidly increasing pain — consider necrotising fasciitis
- Redness extending more than 2 cm from the wound edge
- Swelling, hardness, or crepitus spreading beyond the periwound area
- Lymphangitis (red streaking following the course of a blood vessel)
- Malaise or non-specific deterioration in general health
- Fever, chills, or rigors
- Rapid heart rate (tachycardia)
- Abscess formation

### Wound Swab or Pus Culture

Your healthcare provider may take a wound swab or collect pus to send for laboratory culture. This is typically done when:

- There are signs of spreading or systemic infection
- The infection is not responding as expected to antibiotic treatment
- The wound is at high risk of resistant or unusual organisms (e.g., MRSA, post-surgical wounds, bite wounds, wounds sustained in water, heavily contaminated wounds)

Note: wound swabs guide antibiotic choice — they cannot diagnose infection on their own. Diagnosis is always based on clinical judgement.

## Management

### Emergency Situations

If necrotising fasciitis or sepsis is suspected, emergency hospital assessment is required immediately.

### Spreading Infection

If there are signs of spreading infection (such as cellulitis), your healthcare provider will manage this as a spreading skin infection.

### Wounds Involving a Prosthesis

If you have an underlying prosthesis (such as a joint replacement) near an infected wound, your healthcare provider will seek advice from the relevant surgical specialty before deciding on management.

### Good Wound Care — The Most Important Step

Most localised wound infections do not require antibiotics in the first instance. Good wound care is the priority:

- **Mark the erythema boundary** — your healthcare provider may draw around the redness so you can monitor whether it is spreading. If antibiotics are prescribed, redness and swelling can take 24–48 hours to stop spreading — report any deterioration (fever, feeling unwell) in that time.
- **Surgical wounds with pus** — if there is a collection of pus under a suture line, removing one or alternate sutures to allow drainage may be recommended.
- **Wound dressings to reduce bacterial load** — products such as silver dressings, povidone-iodine (e.g., Betadine), cadexomer iodine, or PHMB (polyhexamethylene biguanide) dressings may be used depending on the wound type. The choice depends on whether the wound is dry or wet, shallow or deep, and any allergies you have. Changing dressings frequently helps absorb exudate, reduce bacteria, and allow monitoring.

### Antibiotics

Antibiotics are not needed for all wound infections. Your healthcare provider may recommend antibiotic treatment if:

- There are clinical signs of infection that persist despite good wound care
- The wound type has specific indications (e.g., skin grafts, bite wounds)

When antibiotics are used, typical options include:

- **Flucloxacillin** (preferred) — usually 1 g three times daily orally. Your healthcare provider may advise you that taking it with food is acceptable despite older labelling.
- **Cephalexin** — an alternative if you are intolerant of flucloxacillin, need a liquid formulation, or have mild penicillin allergy; typically 1 g three times daily.
- **Co-trimoxazole** — used if you have a high-risk penicillin allergy (e.g., anaphylaxis) or if MRSA is suspected; typically 960 mg twice daily (three times daily if weight >90 kg).
- **Children** — weight-based dosing will be used.

Most wound infections are caused by *Staphylococcus aureus* or streptococci. Important exceptions include bite wounds, wounds from water exposure or heavy contamination, and diabetic foot wounds — discuss these with your healthcare provider.

Duration of antibiotics is guided by clinical response, but 5 days is usually sufficient.

### Monitoring

Your healthcare provider will monitor the wound for:

- Progression of the wound and extension of redness
- Signs of deterioration

Note: complete resolution of redness is not always expected at the end of treatment — it often persists, especially if you have venous insufficiency or lymphoedema.

---

## When to Discuss with Your Doctor

Talk to your healthcare provider if:

- The wound is getting larger, more painful, or more red
- You develop fever, chills, or feel generally unwell
- There is new or increasing pus or discharge
- Redness is spreading beyond the wound area
- The infection is not improving after a few days of treatment
- You have an artificial joint or implant near the wound
