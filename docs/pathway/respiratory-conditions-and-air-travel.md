# Respiratory Conditions and Air Travel

> Auckland Region HealthPathways clinical guidance for GPs. Source: files/393920.htm

---

## Key points

- Air travel is safe for most patients with respiratory conditions — only 1 respiratory-related medical emergency per 250,000 passengers per year.
- Cabin pressure in commercial aircraft simulates altitude below 2,500 m (8,000 ft): oxygen ~15% (vs 20% at sea level), gas volume increases 33%.
- Shorter NZ domestic flights carry less hypoxia risk than international flights.
- Patients must be **clinically stable** before flying.

---

## Timing restrictions by procedure/condition

| Condition | Restriction |
|---|---|
| Spontaneous pneumothorax | No flying until 1 week after confirmed radiological resolution |
| Traumatic pneumothorax | No flying until 2 weeks after confirmed radiological resolution |
| Chronic pneumothorax | Seek non-acute respiratory advice |
| Percutaneous transthoracic biopsy | No flying for 2 weeks |
| Intercostal tube removal | No flying until 1 week after radiological confirmation of no intrapleural gas |
| Lobectomy / pneumonectomy / cardiac surgery | Generally no flying for 6 weeks |
| Chronic intrathoracic airspace | Seek non-acute respiratory advice |

---

## CPAP and obstructive sleep apnoea

- Patients generally **do not need to use their CPAP machine during the flight**, regardless of duration.
- Warn of increased tiredness; advise to avoid sedatives.
- Airline policies on in-flight CPAP use vary — advise patient to contact airline directly.

---

## Risk factors for hypoxia with air travel

Seek non-acute respiratory advice if **any** of these are present in patients not already on home oxygen:

- Hypoxaemic at sea level
- Chronic airflow obstruction with baseline SpO₂ < 95% **and** MMRC dyspnoea score of 3 or 4
- Severe asthma (persistent symptoms / frequent exacerbations despite optimal treatment)
- Interstitial lung disease (e.g. pulmonary fibrosis) with DLCO ≤ 50% predicted or exercise desaturation < 95%
- Severe restrictive lung disease (FVC < 1 litre)
- Cystic fibrosis
- Pre-existing oxygen or ventilator support

Most patients with COPD or interstitial lung disease will **not** need supplemental oxygen, especially for flights < 2 hours.

Patients already on home oxygen: seek non-acute respiratory advice.

### MMRC Dyspnoea Scale (for reference)

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 0 | Breathless only with strenuous exercise |
| 1 | Breathless hurrying on level ground or walking up a slight hill |
| 2 | Walks slower than peers on level ground, or must stop for breath at own pace on level |
| 3 | Stops for breath after ~100 metres or a few minutes on level ground |
| 4 | Too breathless to leave the house, or breathless when dressing |

## Requesting in-flight supplemental oxygen

- Contact the airline directly via their website and complete the Medical Information Form for Air Travel (MEDA) or equivalent.
- Additional cost applies at most airlines.
- In-flight oxygen is only used if recommended by the respiratory physician.

---

## When to refer

Seek **non-acute respiratory advice** for:
- Chronic pneumothorax
- Chronic intrathoracic airspace
- Patients on home oxygen
- Patients with risk factors for hypoxia with air travel (listed above)

---

## Pre-travel checklist

- Patient clinically stable ✓
- Travel insurance arranged ✓
- Adequate medication supply (inhalers etc.) ✓
- Timing restrictions met (post-procedure/surgery) ✓
- CPAP: airline notified if planning to use in-flight ✓
- Oxygen: specialist consulted if risk factors present ✓
