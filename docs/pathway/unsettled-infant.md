# Unsettled Infant

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of unsettled infants aged 12 months and under, including excessive crying, difficulty settling, and feeding or sleeping difficulties.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Take your baby to the emergency department or call an ambulance if your infant has:

- Acute onset of irritability with signs of serious illness (e.g., fever, appears very unwell — possible sepsis)
- Poor weight gain
- Vomiting blood (haematemesis)
- Blood in the stool (rectal bleeding)

---

## Background

- An unsettled baby is a very common concern. Most babies do not have an underlying illness, but both baby and family need a thorough assessment.
- Unsettled behaviour in babies aged 12 months and under can include:
  - Prolonged and inconsolable crying
  - Resistance to soothing
  - Frequent waking during the day and night
  - Difficulty settling to sleep
  - Feeding difficulties
- These patterns are common in families and usually improve over time, though for some families they persist for longer.
- It is important that carers have safe strategies to manage their baby through this difficult period.

### Normal Crying

- Most infants go through a stage of increased crying — some more than others.
- Crying typically increases from about 2 weeks of age, peaks at 2 months, then reduces at around 4–5 months.
- Crying is usually worse in the late afternoon or evening.
- An infant may draw up their legs as if in pain, but there is no evidence that excessive crying is caused by an intestinal problem or wind.
- Excessive crying is sometimes called **colic**, but this label can be unhelpful as it may lead parents to believe something is medically wrong, when usually there is not.

### Spilling (Physiological Reflux)

- Spilling is the effortless regurgitation of stomach contents into the mouth — part of the normal spectrum of physiological reflux.
- Very common in healthy infants. It peaks at 4 months (67% of infants).
- Half of infants under 3 months have at least one episode per day; only 5% have any episodes at 10–12 months.
- Most common after feeds.
- Spilling alone does not indicate gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD).

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. Take a history looking for red flags, and ask about crying patterns, spilling, feeding, sleeping, and growth.
2. Ask about **parental and family factors** that may affect the ability to manage an unsettled baby, including:
   - Exhaustion and sleep deprivation
   - Lack of support from a partner, extended family, or friends
   - Anxiety or lack of confidence in managing a baby
   - Unrealistic expectations of infant behaviour
   - Relationship difficulties, family violence, drug or alcohol use
   - Adversity or poor living conditions
   - Mental or physical illness — the mother will be checked for postnatal depression
   - Difficulties in the parent-infant relationship
3. Consider the **effects on the whole family**:
   - How parents cope when overwhelmed or frustrated by the crying
   - Fathers or partners may also be struggling — depression in fathers during the perinatal period is recognised
4. Perform a physical **examination**, including the abdomen (to exclude conditions such as an incarcerated inguinal hernia), and where possible observe baby feeding and the parent-infant interaction.
5. Update **growth charts**.

### Possible Medical Causes to Consider

In the majority of unsettled babies, no medical problem is found. However, your doctor may consider:

- Inadequate milk intake (e.g., breastfeeding difficulties causing insufficient milk supply)
- Cow's milk protein intolerance — more likely if there is also poor growth, loose stools, or early onset of severe eczema
- GORD (gastro-oesophageal reflux disease)
- Urinary tract infection

Investigations are not usually needed if the history is typical and examination is normal.

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following:

### If Red Flags Are Present

Immediate referral for acute paediatric assessment will be arranged.

### Education

Your doctor will talk through:

- What is normal for crying, sleeping, feeding, and irritability at your baby's age.
- The potential frustration of managing an unsettled baby — including the fact that some babies have been harmed by caregivers in this context.
- Having a plan for when the crying becomes too much, such as:
  - Placing the baby in a safe place and taking a short break until feeling calmer
  - Calling a friend
  - Calling **Plunket Line** (free advice, available 24/7): **0800-933-922**

### Soothing Techniques

Your doctor may suggest:

- Establishing a consistent feeding and settling routine
- Reducing excessive stimulation (e.g., noise, light, handling) — most babies find low-level background noise soothing, such as gentle music or white noise
- Carrying baby in a sling or front pack
- Trying massage, rocking, or gentle patting
- Note: frequent formula changes, weaning from breastmilk, colic mixtures, and gripe water are **not** of proven benefit

### Family Support and Parental Wellbeing

Your doctor may:

- Identify and address underlying family issues (e.g., relationship difficulties, mental health, substance use)
- If the mother is depressed or anxious, discuss self-care, family support, counselling, or medications
- Refer to a Plunket nurse or Well Child provider for ongoing support
- Arrange for the practice nurse to check in periodically on how the family is coping
- Consider referral for assessment if there are concerns about parent-infant attachment or the parent's mental health

### Managing an Identified Medical Cause

- Breastfeeding difficulties: your doctor may refer to lactation support services
- Cow's milk protein allergy: your doctor may follow a specific food hypersensitivity pathway
- GORD: your doctor may manage through the reflux and GORD in children pathway
