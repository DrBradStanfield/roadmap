# Rheumatic Fever and Rheumatic Heart Disease

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of acute rheumatic fever (ARF) and rheumatic heart disease (RHD), including who is at high risk and how ongoing prevention works.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

The following symptoms require urgent assessment, especially in Māori or Pacific peoples:

- Painful or swollen joints
- Limping or unable to weight bear
- New or changed heart murmur
- Choreiform movements (jerky, uncoordinated movements)
- Shortness of breath
- Heart failure

---

## Background

Acute rheumatic fever (ARF) is an immune-mediated inflammatory illness that occurs in susceptible people following an untreated Group A *streptococcus* (GAS) infection. It usually develops 14 to 21 days after infection. Between 0.3% and 3% of untreated or incorrectly treated GAS infections will lead to ARF.

The more times a person has a GAS infection, the more exaggerated the immune response and the higher the risk of developing ARF.

ARF can affect joints, the heart, kidneys, and skin, and can cause neurological symptoms. Heart valve damage caused by ARF is called rheumatic heart disease (RHD). Repeated episodes of ARF can cause progressive heart valve damage.

### Who Is at High Risk?

People at high risk of ARF include those with:

- A personal, family, or household history of rheumatic fever or rheumatic heart disease, **or**
- Two or more of the following:
  - Māori or Pacific peoples
  - Aged 3 to 35 years (especially children and adolescents aged 4 to 19 years)
  - Living in crowded accommodation or in deprivation

Māori and Pacific peoples and people from low socio-economic areas are disproportionately affected, likely due to factors including overcrowding and reduced access to primary healthcare.

---

## Assessment

**Important:** Missing a diagnosis of rheumatic fever is common and can lead to long-term health consequences, including cardiac damage and premature death. Suspect rheumatic fever in any high-risk patient — particularly Māori or Pacific peoples aged 3 to 35 years — who has clinical features, even without a history of sore throat.

Your doctor will take a history asking about:

- Whether you belong to a high-risk group (see above)
- Any previous episodes of sore throat or skin infection
- Whether previous antibiotic courses were completed in full
- Recent use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), which can interfere with diagnosis

### Symptoms Your Doctor Will Ask About

- Fever
- Painful, warm, or swollen joints
- Sore throat
- Rash, skin sores, or subcutaneous nodules
- Breathlessness
- Jerky or uncoordinated movements, or worsening handwriting
- Abdominal pain
- Unexplained fatigue

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will examine for:

- Signs of a throat or skin infection (tonsillitis or impetigo — these may be absent or healing, as ARF typically develops 2–3 weeks after the acute infection)
- **Joint involvement:** Usually asymmetrical and migratory polyarthritis affecting large joints (especially ankles and knees), sometimes more painful than the clinical signs suggest. May present as limping or difficulty walking.
- **Sydenham's chorea:** Jerky, uncoordinated movements affecting the hands, feet, tongue, and face; may present as worsening handwriting at school. Can appear up to three months after GAS infection.
- **Erythema marginatum:** An annular erythematous rash (present in fewer than 2% of cases, but highly specific for ARF)
- **Subcutaneous nodules:** Painless nodules 0.5–2 cm found over joints (present in fewer than 2% of cases, but highly specific)
- **Cardiac features:** Resting tachycardia, new heart murmur, or signs of heart failure
- **Prolonged PR interval on ECG**

### Diagnosis

There is no single diagnostic laboratory test for ARF. Diagnosis is a clinical decision made by your doctor, using the New Zealand diagnostic criteria.

## Management

### Acute Management

If ARF or a relapse of rheumatic fever is suspected, your doctor will arrange urgent hospital assessment. NSAIDs should not be started before assessment, as they can interfere with diagnosis.

Hospital specialists will arrange notification and household contact tracing.

### Long-Term Secondary Prevention

The cornerstone of ongoing management is **secondary prophylaxis** — regular intramuscular benzathine penicillin injections to prevent further episodes of ARF.

Your doctor will discuss:

- **Benzathine penicillin injections every 21 or 28 days** to protect against recurrent GAS infection. A 21-day schedule is recommended if you have had a recurrence while on prophylaxis.
- If you are unable to tolerate penicillin injections, your specialist team will consider other options.
- The total duration of prophylaxis will be determined by your specialist team, based on your history and whether you have RHD.

### Follow-Up

Your doctor will arrange ongoing primary care review, typically every 3 to 6 months depending on disease severity:

- At every visit, your doctor will actively look for and manage any new GAS sore throats in you, your immediate family, and household members — early treatment is critical to prevent further ARF and worsening of existing RHD.
- If any household member develops a sore throat, all household members may be swabbed for GAS and treated if positive.
- If you have moderate or severe RHD, you will likely also have regular reviews with a cardiologist or specialist.
- Your doctor will arrange annual influenza vaccination and ensure routine vaccinations are up to date.

### Dental Care

Good dental hygiene is especially important when on secondary prophylaxis. Your doctor may discuss:

- The possible need for endocarditis prophylaxis before certain dental procedures
- Access to dental services (children under 18 have access to free dental care from registered providers; adults with rheumatic fever may also have access — your specialist team can advise)

### Other Considerations

- If you are planning a pregnancy and have a history of ARF or RHD, your doctor will seek specialist advice to assess risks and optimise your treatment before you become pregnant.
- Anticoagulant therapy (e.g., warfarin) may be needed for some patients with RHD — your doctor will monitor this if relevant.
