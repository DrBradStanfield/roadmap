# Cystic Fibrosis (CF)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the identification, assessment, and management of cystic fibrosis (CF) in primary care, including screening, known CF follow-up, and when specialist referral is needed.

---

## Background

CF is a genetic condition that mostly affects the lungs, pancreas, and sweat glands. A build-up of thick, sticky mucus leads to respiratory problems, incomplete digestion, and increased salt loss from the sweat glands.

- It is the most common life-threatening genetic condition, affecting about 1 in 2,500 babies.
- It follows an **autosomal recessive** pattern of inheritance: individuals with CF have two faulty copies of the CFTR gene.
- About 1 in 25 New Zealanders are carriers — asymptomatic with one faulty copy.

**Common clinical symptoms:**
- Meconium ileus (in newborns)
- Malabsorption: loose stools, failure to gain weight
- Frequent respiratory tract infections progressing to chronic sino-pulmonary disease
- Azoospermia in males (affects 97–98% of men with CF; may be the only symptom)

CF is often associated with nasal polyps, rectal prolapse, and liver disease.

---

## Screening

### Newborn screening

All newborn babies in New Zealand are screened for CF within 48 hours of birth via a two-tier test:
1. Immunoreactive trypsinogen (IRT) level (heel-prick test)
2. Genetic testing if IRT is elevated

The screen picks up approximately 95% of babies with CF. Around 6–16 newborn babies are diagnosed annually. One baby with CF may be missed every two years due to low IRT, no CFTR mutations detected, or birth outside New Zealand.

This means some children may require investigations for CF at a later age despite a screen-negative result.

### Pre-pregnancy screening

Consider pre-pregnancy carrier screening if a couple planning a family:
- Is concerned they may have a high chance of being CF carriers, or
- Has a first- or second-degree relative with CF.

## Assessment

### Who to assess

Ask about CF symptoms in any patient presenting with:

**Classic symptoms:**
- Chronic purulent sino-bronchitis
- Features of pancreatic insufficiency: weight loss, steatorrhoea, dyspepsia, abdominal bloating (may be absent in adult-onset CF or mild disease)
- Failure to thrive in children (rare in screen-negative children)
- Male infertility

**Less severe presentations** (a small number present late):
- Lung disease without pancreatic insufficiency
- Male infertility due to azoospermia as the sole symptom

### At-risk groups who may have been missed by screening

- Relatives of newly identified CF cases (potential carriers)
- Adults and children with classic or less severe CF symptoms
- Immigrant populations:
  - Not all countries screen for CF
  - CF most commonly affects people of Northern European or UK descent
  - Less common in Southern European and Middle Eastern descent
  - Rare or absent in people of Asian descent

### Pre-pregnancy genetic counselling

Consider referral for genetic counselling if a couple planning a family:
- Has a high chance of being CF carriers, or
- Has a first- or second-degree relative with CF.

## Management

### New diagnosis / positive newborn screen

If a newborn has a positive screen, the National Testing Centre contacts the lead maternity carer (LMC) and instructs them on appropriate referrals.

If there are clinical symptoms suggestive of CF in a patient not yet diagnosed:
- **Adults:** your doctor will request a non-acute respiratory assessment
- **Children:** your doctor will request a non-acute paediatric assessment

### Known CF — not engaged with specialist services

If a patient with known CF is not currently under specialist care, your doctor will refer them back to specialist services. This is important because CF requires ongoing specialist monitoring.

### Managing exacerbations

CF exacerbations should be managed in collaboration with specialist services. If you have CF, you will typically have a designated clinical nurse specialist or respiratory specialist you can contact directly.

**If acutely unwell and unable to reach your specialist team**, your doctor can arrange urgent respiratory advice or emergency department referral if needed.

### Trikafta (elexacaftor + tezacaftor + ivacaftor)

As of 1 April 2023, Trikafta is funded in New Zealand for eligible patients with CF. Key facts:

- A combination of three oral medicines that correct the underlying CFTR protein dysfunction
- Significantly improves life expectancy
- Can cause **hepatic toxicity** — liver monitoring is required
- May cause weight gain and associated hyperglycaemia in some patients
- Can improve CF-related diabetes in some patients
- Has **several drug interactions** — requires dose adjustment with CYP3A inhibitors

**Important:** If you are on Trikafta and your doctor wants to start a new medicine (short- or long-term), they need to seek specialist respiratory advice first due to drug interaction risks. Do not start new medications without discussing with your CF team.

### Vaccinations

People with CF should be up to date with all routine vaccinations. Additional vaccines are recommended and funded for people with CF — your doctor can advise on which ones apply to you.

### Pre-pregnancy planning

Pre-pregnancy genetic counselling is recommended at Genetic Health Services NZ if:
- One partner has known CF
- A couple is concerned they may be CF carriers
- A couple has a first- or second-degree relative with CF

## When to See Your Doctor Urgently

Seek medical attention promptly if you have known CF and experience:
- Worsening breathlessness, increased sputum, or fever (signs of an exacerbation)
- Jaundice or significant abdominal pain (possible liver complications)
- Haemoptysis (coughing up blood)

Your CF specialist team should be your first point of contact for exacerbations — your doctor can assist if you cannot reach them.
