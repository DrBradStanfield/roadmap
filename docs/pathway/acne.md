# Acne

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and treatment of acne across all severity levels, from mild comedonal acne to severe nodular acne requiring isotretinoin.

---

## About Acne

Acne is a common chronic skin condition caused by a combination of factors:

- Familial tendency (genetics)
- Androgenic hormones (both naturally produced and from external sources)
- Acne-causing bacteria (*Cutibacterium acnes*)
- Immune system inflammation
- Blockage of the hair follicle and oil gland (pilosebaceous unit)

Acne is most common in adolescence and young adulthood but can affect people of all ages:

- It is increasingly seen in children under 10 years, partly because children are maturing earlier.
- Adult females may develop chronic, low-grade acne that persists for many years. The cause is not fully understood.

Acne can have a significant effect on a person's emotional and social life, often regardless of how severe it looks clinically.

---

## What Your Doctor Will Assess

Your doctor will take a history asking about:

- Age of onset and duration of acne
- For females: relationship to menstrual cycle, contraceptive history, pregnancy plans, and signs of polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) such as irregular periods or excess hair growth
- Skin sensitivity and dryness, especially if you have eczema
- Use of topical products that may irritate or block pores (cosmetics, cleansers, sunscreens, hair products, moisturisers)
- Use of steroids (topical or systemic steroids can cause or worsen acne; anabolic steroids used for bodybuilding are also a consideration)
- Previous acne treatments tried and how well they worked
- Presence of psychological distress, depression, or low self-esteem related to acne

Your doctor will also examine all affected areas including the face, back, and chest, looking for:

- How oily the skin is
- Non-inflammatory lesions: closed and open comedones (blackheads and whiteheads), pseudocysts
- Inflammatory lesions: papules and pustules
- Nodules and cysts
- Secondary lesions: scratch marks, red or pigmented marks
- Scarring (hypertrophic or atrophic)
- Signs of picking or scratching (acne excoriée)

### Alternative Diagnoses Your Doctor May Consider

- **Rosacea** — usually in middle-aged or older adults; no comedones but pustules may be present
- **Drug-induced acneiform eruption** — from anabolic steroids, oral corticosteroids, progestogen-only contraceptives, or lithium
- **Folliculitis** — including pityrosporum folliculitis

## Acne Severity

Your doctor will grade your acne to guide treatment:

**Mild acne:**
- Comedones (non-inflammatory) predominate
- A few papules or pustules may be present
- Trunk and chest usually not affected
- Minimal prior treatments

**Moderate acne:**
- Greater numbers of papules, pustules, and comedones
- Trunk and chest may be affected
- Not responding to topical treatments
- Early scarring may be evident

**Severe acne:**
- Widespread papules, pustules, and nodules
- Scarring is usually present
- Usually involves face, chest, and back
- Includes acne of any severity causing significant psychological distress
- Or moderate acne that has not responded to 3 months of appropriate treatment

---

## General Skin Care Advice

Your doctor may advise:

- Avoid washing excessively — this can cause dryness and dermatitis
- Consider over-the-counter face washes containing salicylic acid
- Use water-based, oil-free, non-comedogenic facial products
- Remove all make-up before sleeping
- Avoid picking or squeezing pimples

---

## Treatment: Mild Acne (Topical)

Topical treatments are the first-line approach for mild acne. Your doctor will advise trialling them for 2–3 months before assessing improvement. Different topical treatments can be combined, and they can be applied to all affected areas of skin (not just individual spots). They can be continued long term.

### Benzoyl Peroxide

Available as a cream or gel in 2.5%, 5%, and 10% strengths. Your doctor may note:

- No additional clinical benefit above 5% strength, but more dryness and redness at higher doses
- Most common side-effect is skin irritation — switching to a lower strength helps
- Bleaches clothing, towels, and bedding
- Useful for papulo-pustular acne but not predominantly comedonal acne
- Consider using in the morning and a retinoid at night
- Also available combined with clindamycin (Duac gel) — your doctor may limit this to less than 3 months due to antibiotic resistance risk

### Topical Retinoids

Topical retinoids (adapalene, tretinoin) are particularly effective for comedonal acne and can also treat papulo-pustular acne. Your doctor may advise:

- They are **contraindicated in pregnancy**
- Start slowly and use small amounts — skin irritation is common initially; apply a non-greasy moisturiser if needed
- Apply tretinoin at night as UV light degrades retinoids
- Use sun protection
- They can be used long term

Common preparations include adapalene (Differin) and tretinoin (ReTrieve).

### Azelaic Acid

Available as a 20% cream or lotion. May cause local irritation, skin discolouration, and rarely photosensitivity.

## Treatment: Moderate Acne (Topical + Systemic)

If topical treatment alone is inadequate, your doctor may add a systemic (oral) treatment while continuing topical therapy.

### Combined Oral Contraceptive Pill (for women)

All combined oral contraceptive pills (COCs) increase sex hormone-binding globulin, which reduces free androgens and helps improve acne. Your doctor may consider this option if you also need contraception or if acne flares before your period (especially on the lower face and neck). It usually takes up to 6 menstrual cycles to see an effect.

Note: Progesterone-only contraceptives may worsen acne.

For women with possible PCOS, your doctor may consider an anti-androgenic COC containing cyproterone or drospirenone (e.g., Ginet 84, Yasmin, Yaz) — though these carry an increased risk of venous thromboembolism.

### Oral Antibiotics

Your doctor may prescribe oral antibiotics for a minimum of 3 months, reviewed at that point. Antibiotics are not prescribed for longer than 6 months due to the risk of antibiotic resistance. They are always combined with a topical treatment (retinoid, benzoyl peroxide, or azelaic acid).

**First-line — doxycycline:**
- 100 mg once daily
- If not tolerated, a 50 mg formulation twice daily may be used
- Do not halve, crush, or chew tablets (risk of oesophagitis)
- Warn about photosensitivity and oesophagitis
- Not for use in pregnancy, breastfeeding, or children aged 11 and under (risk of tooth staining)
- Not to be used with isotretinoin (risk of raised intracranial pressure)

**Second-line — minocycline:**
- 50 mg twice daily or 100 mg once daily
- May be associated with pigmentation, liver changes, or lupus-like reactions

**For children aged 11 and under, pregnant women, or those unable to take tetracyclines — erythromycin:**
- 400 mg twice daily with food
- Note: erythromycin-resistant acne bacteria are common

### Spironolactone (selected females)

Your doctor may consider spironolactone for females with PCOS or certain other hormone-related conditions. It has anti-androgen and anti-inflammatory effects and can be combined with other treatments.

- Contraindicated in pregnancy — reliable contraception is required
- Dose range is typically 25–200 mg daily (commonly 100 mg daily)
- Blood pressure and kidney function should be checked before starting
- Potassium level should be rechecked 1 month after starting

## Treatment: Severe Acne (Isotretinoin)

> **Important safety note:** Following a death of a young man from suicide while on isotretinoin, New Zealand's Medsafe has issued guidance on safe prescribing, pre-treatment screening, and monitoring for serious risks — including mental health assessment before and during treatment.

Your doctor may consider oral isotretinoin if you have:

- Severe acne
- Early scarring
- Significant psychological distress from acne
- Failed adequate trials of other treatments

### How Isotretinoin Is Prescribed

Isotretinoin is a potent medication with important safety considerations. Your doctor will:

1. **Check for contraindications**, which include:
   - Pregnancy or risk of pregnancy (isotretinoin is highly teratogenic)
   - Current use of tetracycline antibiotics (risk of raised intracranial pressure)
   - Severe liver disease or very high vitamin A levels
   - Being a commercial pilot (can impair night vision)
   - Having had laser eye surgery within the previous 6 months
   - Significant high triglycerides (relative caution — can still be used with close monitoring)

2. **Discuss serious and common side-effects:**

   - **Pregnancy risk** — Isotretinoin causes severe birth defects and spontaneous miscarriage in approximately half of pregnancies. For all women of childbearing potential, your doctor will:
     - Discuss and arrange effective contraception starting 1 month before treatment, throughout, and 1 month after
     - Ideally use 2 forms of contraception
     - Check a pregnancy test (beta-hCG) before starting and at 1 month
     - Advise seeking urgent medical attention if pregnancy occurs or unprotected sex happens during treatment

   - **Mood changes** — Your doctor will discuss this openly. People with acne are already at higher risk of depression and anxiety. Research in large populations has not shown an increase in depression or suicide at a population level with isotretinoin — and some studies show improvement in mood as skin clears. However, worsening mood, anxiety, depression, and rarely suicidal thoughts have been reported in individual patients. The relationship is not fully understood. Your doctor will monitor your mood regularly during treatment. Seek urgent care if you experience serious depression or suicidal thoughts.

   - **Common dose-dependent side-effects:**
     - Acne flare at the start (especially initially) — mild flares require no action; moderate flares may require dose reduction
     - Dry lips, skin, and nostrils — use emollients and white soft paraffin frequently
     - Sun sensitivity — use sun protection and avoid sunbeds
     - Skin sensitivity — avoid waxing or harsh skin treatments during and for 3 months after treatment
     - Dry eyes — use artificial tears or lubricating gels; contact lens use may not be tolerated
     - Dermatitis on hands and forearms
     - Tiredness, muscle or joint aches, headache — paracetamol or dose reduction may help
     - Impaired night vision — can occur suddenly; pilots are not permitted to use isotretinoin

3. **Obtain informed consent.** Your doctor will document that you understand the risks, including:
   - Not becoming pregnant during treatment
   - Not taking tetracycline antibiotics at the same time
   - Not donating blood during treatment or for 1 month after
   - Stopping oral tetracyclines and topical acne treatments a few days before starting isotretinoin

4. **Arrange blood tests before starting:**
   - Full blood count (FBC)
   - Liver function tests (particularly ALT)
   - Non-fasting lipids (particularly triglycerides)
   - Pregnancy test (beta-hCG) for all women of childbearing potential

5. **Prescribe at an appropriate dose.** The usual starting dose is 10–20 mg once daily with food (ideally with the biggest meal). Low doses minimise adverse reactions. Your doctor will adjust the dose based on your tolerance — many people do not tolerate doses above 40 mg daily.

6. **Monitor with blood tests** at 1 month (FBC, ALT, triglycerides, and pregnancy test if applicable). Further monitoring depends on your results, length of course, and risk factors.

   - About 45% of people experience a rise in triglycerides — this usually resolves when treatment stops
   - About 15% experience a mild transient rise in liver enzyme (ALT) — alcohol intake should be minimised
   - Rarely, a drop in white blood cells (neutropenia) can occur

7. **Review clinically** at 4–12 weeks, then every 3–6 months:
   - Improvement may not be visible until after 4 months
   - Your doctor will assess side-effects and check your mood
   - Dose may be adjusted based on response and tolerability

8. **Duration of treatment.** A typical course is 9–12 months at 20–40 mg daily. Macrocomedonal acne may take 12–18 months on low-dose therapy. Treatment continues for 2–4 months after acne has fully cleared, to avoid relapse.

### Equity Note

Māori and Pacific people are prescribed isotretinoin at approximately one third the rate of other populations, despite no evidence they have lower rates of acne. Your doctor should prescribe isotretinoin when it is clinically indicated, regardless of ethnicity.

---

## When to Seek Specialist Input

Your doctor may refer you to a dermatologist if:

- You are aged 11 or under with severe, scarring acne
- Isotretinoin has failed despite an adequate course in an adherent patient
- There are complications during isotretinoin treatment (severe acne flare, triglycerides above 9 mmol/L, pancreatitis, or other serious reactions)
- You have symptoms of serious depression or suicidal thoughts while on isotretinoin — seek urgent mental health assessment
