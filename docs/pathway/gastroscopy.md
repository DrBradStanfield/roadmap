# Gastroscopy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

A gastroscopy (also called an upper endoscopy) is a procedure where a thin flexible camera is passed through the mouth to examine the oesophagus, stomach, and upper small bowel. Your doctor will assess whether you need one and how urgently, based on your symptoms.

---

## Red Flags — Urgent Gastroscopy Needed

Your doctor will arrange urgent assessment if you have:

- Vomiting blood (haematemesis) or passing black tarry stools (melaena)
- Severe iron deficiency anaemia with symptoms
- Complete difficulty swallowing — unable to swallow solids, or only able to swallow liquids
- Persistent vomiting or regurgitation of food, or significant aspiration at night
- Radiological evidence of gastric (stomach) cancer
- Indigestion (dyspepsia) combined with an abdominal mass found on examination

---

## Alarm Features — Earlier Investigation Likely Needed

Your doctor will consider earlier gastroscopy if you have indigestion or upper GI symptoms plus any of the following:

- Family history of stomach cancer in a relative diagnosed before age 50
- Use of NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen, naproxen) in a high-risk group
- Use of blood thinners (anticoagulants)
- Significant unexplained weight loss
- Difficulty swallowing (even if partial or comes and goes)
- Iron deficiency anaemia
- Previous complicated peptic ulcer disease

---

## Age and Ethnicity Thresholds

Age at which gastroscopy is considered for new or persistent upper GI symptoms:

- **Māori, Pacific, or Asian ethnicity:** from age 45 (or 40 if additional risk factors present)
- **European ethnicity:** from age 50

Risk factors that lower these thresholds include:
- First presentation of unexplained, persistent symptoms
- Family history of gastric cancer with onset before age 50
- Severe or persistent dyspepsia despite adequate treatment
- Previous peptic ulcer disease, particularly if complicated
- Coughing spells or significant nocturnal aspiration

---

## When a Trial of Treatment May Come First

For younger patients without alarm features, your doctor may suggest trying treatment before arranging a gastroscopy:

- **_Helicobacter pylori_ (H. pylori) test and treat** — H. pylori is a stomach bacteria present in around 50% of the global population. Most infections are asymptomatic, but it can cause indigestion and peptic ulcers. Your doctor may test for it and prescribe eradication treatment. Gastroscopy is typically considered after two failed treatment attempts.
- **Acid suppression therapy** — a trial of proton pump inhibitors (PPIs, e.g., omeprazole, pantoprazole) or pro-kinetic medicines for functional dyspepsia
- **Double-dose PPI for reflux** — if reflux symptoms do not improve after 3 months on double-dose PPI, your doctor may consider gastroscopy
- **Iron deficiency without upper GI symptoms** — your doctor will follow an iron deficiency investigation pathway rather than going straight to gastroscopy

Your doctor will review your response to treatment and decide whether further investigation is needed.

---

## What Gastroscopy Can Find

Your doctor may consider gastroscopy to look for:

- Peptic ulcer disease (stomach or duodenal ulcers)
- Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) — acid reflux causing inflammation
- _Helicobacter pylori_ infection (biopsies can be taken)
- Gastric (stomach) cancer or oesophageal cancer
- Oesophagitis or Barrett's oesophagus
- Causes of iron deficiency or unexplained anaemia
- Causes of dysphagia (difficulty swallowing)
