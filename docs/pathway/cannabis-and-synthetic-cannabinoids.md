# Cannabis and Synthetic Cannabinoids

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers recreational or illicit use of cannabis or synthetic cannabinoids — assessment, harm reduction, withdrawal support, and when to seek further help.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Contact emergency services immediately if:

- Aggression
- Psychosis

---

## Background

### Cannabis

Cannabis is derived from the *Cannabis sativa* plant. The main psychoactive ingredient is delta-9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC). Cannabidiol (CBD) is another active ingredient but is less psychoactive.

It comes in three main forms:
- **Marijuana** — dried flower buds, smoked as joints or vaped
- **Hashish** — plant resin, pressed into blocks, smoked via bong or "hotknifing" (spotting), or added to food
- **Hash oil** — the most potent form, a thick oil obtained from hashish

Key facts:
- One cannabis joint contains around 3 times as much tar as a cigarette, and as many carcinogens
- Cannabis is the most commonly used illicit drug in New Zealand
- Approximately 1 in 10 users will develop dependence
- 50–75% of regular heavy users will experience withdrawal symptoms
- Infrequent use is less likely to be associated with serious harm

### Synthetic Cannabinoids ("Synnies")

Synthetic cannabinoids are manufactured chemicals sprayed onto plant matter. They have no direct relationship to natural cannabis but bind to the same receptors in the body.

- Most synthetic cannabinoids are many times more potent than natural cannabis
- "Hot spots" of concentrated chemical on the plant matter have been linked to acute toxicity, seizures, and cardiac arrest
- Over 200 different synthetic cannabinoids exist — users rarely know which type or concentration they are using
- Dependence can lead to severe and unpleasant withdrawal symptoms
- Some (such as MDMB-4en-PINACA and AMB-FUBINACA) carry a particularly high risk of serious harm or death

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess:

1. **Screening context** — Most users do not present for cannabis-related problems. They may present with anxiety, anger, aggression, or paranoia. Screening is especially relevant for:
   - Patients aged under 40
   - Male patients
   - Tobacco smokers
   - Patients with mental health conditions
   - Patients with chronic respiratory symptoms

2. **Severity of use**, including:
   - Age of onset (earlier onset increases dependence risk)
   - Method of use (joints, bongs, spotting, eating, vaping) — spotting carries the highest health risk
   - Frequency of use and money spent per week
   - Time since last dose
   - Risky behaviours such as driving under the influence or operating heavy machinery

3. **Dependence** — assessed if 3 or more of the following apply in the past year:
   - Needing more to get the same effect (tolerance)
   - Withdrawal symptoms (irritability, poor sleep, low mood)
   - Using more than intended
   - Persistent desire to stop but being unsuccessful
   - Spending a lot of time obtaining, using, or recovering
   - Giving up important activities to use
   - Continuing to use despite known harms

4. **Withdrawal symptoms** (if the patient has tried to stop):

   *Cannabis withdrawal:*
   - Symptoms typically appear within 4 hours of last use, peak at day 3–5, and resolve within 2–3 weeks
   - Consider withdrawal if 3 or more of: anxiety or restlessness, depression or irritability, decreased appetite, sleep disturbance (insomnia, odd dreams), physical symptoms (tremors, nausea, chills, stomach pain, vomiting, sweating, headache)
   - Cannabis withdrawal is not physically dangerous; most people manage with mild symptoms only

   *Synthetic cannabinoid withdrawal:*
   - Abrupt withdrawal can be physically dangerous, especially in those using over 5 g per day
   - Symptoms include headache, cravings, vomiting, constipation, anxiety, insomnia, irritability, sweating, and confusion
   - Onset within 1–6 hours; most symptoms peak between 2–6 days after stopping

5. **Other substance use** — including tobacco, alcohol, and other drugs

6. **Mental health** — coexisting psychiatric conditions and current medications (e.g., antipsychotics)

7. **Cannabinoid hyperemesis syndrome** — in chronic cannabis users presenting with recurrent nausea and vomiting. A diagnostically useful clue is temporary symptom relief from hot showers or baths.

## Management

### If Aggressive or Showing Signs of Psychosis

Your doctor will contact emergency services or request urgent mental health assessment.

---

### Cannabinoid Hyperemesis Syndrome

Your doctor may consider:
- Assessing whether oral fluid intake is adequate; intravenous rehydration may be needed
- Advising that a hot bath or shower may provide temporary relief (with caution on water temperature)
- Standard antiemetics are generally ineffective
- Symptoms tend to remit when cannabis is stopped
- Referral to alcohol and drug support services for long-term abstinence support

---

### Driving and Drug Driving Law

Your doctor will advise that:
- Cannabis impairs driving — public perception that it does not is incorrect
- It is illegal to drive while impaired or with a qualifying drug in the bloodstream
- NZ Police now carry out random roadside saliva drug testing, primarily for THC, cocaine, methamphetamine, and MDMA
- A positive roadside test leads to laboratory confirmation and potentially a 12-hour driving ban
- Exceeding blood concentration limits under the Drug Driving Legislation (in effect since 11 March 2023) can result in fines, licence demerit points, or a criminal charge

---

### Patients Under 18 Years

Young people using cannabis are at greater risk of:
- Developing mental health problems
- Becoming dependent on cannabis
- Relationship and home conflicts
- Difficulty with memory, learning, and IQ attainment
- Dropping out of study or work
- Legal and financial difficulties
- Using other addictive substances

Synthetic cannabinoids can have long-term impacts on brain development including memory, emotional regulation, and increased likelihood of developing schizophrenia and depression.

Your doctor may:
- Advise about these specific risks and encourage quitting
- Warn against mixing cannabis and alcohol, which potentiates effects (particularly for driving)
- Discuss access to free drug-checking services (see Patient Resources)
- Arrange follow-up in one month

---

### Not Interested in Reducing Cannabis Use

Your doctor may:
- Raise concerns without judgment and educate on risks
- Explain that cannabis use is associated with:
  - Impaired driving (illegal)
  - Chronic bronchitis and increased risk of respiratory infections
  - Increased risk of acute psychosis and probable increased risk of psychotic disorder (especially with early onset, heavy use, or family history)
  - Mental state destabilisation (e.g., mania in bipolar disorder, psychosis in schizophrenia)
  - Probable increased risk of cardiovascular and cerebrovascular events
  - Growth retardation in pregnancy and subsequent behavioural problems in children
  - Possible increased risk of oropharyngeal and lung cancer
  - Legal consequences
- Remain open to discussion and monitor at subsequent appointments

---

### Agrees to Reduce Cannabis Use

Your doctor may consider:
- Supporting the patient's capacity to change in a non-judgemental way
- Advising to avoid coffee, as it can worsen withdrawal symptoms
- Discussing options:
  - **Immediate cessation**, with or without symptom-relieving medications
  - **Gradual dose reduction** (e.g., fewer joints or bongs per day, delaying first use, smaller amounts, agreed reduction schedule over 1–4 weeks, family involvement if requested)

**Symptom-relieving medications your doctor may consider** (off-label, after careful assessment):

| Symptom | Medication your doctor may consider |
|---|---|
| Agitation or irritability | Quetiapine 25–50 mg (max 200 mg/24 hours) |
| Gastrointestinal cramps or spasms | Hyoscine butylbromide (Buscopan) 10–20 mg every 4–6 hours |
| Headache or muscle cramps | Paracetamol 500 mg–1 g every 4 hours as needed (max 4 g/24 hours) |
| Sleep disturbance | Promethazine 25–50 mg at night, or Quetiapine 50 mg at night |

Note: There is no proven pharmacotherapy to directly treat cannabis withdrawal or support long-term abstinence. Benzodiazepines are not a treatment of choice for cannabis withdrawal.

Your doctor will arrange follow-up over at least 4 weeks and may refer to specialist alcohol and drug support services.

---

### Synthetic Cannabinoid Users

Your doctor may:
- Provide education about the higher risks of synthetic cannabinoids compared to natural cannabis
- Advise on safer use:
  - Avoid mixing with alcohol or other drugs
  - Use a smaller amount
  - Avoid using alone — have a sober person present
  - Stay up to date with alerts about dangerous synthetic cannabinoids (see High Alert, below)
- Discuss withdrawal risks and advise against abrupt cessation without medical support
- Refer to specialist alcohol and drug support services
