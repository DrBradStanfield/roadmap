# Bronchiolitis in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of bronchiolitis in infants and young children, typically aged under 12 months.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance immediately if:

- Oxygen saturation consistently below 92% on room air
- Apnoea (the infant stops breathing, even briefly)
- Marked chest wall retractions or tracheal tug
- Markedly increased or decreased respiratory rate
- Cyanosis (blue lips or fingertips)
- Infant is reluctant or unable to feed
- Increasing irritability or lethargy/fatigue

---

## Background

Bronchiolitis is:

- A clinical diagnosis based on typical history and examination findings
- Most common in children aged under 12 months, but can occur up to 18 months
- Caused by viral infections and can occur at any time of year

The illness is self-limiting. Severity usually peaks around the second or third day, with the acute illness resolving over 7 to 10 days. A residual cough may persist for weeks.

Most children recover fully, but there is a small risk of long-term complications such as bronchiectasis, particularly after severe disease or adenovirus infection.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider bronchiolitis in infants under 12 months who present with:

- An initial upper respiratory tract infection (runny nose, sneezing)
- Followed by respiratory distress and low-grade fever
- One or more of: cough, fast breathing (tachypnoea), chest wall retractions, widespread crackles, or wheeze

### History your doctor will take

- Length and type of symptoms, including cough or wheeze
- Feeding, number of wet nappies, and alertness
- Personal risk factors (see below)
- Environmental risk factors (see below)

**Personal risk factors for more serious illness:**

- Premature birth (gestational age under 37 weeks)
- Aged under 10 weeks at presentation
- Breastfed for less than 2 months
- Failure to thrive or poor nutrition
- Underlying chronic lung disease (e.g., bronchiectasis)
- Congenital heart disease
- Chronic neurological condition
- Primary immunodeficiency or chronic aspiration

**Environmental risk factors:**

- Tobacco smoke exposure (postnatal)
- Living in cold, damp, or overcrowded housing
- Not immunised

### Physical examination

Your doctor will check:

- General appearance, alertness, and hydration
- Vital signs: heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation (concern if below 92% on room air), temperature
- Signs of increased respiratory effort: chest wall retractions, tracheal tug, nasal flaring, grunting
- Breath sounds: reduced air entry, crackles, wheeze

**Acceptable respiratory rates by age (breaths per minute):**

| Age | Normal range |
|-----|-------------|
| Term newborn | 25–60 |
| 3 months | 25–60 |
| 6 months | 20–55 |
| 1 year | 20–45 |
| 2 years | 20–40 |

### Severity grading

| Feature | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---------|------|----------|--------|
| Behaviour | Normal | Some irritability | Increasing irritability and/or lethargy |
| Respiratory rate | Normal or mildly fast | Increased | Markedly increased or decreased |
| Accessory muscles | Nil to mild retractions | Moderate retractions, nasal flaring | Marked retractions, tracheal tug |
| Oxygen saturation | >92% (room air) | 90–92% (room air) | <90% (room air) |
| Apnoea | None | May have brief apnoea | Frequent or prolonged apnoea |
| Feeding | Normal | Reduced or difficulty feeding | Reluctant or unable to feed |

### Differential diagnoses your doctor will consider

- If high fever (above 39°C) with focal crackles: pneumonia
- If high fever (above 39°C): sepsis
- Urinary tract infection (if infant under 2 months with minor respiratory symptoms and temperature above 38°C)

No investigations are routinely required for suspected bronchiolitis.

---

## Management

> **Important:** Salbutamol, corticosteroids, antivirals, and antibiotics have no role in the management of bronchiolitis at any age. Your doctor will not prescribe these.

### Severe bronchiolitis

Your doctor will:

1. Manage airway, breathing, and circulation
2. Give supplemental oxygen if oxygen saturation is consistently below 92%
3. Arrange hospital assessment — this typically requires an ambulance

### Mild to moderate bronchiolitis

Mild to moderate bronchiolitis can usually be managed at home.

Your doctor may:

- Use saline nasal drops to clear the infant's nose and observe a feed before deciding on management
- Arrange review the following day, especially if there is uncertainty about the diagnosis, or if there is limited social support
- Consider hospital assessment if the infant has risk factors for more serious illness (listed above)
- Seek paediatric advice if the moderate case is not improving, or if the family has limited ability to access care should the child deteriorate

### Follow-up

Your doctor will:

- Ask you to return for review at 6 weeks to check for any unresolved symptoms (e.g., chronic moist cough)
- Set a recall for annual influenza vaccination if your child is under 5 years

**Advice for caregivers:**

- Frequent handwashing by all family members during respiratory viral season reduces spread
- Know the warning signs of deterioration and when to seek urgent care (see Red Flags above)
- If any household members smoke, ask your doctor about smoking cessation support
- For very young infants with severe or recurrent presentations, consider alternatives to group childcare
