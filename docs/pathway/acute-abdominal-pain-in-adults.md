# Acute Abdominal Pain in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to acute abdominal pain in adults aged 15 years and over — assessing the cause and determining urgency.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Call an ambulance if any of the following are present or suspected:

- Signs of shock, sepsis, peritonitis, or bowel obstruction
- Suspected abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)
- Ectopic pregnancy
- Ischaemic bowel (pain out of proportion to clinical findings)
- Acute coronary syndrome
- Diabetic ketoacidosis

**Also seek emergency assessment for:**
- Acute scrotal or groin pain, or inability to pass urine
- Recent significant trauma (e.g., suspected ruptured liver or spleen after a motor vehicle accident)
- Recent abdominal surgery with new pain
- Suspected perforated viscus
- Suspected severe pancreatitis or cholangitis

---

## Background

It is important to distinguish between abdominal pain that might require urgent surgery (an "acute abdomen") and a flare-up of a known chronic condition. Patients with chronic pain can also develop a new acute problem.

Chronic pain flare-ups are an expected part of the natural history of chronic pain syndromes. For acute pain, the priority is identifying conditions requiring urgent surgical intervention.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will carry out a focused assessment to rule out red flags and acute surgical causes before considering other diagnoses.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- **Location:** abdominal, pelvic, flank/back (retroperitoneal)
- **Nature of pain:** severity (mild/moderate/severe); dull and ill-defined (visceral); sharp and localised (somatic); constant with patient lying still (peritoneal); intermittent with patient writhing (colicky)
- **Radiation:** to the back (retroperitoneal), to the groin and thighs (genitourinary or vascular), to the shoulder (diaphragmatic irritation)
- **Aggravating and relieving factors**
- **Associated symptoms:** nausea, vomiting, appetite or weight changes, pallor, jaundice, fever, heartburn, swallowing difficulty, bloating, rectal bleeding, altered bowel habit
- **Gynaecological history** (if applicable): last menstrual period, possibility of pregnancy, vaginal discharge or bleeding, sexual history, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) risk factors
- **Urinary symptoms** or scrotal pain
- **Past surgical history** (adhesions, previous pancreatitis)
- **Medical history:** heart disease, atrial fibrillation, vascular problems
- **Medications:** anticoagulants, NSAIDs, steroids, antibiotics, immunosuppressants, chemotherapy

Some patients are at higher risk of serious underlying disease and may present atypically:
- Elderly (high likelihood of surgical disease)
- Immunocompromised
- Pregnant
- Post-operative
- Psychiatric patients
- Patients on renal dialysis
- Patients with cognitive disability affecting communication

### Examination

Your doctor will typically:

- Check vital signs and general appearance (jaundice, pallor, cachexia)
- Perform a detailed abdominal examination, looking for:
  - Distension
  - Bowel sounds (absent in peritonitis/ileus; high-pitched in obstruction)
  - Masses (including pulsatile mass suggesting AAA)
  - Rebound tenderness, guarding, rigidity (signs of acute abdomen)
  - Rovsing's sign, McBurney's point tenderness, psoas/obturator signs (appendicitis)
  - Murphy's sign (cholecystitis)
  - Hernias, skin changes, surgical scars
  - Flank tenderness (urological causes)

Depending on the clinical picture, your doctor may also examine:
- Pelvis (if gynaecological cause suspected)
- Rectum (faecal impaction, masses, or tenderness)
- Testes and scrotum (torsion, epididymo-orchitis, hernia)
- Heart and lungs (if pneumonia or acute coronary syndrome is considered)
- Signs of liver disease or vascular disease

## Possible Causes

### By Location of Pain

Pain location provides a useful starting point, though the true source may differ from where the patient feels it most.

Common causes include:

| Location | Common causes |
|---|---|
| Right upper quadrant | Cholecystitis, gallstones, hepatitis, pancreatitis |
| Epigastric | Peptic ulcer, pancreatitis, GORD, gastritis, ACS |
| Left upper quadrant | Splenomegaly, gastric causes, renal |
| Right lower quadrant | Appendicitis, ovarian pathology, inguinal hernia, Crohn's disease |
| Left lower quadrant | Diverticulitis, ovarian pathology, constipation |
| Central/periumbilical | Early appendicitis, small bowel obstruction, mesenteric ischaemia |
| Loin/flank | Renal colic, pyelonephritis |
| Generalised | Peritonitis, bowel obstruction, mesenteric ischaemia |

### Specific Surgical Presentations

- **Perforated viscus:** sunken tense abdomen, diminished bowel sounds, loss of liver dullness, guarding or rigidity
- **Peritonitis:** patient lies motionless, absent bowel sounds, rebound tenderness, guarding or rigidity
- **Inflamed mass or abscess:** tender mass (abdominal, rectal, or pelvic)
- **Intestinal obstruction:** visible peristalsis, diffuse pain without rebound, tinkling bowel sounds, hernia or rectal mass
- **Ischaemic or strangulated bowel:** severe pain, often with surprisingly little tenderness
- **Bleeding:** pallor, shock, distension; pulsatile mass (AAA) or tenderness (ectopic pregnancy)

### Medical Causes

- GORD
- Gastroenteritis
- Herpes zoster (shingles)
- Infectious mononucleosis
- Opioid withdrawal
- Pyelonephritis
- Gallstones
- Pulmonary embolism
- Pneumonia
- Renal colic
- Toxins (lead or other heavy metal poisoning)
- Vaccine-induced thrombosis with thrombocytopenia (VITT) — rare; consider if viral vector COVID-19 vaccine received in the past 4–42 days; can present as splanchnic vein thrombosis; heparin and enoxaparin are contraindicated

## Investigations

Your doctor may decide investigations are unnecessary if you are young with no other medical conditions, have had previously investigated recurring pain, or your pain is mild or resolving.

When investigations are needed, your doctor may consider:

- **Urine:** dipstick urinalysis (if UTI or kidney stones suspected); pregnancy test for all women of childbearing age
- **Blood sugar level**
- **ECG** — especially if upper abdominal pain and aged over 50
- **Blood tests:**
  - Full blood count (FBC), CRP
  - Electrolytes, urea, creatinine, glucose
  - Lipase (if pancreatitis suspected)
  - Liver function tests (if hepatobiliary disease, pancreatitis, neoplasm, sepsis, or peritonitis suspected)
  - Faecal calprotectin (if inflammatory bowel disease flare suspected)
  - Cardiac enzymes (if upper abdominal pain, aged over 50, or clinically indicated)
- **Imaging:**
  - Ultrasound abdomen — not routinely recommended as an acute investigation
  - Ultrasound pelvis — for pelvic pain in women when criteria are met
  - CT KUB/renal tract — for suspected renal colic when criteria are met
  - CT abdomen — not routine in the community; requires specialist endorsement
  - Chest X-ray — if pneumonia or hiatal hernia suspected

## Management

### Emergency

If red flags or acute surgical causes are present, your doctor will call an ambulance immediately and not delay for investigations. While awaiting the ambulance:

- You will be kept nil by mouth
- Oxygen and IV fluids may be given if needed
- Pain relief will be provided — opioid analgesics can be safely given before a full diagnosis is made, and are given by injection or IV

### Acute Presentations

Your doctor will manage you according to the most likely cause:

- **Suspected gynaecological cause:** managed via the pelvic pain pathway
- **Other acute causes:** managed via the relevant specialist pathway (e.g., urology, gastroenterology)
- **If the cause is unclear or inpatient assessment is needed:** your doctor may arrange assessment by general surgery, gynaecology, or general medicine
- **If you are already under a specialist's care** (e.g., gastroenterologist, oncologist): your doctor may seek advice from your existing specialist

### Chronic or Non-Urgent Conditions

For pain that is not immediately dangerous, your doctor may:

- Follow the relevant community pathway (e.g., irritable bowel syndrome, inflammatory bowel disease, diverticulitis, GORD)
- Check whether you have an existing care plan
- Recommend **conservative measures:**
  - Heat (hot water bottle, heated wheat bag, or warm bath)
  - Clear fluids
  - Reduce coffee, tea, and alcohol (these can worsen pain)
  - Rest
  - Managing bowel regularity
  - Dietary advice or dietitian referral

**Medications your doctor may consider:**
- Analgesia (pain relief) — note that aspirin and anti-inflammatory medications (NSAIDs) can worsen some types of abdominal pain
- Oral antibiotics (e.g., for diverticulitis)
- Antispasmodics

Your doctor will arrange a follow-up review and advise you to return sooner if your pain worsens or new symptoms develop.
