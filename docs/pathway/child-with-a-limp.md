# Child with a Limp

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to a child presenting with a limp or reluctance to weight-bear — identifying urgent causes and guiding initial management.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

Take the child for urgent medical assessment if a limp is present with any of the following:

- Fever > 38.5°C
- Pain that is constant and severe, is getting worse, or occurs at night
- Complete refusal to weight-bear despite pain relief
- Aged > 8 years with an unexplained limp and hip pain
- More than 3 days of symptoms
- Unexplained bruising

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider three key factors: the child's age, whether fever is present, and whether the problem is acute or chronic.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Onset — was there any injury or fall?
- Duration — is it acute, chronic, or intermittent? Have there been similar episodes before?
- Progression — has it worsened over time?
- Location — which joint is affected? Note that back pain can refer to the hip, and hip pain to the thigh or knee. Young children may not localise pain well.
- Joint swelling at any point
- Pain — in very young children this may show as a change in behaviour, such as reverting to crawling instead of walking
- Fever, lethargy, or feeling unwell
- Recent illness, especially upper respiratory tract infection or gastroenteritis
- Stiffness after waking or after sitting still — this can suggest inflammatory arthritis

### Examination

Your doctor will examine:

**General:**
- Whether the child looks unwell — pallor or bruising
- Temperature and vital signs, especially heart rate

- Rash, nail changes, or red eyes (can suggest inflammatory arthritis)

**Lower limbs:**
- Gait pattern — antalgic (pain-avoiding), short leg gait, proximal weakness pattern, toe walking, in-toeing or out-toeing
- Leg length discrepancy or fixed joint deformity
- Feet — painful lesions such as foreign bodies, blisters, or plantar warts
- All joints for swelling, heat, pain, or skin redness
- Range of motion at hip, knee, ankle, and foot (leaving the suspected painful area until last)
- Muscle tone, strength, and reflexes

**If no clear limb focus:** Your doctor will also examine the back, abdomen, groin, and scrotum.

## Possible Causes

### All Ages

**Fracture and injury**
- Toddler's fracture — an undisplaced spiral fracture of the lower shin, common in children under 3 years from a simple twist
- Repetitive-activity injuries in older children: tibial stress fracture, patellofemoral maltracking, osteochondritis dissecans of the knee
- Benign bony lesions that may cause pain or fracture: osteochondroma, osteoid osteoma, bone cysts
- Foot pain from avascular necrosis of the navicular (Köhler disease, usually boys aged 4–8) or second metatarsal head (Freiberg disease, usually girls aged 10–14)
- Growth-related disorders such as Sever's disease

**Bone or joint infection (septic arthritis / osteomyelitis)**

An acute limp with fever and a swollen or painful joint should be treated as septic arthritis or osteomyelitis until proven otherwise. This requires urgent hospital assessment.

- *Septic arthritis:* More common under age 4, usually the hip or knee. The child is generally unwell, has a fever (usually > 38.5°C), and holds the joint in a position of comfort (knee slightly flexed; hip flexed, turned out). Pain is severe on passive movement and unlikely to respond to simple pain relief once established.
- *Osteomyelitis:* Can look similar to septic arthritis. May present as an acutely unwell child with high fever, or more gradually over several days with a limp or refusal to use the limb, sometimes with only a low-grade fever.

**Inflicted injury**
- An unexplained significant fracture (e.g., femoral fracture in a child under 3) raises concern for non-accidental injury.

### Preschool Age

**Transient synovitis (irritable hip)**
- Most common in children under 5, though can occur up to age 10
- A type of reactive arthritis often following a viral illness 1–2 weeks earlier
- The child is usually afebrile and otherwise well — more likely to limp than refuse to walk, and may walk normally after pain relief
- Mild to moderate reduction in hip movement, especially internal rotation
- Benign and self-limiting, but can be difficult to distinguish from septic arthritis early on — it is a diagnosis of exclusion

**Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH)**
- The femoral head has an abnormal relationship with the hip socket — it may be dislocated, partially dislocated, or unstable
- Occurs in 1–2 per 1000 children; five times more common in girls
- If missed in infancy, DDH can present as a gait problem in toddlers
- Signs include a waddling gait (if both hips), reduced hip abduction, leg length discrepancy, or asymmetric buttock creases
- Diagnosed on hip X-ray from age 4 months; requires orthopaedic referral

### Aged 4 to 10 Years

**Transient synovitis** — see above

**Perthes disease**
- Avascular necrosis of the femoral head: the blood supply is lost, the head collapses, then slowly heals and remodels over 2–4 years
- Affects mainly children aged 4–8; more than 4 times as common in boys; bilateral in 10–20%
- Presents with a chronic or intermittent painful limp and reduced hip abduction
- Hip X-ray is diagnostic in established disease (shows a collapsed, dense epiphysis)
- Early orthopaedic referral is needed to keep the femoral head within the socket during healing

**Overuse syndromes** — e.g., Osgood-Schlatter disease (pain and swelling at the tibial tuberosity, worse with activity)

### Aged Over 10 Years

**Slipped upper femoral epiphysis (SUFE)**
- The femoral head slips off the top of the thigh bone through the growth plate during the adolescent growth spurt
- Typically affects children aged 8–15 years who are above the 90th percentile for weight
- Presents as hip, thigh, or knee pain and a limp; may be both sides
- Often gradual onset — early recognition is important, as a fully slipped epiphysis can cause permanent damage
- X-ray (including frog-leg view of the hips) is diagnostic; surgical fixation is required

**Transient synovitis** — see above (unusual at this age but possible)

**Overuse syndromes** — e.g., Osgood-Schlatter disease

### Less Common Causes

**Inflammatory arthritis**
- *Reactive / post-infectious arthritis:* An immune response to infection (parvovirus, EBV, rubella, gut bacteria). Usually affects more than one joint, resolves within 4–6 weeks. Travel history relevant (chikungunya, Ross River virus).
- *Juvenile Idiopathic Arthritis (JIA):*
  - Oligoarticular type (≤ 4 joints): more common in girls, preschool onset, often the knees and ankles; morning stiffness is a clue
  - Enthesitis-related type: older boys, often HLA B-27 positive, affects feet, patellar tendon, and sometimes hip
- Rarer: lupus, inflammatory bowel disease, dermatomyositis

**Rheumatic fever**
- Migratory polyarthritis (pain moving between joints) following a Streptococcal infection (usually a throat infection), developing within 6 weeks
- Higher rates in Māori and Pacific children

**Haemophilia**
- Most moderate-to-severe cases present in infancy with a joint or muscle bleed as the child starts moving; 30% have no family history
- Large joints (hip, knee, ankle) or muscles (iliopsoas, quadriceps) are typically affected; the child may develop recurrent bleeds into a "target" joint

**Malignancy**
- Suspect if symptoms have been present a long time, there is night pain as well as day pain, or systemic symptoms (fever, weight loss, feeling unwell)
- Causes include primary bone tumours (osteosarcoma, Ewing sarcoma), bony metastases (neuroblastoma), or acute lymphoblastic leukaemia (classically causing hip pain)

**Joint hypermobility**
- More common in girls; pain typically occurs after activity
- Predisposes to joint instability and injury

**Neuromuscular conditions**
- Neurological or muscular conditions may cause a chronic painless gait problem, e.g., cerebral palsy or muscular dystrophy
- Viral myositis causes painful muscle weakness

**Abdominal / pelvic causes**
- Occasionally, pelvic pathology presents as hip pain or a limp — e.g., appendicitis, psoas abscess, inguinal hernia, or testicular torsion
- Important to consider in non-verbal children

## Investigations

Your doctor will consider investigations based on the suspected diagnosis:

**Imaging**
- X-ray is used to look for fractures (acute and healing), joint effusions, abnormal bone (tumour, avascular necrosis, chronic osteomyelitis), and specific hip problems (SUFE, Perthes, DDH)
- Further imaging (ultrasound, CT, MRI, bone scan) may be considered for specific situations

**Blood tests**
- Full blood count (FBC)
- Inflammatory markers — ESR and CRP. Both are needed when bone or joint infection is suspected, as CRP alone has a high false-negative rate

If red flags are present or a serious cause is suspected, your doctor may proceed directly to urgent hospital assessment without arranging tests first.

---

## Management

Management depends on the identified or suspected cause. Your doctor will make these decisions in consultation with you.

- **Red flags or suspected serious cause** (bone/joint infection, SUFE, unstable fracture, malignancy): Urgent hospital assessment is needed. Your doctor may keep the child fasted in case surgery is required.
- **Uncertain cause:** Your doctor may arrange urgent paediatric or orthopaedic assessment, especially if haemophilia is suspected or transient synovitis cannot be confirmed.
- **Pain relief:** Your doctor may provide ibuprofen or paracetamol. Note: non-steroidal anti-inflammatories (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen should be avoided if rheumatic fever is suspected.
- **Transient synovitis:** Your doctor may manage this with anti-inflammatory medication (ibuprofen) and rest. It usually resolves within 1–2 weeks but can take up to 6 weeks. Review within 3 days, returning sooner if new fever or worsening pain develops. If it fails to resolve, Perthes disease or inflammatory arthritis should be reconsidered.
- **Perthes disease:** Referral for orthopaedic assessment and monitoring.
- **Developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH):** Referral for orthopaedic assessment.
- **Rheumatic fever:** Managed according to rheumatic fever pathway — NSAIDs are part of treatment.
- **Inflammatory arthritis / JIA:** Your doctor may consider anti-inflammatories (ibuprofen, naproxen) for acute flares. Specialist referral is needed to consider intra-articular steroids for oligoarticular disease, or disease-modifying drugs (DMARDs) or biologics for polyarticular disease. Regular eye checks are also arranged.
- **Stable fracture:** Immobilisation and follow-up at a fracture clinic; or community ACC management if appropriate.
- **Overuse syndrome (e.g., Osgood-Schlatter):** Referral for orthopaedic assessment.
- **Neuromuscular disorder:** Referral for paediatric assessment.
- **Suspected inflicted injury:** Your doctor will follow the appropriate child protection pathway.

Have a low threshold for seeking specialist input for Māori and Pacific children.
