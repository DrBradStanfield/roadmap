# Shoulder Dislocation and Chronic Instability

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of a suspected acute shoulder dislocation or chronic shoulder instability.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if you have any of the following:

- Signs of septic arthritis (red, hot skin over the joint, fever)
- Shoulder pain following a trauma, epileptic fit, or electric shock
- Severe persistent pain that may suggest a possible tumour
- Suspected rupture of the brachial artery or nerve injury — especially with a posterior dislocation

---

## Background

Your doctor will consider the following when assessing your shoulder:

**First-time dislocations** cause severe pain and usually follow significant trauma.

**Recurrent dislocations** may be less painful and are often associated with underlying shoulder instability.

**Anterior dislocation** is the most common type and can often be reduced (put back into place) without sedation, provided the patient is adequately relaxed.

**Posterior (locked) dislocation** is less common and usually occurs after a seizure or electrocution. It is more complex to reduce and more often associated with fractures and nerve or blood vessel injury.

Younger patients have a higher risk of recurrent dislocation after an initial episode.

**Shoulder instability** is typically characterised by:
- A history of partial or complete shoulder dislocation
- Age under 35
- A sensation of the shoulder giving way or subluxing
- Pain or apprehension with overhead or abduction-external rotation movements
- Catching pain during activities

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will typically:

**Take a history**, asking about:
- Any previous shoulder dislocations or symptoms of instability
- The mechanism of injury — how the injury happened (e.g. fall, contact sport, seizure, electrocution)
- Whether recurrent dislocations occur with minor movements such as turning over in bed

**Examine the shoulder**, looking for:
- Signs of anterior dislocation: slight abduction and external rotation, acromial prominence, loss of rounded appearance of the deltoid (squared-off shoulder)
- Signs of posterior dislocation: flattening at the front with prominence at the back, arm held in adduction and internal rotation
- Bony tenderness at the shoulder and neck
- Axillary nerve function — sensation over the lateral upper arm (deltoid region)
- Radial nerve function — thumb, wrist, and elbow extension power and hand sensation
- Vascular injury — checking brachial and radial pulses

**Arrange X-rays** (anteroposterior, axial, and lateral views) if:
- This is a first-time dislocation
- You are aged 40 or older (higher risk of associated fractures)
- There is a history of instability or recurrent dislocation (to look for Bankart lesion, Hill-Sachs lesion, new fracture, or osteoarthritis)

A **Bankart lesion** is a fracture of the front of the glenoid socket following dislocation. A **Hill-Sachs lesion** is an indentation of the back of the humeral head following dislocation.

---

## Management

### Acute Shoulder Dislocation

Your doctor will:

1. Provide **pain relief** and support the arm in a comfortable position (usually elbow bent, arm supported — do not force the arm into a sling if it is locked in a position of abduction).

2. **Refer urgently** to orthopaedics or the emergency department if:
   - Any red flags are present
   - The dislocation is associated with a fracture, nerve injury, or suspected vascular injury
   - Posterior dislocation is confirmed
   - Reduction cannot be achieved in a general practice setting

3. If confident and appropriately equipped, your doctor may **attempt reduction** (putting the joint back into place):
   - Using adequate pain relief, or in some cases conscious sedation (e.g. intravenous fentanyl and midazolam, with Entonox) — only in a fully equipped setting
   - Using the **heavy object method**: lying face-down on a couch with the arm hanging vertically, holding a weighted object to allow gravity to gradually reduce the joint over 5–10 minutes
   - No more than two attempts, avoiding excessive force
   - If reduction is unsuccessful, urgent orthopaedic assessment will be arranged

### Post-Reduction Management

After a successful reduction, your doctor will:

- Arrange a **post-reduction X-ray** to confirm the joint is back in place and to check for fractures
- Recheck nerve and vascular function

**If this is your first dislocation:**
- Wear a broad arm sling for 2–3 weeks
- Regularly move your elbow, wrist, and hand while in the sling
- Avoid returning to sport for at least 6 weeks, or until near-normal muscle strength is restored
- Your doctor will refer you to physiotherapy once out of the sling
- If you are under 30, your doctor will likely refer you to orthopaedics to discuss the risk of recurrence and possible further investigation (MR arthrogram) and stabilisation surgery

**If this is a recurrent dislocation:**
- Wear a broad arm sling until comfortable, usually 1–3 weeks
- Physiotherapy will be requested once comfortable
- If you have had more than 2 dislocations confirmed on X-ray, orthopaedic assessment will be arranged

### Chronic Shoulder Instability

Your doctor may consider:

- **Analgesia** for pain relief, such as paracetamol or non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) — NSAIDs carry side-effects and may not have a significant advantage over paracetamol
- **Activity modification**: limit activities to shoulder height; avoid overhead use, throwing actions, swimming, tennis, painting, and plastering. Avoid open water activities (e.g. kayaking, surfing) due to the risk of drowning if a dislocation occurs in the water
- **Physiotherapy** to strengthen shoulder muscles — this is useful even if surgery is being considered
- **Orthopaedic assessment** if instability persists despite conservative management
