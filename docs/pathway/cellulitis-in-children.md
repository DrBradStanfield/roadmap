# Cellulitis in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to cellulitis in children — assessing severity, identifying red flags, and guiding treatment.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Take your child to the emergency department or call an ambulance immediately if they have any of:

- Fever (temperature above 38°C) with fast heart rate, or appear systemically unwell
- Aged less than 3 months
- Immunocompromised (weakened immune system)
- Swelling or redness around the eyes or a joint
- Large red area or redness that is spreading rapidly

---

## Background

Cellulitis is a common bacterial infection of the skin that can occur at any age. It usually affects a limb but can develop anywhere on the body. The most common bacteria responsible are *Streptococcus pyogenes*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, and group C or D streptococci.

If severe or rapidly progressive, cellulitis can lead to serious complications including bloodstream infection (septicaemia), bone infection (osteomyelitis), joint infection (septic arthritis), or necrotising fasciitis.

New Zealand has some of the highest rates of serious skin infections in children, particularly among Māori and Pacific communities — likely due to a combination of overcrowding and reduced access to primary healthcare.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess for cellulitis if there is an acute area of skin that is red, warm, painful, and swollen — usually on one side of the body. Fever, lethargy, or vomiting may also be present.

**History your doctor will take:**

- Symptoms: pain, itch, weeping, crusting, swelling, colour, and whether it limits normal activity
- Systemic symptoms: fever, vomiting, chills, rigors, lethargy, inability to drink
- Duration and how quickly the infection has progressed
- Any recent breaks in the skin (insect bites, cuts, abrasions, bite wounds)
- Immunisation history — *Haemophilus influenzae* is an important cause in unimmunised children
- Recent immunisation (limb swelling after vaccination can mimic cellulitis — usually starts within 24 hours, child is well, and there is relatively little pain)
- Current or recent antibiotic or topical treatments
- Previous episodes of skin infection or eczema
- Whether other household members have similar symptoms
- Allergies

**Examination your doctor will perform:**

- Assess the skin for redness, warmth, tenderness, swelling, and any tracking (red lines spreading away from the main area)
- Look for areas of fluctuance (fluid collection) or pus suggesting an abscess
- Measure temperature, pulse, blood pressure, and respiratory rate
- Weigh the child
- Look for predisposing causes such as eczema, insect bites, or trauma

**Signs that may suggest severe cellulitis:**

- Rapidly spreading swelling, redness, tenderness, or warmth
- Pain out of proportion to what can be seen (raises concern for necrotising fasciitis — a surgical emergency)
- Loss of joint function
- Location around the eye (periorbital cellulitis)
- Signs of systemic infection

**Conditions your doctor will consider alongside cellulitis:**

- Necrotising fasciitis — a medical and surgical emergency with rapidly advancing tissue involvement, pain disproportionate to appearance, and signs of sepsis
- Soft tissue abscess (boil, furuncle) — pus-filled collection, often caused by *Staphylococcus aureus*
- Septic arthritis or osteomyelitis (joint or bone infection)
- Infected eczema
- Trauma with possible fracture or foreign body

**Investigations:**

Your doctor may take a wound swab or pus sample if there is an open wound or pustule. A swab for MRSA (methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*) may be taken if there are risk factors such as:

- Frequent antibiotic courses
- Infection not responding to standard treatment
- Family history of MRSA
- Recent stay in a healthcare setting with high MRSA risk
- Crowded living conditions

Blood tests and X-rays are not usually needed for uncomplicated skin infections.

## Management

### When hospital admission is needed

Your doctor will arrange urgent hospital assessment if your child has any red flags, signs of sepsis, or suspected necrotising fasciitis.

Your doctor will also consider seeking paediatric advice or arranging further assessment if:

- Your child is under 6 months of age
- Your child has an underlying serious illness (e.g., diabetes)
- The cellulitis is in the genital area, on the hand or foot, or wraps all the way around a limb
- The infection was caused by a human bite
- The redness is spreading rapidly or showing signs of tracking
- There are concerns about pain control, wound care, or the ability to follow up

### Antibiotic treatment at home

Your doctor may consider oral antibiotics for most cases of uncomplicated cellulitis. Intravenous antibiotics are no longer recommended in primary care.

**If no penicillin allergy, your doctor may consider one of:**

- **Cefalexin** — palatable suspension, well tolerated, funded. Dose: 25 mg/kg three times a day (maximum 1 g per dose) for 5 days
- **Flucloxacillin** — capsule form preferred; suspension is unpalatable. Dose: 25 mg/kg four times a day (maximum 1 g per dose) for 5 days

**If penicillin allergy:**

- **Trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole** — 24 mg/kg twice a day for 5 days (maximum 960 mg per dose)

**If known current MRSA:**

- Treat based on known sensitivities, or use trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole as above

**If a previous history of MRSA but no penicillin allergy:**

- Your doctor may still trial a beta-lactam first — a past history of MRSA does not necessarily mean it is currently present

### Pain relief

Your doctor will prescribe appropriate pain relief. Cellulitis is painful, and adequate analgesia is important.

### Wound care

For any broken skin, your doctor will provide wound care advice and check whether tetanus management is needed.

### Monitoring

Your doctor will advise you to watch for:

- Fever — this is the most important sign of how the infection is responding to antibiotics
- It is normal for the redness to spread a little in the first 48 hours

**Return immediately if your child develops:**

- Vomiting or inability to tolerate the antibiotics
- Worsening pain
- New or worsening lethargy, fever, rigors, or reduced fluid intake
- Any other concerns

**School or early childhood centre:** If sores cannot be covered, your child should stay home until sores have dried or until at least 24 hours after antibiotics have been started.

### Follow-up

Your doctor will arrange a follow-up appointment at 48 hours to check that systemic symptoms (especially fever) are resolving. Fever is a more reliable indicator of improvement than the redness itself.

**At follow-up, your doctor will arrange urgent hospital assessment if your child is on antibiotics and has:**

- Vomiting or inability to tolerate the antibiotics
- Worsening pain
- New lethargy, rigors, or reduced fluid intake
- Ongoing fever 48 hours or more after starting antibiotics
- Redness continuing to spread beyond 48 hours

If systemic symptoms have resolved, your doctor may mark around the edge of the redness with a pen to help track any further spread.

**Your doctor will also advise:**

- Continue antibiotics until the full course is finished
- Return promptly if redness starts spreading again
- Watch for any new or unusual symptoms over the next 2–4 weeks that could indicate post-streptococcal complications (rheumatic fever or glomerulonephritis)
- Address any underlying conditions such as eczema or fungal skin infections
- Healthy skin habits: wash hands often with soap and water, keep cuts and sores clean and covered with plasters, cut and file fingernails, use your own towels and sheets, wash sheets and towels regularly
