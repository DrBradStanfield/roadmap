# Postherpetic Neuralgia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers postherpetic neuralgia — the chronic nerve pain that can persist after a shingles (herpes zoster) infection.

---

## Background

- Postherpetic neuralgia is the most common complication of herpes zoster (shingles).
- It presents as chronic pain along cutaneous nerves, with sensory changes to the overlying skin.
- Pain may persist for weeks, months, or years after the rash has healed.
- Most cases can be managed in primary care.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider postherpetic neuralgia if you have intense pain — typically burning or shooting — along a dermatome (a strip of skin supplied by a single nerve) that was previously affected by shingles.

- The pain may follow acute shingles directly, or arise in an area that was affected in the past.
- It may also present as a persistent itch rather than pain.

**Risk factors your doctor will check for:**

- Site affected:
  - Higher risk: trigeminal region, particularly the ophthalmic (eye) area
  - Moderate risk: thoracic (chest/back) region
  - Lower risk: jaw, neck, lumbar, or sacral regions
- Severe pain before the rash appeared (prodromal pain)
- Severe pain or rash during the shingles infection
- Age over 50
- No previous shingles (zoster) vaccination — vaccination reduces the risk of developing postherpetic neuralgia

**Physical examination typically includes:**

- Looking for scarring from a shingles infection
- Checking for altered sensation — tingling, hypersensitivity, or decreased feeling — over the affected area
- Note: a normal examination does not exclude postherpetic neuralgia

---

## Management

Your doctor will discuss treatment options with you. Less than half of patients will benefit from medications alone, so psychological support and other chronic pain approaches are also important.

### Oral Medications

Medications can be used alone or in combination. Research shows that combining nortriptyline and gabapentin is more effective than using either on its own.

**Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs)**
- Examples: amitriptyline, nortriptyline
- Your doctor will start at a low dose and increase slowly
- Extra care is taken in older patients due to the risk of cardiac side effects

**Anticonvulsants**
- Example: carbamazepine

**Gabapentinoids**
- Examples: gabapentin, pregabalin
- Your doctor will take special care if you are also taking opioids or have a history of substance use disorder, as gabapentinoids can enhance the effect of opioids and recreational drugs — increasing the risk of adverse effects and accidental overdose
- Pregabalin may have a greater potential for misuse due to higher bioavailability at high doses

**Other analgesia**
- Most other pain relievers are not particularly helpful for this type of nerve pain
- Tramadol may provide some reduction in pain
- Opiates may have a role in severe cases, particularly when combined with gabapentin

### Topical Agents

**Topical capsaicin 0.075%**
- Can be applied to healed skin lesions up to four times a day
- Works by depleting substance P — it acts on the nerves rather than directly on inflammation
- Your doctor may advise:
  - Do not use on broken skin or unintended areas
  - Takes time to work — expect an initial response around 1 week, and full effect at 4 to 6 weeks
  - Build up gradually — there may be a burning sensation at first, which will ease with continued regular use
  - Apply regularly 3 to 4 times a day; do not massage in
  - Wash hands after application to avoid contact with unintended areas or mucous membranes (unless hands are the treated area)

**Rubefacients**
- Examples: products containing eucalyptus oil, menthol, or methyl salicylate
- May help with pain relief
- Do not use on broken skin; wash hands after application

### Prevention

Treating shingles promptly with oral antiviral medication reduces the risk of developing postherpetic neuralgia.

### When Specialist Input May Be Needed

If symptoms are severe and not responding to chronic pain management, your doctor may consider referral for a specialist chronic pain assessment.
