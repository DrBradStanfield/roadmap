# Falls Prevention

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers falls risk screening, multifactorial assessment, and management for older adults. Falls are a leading cause of injury and loss of independence — most falls risk is modifiable with the right approach.

---

## Background

- Falls are a major cause of injury, unintentional death, and hospital admission in New Zealand, particularly in people aged 80 and over.
- In the community, 1 in 3 people aged over 65 fall each year. This rises to 1 in 2 for those aged over 80.
- Falls cause significant disability, loss of independence, and social restrictions in older adults.

---

## Screening for Falls Risk

Your doctor will routinely screen older patients for falls risk using 3 key questions:

1. Have you slipped, tripped, or fallen in the last year?
2. Do you need to use your hands to get out of a chair?
3. Are there activities you've stopped because you're afraid of losing your balance, or do you worry about falling?

**Why these questions matter:**
- A previous fall is the strongest predictor of a future fall.
- Needing hands to rise from a chair suggests lower-limb weakness and balance problems.
- Restricting activities due to fear of falling reduces fitness and increases risk further.

If you answer yes to any of these, your doctor will look more closely at your risk factors.

---

## Risk Factors for Falls

Your doctor will consider whether any of the following apply:

- Age over 75 years
- Visibly frail, slow, or unsteady gait
- Fall in the last 12 months
- Taking more than 4 regular medications, or taking at-risk medications (see below)
- Postural hypotension (blood pressure drop on standing)
- Cognitive impairment
- Fear of falling that restricts activities
- Relevant conditions: heart disease, stroke, arthritis (including after joint replacement), Parkinson's disease, osteoporosis, urinary urgency or incontinence, peripheral neuropathy, or recent surgery
- Vision problems, including wearing multifocal or bifocal lenses
- Living alone
- Poor nutrition

---

## Assessment

If you are identified as being at risk, your doctor will carry out a more detailed assessment. This may include:

### Physical tests

- Observing gait and balance as you walk and stand
- **Timed Up-and-Go (TUG) test** — measures how quickly you can rise from a chair, walk 3 metres, turn, and return
- **Four-stage balance test** — progressively harder standing balance positions
- **30-second chair stand test** — counts how many times you can stand from a chair in 30 seconds without using your arms

### History and clinical review

- Detailed account of any falls — what happened, when, what you were doing
- Standing and lying blood pressure to check for **postural hypotension** (a drop of more than 20 mmHg on standing with dizziness)
- Review of all medications (including over-the-counter) for fall-related risk

### At-risk medications

Medications with a strong association with falls:
- Benzodiazepines (e.g., diazepam, temazepam, lorazepam)
- Tricyclic antidepressants (e.g., amitriptyline, nortriptyline)
- Antipsychotics (e.g., quetiapine, haloperidol)

Medications with a more modest association:
- Antihypertensives
- Nitrates
- Diuretics

Your doctor will weigh up the risks and benefits of continuing, reducing, or stopping any of these.

### Other assessments

- **Cognition** — a short cognitive assessment if there are any concerns about memory or thinking
- **Vision** — checked every 2 years in adults over 65; multifocal and bifocal lenses increase falls risk in frail older adults; monofocal lenses are safer
- **Feet and footwear** — sensory loss in the feet and inappropriate footwear are common risk factors
- **Mobility aids** — whether current aids are appropriate
- **Nutrition** — if there is concern about weight loss, social isolation, or poor diet
- **Home environment** — your doctor may suggest a home safety check for hazards such as loose rugs, poor lighting, or cluttered walkways

---

## Risk Classification

Your doctor will classify your falls risk as:

- **Low risk** — not visibly frail, with negative answers to all 3 screening questions
- **Moderate risk** — not visibly frail, but with a positive answer to one screening question
- **High risk** — visibly frail, with positive answers to 2 or 3 screening questions, or failed balance or strength tests

---

## Management

### Low Risk

Your doctor will likely:

- Encourage self-management and staying active
- Recommend resources on falls prevention
- Manage any relevant conditions (e.g., blood pressure, diabetes) with regular review

### Moderate Risk

Your doctor may consider:

- Identifying the most likely cause of any previous fall
- Correcting modifiable risk factors identified in the assessment
- Referring to a **community group strength and balance programme**; if too frail for a group class, referral to an **in-home strength and balance service**
- Managing postural hypotension — including reviewing causative medications, increasing fluid intake, and possibly recommending morning caffeine or compression stockings; review in 6 weeks
- Recommending a home safety check
- Encouraging daily exercise focused on **strength, balance, and flexibility** — effective even if modest in gains, and important for maintaining safe daily function
- Recommending home-based exercise using gym equipment or incorporating strength and balance into daily routines (e.g., standing on one leg while waiting for the kettle)
- **Vitamin D supplementation** — your doctor will assess whether this is appropriate
- **Bisphosphonate medication** (e.g., alendronate, risedronate) for fragility or low-impact fractures in older patients — discussed under the osteoporosis pathway
- Healthy eating guidance
- Treating nutritional concerns
- Using a **personal medical alarm**
- Regular primary care review

### High Risk

Your doctor may consider:

- Referral to an **in-home strength and balance service** as first priority; if criteria not met, referral to community group programme
- Review of medications that may cause postural hypotension — reducing or stopping where possible
- Specialist assessment if the cause of falls is unclear or falls are becoming more frequent
- Home safety interventions focused on uncluttered space and safe mobility with walking aids
- Referral for a home occupational therapy assessment
- A **personal medical alarm** — strongly recommended
- Supervised strength and balance training; private physiotherapy may be considered for frail patients needing a personalised programme
- **Vitamin D supplementation** for all patients
- **Bisphosphonate therapy** — common in this group due to high rate of fragility fractures
- Treating nutritional concerns
- Regular primary care review

---

## If There Has Been an Injury

If you have been injured in a fall, ensure an ACC claim is lodged with your doctor. This may open access to:

- Physiotherapy for injury treatment and a personalised exercise programme
- A home assessment for adaptive equipment or housing modifications
- ACC-funded prevention services (note: this does not apply to people in aged residential care facilities)
