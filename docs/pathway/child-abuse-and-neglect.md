# Child Abuse and Neglect

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the identification and initial management of suspected child abuse or neglect in clinical settings.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Advice

If any of the following are present, the clinical team will seek urgent advice from a child protection specialist:

- Any injury to a young, non-ambulatory child
- Any injury without adequate explanation, an identified event, or with a delay in presentation — or inconsistent with the child's developmental capacity
- Unexplained loss of consciousness, persistent vomiting, or oro-nasal bleeding in a young infant (which could indicate a head injury or asphyxia)
- Injuries which appear to have been inflicted

---

## Background

Child abuse is the physical, emotional, or sexual harming of any child or young person. Child neglect is a form of abuse — any act or omission that results in impaired wellbeing, physical functioning, injury, or development of a child.

Key points:

- Children are inherently vulnerable. Consider the possibility of abuse and neglect for any child, regardless of socio-economic background.
- Consequences of abuse and neglect may include physical injury, effects on brain development (faltering growth, developmental delay), learning and behavioural difficulties, reduced IQ, disordered attachment, and persisting negative effects in adulthood affecting health, relationships, employment, and parenting.
- If a person is abusing a child, they may also be abusing the child's primary carer. These are complex, interrelated forms of family violence and must always be identified and addressed together.
- The term "inflicted injury" replaces "non-accidental injury" (NAI).

---

## Assessment

There are no reliable, validated screening tools for child abuse and neglect. Very few signs and symptoms are specific for abuse. Children may experience abuse and neglect and show no signs at all.

Your doctor will not routinely screen for abuse at every visit, but will observe general health, hygiene, and demeanour at clinical encounters (such as immunisations).

### Risk Factors for Vulnerability

Risk factors are not reliable indicators of abuse in any individual case, but your doctor may consider:

- Any history of previous abuse or suspected abuse
- Family violence or parents who were abused as children
- Caregiver who appears indifferent, intolerant, or views the child as troublesome
- Severe social stress, isolation, or lack of support
- Dangerous alcohol or drug use in the household
- Mental illness in a caregiver, including postnatal depression
- Young parents with inadequate support
- Frequent changes of address
- Caregiver who actively avoids interaction with healthcare providers or family support agencies
- Child in Oranga Tamariki care
- Child who is medically fragile, has a disability, or was born prematurely

### Indicators of Neglect

Your doctor may consider the following types of neglect:

- **Physical neglect** — not providing necessities of life (e.g., warmth, food, clothing). In babies or young children, this may present as poor growth.
- **Neglectful supervision** — leaving children home alone or without safe supervision.
- **Emotional neglect** — not providing comfort, attention, and affection through play, talk, and everyday care.
- **Medical neglect** — failure to attend to children's health needs.
- **Educational neglect** — allowing chronic truancy, failure to enrol in school, or inattention to special education needs.

### Concerning Features in Child-Caregiver Interaction

These behaviours are not reliable indicators of abuse in any individual case, but your doctor may note:

- Lack of emotional warmth or bonding
- Dismissive or unresponsive behaviours
- Angry, threatening, aggressive, or coercive interaction
- Calling the child names, using harsh verbal discipline, or mocking the child
- Threatening to hurt or abandon the child
- The child stating they are fearful of, or have been hurt by, their caregiver

### Assessment of Injuries

Your doctor will be particularly alert to:

- Injuries that don't make sense or are not consistent with the child's developmental abilities (e.g., unexplained bruising in a child who is not yet independently mobile)
- Injuries associated with an unexplained delay in seeking care
- Unexplained head injuries
- Bruises, welts, cuts, or abrasions — particularly in unusual locations, clustered, patterned, or in unusually large numbers
- Fractures, especially in a child not yet independently mobile
- Burns

Your doctor will take a thorough history of the event, document who provided the history and who witnessed the injury, and note the child's developmental level. Full physical examination and recording of any injuries using body diagrams will be performed, and photographs may be taken.

Your doctor will also consider cultural practices that may mimic injury — for example, coining (cao gio) and cupping, which can produce bruising patterns sometimes mistaken for abuse.

## Management

Management depends on the level of concern:

- **Red flags present or immediate protection required:** Your doctor will seek urgent advice from the on-call child protection paediatrician. If injury appears unexplained or inflicted, Oranga Tamariki and police may also be notified. Your doctor will explain their concerns honestly to caregivers.

- **Accidental injury but concern about supervision:** Your doctor will explain concerns about the child's environment to the caregiver and may seek advice from Oranga Tamariki or the child protection team.

- **Clearly accidental with no supervision or neglect concerns:** No further action required.

- **Neglect suspected:** Your doctor will assess the level of risk:
  - Low risk — community support services may be requested.
  - Medium or high risk — your doctor will discuss with Oranga Tamariki.

- **Unsure whether abuse or neglect is occurring:** Your doctor may seek advice from Oranga Tamariki or the on-call child protection paediatrician, and request child and parenting community support.

- **Vulnerability identified in children of parents with mental illness or addiction:** Your doctor may involve appropriate support agencies.

Your doctor is obliged to refer to Oranga Tamariki if genuinely concerned about a child's safety. Parental consent is not required to make this referral, though it is good practice to discuss concerns with the parent first if it is safe to do so.
