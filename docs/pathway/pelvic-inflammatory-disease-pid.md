# Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID)

> **Note:** This is a clinical reference tool. The information below reflects GP-level guidance and is intended to support — not replace — a consultation with your doctor. PID requires examination and, in many cases, urgent treatment.

---

## What Is PID?

PID (pelvic inflammatory disease) is infection of the upper genital tract in women, encompassing endometritis, salpingitis, tubo-ovarian abscess, and pelvic peritonitis.

**Causes:**
- Sexually transmitted: Chlamydia trachomatis, Neisseria gonorrhoeae, Mycoplasma genitalium, mixed anaerobes
- Non-sexually acquired: following IUD insertion, termination of pregnancy, or childbirth

About 60% of cases are subclinical with minimal symptoms; up to 70% have no identifiable cause. PID is uncommon in pregnancy after 12 weeks.

**Why prompt treatment matters:** Untreated or delayed treatment risks tubal infertility, ectopic pregnancy, chronic pelvic pain, and Fitz-Hugh-Curtis syndrome (perihepatitis).

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

These suggest an acute emergency rather than PID. Go to ED or call for urgent assessment if:

- Signs of **ectopic pregnancy** (sharp one-sided pain, shoulder tip pain, haemodynamic instability)
- **Ruptured ovarian cyst** (sudden severe pelvic pain)
- **Acute abdomen** features suggestive of appendicitis (right iliac fossa tenderness, rebound, guarding)

---

## Symptoms

- Lower abdominal pain — typically bilateral, may feel like period pain, may worsen with movement or refer to the right upper quadrant
- Deep dyspareunia (pain during sex)
- Abnormal vaginal bleeding or change in discharge
- Fever, nausea, vomiting

---

## Risk Factors

- Age < 30 years
- Partner with an STI
- Recent change in sexual partner
- Recent STI
- Recent pregnancy
- Recent upper genital tract instrumentation or IUD insertion

---

## How PID Is Diagnosed

Diagnosis is clinical. A doctor will:

1. Take a gynaecological, obstetric, and sexual history
2. Check vital signs (temperature, pulse, BP)
3. Perform abdominal examination, speculum, and bimanual pelvic examination
4. Arrange investigations:
   - Urine pregnancy test (if positive → urgent pelvic ultrasound to exclude ectopic)
   - MSU if UTI symptoms
   - STI screening
   - Consider FBC and CRP

**Pelvic ultrasound** is not routinely needed — only if pelvic mass suspected, or to exclude alternatives after 6+ weeks of unexplained pelvic pain.

**Diagnosis of PID requires (after excluding ectopic pregnancy):**
- Lower abdominal pain, PLUS
- On bimanual exam: cervical motion tenderness, uterine tenderness, or adnexal tenderness

**Supportive features:** mucopurulent discharge, fever > 38°C, elevated WCC or CRP, confirmed STI or bacterial vaginosis.

**Differential diagnoses to consider:** ectopic pregnancy, appendicitis, ovarian cyst complication, UTI/pyelonephritis, renal colic, endometriosis.

---

## Severity Assessment

| Feature | Implication |
|---|---|
| Unable to tolerate oral therapy | Severe — hospital |
| Pregnancy | Severe — hospital |
| Fever > 38°C | Severe — hospital |
| Tachycardia, vomiting, sepsis | Severe — hospital |
| Severe pain | Severe — hospital |
| No response at day 3 review | Escalate to hospital |
| None of the above | Mild to moderate — treat in community |

---

## Treatment

> Your doctor will prescribe the right regimen. Do not start antibiotics without a proper assessment.

### Sexually Acquired PID (standard regimen)

- Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM (single dose) + doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily × 14 days + metronidazole 400 mg orally twice daily × 14 days

**If pregnant, breastfeeding, or adherence a concern:**
- Ceftriaxone 500 mg IM (single dose) + azithromycin 1 g orally (repeat at 1 week) + metronidazole 400 mg twice daily × 14 days

### Non-Sexually Acquired PID

- Amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 500/125 mg orally three times a day
  - Duration: 14 days post-procedural (post-abortion/IUD); 7 days for postpartum endometritis
- Penicillin allergy: metronidazole 400 mg twice daily + trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole 160/800 mg twice daily

### If Source Uncertain

Treat as sexually acquired PID. Do not wait for test results — start treatment immediately.

### IUD Management

- IUD should ideally stay in place during treatment — evidence shows it does not worsen outcomes.
- Consider removal if: no response after 48–72 hours, patient requests it, or ongoing high risk.
- If removed: delay until ~24 hours into antibiotics; consider emergency contraception.

---

## Follow-Up

- **Day 3 review:** assess treatment response, discuss results, check for re-exposure
- **1–2 week review:** confirm completion, check contacts notified
- **3-month STI rescreen:** reinfection is common

If not improving by day 3, your doctor should arrange acute gynaecology assessment.

---

## Sexual Contacts (Sexually Acquired PID)

- Abstain from sex (or use condoms) for 2 weeks from start of treatment, and until partner(s) tested and treated
- All sexual contacts in the last 3 months should be notified and offered testing
- Contacts should receive empirical treatment: doxycycline 100 mg twice daily × 7 days
- Note: patient-delivered partner therapy is not legal in New Zealand

---

## When to Escalate to a Doctor or Hospital

**Call your GP or go to ED if:**
- Severe pain, vomiting, fever, or signs of sepsis
- Symptoms not improving after 3 days on antibiotics
- You are pregnant
- Ectopic pregnancy cannot be excluded

**Your GP should seek specialist gynaecology input if:**
- Complicated or recurrent infection
- Allergy to standard antibiotics
- Poor response to treatment
