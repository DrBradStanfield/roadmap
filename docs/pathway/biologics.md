# IBD Medications — Biologics and Immunomodulators

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the four main medication groups used in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), for both acute episodes and long-term maintenance.

> **Important:** These medications require specialist involvement and ongoing monitoring. This information is a general guide — your doctor will tailor treatment to your situation.

---

## Important Safety Note — Immunosuppression

Patients on steroids, thiopurines, methotrexate, or biologics are immunosuppressed. Your doctor should:

- Note this on your medical record
- Recall you annually for flu vaccination

---

## 1. Aminosalicylates (ASAs)

**Drugs in this group:** mesalazine (Pentasa, Asacol), olsalazine, sulfasalazine

### Indications

- Core drugs for **ulcerative colitis (UC)** — used first-line for remission and maintenance
- Limited effect in active Crohn's disease
- Mesalazine is effective and well-tolerated
- Sulfasalazine and olsalazine are used less commonly due to side effects

### Dosing

- **Mesalazine (Asacol):** Oral tablets, releases in colon only. Up to 3.2 g/day in divided doses. Also available as suppository.
- **Mesalazine (Pentasa):** Releases throughout the GI tract. Up to 4 g/day in divided or single dose. Available as tablets, sachets, suppository, or enema.
- **Sulfasalazine:** Acute disease — 1–2 g four times daily. Maintenance — 500 mg four times daily. May cause yellow-orange discolouration of skin, urine, and body fluids; can stain soft contact lenses.
- Rectal therapy (suppositories, enemas) is effective for distal disease, used alone or with oral ASA.

### Monitoring

- Every 6 months: creatinine and urine dipstick

### Blood Disorder Warning

ASAs can cause blood disorders. Patients should report any unexplained bleeding, bruising, purpura, sore throat, fever, or malaise. Your doctor will check a blood count and stop the drug if a blood dyscrasia is suspected.

## 2. Steroids

**Drug in this group:** prednisone

### Indications

- Used to obtain remission in moderate-to-severe flares of both UC and Crohn's disease
- Requires a sufficiently long course (typically 4–8 weeks) with slow reduction to prevent early relapse
- **No role in maintenance therapy**
- Carries risks of adverse effects and increased risk of sepsis

### Dosing

- Prednisone 40 mg/day, weaned over approximately 8 weeks, reducing by 5 mg per week

> **Your doctor may consider:** If you need more than two courses of steroids per year, or prolonged courses, referral to gastroenterology for assessment of immunomodulator or biologic therapy.

### Monitoring

- Consider bone protection (vitamin D, calcium intake, bone density scan) given the risk of osteoporosis with repeated or prolonged steroid use

---

## 3. Immunomodulators

### Thiopurines: Azathioprine and Mercaptopurine (6MP)

Both are first-line immunomodulators.

**Indications:**
- Maintenance therapy when 5-ASA is ineffective in UC, or repeated steroid courses are required
- Induction of remission in Crohn's disease with severe or complex symptoms
- Only started by gastroenterologists after full risk discussion
- Generally safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding (specialist advice required)

**Key points:**
- Slow onset — may take 3–6 months for remission
- TPMT (thiopurine methyltransferase) enzyme level is checked before starting, to identify patients at risk of toxic effects. Those with very low TPMT are at risk of severe myelosuppression.
- Thiopurine metabolites (6-TGN, 6MMP) can be measured to guide dose adjustment and check adherence
- **Avoid co-prescribing with allopurinol** — significantly increases risk of myelosuppression
- Do not give live vaccines (see below)

**Side effects:**
- Nausea and vomiting, flu-like illness (headache, muscle aches)
- Myelosuppression, liver toxicity
- Long-term: immunosuppression, small increased risk of skin cancer and lymphoma

**Monitoring:**
- Azathioprine and 6MP: FBC and LFTs weekly for the first 4 weeks, then every 3 months long-term

---

### Methotrexate (second-line immunomodulator)

**Indications:**
- Used when patients are intolerant of, or the disease is refractory to, thiopurines

**Key points:**
- Given weekly — either orally or subcutaneously
- **Absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy** — causes birth defects. Effective contraception is required. Both men and women should delay pregnancy for at least 3–6 months after stopping.
- Co-prescribed with folic acid 5 mg once weekly (not on the same day as methotrexate)
- Use is limited by side effects

**Monitoring:**
- FBC and LFTs every 3 months

---

## 4. Biologics (TNF Blockers)

**Drugs in this group:** infliximab (Remicade), adalimumab (Amgevita, Humira)

### Indications

- Potent anti-inflammatory effect
- Used in moderate-to-severe IBD not responding to other therapies
- Initiated and managed by gastroenterologists

### Funding in New Zealand

- **Adalimumab (Amgevita):** Available for new patients via Pharmac special authority (SA). Initial SA application is made by a gastroenterologist only; GPs may apply for SA renewal.
- **Adalimumab (Humira):** Available via special authority for patients already established on Humira who meet the criteria.
- Response is confirmed at 6 months.
- Prescriptions should clearly specify the brand.

### Monitoring and Safety Warnings

Before starting biologics, your doctor will arrange screening tests including:
- Varicella zoster virus (VZV) and hepatitis status
- Tuberculosis screening (biologics can reactivate TB)

Ongoing safety points:
- **Withhold the dose if you have signs of active infection** — tell your doctor before taking your next dose
- **Withhold before major surgery** — discuss timing with your surgeon and gastroenterologist
- Flu and pneumococcal vaccinations may be less effective while on biologics — still recommended, but response may be reduced
- **Do not receive live vaccines** (see below)
- Adalimumab is a subcutaneous injection — your gastroenterology team will train you on how to administer it
- Regular review by gastroenterologist (at least every 6 months once stable)

---

## Live Vaccines — Avoid on Immunosuppressants

Patients on thiopurines, methotrexate, or biologics should **not** receive live attenuated vaccines, including:

- Herpes zoster (shingles) vaccines
- Varicella (chickenpox)
- MMR (measles, mumps, rubella)
- Oral polio
- Intranasal influenza
- BCG, oral typhoid, yellow fever

Inactivated vaccines (e.g., injectable flu, pneumococcal) are generally recommended and safe.
