# Haematospermia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of haematospermia (blood in the semen or ejaculate) in adults. The condition is nearly always benign and self-limiting, and specialist assessment is rarely required.

---

## Red Flags — Discuss with Your Doctor Promptly

The following requires prompt medical assessment:

- Haematuria (blood in the urine)

If you have blood in your urine as well as your semen, tell your doctor — this changes the assessment.

---

## Background

- Haematospermia is defined as the presence of blood in the ejaculate. It is nearly always benign and self-limiting.
- Most often caused by infection, inflammation, trauma (e.g. urological procedures), or benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), though the exact cause is often not determined.
- Malignancy is extremely rare.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history and assess risk factors, including:

**Risk factors for infection or STI:**
- Multiple sexual contacts or a recent change in sexual contact
- A sexual partner or contact with an STI
- Sexually active and aged under 30
- Men who have sex with men (MSM)
- History of tuberculosis
- Contact with infested water in areas endemic for urogenital schistosomiasis (parts of Africa, Middle East, Corsica)

**Systemic risk factors:**
- Hypertension
- Use of anticoagulants or antiplatelet agents

**Risk factors for malignancy:**
- Personal or family history of urological cancer (e.g. prostate)
- Exposure to benzenes or aromatic amines (e.g. industrial dyes)
- Smoking

**History questions your doctor will ask:**
- How often has it occurred, and for how long?
- Is there pain on ejaculation (suggests prostatitis or obstruction)?
- Are there urinary symptoms — burning, frequency, difficulty urinating?
- Any abdominal, genital, or perianal pain?
- Fever or general malaise?
- Recent urological procedures, trauma, vigorous exercise, sexual activity, or prolonged abstinence?
- Medications including anticoagulants or NSAIDs?

**Physical examination typically includes:**
- Blood pressure and temperature
- Abdomen for palpable masses
- Skin for petechiae (small bleeding spots)
- Examination of the penis, urethral meatus, and testes for lumps
- Digital rectal examination (DRE) of the prostate

---

## Possible Causes

- Most commonly idiopathic (no identifiable cause), self-limited, and almost invariably benign
- Infection — e.g. urethritis, prostatitis, tuberculosis, schistosomiasis
- Other genitourinary disorders — e.g. BPH, stones, polyps, cysts, haemangioma
- Trauma — including recent surgery or instrumentation of the lower urinary tract (e.g. cystoscopy) within the past 2 months
- Prolonged abstinence
- Systemic conditions — e.g. hypertension, coagulopathy (bleeding disorder)
- Malignancy — e.g. prostate, bladder, testicular, or urethral cancer (rare)

---

## Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- **Midstream urine (MSU)** for microscopy; culture and sensitivity added if there are symptoms of infection
- **STI testing** — PCR of first-pass urine for chlamydia and gonorrhoea if high STI risk or symptoms of urethritis; mycoplasma and trichomoniasis testing may follow if those are negative
- **Blood tests** — full blood count (FBC), electrolytes, liver function tests (LFTs), and coagulation screening if a systemic illness or coagulopathy is suspected
- **PSA (prostate-specific antigen)** — if aged over 50, if the DRE is abnormal, or if there are other risk factors for prostate cancer
- **Urine analysis for acid fast bacilli or parasites** — if tuberculosis or schistosomiasis is suspected

Note: Ultrasound scanning is not routinely indicated for haematospermia.

---

## Management

**If investigations are normal and there are no concerning features:**
Your doctor will reassure you. Most cases resolve on their own without treatment.

**If concerning features are present, your doctor may consider:**

- **Haematuria** — further assessment following a separate haematuria pathway
- **Abnormal PSA or DRE** — referral pathway for prostate cancer assessment
- **Abnormal testicular examination** — assessment for scrotal lumps
- **Infection** — treatment with appropriate antibiotics (e.g. for UTI, prostatitis, or an STI such as chlamydia or gonorrhoea); you will be advised to return if haematospermia persists after treatment
- **Following urological surgery, biopsy, or instrumentation** — reassurance that it will resolve; return if it persists
- **Suspected coagulopathy** — haematology review
- **Undertreated hypertension** — blood pressure correction
- **Persists longer than 2 months** (after excluding hypertension and coagulopathy) — your doctor may seek urology advice
