# Acute Lower Abdominal and Pelvic Pain in Women

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to acute lower abdominal and pelvic pain in women — assessing the cause and determining urgency.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department if there is suspicion of:

- **Ectopic pregnancy** — especially with a positive pregnancy test, severe one-sided pain, and/or shoulder tip pain
- **Heavy vaginal bleeding or internal blood loss**
- **Shock** — collapse, extreme pallor, rapid weak pulse

These require immediate assessment. Do not delay.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history asking about:

- Pain characteristics — onset, site, severity, aggravating and relieving factors, relation to the menstrual cycle
- Systemic symptoms — fever, nausea, vomiting
- Gynaecological symptoms — abnormal vaginal bleeding (intermenstrual, postcoital, heavy periods), vaginal discharge
- Genitourinary symptoms — painful urination (dysuria), urinary frequency or urgency, blood in urine (haematuria)
- Gastrointestinal symptoms — diarrhoea, rectal bleeding, change in bowel habits, bloating, pain on defecation, constipation
- Gynaecological, menstrual, obstetric, and sexual history
- Past abdominal or pelvic surgery

Your doctor will consider whether you are at higher risk of pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) — for example if you are under 30, have had a new sexual partner recently, have had a previous STI, or have had recent intrauterine instrumentation.

Physical examination typically includes:

- Vital signs assessment (blood pressure, heart rate, temperature, respiratory rate) — to check for sepsis or shock
- Abdominal examination — looking for tenderness, guarding, rigidity, rebound tenderness, abdominal mass, or renal angle tenderness
- If you are an adult and have been sexually active, your doctor may also perform a pelvic bimanual and speculum examination to assess for uterine, adnexal, or cervical motion tenderness

---

## Possible Causes

### Gynaecological

- **Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)** — a common cause in young women with a negative pregnancy test; may be sexually or non-sexually acquired
- **Ectopic pregnancy or miscarriage** — especially if pregnancy test is positive
- **Ovarian/adnexal cyst** — with torsion, rupture, or bleeding
- **Endometriosis**
- **Dysmenorrhoea** (painful periods)
- **Tubo-ovarian abscess**
- **Mittelschmerz** — mid-cycle ovulation pain
- **Acute flare of chronic pelvic pain**

### Urological

- Urinary tract infection or pyelonephritis
- Ureteric colic (kidney stone)
- Interstitial cystitis

### Gastrointestinal

- Appendicitis
- Gastroenteritis
- Diverticulitis
- Mesenteric adenitis
- Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)
- Bowel obstruction or volvulus
- Hernia
- Constipation
- Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)

---

## Investigations

Your doctor may arrange tests depending on the likely cause, including:

- Urine pregnancy test (and possibly a blood beta-hCG if the urine test is negative but pregnancy is still suspected)
- Blood tests — FBC and CRP
- Swabs for STIs (self-swabs or examined swabs)
- Urine dipstick or mid-stream urine (MSU)
- Stool (faeces) PCR — if diarrhoea has persisted for more than one week
- Pelvic ultrasound — if a gynaecological cause is suspected
- CT-KUB scan — if ureteric colic (kidney stone) is suspected

---

## Management

### Emergency — Clinically Unstable

If you have heavy vaginal bleeding, shock, or collapse, your doctor will:

- Arrange immediate ambulance transfer to hospital
- Keep you nil by mouth
- Provide oxygen and IV fluids if needed
- Give pain relief

### Pain Relief

Your doctor may consider:

- **Mild to moderate pain:** paracetamol, NSAIDs (e.g. ibuprofen), or both
- **Moderate to severe pain:** injectable diclofenac 75 mg intramuscularly (up to 150 mg total in 24 hours); if NSAIDs are contraindicated, morphine sulfate 5–10 mg with ondansetron 4 mg for nausea

### If Pregnancy Test is Positive

Your doctor will follow the pathway for pain and bleeding in early pregnancy — to rule out ectopic pregnancy or miscarriage.

### If Sepsis is Suspected

Your doctor will follow the sepsis pathway — early treatment is vital.

### If Pelvic Inflammatory Disease (PID) is Suspected

Your doctor may treat empirically for PID even without a confirmed diagnosis — especially if no other obvious cause is found. Rapid improvement with antibiotics supports the diagnosis. PID is often underdiagnosed as a cause of new-onset pelvic pain.

Note: PID may be sexually or non-sexually acquired (up to 70% of cases have no identifiable cause). Your doctor will discuss this sensitively with you.

### If Severe Pain or Systemically Unwell but Stable

Your doctor may refer for specialist assessment:

- Under 15 years: paediatric medicine or paediatric surgery assessment
- 15 years or older: acute gynaecology or acute general surgery assessment, depending on the likely cause

### Ongoing Management

Your doctor will treat the most likely identified cause and arrange follow-up if symptoms persist.
