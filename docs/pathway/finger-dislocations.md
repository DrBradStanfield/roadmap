# Finger Dislocations

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers isolated finger dislocations, including proximal interphalangeal (PIP), distal interphalangeal (DIP), and metacarpophalangeal joint (MCPJ) dislocations.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Care

Your doctor will arrange urgent hand surgery assessment if any of the following are present:

- Dislocation with an associated fracture
- Reduced blood flow or nerve function in the affected finger (digital neurovascular compromise)
- Open (compound) dislocation
- PIP volar, lateral, or complex dorsal MCPJ dislocations — these often require open reduction in hospital
- Joint instability after attempted reduction
- Restriction of full range of motion after reduction
- Reduction was not successful

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Mechanism and time of injury
- Symptoms
- Whether the finger was dislocated and then reduced before arriving (e.g., pulled back into place on the sports field)
- Whether the injury is to the dominant hand
- Any other associated injuries

Physical examination includes:

- Assessing blood flow and nerve function (neurovascular status) in the affected finger
- Checking for signs of fracture

An **X-ray of the hand** is arranged to confirm the dislocation and check for fractures. The X-ray identifies whether the dislocation is dorsal (backward), lateral (sideways), or volar (forward).

---

## Background

Each finger joint is supported by two collateral ligaments and a volar plate (a fibrocartilage plate on the palm side of the joint).

- **DIP dislocations** (distal joint, closest to the fingertip): Uncommon. Most are dorsal and often associated with a fracture.
- **PIP dislocations** (middle joint): The most common finger dislocation. Often reduced before the person seeks medical attention.
- **MCPJ dislocations** (knuckle joint): Less common. Some types require surgical reduction.

---

## Management

### Reduction

Your doctor will only attempt reduction if there is no associated fracture (unless neurovascular compromise makes it urgent).

Preparation before reduction:

- Rings are removed from the affected hand
- Pain relief is provided — your doctor may consider a **digital nerve block** (local anaesthetic injected around the nerve supplying the finger) or **nitrous oxide** (inhaled pain relief) if available
- The hand is stabilised

Reduction technique depends on dislocation type:

**Simple interphalangeal (PIP or DIP) dislocations:**

- *Dorsal:* Traction applied to the finger with gentle hyperextension while the base of the displaced bone is pushed back into position
- *Volar:* Traction with gentle hyperflexion while pushing the base of the displaced bone into position
- *Lateral:* Traction with gentle hyperextension while pushing on the base to correct the sideways deformity

**Simple MCPJ (knuckle) dislocations:**

- *Dorsal:* The wrist is flexed and gentle pressure applied over the back of the base of the proximal phalanx. Longitudinal traction is avoided as it may pull the volar plate into the joint. Excessive hyperextension is avoided to prevent converting a simple dislocation into a complex one.
- *Volar:* Direct pressure over the palm-side base of the proximal phalanx with the joint in flexion

No more than two reduction attempts are generally recommended before seeking specialist assessment.

### After Reduction

- Collateral ligament stability and range of motion are tested
- Post-reduction X-rays are arranged
- If range of motion is restricted, ligament instability is found, or reduction was unsuccessful, your doctor will request urgent hand surgery assessment

### Post-Reduction Splinting by Dislocation Type

**DIP dislocations (distal joint):**

- *Dorsal:* Immobilise in 20–30 degrees of flexion (short Zimmer splint) for 2–3 weeks to prevent re-subluxation. Range of motion checked at 2 weeks.
- *Volar:* Splint in full extension for 6 weeks. Non-urgent hand surgery assessment is arranged.
- *Lateral:* Buddy strapping (taping the injured finger to the adjacent finger) for 2–3 weeks. Range of motion checked at 2 weeks.

**PIP dislocations (middle joint):**

- *Dorsal or lateral:* A dorsal blocking splint for 2 weeks — this splint runs along the back of the finger, holds the middle joint in 30 degrees of flexion, and prevents full extension while allowing the finger to bend. If the splint is not available, buddy strapping is used. Hand therapy is arranged. Range of motion checked at 2 weeks.
- *Volar:* Uncommon. Often associated with a boutonnière injury (rupture of the central slip of the extensor tendon, causing a flexion deformity at the PIP joint and hyperextension at the DIP joint). Splinted in extension and urgent hand surgery assessment is requested.
- *Compound (open):* Wound is washed out, finger splinted, antibiotics started, and urgent hand surgery assessment is requested.

**Thumb MCPJ dislocations:** Urgent hand surgery assessment is always arranged.
