# Thyroid Function Tests

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers when to arrange thyroid function tests, which tests to order, and how to interpret common result patterns.

---

## Key Principle — Start with TSH

TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) is the most sensitive initial test for thyroid dysfunction. In most situations, TSH alone is the appropriate first test.

**Asymptomatic patients without risk factors do not need routine thyroid screening.**

---

## When to Arrange a TSH

Your doctor will arrange a TSH if you have symptoms that could suggest thyroid dysfunction, including:

- Agitation, anxiety, or tremor
- Confusion, depression, or nervousness
- Constipation or diarrhoea
- Dry skin or hair loss
- Goitre (neck swelling)
- Heart rate abnormalities
- Heat or cold intolerance
- High blood pressure
- Lethargy or fatigue
- Menstrual irregularities
- Unexplained weight gain or loss

---

## What Happens After the TSH Result

The lab (Awanui Labs / Labtests) performs automatic reflex testing when TSH is abnormal:

- **TSH elevated** → lab automatically adds FT4
- **TSH low** → lab automatically adds FT3 and FT4

If TSH is normal but clinical concern remains, your doctor may repeat TSH together with FT4, and consider specialist advice.

---

## When Full Thyroid Function Tests (TSH + FT4 + FT3) Are Ordered Upfront

Your doctor will order a full TFT panel in specific situations:

- **Non-compliance with replacement therapy** — discordant TSH and FT4 values may be seen
- **Early in treatment** (first 2 months) — FT4 is more useful than TSH in this window because TSH takes up to 6 weeks to reach steady state
  - In hyperthyroidism: monitor every 4–6 weeks using FT4, FT3, and TSH; TSH may remain suppressed for 3–6 months after starting antithyroid medication
  - In hypothyroidism: TSH is appropriate once stable, but use FT4 if assessment is needed before 6–8 weeks
- **Pregnancy or postpartum**
- **On amiodarone or lithium**
- **Rapidly changing thyroid status** (e.g. after radioactive iodine treatment, thyroiditis)
- **Suspected central (secondary) hypothyroidism** — see below

---

## Screening in Asymptomatic Patients with Risk Factors

Your doctor may arrange thyroid tests even without symptoms if you have:

- Autoimmune conditions (type 1 diabetes, coeliac disease, Addison's disease, pernicious anaemia)
- Down syndrome or Turner's syndrome
- Certain medications (see below)

---

## Medications That Affect Thyroid Function

Several medicines can cause thyroid problems or interfere with thyroid tests:

| Medication | Effect | Monitoring |
|------------|--------|------------|
| Carbimazole, propylthiouracil (antithyroid drugs) | Can cause hypothyroidism | Regular TFT — dose adjusted accordingly |
| Amiodarone, iodine contrast agents | Can cause hypo- or hyperthyroidism | TFT every 6 months, and 12 months after stopping; check immediately if new symptoms (hyperthyroidism can develop rapidly) |
| Lithium | Can cause hypo- or hyperthyroidism | TFT every 6 months |
| Monoclonal antibodies (e.g. pembrolizumab, nivolumab, tyrosine kinase inhibitors) | Thyroid autoimmune disease in ~1 in 3 patients | Thyroid antibodies (TPOAb, TgAb) and TSH every 3 months during treatment |
| Iron, calcium carbonate, bile acid resins, PPS | Can interfere with thyroxine absorption | Take thyroxine separately |
| Biotin (high-dose supplements) | Does not affect thyroid function, but can give false test results | Stop biotin before testing if high-dose use |

---

## Thyroid Autoantibody Testing

Thyroid autoantibodies are present in 10–15% of the population (higher in older women). Your doctor will test for antibodies only once.

- **Anti-TPO antibodies** — tested when TSH is 4–6 (subclinical hypothyroidism). If positive, 5% per year will progress to overt hypothyroidism. Present in almost all autoimmune hypothyroidism.
- **Anti-TSH receptor antibodies** — tested to determine the cause of hyperthyroidism. Highly sensitive and specific for Graves' disease. Never tested in patients with hypothyroidism.

If autoantibody testing is being considered outside these situations, your doctor will seek specialist advice.

---

## Interpreting Results — Common Patterns

### Elevated TSH

| FT4 | Likely explanation |
|-----|--------------------|
| High | Heterophile antibody interference (most common idiopathic cause); thyroxine replacement; amiodarone; central hyperthyroidism; Macro TSH (rare); non-thyroid illness; lab error |
| Normal | Subclinical hypothyroidism; suboptimal thyroxine replacement; medication interference (iron, amiodarone, lithium); malabsorption (IBD, coeliac); neonatal period; lab error; hypoadrenalism (mild TSH elevation only) |
| Low | Primary hypothyroidism |

### Normal TSH

| FT4 | Likely explanation |
|-----|--------------------|
| High | Thyroxine replacement; amiodarone, heparin, steroids, beta blockers; thyroid hormone resistance; non-thyroid illness; lab error |
| Normal | Normal result; some anticonvulsants or bexarotene |
| Low | Central (pituitary or hypothalamic) hypothyroidism; severe non-thyroid illness (sick euthyroid — recheck in 3–4 weeks); isolated TSH deficiency (very rare) |

### Low TSH

| FT4 | Likely explanation |
|-----|--------------------|
| High | Primary hyperthyroidism; pregnancy-related (high beta hCG); excessive thyroxine replacement; recent iodine load; non-thyroid illness (less common) |
| Normal | Subclinical hyperthyroidism; recent hyperthyroidism treatment; mild thyroxine over-replacement; non-thyroid illness; steroids or dopamine |
| Low | Central (pituitary or hypothalamic) hypothyroidism; severe non-thyroid illness (sick euthyroid); T3 replacement |

---

## Central (Secondary) Hypothyroidism

This is an uncommon cause of hypothyroidism (less than 1% of cases) due to pituitary or hypothalamic failure rather than thyroid gland failure. Your doctor will consider this if:

- You have known pituitary or hypothalamic disease
- Hypothyroid symptoms occur alongside other pituitary symptoms (visual changes, headaches, low cortisol, sex hormone deficiency)
- TSH is normal or borderline but inconsistent with symptoms or FT4 level

Note: severe illness can mimic central hypothyroidism.

---

## When Results Don't Fit a Clear Pattern

Some results show variation due to:

- Acute or resolving non-thyroid illness (including subacute thyroiditis)
- Biological and analytical variation
- Interfering antibodies in the laboratory sample

Your doctor may retest in 3 months or another clinically appropriate interval. If an abnormal pattern persists, specialist (endocrinology or chemical pathology) input is sought.

---

## What Happens Next

Based on your results, your doctor will direct you to the appropriate management pathway:

- **Hypothyroidism or subclinical hypothyroidism** — managed with thyroxine or monitoring
- **Hyperthyroidism** — managed with antithyroid medication, radioactive iodine, or surgery depending on the cause
- **Uncertain results** — your doctor will decide whether to retest, adjust timing, or seek specialist advice

*All thyroid test results and treatment decisions should be discussed with your doctor. This information is a guide to how doctors interpret these tests — it is not a substitute for clinical assessment.*
