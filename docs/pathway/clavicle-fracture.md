# Clavicle Fracture

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of clavicle (collarbone) fractures in both children and adults.

---

## Background

- Clavicle fractures are common in children over 7 years as greenstick fractures, and these heal well.
- In adults, the forces involved are greater, so healing can take longer and surgery may need to be considered.
- Fractures have traditionally been treated conservatively, but surgical fixation is now frequently recommended if the fracture is significantly displaced.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess for the presence and type of fracture by:

- Looking and feeling along the length of the clavicle for swelling or deformity
- Checking for skin trauma or **skin tenting** (where bone pushes up against the skin — this can cause pressure necrosis and lead to an open fracture)
- Checking neurovascular status of the arm and hand, as displaced fragments can damage blood vessels or the brachial plexus
- Performing lung examination to exclude a pneumothorax

An **X-ray** of the shoulder and clavicle is used to assess the location and degree of displacement:

- **Class A** — fracture of the middle third (shaft fracture)
- **Class B** — fracture of the distal (lateral) third:
  - Type 1: No displacement
  - Type 2: Displaced
  - Type 3: Involves the AC (acromioclavicular) joint
- **Class C** — fracture of the proximal (medial) third

Your doctor will also consider risk factors for delayed healing or non-union, including: older age, female sex, significant displacement (more than 150%), high-energy injury, and smoking.

---

## Management

### Undisplaced Fractures — Conservative Treatment

Your doctor may recommend:

- Adequate pain relief and ice
- A **broad arm sling** (not collar and cuff) worn under clothing for about 2 weeks until tenderness settles
- Regular **shoulder exercises** to maintain movement
- Home help if you are elderly or living alone

### Significantly Displaced or High-Risk Fractures

Your doctor may refer you for urgent orthopaedic assessment if:

- The fracture is significantly displaced (e.g. 150% displaced or 2.5 cm shortened)
- There is skin tenting or neurovascular damage
- The fracture is in the distal (lateral) third with displacement, as these have a high rate of non-union

### Other Displaced Fractures — Conservative with Monitoring

Your doctor may still manage conservatively with pain relief, ice, broad arm sling, and shoulder exercises, but will monitor for non-union. If the fracture is not showing signs of healing, or if you remain in significant pain at 6 weeks, your doctor may refer you for orthopaedic assessment to consider surgery. Not all non-unions are symptomatic.

---

## Follow-Up

Your doctor will typically review you at:

- **2 weeks:** Check swelling and skin over the fracture. If significantly displaced, a repeat X-ray may be arranged. Begin mobilising the shoulder as comfort allows.
- **6 weeks:** Check shoulder range of movement and arrange X-ray to confirm callus formation. If there is severe ongoing pain, orthopaedic assessment may be requested.
- **8–12 weeks:** Assess shoulder range of motion. If there is significant loss of movement (less than 90° of abduction or less than 30° of external rotation), physiotherapy may be recommended.
- **12 weeks:** Return to active sport or work may be advised once you are pain-free with full range of movement and the fracture has united. Physiotherapy may be recommended if there is significant weakness or functional limitation affecting daily activities or work.
