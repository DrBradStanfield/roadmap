# Spasticity in Spinal Cord Impairment

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of spasticity in adults with spinal cord impairment (SCI). Spasticity management is complex and requires medical supervision. This information is a guide only — always discuss with your doctor.

---

## Background

Spasticity results from disordered sensory-motor control following an upper motor neuron (UMN) lesion. It presents as intermittent or sustained involuntary muscle activation.

- **Lesions above the conus:** UMN pattern — muscle spasticity
- **Lesions below the conus:** Lower motor neuron (LMN) pattern — flaccid muscles, no spasticity
- **Conus lesion:** May produce a mixed UMN and LMN pattern

**Some spasticity can be beneficial.** It maintains muscle tone, helps venous return, enables reflex urination, and may assist with rolling in bed, sitting balance, standing, and walking (in lower-level SCI).

Spasms need treatment if they impact safety or function, or cause pain.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will look for clinical features of spasticity:

- Increased deep tendon reflexes
- Increased muscle tone
- Muscle spasms
- Clonus (rhythmic involuntary contractions)

Your doctor will determine whether spasticity is **new** or **pre-existing and worsening**.

### Impact on Daily Life

Your doctor will ask whether the spasticity or spasms:

- Are painful
- Interfere with function (transfers, sitting in wheelchair, dressing, intimacy, daily activities)
- Have caused injuries (falls, pressure injuries from skin rubbing)
- Have contributed to contractures
- Disturb sleep

### Causes of Increasing Spasticity

Worsening spasticity often has an underlying cause. Your doctor will look for:

- Urinary tract infection (UTI)
- Constipation
- Pressure ulcer or wound
- Tight clothing
- Unrecognised fracture
- Ingrown toenail
- Intrathecal baclofen pump dose insufficient (if you have one)
- Abdominal or pelvic pain (if you have sensation in that area)
- Occult internal pathology (gallbladder, bowel, kidney, gynaecological) — especially if you do not have sensation in that area

### Modified Ashworth Scale

Your doctor may use this scale to grade spasticity:

| Score | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 0 | No increase in muscle tone |
| 1 | Slight increase — catch or minimal resistance at end range of movement |
| 1+ | Slight increase — catch or resistance through less than half range of movement |
| 2 | Marked increase in tone — some limitation of movement |
| 3 | Movement is difficult |
| 4 | Limb held rigid in flexion or extension |

## Management

### Step 1: Eliminate Triggering Causes

Your doctor will first identify and treat any underlying nociceptive cause (UTI, constipation, pressure injury, ingrown toenail, tight clothing, unrecognised fracture, pain).

### Step 2: Non-Pharmacological Treatment

Your doctor or physiotherapist may recommend:

- **Education** for you and your carers
- **Positioning** to break the UMN spasticity pattern
- **Stretching** — regular stretching of affected muscles
- **Standing frame** — if appropriate
- **Strengthening opposite muscle groups:**
  - If spastic flexor pattern → strengthen extensors
  - If spastic extensor pattern → strengthen flexors

### Step 3: Medication — Baclofen

If pharmacological treatment is needed, your doctor will typically start with **baclofen**, titrated slowly upward. Your doctor may seek specialist advice when starting or adjusting baclofen.

**Important:**
- Do not stop baclofen abruptly — sudden withdrawal can cause serious effects
- If you are already on a high dose (taken 4 times daily) with no improvement, your doctor will seek specialist assessment before making further changes
- Other medication options (diazepam, clonidine, benzodiazepines, botulinum toxin) may be considered with specialist input

### Step 4: Specialist Interventions

If spasticity is intractable despite the above, your doctor will refer for specialist assessment. Options include:

- Selective dorsal rhizotomy
- Intrathecal baclofen pump

---

## Intrathecal Baclofen Pump Monitoring

If you have an intrathecal baclofen pump, be alert for:

- **Pump alarm:** This indicates the pump needs refilling. Attend scheduled refill appointments and contact your managing service if the alarm sounds.
- **Signs of baclofen overdose:** Sudden increase in flaccidity (muscle weakness/looseness), diminished strength, or possible cognitive decline — seek emergency assessment immediately.
- **Signs of under-dose:** Increasing spasticity — contact the managing physician promptly.

---

## When to Seek Help

Contact your doctor or seek urgent care if:

- Your spasticity is suddenly worse than usual (look for a triggering cause)
- You suspect baclofen overdose (sudden weakness, cognitive change) — **emergency assessment required**
- Your intrathecal baclofen pump alarm activates
- Spasticity is causing significant pain, interfering with function, or causing skin injury
- Current medication is not controlling spasticity adequately
