# Vaginal Discharge

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to vaginal discharge — assessing the cause and determining appropriate management.

> This information is for general education. Persistent, unusual, or concerning vaginal discharge should always be assessed by your doctor. Do not attempt self-diagnosis or self-treatment for new or worsening symptoms.

---

## Background

Vaginal discharge is a common complaint. The most common cause in people of reproductive age is normal physiological (healthy) discharge. Abnormal discharge can have infective or non-infective causes.

**Common causes of abnormal vaginal discharge:**

*Infective — not sexually transmitted:*
- Bacterial vaginosis (BV)
- Candidiasis (thrush)
- Bacterial vaginitis (rare)

*Infective — sexually transmitted:*
- Chlamydia
- Gonorrhoea
- Trichomoniasis
- Herpes simplex virus

*Non-infective:*
- Hormonal contraception
- Cervical ectropion or polyps
- Retained foreign bodies (tampons, condoms)
- Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (atrophic vaginitis)
- Urogenital tract cancer
- Fistula
- Dermatological disease (eczema, lichen planus)

---

## Recognising the Discharge

Different causes tend to produce different types of discharge — though accurate diagnosis requires a clinical assessment:

| Discharge appearance | Possible cause |
|---|---|
| Thick, white, curd-like, non-offensive | Candidiasis (thrush) |
| Thin, grey-white, offensive / fishy smell | Bacterial vaginosis |
| Frothy, green-yellow, offensive | Trichomoniasis |
| Purulent, with cervical inflammation | STIs (chlamydia, gonorrhoea, Mycoplasma genitalium) |

---

## What Your Doctor Will Do

### History

Your doctor will ask about:
- Characteristics of the discharge (onset, colour, consistency, odour)
- Associated symptoms: itch, vulval soreness, abnormal bleeding, pain with sex, pelvic pain, dysuria
- Sexual history and STI risk factors
- Gynaecological history: pregnancy, recent IUD insertion, contraception
- Medical conditions (diabetes, immunosuppression)
- Smoking, recent antibiotic use, and vaginal hygiene practices (soaps, perfumed products, douching)
- Possibility of a retained tampon, condom, or foreign body

### Examination

Your doctor will typically examine the vulva and perform a speculum and bimanual internal examination to assess:
- Vulval appearance (redness, fissures, skin conditions)
- Type of discharge
- Signs of cervicitis (inflammation of the cervix — mucopurulent discharge, bleeding on contact)
- Signs of pelvic inflammatory disease (uterine or adnexal tenderness)

### Investigations

- Vaginal swab for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and trichomonas (PCR/NAAT)
- Culture swab for bacterial vaginosis and candida
- Cervical swab if gonorrhoea is suspected (to test antibiotic sensitivity)
- Herpes swab and syphilis blood test if genital ulcers are present
- Pregnancy test if relevant
- Cervical screening (HPV and cytology co-test) if due or if a cervical cause is suspected

## Treatment

### Empirical (Presumed Cause) Treatment

If the type of discharge is characteristic, your doctor may treat before test results return:

**Suspected thrush (thick, white, curd-like, itchy):**
- Clotrimazole 2% vaginal cream for 3 nights, OR
- Fluconazole 150 mg tablet (single dose, not in pregnancy), OR
- Clotrimazole 1% cream for 6 nights (preferred in pregnancy)

**Suspected bacterial vaginosis (grey-white, fishy smell, no inflammation):**
- Metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 7 days (preferred in pregnancy), OR
- Metronidazole 2 g as single dose (less effective)

**Suspected trichomoniasis (frothy, offensive, green-yellow, with vulvitis):**
- Metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 7 days (preferred in pregnancy), OR
- Metronidazole 2 g as single dose

### Treatment by Confirmed Cause

Your doctor will manage each confirmed cause according to its specific pathway (BV, candidiasis, trichomoniasis, chlamydia, gonorrhoea, herpes, pelvic inflammatory disease).

## Other Causes

**Retained foreign body:** Your doctor will remove it and may prescribe metronidazole.

**Physiological discharge:** If no infection is found and the discharge is normal, your doctor will advise general genital skin care:
- Avoid soaps, perfumed products, and tight synthetic clothing
- Prefer tampons or menstrual cups over scented pads
- Use emollients or barrier creams if there is irritation
- A weak topical steroid cream (hydrocortisone 1%) may be prescribed for itch

**Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM / atrophic vaginitis):** After ruling out infection, your doctor will discuss treatment options:
- Vaginal oestrogen cream (estriol): highly effective; safe even after certain gynaecological cancers and for those with a history of blood clots. Applied nightly for 2 weeks then twice weekly. No progesterone needed.
- If you decline hormonal treatment: vaginal moisturisers (e.g., Replens, YES VM) used 3 times weekly; water-based or silicone-based lubricants during intercourse
- Pelvic floor physiotherapy may also be recommended

**Skin conditions (lichen sclerosus, lichen planus, eczema):** Managed according to specific dermatology/gynaecology pathways.

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you if:
- The cervix appears abnormal or suspicious for cancer — urgent colposcopy referral
- Discharge may be due to a fistula, malignancy, or other gynaecological concern
- Symptoms persist despite negative test results and initial treatment
- Discharge is persistent, offensive, and blood-stained without a clear explanation
