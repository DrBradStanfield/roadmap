# Spirometry Testing

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Spirometry is a breathing test used to measure how well your lungs work. It helps diagnose and monitor conditions such as asthma and COPD. Your doctor will arrange spirometry if they think it will help answer a clinical question about your lung function.

---

## Background

Spirometry measures the volume and speed of air you can breathe out. Key measurements include:

- **FEV1** — the volume of air breathed out in the first second of a forced breath
- **FVC** — the total volume of air breathed out during a full forced breath
- **FEV1/FVC ratio** — used to distinguish obstructive (e.g. asthma, COPD) from restrictive lung disease

Spirometry requires significant patient effort and cooperation. It can only be performed by trained, accredited providers using appropriate equipment.

If a screening device (e.g. PICO6 or hand-held spirometer) shows an FEV1/FEV6 ratio below 70%, a full diagnostic spirometry with a flow volume loop is needed for an accurate result.

---

## When Spirometry Is Used

Your doctor may recommend spirometry to investigate:

- Suspected asthma or COPD
- Unexplained breathlessness or chronic cough
- Monitoring of known lung disease
- Pre-operative respiratory assessment

---

## Contraindications

Your doctor will check whether spirometry is safe for you before arranging it.

### Absolute Contraindications (spirometry should not proceed)

- Active infection — e.g. tuberculosis (until treated for at least 2 weeks), COVID-19 infection or recent household contact (defer until 2 weeks after diagnosis or exposure), new acute respiratory viral symptoms
- Conditions where forced expiration could cause serious harm — e.g. dissecting or unstable aortic aneurysm, current pneumothorax, or recent surgery (including eye, chest, abdominal, or brain surgery)

### Relative Contraindications (your doctor will weigh up the risks)

- Suspected respiratory infection in the last 4 to 6 weeks
- Undiagnosed chest symptoms, such as coughing up blood
- History of pneumothorax
- Recent cardiovascular events — e.g. heart attack within the past month, uncontrolled high blood pressure, pulmonary embolism, or stroke
- Previous thoracic, abdominal, or eye surgery
- Being too unwell to perform forced expiration
- Significant communication difficulties (e.g. learning disability, confusion)

---

## What to Expect

Spirometry involves breathing into a mouthpiece connected to a machine. You will be asked to breathe in fully, then breathe out as hard and fast as possible. The test is usually repeated several times to get a reliable result.

The test is non-invasive and generally safe. Some people find the forced breathing effort tiring, particularly if they are elderly or already have significant airway disease — full exhalation can take up to 20 seconds.

Your doctor will review the results and explain what they mean for your health.
