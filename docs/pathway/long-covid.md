# Long COVID

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the long-term effects of COVID-19 infection — symptoms that have persisted or developed 4 or more weeks after acute infection.

---

## Background

Long COVID is the term for signs and symptoms of COVID-19 that continue or develop after acute infection. It includes:

- **Ongoing symptomatic COVID-19** — symptoms lasting from 4 to 12 weeks after testing positive
- **Post-COVID-19 syndrome** — symptoms continuing for more than 12 weeks that cannot be explained by another diagnosis

Long COVID appears more common after severe acute illness, but it can also affect those who had mild or moderate initial symptoms. There is no single time course — symptoms may improve one week and relapse the next.

Common presentations include:

- Prolonged non-specific post-viral symptoms (fatigue, breathlessness, palpitations, dizziness, poor concentration and memory)
- Specific serious complications from the acute infection
- Recovery after severe illness requiring intensive care (post-intensive care syndrome, PICS)
- Psychosocial effects of prolonged symptoms and functional impairment

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will typically:

**Take a history including:**

- Relevant dates — symptom onset, first positive test, any recent COVID-19 tests
- Symptoms experienced during the acute illness
- Whether the patient was hospitalised — details of management, complications, and follow-up
- Pre-existing conditions and the effect of COVID-19 on these
- Current symptoms (see common post-acute symptoms below)
- Mental health — anxiety, depression, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), and suicidal ideation
- Impact on quality of life — home situation, employment, financial and social supports, bereavement

**Common post-acute symptoms include:**

- Fatigue, exercise intolerance, inability to return to usual activity levels
- Post-exertional malaise (PEM) or post-exertion symptom exacerbation (PESE) — worsening of symptoms or tiredness after even small amounts of physical or mental effort, lasting more than 24 hours
- Breathlessness and abnormal breathing patterns
- Cough
- Joint or muscle pain
- Chest pain or tightness
- Changes in sense of smell or taste (anosmia, hyposmia, parosmia, phantosmia)
- Neurological symptoms — memory and concentration difficulties (brain fog), slowed information processing, headaches, dizziness
- Autonomic dysfunction symptoms, including postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS) — palpitations, dizziness, orthostatic tachycardia, gastrointestinal disturbances
- Sleep disturbances
- Swallowing difficulties
- Changes in appetite and eating patterns
- In older adults, long COVID may present as delirium, deconditioning, or worsening frailty or dementia

**Physical examination may include:**

- Vital signs — pulse, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation (SaO2)
- Lying and standing blood pressure and heart rate (if autonomic dysfunction is suspected)
- Examination of the nasopharynx (if altered smell or taste — to exclude nasal polyps, rhinosinusitis, or other causes)
- Height, weight, BMI, and assessment for weight loss
- Mobility assessment
- Mental state examination and cognitive assessment

**Investigations:**

- Basic blood tests as appropriate: FBC, thyroid function tests (TFT), creatinine and electrolytes, liver function tests (LFT), calcium, CRP, B12 and folate if clinically indicated
- Avoid excessive investigations — non-specific abnormalities are common
- Further tests guided by the patient's specific symptoms or concerns

If any symptoms could indicate a significant underlying condition (such as heart failure, acute coronary syndrome, pulmonary embolism, or pneumonia), your doctor will assess those first.

## Management

### Education and Reassurance

Your doctor may explain that:

- Prolonged symptoms are common and, although significant, will often improve with time
- Existing symptoms may change, new symptoms may develop, or there may be a relapsing course

### Fatigue and Exercise Intolerance

Your doctor may advise:

- Pacing, planning, and prioritising activities — avoid doing too much at once
- Breaking tasks down into smaller steps
- Resting body and mind, including good sleep hygiene
- Eating well with small regular meals
- Being aware that malaise can be triggered by cognitive and emotional effort, not just physical activity
- Keeping a symptom diary to identify individual fatigue patterns (with care — prolonged diary use may heighten anxiety)
- Graded exercise therapy (gradually increasing activity) — **not** recommended if post-exertional malaise (PEM) is present

If PEM or persistent fatigue is a concern, your doctor may discuss referral to a physiotherapist or occupational therapist.

### Breathlessness

Your doctor may advise:

- Keeping some activity going — stopping all activity leads to muscle deconditioning
- Leaning forward while sitting or leaning on a stable surface when breathless
- Breathing control techniques (e.g., "Blow As You Go" — exhaling during the effortful part of an activity, avoiding breath-holding)
- Breathing exercises: sit in a supported position, breathe in through the nose and out through the mouth, allowing the tummy to rise, aiming for an inspiration-to-expiration ratio of 1:2
- Referral to respiratory physiotherapy if breathlessness is persistent

### Cough

Your doctor may advise:

- **Dry cough:** breathe slowly through the nose, drink regularly, sip fluids slowly, suck on ice or a lozenge, try a warm drink
- **Productive cough:** drink regularly, try gentle movement to help clear sputum, use the active cycle of breathing technique (ACBT), try sputum clearance in the shower, use a saline nasal spray to reduce post-nasal drip
- Referral to respiratory physiotherapy if the cough is persistent

### Changes in Taste and Smell

Your doctor may advise:

- Eating foods that are most appealing and continuing to try a wide range of foods
- Keeping the mouth clean and healthy
- Adding strong flavours to food, and experimenting with different textures and temperatures
- Using plastic cutlery or glass cookware to reduce metallic taste
- Smell training if symptoms persist beyond 2 weeks (involves smelling four distinct scents twice daily)

If parosmia or phantosmia is not improving after 12 weeks, your doctor may seek specialist advice (ear, nose and throat, or neurology) depending on whether other neurological symptoms are present.

### Chest Pain

If significant cardiac and respiratory causes have been excluded, your doctor will provide reassurance and education about symptoms that would need further review.

### Palpitations

Your doctor may explain that benign palpitations are common after COVID-19 and many resolve by 12 weeks. If symptoms are distressing or persistent, further assessment will be arranged.

### Neurological Symptoms (Headache, Dizziness, Brain Fog)

Your doctor may advise:

- Developing a daily routine that rotates between periods of rest and periods of low to medium activity
- Reducing sensory stimuli (e.g., light or noise) in the short term
- Breaking tasks down into smaller manageable steps
- Using a calendar or writing things down to support memory

### Musculoskeletal Pain

Your doctor may recommend:

- Gentle activity and flexibility exercises (stretching, yoga, tai chi)
- Simple over-the-counter analgesia
- Referral to physiotherapy for pain management or rehabilitation if symptoms persist

### Swallowing Difficulties

Your doctor may advise:

- Sitting upright when eating and drinking
- Concentrating fully on eating — avoid talking while eating
- Eating when most alert; considering eating the main meal earlier in the day
- Avoiding dry foods (e.g., cornflakes, coconut, pastry) that can catch in the throat
- Staying upright for 30 minutes after eating or drinking
- Referral to a speech language therapist if difficulties continue

### Mental Health

If anxiety, low mood, PTSD, or other mental health challenges are present, your doctor may:

- Address contributing factors — pain, fear, social determinants, loneliness
- Recommend keeping a routine, setting achievable goals, staying connected, limiting alcohol
- Consider referral to counselling or psychology

### Ongoing Support

Your doctor may also:

- Optimise management of any pre-existing long-term conditions
- Discuss COVID-19 vaccination — there is some evidence that vaccination in unvaccinated people with long COVID may help improve symptoms
- Refer to allied health services depending on symptom severity:
  - **Physiotherapist** — for breathlessness, breathing dysfunction, respiratory muscle strengthening, tailored exercise
  - **Occupational therapist** — for difficulty with self-care, work, leisure goals, or pacing strategies
  - **Dietitian** — if poor appetite, unintentional weight loss, or persistent gastrointestinal symptoms
  - **Speech language therapist** — for communication or swallowing difficulties
  - **Psychologist** — for anxiety, distress, sleep, or processing the circumstances of acute infection

Advise your doctor if any new or worsening symptoms develop, or if current symptoms are not improving with self-management by 12 weeks.
