# Dysmenorrhoea

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: October 2015.*

Dysmenorrhoea means painful periods. It is common in women during their reproductive years. When severe, it can get in the way of daily activities and often leads to time off school or work.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to See Your Doctor

**See your doctor if period pain comes with any of these, as they may point to an underlying cause:**
- Bleeding between periods or after sex.
- Pain during sex.
- Vaginal discharge.
- Pain on one side only.

**Your doctor may treat your symptoms as severe if:**
- You are distressed and need time off work or school with your periods.
- Self-care and pain relief are not helping.
- The pain starts more than 1 day before your period.
- Endometriosis is suspected.

---

## Background

- **Primary dysmenorrhoea** is recurrent, crampy, lower abdominal pain during periods, with no disease found that could explain it. The pain starts just before or with the start of bleeding, then gradually eases over 12 to 72 hours.
- It is caused by strong contractions of the uterus that reduce its blood supply. Prostaglandins released from the lining of the uterus at the start of a period are a major cause of these contractions.
- Risk factors include longer periods, an early first period, smoking, alcohol, and obesity.
- **Secondary dysmenorrhoea** has the same features but occurs in women with a condition that could explain the pain, such as pelvic inflammatory disease, endometriosis, adenomyosis, or uterine fibroids.

---

## Assessment

**Your doctor may:**
- Ask about your gynaecological and pregnancy history, looking for features of an underlying cause (see above).
- Ask about smoking.
- For moderate symptoms (discomfort, but you can keep up with daily tasks): examine your abdomen and pelvis.
- For severe symptoms: ask how often the pain is debilitating, and do a pelvic examination.
- A pelvic examination is not needed for a young teenager unless she is sexually active.
- Update your cervical smear, and take swabs for sexually transmitted infections (STIs) if appropriate.
- If endometriosis is suspected, follow the approach for endometriosis.

---

## Management

**Your doctor may:**
- Encourage you to stop smoking.
- Prescribe antibiotics if you have an STI or pelvic inflammatory disease (PID).

### Treatment options

**Your doctor may consider:**
- **NSAIDs** (anti-inflammatory pain relief).
- **Tranexamic acid**, if you also have heavy menstrual bleeding.
- **The combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP)**, or **desogestrel** (a progestogen-only pill that stops ovulation).
- **Medroxyprogesterone acetate.**
- **A levonorgestrel IUD (Mirena).**

Your doctor may suggest trying NSAIDs or the combined pill for 3 months before judging how well it works. Some women benefit from using both together. If it works well, treatment continues.

### Taking the combined pill continuously or in an extended way

Extended or continuous use of the combined pill gives better relief of period symptoms than the usual monthly cycle.

- **Continuous use** (no hormone-free breaks): studies show no increase in serious adverse events, however long it is used. Breakthrough bleeding is likely to improve with time, or it can be managed as in tricycling.
- **Tricycling:** skip the placebo tablets and take 3 packets of hormone tablets in a row.
  - Breakthrough bleeding can sometimes happen. If it is bothersome, your doctor may suggest a hormone-free break of 4 to 7 days. Shorter 4-day breaks cause fewer hormone withdrawal side effects and give better contraceptive protection.
  - You will have a withdrawal bleed during the break.
  - After the break, start another 3 packs in a row.
- For women after their first period and less than 40 years, the combined pill is medical eligibility category 1.

### Levonorgestrel IUD

- **Standard levonorgestrel IUD:** fully funded.
  - It is supplied on prescription. Most pharmacies need to order it in, so take your prescription in at least a few days before your fitting appointment.
- **Smaller, lower-dose levonorgestrel IUD (Jaydess):** fully funded and licensed for 3 years.
  - Not recommended for period control. Periods stop less often than with the standard device.
  - Slimmer and smaller, so it may be easier to insert in women who have not given birth.

### Pelvic ultrasound

Your doctor may arrange a pelvic ultrasound if:
- A pelvic mass is found on examination, or
- Severe symptoms have not responded after at least 3 months of appropriate medical treatment.

Ultrasound rarely adds diagnostic information in young patients with period pain. Its value in endometriosis is limited.

---

## When to Refer

Your doctor may refer you for a gynaecology assessment if severe symptoms have not responded after at least 3 months of treatment. The pelvic ultrasound results go with the referral.
