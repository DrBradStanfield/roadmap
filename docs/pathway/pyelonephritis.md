# Pyelonephritis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers acute pyelonephritis (upper urinary tract infection) in adults — how it is diagnosed, treated, and when hospital assessment is needed.

---

## Red Flag — Seek Urgent Review

- Persistent fever or loin pain despite 24 to 48 hours of antibiotics (possible obstructed kidney, renal stone, or abscess)

---

## What is Pyelonephritis?

Pyelonephritis is infection of the kidney (upper urinary tract), as distinct from cystitis (lower urinary tract / bladder infection). Symptoms of cystitis (frequency, urgency, dysuria) may or may not be present. Diagnosis requires at least one of:

- Fever greater than 38°C
- Rigors
- Loin pain or tenderness

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Ask about cystitis symptoms (frequency, urgency, dysuria), flank pain, fever, rigors, malaise, nausea/vomiting, and mental status changes (especially in elderly patients)
- Examine vital signs, hydration status, and abdominal/flank/suprapubic tenderness
- Consider pelvic examination in women to exclude pelvic inflammatory disease
- Perform a urine dipstick (if no abnormality on dipstick, diagnosis is less likely or the collecting system may be obstructed)

**Investigations typically include:**
- Urine pregnancy test (if applicable)
- Urine for urgent microscopy and culture
- FBC and serum creatinine (especially if IV antibiotics are needed)
- CT-KUB if renal colic or history of renal stones is possible

**Other diagnoses to consider:** appendicitis, cholecystitis, gastroenteritis, pelvic inflammatory disease, pneumonia, renal stone.

---

## Uncomplicated vs Complicated Pyelonephritis

Your doctor will classify the infection to guide treatment.

### Uncomplicated (ALL of the following must apply)
- Female, aged 15 to 64 years
- No features of sepsis
- Normal kidney function
- Normal renal structure
- Not pregnant
- No complicating disease (e.g., diabetes)

### Complicated (ANY of the following)
- Age over 65 years
- Male
- Features of sepsis
- Known or suspected renal impairment
- Abnormality of the renal tract
- Known or suspected renal stones
- Bilateral pyelonephritis
- Pregnancy
- Immunosuppression (e.g., diabetes, steroids, renal transplant)
- Spinal cord impairment
- History of UTI with resistant organisms (e.g., ESBL)
- Recent hospital admission with UTI, or hospital-acquired UTI
- UTI following urological instrumentation
- Catheter in situ
- Recent travel to India (risk of resistant organisms)
- Failure to respond to a single dose of IV antibiotics

---

## Treatment

**Key principle:** Expect improvement within 48 hours. Uncomplicated pyelonephritis should improve within 24 hours of a single IV antibiotic dose or within 48 hours of starting oral antibiotics.

### Complicated Pyelonephritis

Hospital or specialist input is needed. Your doctor will refer depending on the situation:
- Pregnancy: urgent obstetric or general medicine assessment (see below)
- Renal transplant: acute nephrology advice
- Other complicated cases: general medicine advice

### Uncomplicated Pyelonephritis with Vomiting or Dehydration

If you cannot tolerate oral fluids or antibiotics, your doctor may give IV antibiotics in the community (if circumstances are suitable) or refer to hospital.

**IV antibiotic treatment:**
- **Gentamicin IV: 4 mg/kg (max 320 mg), based on ideal body weight** — given as a single dose, either slow IV push over 3–5 minutes or infused over 30 minutes. No blood monitoring is required for a single dose in uncomplicated patients.
- If gentamicin is not immediately available: ceftriaxone 1 g IV or IM (intramuscular ceftriaxone uses 1% lignocaine as diluent).
- Other IV antibiotics (cefazolin, amoxicillin, augmentin) are **not appropriate** for pyelonephritis — a single dose does not achieve adequate concentrations in the kidney.

After IV treatment, your doctor will review at 24 hours:
- **Significant improvement:** Oral antibiotics for 7 days (see below), with advice to return if symptoms worsen.
- **Ongoing fever, loin pain, or dehydration:** Hospital assessment arranged.

### Uncomplicated Pyelonephritis without Vomiting or Dehydration

Oral antibiotics are started. IV antibiotics offer no additional benefit in this situation.

**Antibiotic selection:**
- **First choice: Cefalexin 1 g, three to four times a day for 7 days**
- If severe penicillin or cephalosporin allergy, or any allergy to amoxicillin/cephalosporins:
  - Co-trimoxazole (trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole) 960 mg twice a day for 7 days
  - Ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice a day for 7 days — reserved for severe beta-lactam/sulfonamide allergy or documented resistance. Note: local E. coli resistance to ciprofloxacin exceeds 10%. Risks include tendon disorders, hypoglycaemia, and neuropsychiatric effects.
- **Nitrofurantoin is not appropriate** for pyelonephritis — it does not achieve therapeutic concentrations in kidney tissue.
- Past urine culture results (if available) should guide antibiotic choice, especially if prior UTI history.
- Antibiotics may need to change once culture and sensitivity results return — a significant proportion of organisms are not sensitive to empirical choices.
- Seek infectious diseases input if ESBL carrier, multi-drug resistant risk, or slow improvement.

**Supportive care:**
- **Paracetamol** for pain and fever — avoid NSAIDs (risk of renal impairment)
- Encourage good fluid intake
- Your doctor will arrange a 24-hour review and a safety plan in case of deterioration

---

## Follow-up

- Renal ultrasound is **not needed** during the acute episode of uncomplicated pyelonephritis.
- A **non-acute renal ultrasound** should be arranged if there has been no renal imaging in the past 6 months and:
  - Male with acute pyelonephritis
  - Female with 2 episodes of acute pyelonephritis within the last 12 months
- If ultrasound shows a significant abnormality, nephrology or urology review will be arranged.

---

## When Hospital or Specialist Assessment Is Needed

Your doctor will arrange the appropriate referral:

- **Emergency / acute hospital assessment:** Sepsis features, moderate-severe dehydration, failure of community IV treatment, acute kidney injury concerns, lack of improvement
- **Acute obstetric assessment:** Pyelonephritis at 20+ weeks gestation
- **Acute general medicine assessment:** Pyelonephritis at <20 weeks gestation, or any complicated case without response to treatment
- **Nephrology advice:** Complicated pyelonephritis with renal transplant, or significant ultrasound abnormality
- **Infectious diseases advice:** ESBL carrier, multi-drug resistant organism risk, slow response to treatment
