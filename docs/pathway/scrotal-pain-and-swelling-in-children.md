# Scrotal Pain and Swelling in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to scrotal pain and swelling in children younger than 15 years — assessing the likely cause and determining urgency.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

**Go to the emergency department immediately** if there is an acutely painful scrotum. This is a potential surgical emergency. Any acute scrotal pain must be treated as testicular torsion until proven otherwise, as delay can cause permanent damage to the testis.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take an age-appropriate history including:

**History of the pain or swelling:**
- In infants — irritability and poor feeding may be the only clues
- Site — some children report abdominal pain only; upper pole testicular pain suggests a testicular appendage cause
- Onset and progression — gradual or sudden, worsening, stable, or improving
- Character — dull or sharp
- Severity and radiation (to flank or iliac fossa)
- Timing — episodic, constant, intermittent (intermittent episodes suggest intermittent torsion)
- History of trauma

**Pain patterns that help distinguish causes:**
- Torsion: sudden onset, severe pain, not relieved by lying still — consider in older children and adolescents with acute or unexplained abdominal pain
- Torted testicular appendage: gradual onset, progressively worsening, but usually does not prevent the child from doing activities
- Infection: gradual onset, mild to moderate, often with fever, dysuria, and redness
- Idiopathic scrotal oedema: often painless, swollen and red, may cross the midline, sometimes itchy

**Associated symptoms:**
- Nausea or vomiting (associated with testicular torsion and strangulated hernia)
- Fever or rigors (more common with infection; torsion is usually afebrile)
- Urethral discharge, urinary frequency, urgency, or dysuria (suggests epididymo-orchitis)
- Abdominal pain (can occur with torsion)
- Parotid swelling (associated with mumps orchitis)
- Possible cancer symptoms: back pain, weight loss, shortness of breath, bone pain, night sweats

**Previous history:**
- Scrotal trauma, testicular surgery, undescended testis, or renal cancer
- Note: if a child has had an orchidopexy (surgical testis fixation), testicular torsion is very unlikely

**Risk factors for testicular cancer (if relevant):**
- Cryptorchidism (undescended testis)
- Family history of testicular malignancy in a first-degree relative
- Klinefelter's syndrome
- Past history of testicular cancer

**In adolescents:**
- Sexual health history, including risk of sexually transmitted infection (STI)

**Physical examination** will typically include:
- Observation of gait, position, and level of discomfort
- Vital signs (temperature, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate)
- Abdominal examination
- Testicular examination (always performed — it is the most important investigation for these patients):
  - Swelling and asymmetry, including whether it extends into the groin
  - Testicular lie — a horizontal "bell-clapper" lie predisposes to torsion
  - Erythema — does it cross the midline or extend into the groin? (suggests idiopathic scrotal oedema)
  - Cremasteric reflex — absent in testicular torsion
  - "Blue dot" sign — a small blue area at the upper pole of the testis, seen in about 20% of torted appendages
  - Transillumination — cystic swellings (hydrocele, epididymal cyst) transmit light; solid tumours and hernias usually do not

## Possible Causes

### Testicular Torsion

- Most common in the neonatal period and between ages 10–25 years, but can occur at any age
- Usually sudden onset of severe pain of less than 12 hours' duration, often with nausea and vomiting
- Scrotum may be swollen and red on the affected side
- Testis may feel high-riding, lie transversely, or feel firmer than the normal side
- Cremasteric reflex is absent
- Urine examination is normal
- Intermittent torsion: short episodes of sudden severe pain (seconds to minutes) that resolve spontaneously, with horizontal lie on standing

### Torsion of Testicular Appendage

- More common in prepubertal children but can occur at any age
- Gradual onset of pain localised to the upper pole of the testis, moderate to severe
- Testis position and lie are symmetric; cremasteric reflex is usually present
- "Blue dot" sign may be visible in about 20% of cases
- Scrotum is usually normal but can become swollen and red over time

### Epididymo-orchitis

- Most common in teenagers (STI-related) and young infants
- Can also occur with urinary tract infection (UTI), viral infections (mumps, rubella, coxsackie, parvovirus), or Henoch-Schönlein purpura (HSP)
- Gradual onset of pain or swelling, often with fever, dysuria, urinary frequency, or urethral discharge
- Tender upper pole or diffuse tenderness; cremasteric reflex present
- Pain may be relieved by elevating the scrotum (Prehn's sign positive)

### Idiopathic Scrotal Oedema

- Usually seen in children aged 4–6 years
- Rapid onset of swelling — usually painless but may cause low-grade discomfort, sometimes itchy
- Oedema often spreads from the perineum up into the scrotum, groin, and shaft of penis; may be associated with puffiness around the eyes
- Self-limiting, resolves after a few days

### Mumps Orchitis

- Occurs in 15–20% of post-pubertal males with mumps (sterility is rare)
- Onset 5–10 days after parotitis
- Abrupt fever (39–41°C), severe testicular pain, swelling, and erythema of the scrotum

### Trauma, Haematoma, or Testicular Rupture

- History of significant trauma followed by variable onset of pain and swelling
- Bruising or haematoma may be present

### Irreducible Inguinal Hernia (with or without Strangulation)

- Fixed, firm, palpable mass in the inguinal canal, with or without scrotal involvement
- Suspect strangulation if: tender and irreducible hernia, skin changes (erythema, discolouration), child is unwell with abdominal distension or systemic signs

### Hydrocele

- Collection of fluid in the scrotum; in infants usually due to a patent processus vaginalis
- Generally painless unilateral swelling; testis is normal and non-tender
- Transilluminates brightly

### Hydrocele of the Spermatic Cord

- Firm, spherical mass palpable separately from the testis, which moves with testicular traction
- Can feel like a "third testicle"
- Transilluminates

### Varicocele

- Enlarged spermatic cord veins; more common in adolescence and predominantly left-sided
- Non-tender "bag of worms" swelling above the testis; more prominent when standing or with the Valsalva manoeuvre
- May cause a dull aching or "dragging" sensation

### Reducible Inguinal Hernia

- Intermittent or reducible mass over the inguinal canal or in the scrotum
- May appear with crying or straining; often reducible when lying down

### Epididymal Cyst (Spermatocele)

- Non-tender cyst at the head of the epididymis
- Transilluminates

### Tumour or Leukaemia (Rare)

- Unilateral, firm, solid scrotal swelling; leukaemia infiltration may be bilateral
- Does not transilluminate
- May be painful if rapidly enlarging

### Appendicitis

- Retrocaecal appendicitis can rarely present with acute scrotal pain

### Urolithiasis (Kidney Stone)

- Consider if referred abdominal or flank pain, urinary symptoms, nausea and vomiting, or blood on urine dipstick

## Management

Management depends on the identified or suspected cause. Many acute presentations require urgent hospital assessment — your doctor will not delay to arrange investigations if torsion or another surgical emergency is suspected.

**Your doctor will arrange urgent paediatric surgery assessment (same day, to hospital) for:**
- Acute scrotal pain with or without swelling
- Suspected testicular torsion
- Irreducible inguinal hernia with or without signs of strangulation or bowel obstruction
- Acute testicular trauma where the testis integrity cannot be confirmed on examination
- Suspected epididymo-orchitis in an infant or child younger than 12 years
- Suspected appendicitis, urolithiasis, or acute scrotal pain of unknown cause

Surgery for torsion should ideally be performed within 6 hours to prevent permanent damage.

**Suspected malignancy:** Your doctor will seek urgent specialist advice.

**For less urgent presentations, your doctor may consider:**

**Epididymo-orchitis in a patient aged 12 years or older:**
- If most likely due to an STI: ceftriaxone 500 mg intramuscular injection (single dose) and doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 14 days. Contact tracing and partner treatment are also initiated.
- If most likely due to a urinary pathogen: amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 625 mg orally three times daily for 10 days (first line for patients over 40 kg); trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole 960 mg twice daily for 10 days (second line). Treatment is adjusted based on urine culture results.

**Idiopathic scrotal oedema:**
- No specific treatment is usually needed — the condition is self-limiting and resolves after a few days
- Your doctor may recommend paracetamol or a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) for comfort, and antihistamines may also help

**Non-acute surgical referral:** Your doctor will arrange a non-urgent paediatric surgery assessment for:
- Hydrocele persisting after 18 months of age, rapidly enlarging acquired hydrocele, or hydrocele with atypical features
- Varicocele
- Non-acute scrotal mass, pain, or swelling of uncertain cause
- Epididymo-orchitis, for follow-up

**After any surgical intervention or repair**, your doctor will check wound healing and testis position at 2–3 weeks.
