# Cervical Polyps

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of cervical polyps found during pelvic examination.

---

## Background

Cervical polyps are common benign lesions of the cervix, found in about 4% of the gynaecological population. There are two main types:

- **Endocervical polyps** — most common in the fourth to sixth decade of life. They are cherry red, pedunculated lesions on a stalk of varying length, and may be single or multiple.
- **Cervical polyps** — usually single, smooth, grey-white lesions.

Most polyps are asymptomatic, but they may affect the quality of cervical screening and can be associated with abnormal vaginal bleeding. Malignancy is rare (less than 1 in 200).

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. **Take a history** — asking about symptoms such as bleeding, pain, or discharge, and taking a complete menstrual and gynaecological history, including previous cervical screening results.

2. **Perform a pelvic examination** — estimating the size of the polyp and noting its appearance:
   - Benign endocervical polyps are smooth, cherry red, and may be single or multiple on a stalk.
   - Benign cervical polyps are smooth, grey-white, and usually single.

3. **Take an HPV and cytology co-test** — a cervical liquid-based cytology (LBC) sample is required.

4. **Consider differential diagnoses** — such as a prolapsed endometrial polyp or a prolapsed fibroid.

5. **Arrange investigations if needed** — a pelvic ultrasound is not routinely required for simple cervical polyps, but your doctor may request one if the polyp appears to be coming from the uterus, or if simple twisting does not result in easy removal.

---

## Management

Your doctor will manage you based on the polyp's size, appearance, and any associated symptoms.

**If there is abnormal bleeding**, your doctor will first assess other possible causes — such as post-coital bleeding, abnormal uterine bleeding, or postmenopausal bleeding — before attributing it to the polyp alone.

**If the polyp has an abnormal appearance** (e.g., not smooth), your doctor will refer you for a gynaecology assessment.

**If the polyp is larger than 2 cm**, your doctor will refer you for a gynaecology assessment.

**If the polyp is 2 cm or smaller with a thin pedicle and visible base**, your doctor may manage this in primary care:

- You will be reassured that heavy bleeding is very uncommon after removal, and that the procedure is usually painless as polyps do not have a nerve supply.
- The polyp is removed by grasping with sponge forceps and twisting repeatedly until it separates from the cervix. Any minor bleeding is managed with pressure or silver nitrate cautery.
- The polyp is sent for histological examination (laboratory analysis).
- If removal is not straightforward, your doctor will stop and arrange a pelvic ultrasound and gynaecology referral.

**After successful removal**, you will be advised to avoid tampons and vaginal intercourse for 2 days.

**Follow-up after removal:**

- A repeat speculum examination and HPV and cytology co-test will be arranged **3 months after removal**, regardless of the result taken immediately before removal. This is because the polyp can block access to cervical cells, potentially giving a false negative result, and also to confirm the polyp has been completely removed.
- If you had abnormal bleeding before removal, a review will be arranged at 4 weeks. If bleeding continues, further investigation will follow.
- Recurrent polyps are managed in the same way, with histology and a repeat co-test at 3 months.

**If histology confirms malignancy**, your doctor will refer you urgently to gynaecology.
