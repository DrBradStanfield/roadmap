# Medications in Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers key principles for prescribing medicines during pregnancy and while breastfeeding. Managing medications in these periods requires careful weighing of benefit to the mother against potential risk to the baby. Always discuss any medication changes with your doctor.

---

## Prescribing in Pregnancy

### Key Principles

- Almost all medicines taken during pregnancy cross the placenta to some extent. Expected benefits to the mother must be carefully weighed against the risk of adverse effects on the fetus.
- Avoid all non-essential drug therapy; consider non-pharmacological options where possible.
- Consider alternative routes of administration that may reduce systemic absorption (e.g., topical application, nasal spray, inhaler, eye drops).
- Avoid polypharmacy.
- Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration possible.

### Timing of Risk

- **First trimester (weeks 3–11):** Greatest risk of teratogenesis — this is when organs are forming.
- **Second and third trimesters:** Drugs can affect growth or functional development of the fetus, or have toxic effects on fetal tissues.
- **Near term or during labour:** Drugs can have adverse effects on labour or on the newborn after delivery.

### Drugs With Proven Teratogenicity

Your doctor will avoid these in pregnancy where possible:

- ACE Inhibitors
- Alcohol
- Amiodarone
- Androgens
- Anticonvulsants (especially valproate sodium and topiramate)
- Carbimazole
- Cytotoxic agents
- Leflunomide
- Lithium
- Methotrexate
- Misoprostol
- Retinoids
- Tetracyclines
- Thalidomide
- Trimethoprim (first trimester)
- Warfarin

### Predictable Risks by Drug Class

- **NSAIDs (third trimester):** Can cause premature closure of the fetal ductus arteriosus.
- **Certain antihypertensives:** May reduce placental perfusion, potentially causing fetal hypoxia or restricted growth.
- **Opioids:** Can cause respiratory depression in the newborn due to in-utero exposure.
- **Beta-agonists and NSAIDs:** May delay delivery.
- **Misoprostol:** Can cause abortion.

### Pre-Conception Planning

Your doctor may consider reviewing your medications before you conceive:

- Identifying and eliminating non-essential medications
- Switching to medications with a more established safety record in pregnancy

## Prescribing While Breastfeeding

### Key Principles

- The advantages of breastfeeding generally outweigh the risks of most drugs.
- Avoid all non-essential drug therapy, including herbal products and high-dose vitamins.
- Consider both the effect of the medication on the infant and the effect on breastfeeding or milk production.
- Use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration possible.
- Most medications will cross into breast milk, but many factors affect how much the infant actually receives.

### Drugs to Avoid While Breastfeeding

Your doctor will generally avoid prescribing the following:

- Amiodarone
- Anti-neoplastics (chemotherapy agents)
- Ergotamine
- Methotrexate
- Lithium
- Opioids
- Radio-pharmaceuticals
- Retinoids

### Factors That Affect Infant Exposure

The amount of drug reaching the infant depends on:

- Concentration of the drug in maternal plasma
- Molecular weight of the drug
- Protein binding and lipid solubility
- Whether the baby is premature or a newborn (reduced clearance)
- Volume of milk intake
- Oral bioavailability of the drug in the infant

### Practical Advice for Breastfeeding Mothers

- Feed your baby or express milk just before taking your dose — this minimises the drug concentration in subsequent feeds.
- Monitor your baby for signs of adverse effects, including:
  - Poor suckling
  - Failure to thrive
  - Irritability
  - Gastrointestinal side-effects
  - Sedation
- Watch for changes in your milk production, especially in the first 2–4 days after starting a new medication.
- Extra caution is needed with premature babies and newborns.

---

## Management

Management depends on the clinical situation. Your doctor may consider:

- Choosing medications with the most established safety data in pregnancy or lactation
- Selecting alternative drug classes with a lower risk profile
- Monitoring maternal and fetal wellbeing when certain medications are necessary
- Seeking specialist pharmacist or obstetric advice for complex cases

Specific conditions are managed according to their own guidelines (e.g., asthma, epilepsy, hypertension, depression, nausea, and thyroid disease in pregnancy).
