# Chronic Cough in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway applies to adults with a subacute cough (3 to 8 weeks) or a persistent cough lasting more than 8 weeks.

> **Note:** This information is for educational purposes. Always discuss your symptoms and any treatment changes with your doctor or healthcare provider.

---

## Red Flags — See Your Doctor Urgently

Seek urgent medical assessment if you have:

- Coughing up blood (haemoptysis)
- Symptoms suggesting malignancy or tuberculosis

These require prompt investigation to rule out serious causes.

---

## What Causes Chronic Cough?

In adults with a non-productive cough and a normal chest X-ray, 95% of cases are due to one or more of:

- **Postnasal drip** from rhinorrhoea or sinus disease
- **Chronic bronchitis** due to eosinophilic inflammation (asthma) or neutrophilic inflammation (usually low-grade infection)
- **Gastro-oesophageal reflux (GORD)**
- **Medication side effects** (especially ACE inhibitors)
- A **combination** of the above

---

## How Your Doctor Assesses Chronic Cough

### History Your Doctor Will Take

**About the cough itself:**
- Onset — more than 75% of chronic coughs begin after a viral upper respiratory tract infection (URTI)
- Triggers, work environment, upper airway symptoms
- Sputum production, any aspiration, reflux symptoms
- Symptoms suspicious of malignancy (e.g., coughing up blood, voice change in a smoker or ex-smoker)

**Medications:**
- Current or recent ACE inhibitor use (e.g., lisinopril, ramipril, perindopril)

**Other relevant history:**
- Childhood asthma or bronchitis
- Smoking history
- Contact with infectious illness; immunisation status (e.g., whooping cough)
- Snoring and sleep apnoea — these cause airway drying and increased reflux
- Previous cough treatments and response: dose, duration, technique, compliance (most patients need a 6–8 week trial to judge effectiveness)

---

### Common Causes Your Doctor Will Consider

#### Post-infective Cough

A viral, bacterial, or fungal infection can trigger chronic cough through:

- Exacerbation of asthma
- Precipitation of non-asthmatic eosinophilic bronchitis
- Precipitation of vocal cord dysfunction
- A cough-reflux-cough cycle

Your doctor will assess for other contributing causes even if the cough started after an infection.

#### Rhinosinusitis (Postnasal Drip)

Signs suggesting rhinosinusitis:
- Nasal discharge or congestion
- Postnasal drip
- Rhinorrhoea
- Sneezing
- Facial pain
- History of sinus problems

Post-infectious cough is often prolonged by unrecognised rhinosinusitis.

#### Asthma / Eosinophilic Bronchitis

Signs suggesting asthma:
- Nocturnal cough
- Chest tightness
- Shortness of breath
- Wheeze, worsened by exercise or other triggers
- Personal or family history of atopy or hay fever

#### Gastro-oesophageal Reflux (GORD)

Signs suggesting GORD:
- Coughing or choking during sleep
- Cough with eating or talking
- Acid or bile regurgitation (sour or bitter taste) at night

Important: up to 70% of people with GORD-related cough have no heartburn symptoms.

#### ACE Inhibitor-Related Cough

- ACE inhibitors (used for blood pressure or heart failure) can cause or worsen cough
- The cough can take up to 3 months to resolve after stopping the medication
- ARB medications (e.g., candesartan) are less likely to cause cough but it is still a reported side effect

#### Other Causes

- **Snoring and sleep apnoea** — cause airway drying and worsen reflux
- **Micro-aspiration from poor dental hygiene** — particularly in older adults; poor oral hygiene and tooth disease is a significant risk factor for aspiration-related cough and pneumonia

### Lung Cancer: When Your Doctor Considers It

Your doctor will specifically assess for lung cancer risk if you have a cough lasting ≥ 3 weeks plus any risk factors.

**Lung cancer risk factors:**
- Current or past smoking history
- Occupational exposure to asbestos
- Personal history of any cancer (including previous lung or head and neck cancer)
- First-degree family member with lung cancer
- Pre-existing lung disease (COPD, interstitial lung disease, or tuberculosis)
- Passive smoking
- Heavy marijuana use
- Long-term air pollution exposure
- Age over 40 years
- Māori or Pacific ethnicity

**Symptoms that may suggest lung cancer:**
- Unexplained or persistent cough (> 3 weeks)
- Coughing up blood
- Chest or shoulder pain
- Shortness of breath
- Hoarse voice
- Weight loss > 10%
- Fatigue
- Unresolved chest infection
- Symptoms suggesting spread (e.g., to liver, bone, brain, or skin)
- Blood clots (venous thromboembolism)

---

### Investigations Your Doctor May Order

**Blood tests:**
- Full blood count (FBC), including **eosinophil count**
  - A higher eosinophil count (e.g., 0.5 vs 0.2) increases the likelihood of eosinophilic airway disease
- Total IgE
- CRP (inflammation marker)
- NT-proBNP (if heart failure is a possibility)
  - A level below 35 pmol/L makes heart failure unlikely
  - Elevated levels warrant further cardiac assessment

**Chest X-ray** (posteroanterior and lateral views), if you have:
- Lung cancer risk factors and cough lasting ≥ 3 weeks
- Coughing up blood (non-emergency) once local or minor causes excluded
- Not responded to 6–8 weeks of treatment

**Spirometry** — to assess lung function

**Sputum culture** — for productive cough; helps identify infection

**Mycobacterial culture** — if tuberculosis is suspected based on chest X-ray

## Management

> **Important:** Most treatments take up to 2 months to work. Regular follow-up with your doctor is essential to check whether your cough is resolving and to rule out underlying persistent conditions such as lung cancer.

If you have blood in your sputum, or tuberculosis or cancer is suspected, your doctor will follow a specific urgent pathway.

---

### If Your Cough Is Productive (Bringing Up Phlegm)

Your doctor will look for and treat primary lung conditions such as:
- Bronchiectasis
- COPD
- Smoker's cough (smoking-related chronic bronchitis)
- Tuberculosis

Additional causes to address:
- **Rhinosinusitis** — treatment of sinus disease often resolves the cough
- **Poor dental hygiene and micro-aspiration** — careful oral care, removal of food debris after meals, and antiseptic mouthwash may reduce aspiration-related cough, particularly in older adults
- **Long COVID** — cough is a recognised feature of post-COVID illness

---

### If Your Cough Is Non-Productive or Minimally Productive

**Step 1: Eliminate aggravating factors**
- If you smoke or have a hoarse voice (especially if a smoker or ex-smoker), your doctor will address smoking and voice change first
- If you are on an ACE inhibitor, your doctor may switch you to an ARB instead — note that ACE-related cough can take 3 months to fully resolve after stopping

**Step 2: Treat the likely underlying cause**

**Asthma or eosinophilic bronchitis:**
- Blood eosinophil count ≥ 0.2 (especially with elevated IgE or history of atopy) suggests asthma
- Your doctor may trial inhaled or oral steroids
- Eosinophil count < 0.2 may suggest neutrophilic bronchitis (low-grade bacterial inflammation), which is managed differently

**Gastro-oesophageal reflux:**
- High-dose proton pump inhibitor (PPI) is often needed — e.g., omeprazole 40 mg twice daily for 3 months
  - Not suitable in nephritis; kidney function is checked first
- PPIs do not stop volume reflux; your doctor may also recommend:
  - Sleeping with the head of the bed elevated
  - Avoiding food and drink for 3 hours before lying down
  - Adding a prokinetic agent (e.g., metoclopramide)
  - Adding Gaviscon at night
- Treatment should be continued for at least 1 month even if symptoms settle quickly
- Do not consider a reflux trial unsuccessful unless it has run for at least 3 months

**Other treatable causes:**
- Sleep apnoea
- Pertussis (whooping cough)
- Rhinosinusitis
- Long COVID

**Step 3: Symptom relief while awaiting treatment response**
- Over-the-counter cough medicines are generally not effective (do not contain cough-suppressing doses)
- Your doctor may consider **codeine phosphate 15 mg**, one to two tablets, up to 3 times a day or at night, for symptom relief

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will refer you to a respiratory specialist if:

- There is diagnostic uncertainty about the cause of your cough
- Your cough persists despite adequate treatment, and both chest X-ray and spirometry are normal

These situations require a **non-acute respiratory assessment** to look for less common causes.
