# Smoking Cessation Advice

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Stopping smoking is one of the most important health changes you can make. This pathway outlines how your doctor will approach smoking cessation, the medications that may be considered, and how you can access support.

---

## Background

- Stopping smoking is one of the most important health changes a person can make.
- The ABC approach — **Ask, Brief advice, Cessation support** — is the recommended format for healthcare workers to use.
- The best results come from combining specialist behavioural support with stop-smoking medication.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about your smoking status:

- **Currently smoking** — smoked in the last 28 days (vaping not included) and smoked more than 100 cigarettes in your lifetime
- **Ex-smoker** — smoked more than 100 cigarettes in your lifetime but not smoked any tobacco in the last 28 days
- **Never smoked** — smoked fewer than 100 cigarettes in your lifetime

Your doctor will ask how soon after waking you smoke your first cigarette and how many cigarettes you smoke per day — this helps determine your level of nicotine dependence and guides medication dosing.

---

## Management

### Personalised Advice

Your doctor will provide brief, personalised advice to stop smoking — for example, related to your health or finances. A [quit calculator](https://www.smokefree.org.nz/smoking-its-effects/cost-of-smoking/cost-of-smoking-calculator) can estimate potential financial savings from quitting.

### Cessation Support

Your doctor will strongly encourage you to use cessation support and help you access it. Options include:

- Free telephone, text, and online support through the [Quit Programme](https://quit.org.nz/) — a 3-month programme including telephone, online, and text support
- Pharmacists can provide funded nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) without a prescription at some pharmacies

If your practice has capacity, your doctor may arrange a minimum of 4 follow-up sessions for the best chance of cessation.

### Special Considerations

#### Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

- Continuing to smoke during pregnancy is a major risk factor for Sudden Unexpected Death in Infancy (SUDI).
- Your doctor will strongly recommend accessing a stop-smoking service if you are pregnant.
- NRT products can be used during pregnancy or breastfeeding if you would otherwise continue to smoke. Your doctor will do a risk-benefit assessment — NRT is safer than smoking if you cannot quit without it. If using a patch during pregnancy, remove it overnight.
- Postnatal relapse is common in women who stop smoking during pregnancy. Referral to a stop-smoking service around the time of birth may help.

#### Mental Health and Smoking

- Smokers with mental illness are just as likely to want to stop smoking as other smokers.
- Quitting smoking will not interfere with mental health recovery. Evidence consistently shows that stopping smoking is associated with improvements in depression, anxiety, stress, and psychological quality of life.
- If you are on antipsychotic medications, your doctor may need to adjust your dose while you are quitting, as some antipsychotic drug levels increase with smoking cessation. Discuss this with your doctor.

#### Weight Gain

- Weight gain occurs in about 75% of people who quit, with an average gain of around 7 kg.
- Increased appetite may last 2–3 months.
- Some stop-smoking medications can help delay weight gain (oral NRT such as gum, lozenge, inhalator, or oral spray; and bupropion or varenicline).
- Your doctor may refer you for healthy eating and physical activity support.

#### Withdrawal Symptoms

Not all symptoms affect all people, and most resolve within 4 weeks. Symptoms can be eased with stop-smoking medicines and behavioural strategies.

| Symptom | Typical Duration |
| --- | --- |
| Irritability | Less than 4 weeks |
| Depression | Less than 4 weeks |
| Restlessness | Less than 4 weeks |
| Poor concentration | Less than 2 weeks |
| Sleep disturbances | Less than 1 week |
| Increased appetite | More than 10 weeks |
| Urge to smoke | More than 2 weeks |
| Mouth ulcers | More than 4 weeks |
| Constipation | More than 4 weeks |

## Medications Your Doctor May Consider

### Nicotine Replacement Therapy (NRT)

NRT provides some of the nicotine you would receive from tobacco, reducing the urge to smoke and the severity of withdrawal symptoms. It roughly doubles the rate of quitting long-term. Using multiple NRT products together is more effective than using one alone.

**Products available:**
- Patches, lozenges, gum, inhalator, oral spray (fast-acting), Cooldrops
- All are equally effective but provide different types of relief — patches relieve background craving, while gum or lozenges can relieve acute urges
- Subsidised options include Habitrol patches, gum, and lozenges. Inhalator, QuickMist spray, and Cooldrops are not subsidised but may be accessible through local smokefree teams

**Dosage:** Your doctor will determine the right dose based on how soon after waking you smoke your first cigarette and how many cigarettes you smoke per day. Advise using NRT for at least 8 weeks, continuing beyond 12 weeks if needed.

**Cautions (discuss with your doctor):**
- Generally not recommended under age 12
- Use with caution if you have nicotine hypersensitivity, Prinzmetal-variant angina, recent heart attack (within 3 months), or certain cardiac arrhythmias
- During pregnancy, your doctor will do a risk-benefit assessment

**Possible side effects:**
- Rash at patch site — change patch location daily
- Hiccups and upset stomach with gum — use the chew-suck-park method (chew until taste is strong, park between cheek and gum, repeat)
- Throat irritation with inhalator

**Signs of too much nicotine (overdose):** Nausea (main symptom), vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, dizziness, headache, confusion, flushing, or fast heart rate. Contact your doctor if this occurs.

---

### Varenicline (Champix)

Varenicline was developed specifically for smoking cessation. It acts on nicotine receptors to reduce the "reward" associated with smoking and is more effective than bupropion, nortriptyline, or single-product NRT.

**How your doctor may prescribe it:**
- Start varenicline while still smoking, with an aim to stop smoking between weeks 1 and 4 of treatment
- Take at least 1 week before your target quit date (flexibility: stop smoking between days 8–35 of treatment)
- Take with food and water, not too close to bedtime

**Dosage:**
- 0.5 mg once daily for 3 days → 0.5 mg twice daily for 4 days → 1 mg twice daily from day 8
- Reduce to 0.5 mg twice daily if not tolerated
- Continue for a minimum of 12 weeks

**Funding:** Subsidised on Special Authority in New Zealand. Criteria include: readiness to stop smoking, enrolment in a comprehensive support programme, and two unsuccessful trials of NRT (one with a support programme) or a failed trial of bupropion or nortriptyline. Not funded more than once every 6 months.

**Cautions (discuss with your doctor):**
- Not recommended during pregnancy or breastfeeding, or under age 18
- Use with caution if you have a psychiatric disorder or predisposition to seizures

**Possible side effects:** Nausea, headache, insomnia, abnormal dreams. More serious side effects (depression, suicidal ideation) have been reported historically; recent RCT evidence does not show increased risk of moderate-to-severe neuropsychiatric events compared to other quit-smoking medications. Discuss any concerns with your doctor.

---

### Bupropion (Zyban)

Bupropion is a fully subsidised antidepressant that is effective in reducing nicotine withdrawal and alleviating cravings. It is as effective as NRT and nortriptyline.

**How your doctor may prescribe it:**
- Start while still smoking, with quit date set for 8–14 days later
- 150 mg once daily for 3 days, then 150 mg twice daily (at least 8 hours between doses)
- Treat for a minimum of 7 weeks; can extend beyond 12 weeks if needed

**Cautions (discuss with your doctor):**
- Not recommended if you have a seizure disorder, are withdrawing from alcohol or benzodiazepines, have taken MAOIs within the past 14 days, have a CNS tumour, or have a history of bulimia or anorexia nervosa
- Not recommended under age 18, during pregnancy or breastfeeding
- Use with caution if you have psychiatric disorders, liver or kidney disease, alcohol abuse, or diabetes

**Possible side effects:** Dry mouth, insomnia, headache, rash. Seizures are reported in approximately 1 in 1000 people.

**Note:** Your doctor may consider combining bupropion and NRT if you are struggling with either alone — this can increase success without significant interactions.

---

### Nortriptyline

Nortriptyline is a tricyclic antidepressant found to be effective in aiding smoking cessation. It reduces the severity of withdrawal symptoms and roughly doubles the chances of stopping smoking. It works independently of its antidepressant effect.

**How your doctor may prescribe it:**
- Start while still smoking, with quit date set for 10–28 days later
- Start at 25 mg per day, increasing gradually over 10 days to 5 weeks to 75–100 mg, two to four weeks before quit date
- Continue for 3–6 months; taper dose slowly when stopping

**Cautions (discuss with your doctor):**
- Not recommended if you have taken MAOIs within the last 14 days, had a recent heart attack, are in the manic phase of bipolar disorder, or have arrhythmias or risk of prolonged QT interval
- Not recommended under age 18, during pregnancy or breastfeeding
- Use with caution if you have hyperthyroidism, epilepsy, glaucoma, or risk of serotonin syndrome

**Possible side effects:** Drowsiness and dry mouth.

---

### Vaping (E-cigarettes)

- Vaping devices work by heating a liquid (with or without nicotine) to produce an aerosol to inhale.
- Emerging evidence suggests e-cigarettes with nicotine may be more effective for smoking cessation than NRT when both are accompanied by behavioural support. However, vaping is not currently a Ministry of Health-approved product for smoking cessation — your doctor will continue to recommend and prescribe approved products and refer to cessation support.
- If you smoke and are considering vaping, discuss it with your doctor. Switching completely from smoking to vaping may reduce your health risks compared to continued smoking. The nicotine content of the vaping solution should then be progressively lowered, and vaping also stopped over time.
- Non-smokers — particularly young people — are strongly advised not to start vaping, as it is likely associated with some degree of harm and carries a risk of nicotine addiction.
