# Genital Skin Lesions

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways (Topic Code: 501297) and cross-referenced Genital Dermatology pathway*

Auckland Regional Community HealthPathways does not currently have a full local pathway for genital skin lesions. The primary reference is the **New Zealand Sexual Health Society (NZSHS) — Genital Skin Lesions Management Summary**.

The information below is drawn from the Auckland Genital Dermatology pathway, which cross-references genital skin lesions extensively.

> **Note:** Genital skin lesions require clinical examination to diagnose. This information is for general understanding only. Always discuss any new or changing genital skin lesion with your doctor.

---

## Why Assessment Matters

Genital skin lesions are common and most are benign or infectious in origin, but some can indicate sexually transmitted infections (STIs) or, less commonly, pre-cancerous or cancerous change. Early assessment is important because:

- Appearance alone cannot reliably distinguish benign from malignant lesions
- Some STIs (e.g., syphilis, herpes) can present as genital lesions
- Immunocompromised patients and those with HIV are at higher risk of atypical or malignant presentations
- Pre-malignant lesions (VIN, PeIN, AIN) can be treated to prevent cancer progression

## Types of Genital Skin Lesions

### Non-Ulcerative Lesions (Lumps, Bumps, Plaques)

**In females — common:**
- Normal anatomical variants: Fordyce spots, vestibular papillae (uniform, symmetrical tubular projections)
- Minor skin problems: milia, inflamed papules (sebaceous adenitis)
- Genital warts (HPV) — cauliflower-like or flat papules
- Molluscum contagiosum — pearly, umbilicated papules
- Bartholin's or Skene's cyst

**In females — less common (requires assessment):**
- Vulval intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN)
- Anal epithelial neoplasia (AIN)
- Genital squamous cell carcinoma
- Mpox — painful pustules or ulcers

**In males — common:**
- Pearly penile papules (coronal papillae) — soft, pearly, dome-shaped papules around the corona; normal variant
- Fordyce spots / Tyson glands (foreskin) — yellow or white smooth papules; normal variant
- Boil/folliculitis
- Genital warts, molluscum contagiosum

**In males — less common (requires assessment):**
- Peyronie's disease — fibrous band causing contracture deformity on erection
- Penile intraepithelial neoplasia (PeIN)
- Penile or anal cancer
- Lichen planus
- Lymphogranuloma venereum, donovanosis
- Mpox

### Ulcerative Lesions

Genital ulcers most commonly have an infectious cause:

- **Genital herpes (HSV)** — most common cause; painful vesicles that rupture to form ulcers
- **Syphilis** — primary chancre is typically a painless, indurated ulcer; secondary syphilis can cause skin rashes including psoriasis-like genital lesions
- **Chancroid** — painful ulcer with soft base (less common in NZ)
- **Lymphogranuloma venereum (LGV)** — small painless ulcer followed by inguinal lymphadenopathy
- **Donovanosis** — progressive, non-painful, beefy-red ulcers (rare in NZ)
- **Mpox** — painful papules/pustules that may ulcerate

Non-infectious causes of genital ulcers include:
- Aphthous ulcers
- Behçet's disease
- Crohn's disease
- Trauma
- Fixed drug eruption
- Herpes zoster (shingles)

## When to See Your Doctor

See your doctor for assessment of any genital skin lesion, especially if:

- The lesion is new, growing, or changing
- It is painful, bleeding, or not healing
- You have had unprotected sex or a new sexual partner recently
- You are immunocompromised or have HIV
- The lesion has not responded to any treatment after 4–6 weeks
- You have systemic symptoms (fever, rash elsewhere, swollen glands)

### Seek Urgent Assessment If:

- A firm, persistent ulcer or lump that is not healing (possible malignancy or syphilis)
- Severe pain, extensive ulceration, or spreading lesions
- Suspected STI requiring treatment to prevent transmission

## What Your Doctor Will Do

Your doctor will:

1. Take a full history including sexual history, symptom timeline, and any previous episodes
2. Examine the lesion — including size, borders, consistency, associated lymph nodes
3. Consider STI testing as appropriate (swabs, blood tests)
4. Arrange biopsy if diagnosis is uncertain or malignancy is suspected
5. Treat the identified cause or refer to a specialist

**Swabs:** Used to test for herpes virus (PCR swab), bacteria (MCS swab), and other infections.

**Blood tests:** May be arranged for syphilis (TPHA/RPR), HIV, and in some cases other blood tests depending on clinical suspicion.

**Biopsy:** Considered if lesion appearance is atypical, does not respond to treatment, or there is clinical concern about malignancy (VIN, PeIN, AIN, SCC, EMPD).

## Common Treatments

Treatment depends entirely on the cause:

| Condition | Treatment approach |
|---|---|
| Genital warts (HPV) | Topical treatments (imiquimod, podophyllotoxin), cryotherapy, or excision |
| Molluscum contagiosum | Often self-resolving; cryotherapy or curettage if needed |
| Genital herpes | Antiviral medication (valaciclovir) |
| Syphilis | Benzathine penicillin (or doxycycline if penicillin allergic) |
| Bacterial ulcers | Antibiotics directed by organism |
| VIN, PeIN, AIN | Specialist management — excision, laser, or topical treatments |
| Penile/vulval/anal cancer | Urgent specialist surgical/oncology assessment |
| Benign/normal variants | Reassurance; no treatment needed |

## Referral

Your doctor may refer you to:

- **Sexual health service** — for STI-related lesions or complex sexual health needs
- **Dermatology** — for atypical or treatment-resistant skin conditions
- **Gynaecology** — for suspected VIN or vulval cancer
- **Urology** — for suspected PeIN or penile cancer
- **General surgery / colorectal surgery** — for suspected anal cancer

Suspected cancer referrals are marked as high priority.
