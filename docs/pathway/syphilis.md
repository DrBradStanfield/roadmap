# Syphilis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers testing for syphilis, interpreting serology, and managing confirmed or suspected infection. Syphilis is complex — serology can be difficult to interpret and management should be led by sexual health specialists.

> Note: This pathway uses the terms male, female, men, and women in a cis-gendered way to refer to genital anatomy. It recognises that gender identity may not match the sex that a person was assigned at birth.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Advice

The following require urgent medical assessment:

- **Syphilis during pregnancy** — urgent action needed; risk of congenital syphilis
- **Suspected neurosyphilis** — syphilis affecting the nervous system; can occur at any stage

---

## Background

Syphilis is a bacterial STI caused by *Treponema pallidum*. It is increasing in many countries, including in heterosexual men, young women, and pregnant women. It can mimic many other conditions, may be asymptomatic, and has significant complications if untreated.

---

## When to Test

Your doctor will offer syphilis testing if you:

- Are at increased risk of a sexually transmitted infection (STI)
- Have signs or symptoms that could be syphilis
- Are a sexual contact of someone with syphilis
- Are pregnant (routine antenatal screening; rescreening in the third trimester recommended)
- Are undergoing immigration health testing
- Are having a routine sexual health check

**Have a low threshold for testing** — early syphilis can have variable or absent symptoms.

---

## Stages of Infection

Syphilis progresses through stages if untreated. Your doctor will assess which stage applies:

### Primary Syphilis
- Typically presents as a painless ulcer (chancre) at the site of infection
- Spontaneously resolves but infection persists without treatment

### Secondary Syphilis
- Occurs weeks to months after primary infection
- Can cause rash (often on palms and soles), flu-like illness, mucous patches, condylomata lata
- May mimic HIV seroconversion, drug rashes, or dermatological conditions

### Early Latent Syphilis (less than 2 years)
- No symptoms, but infection is present
- Diagnosed on serology; still infectious

### Late Latent Syphilis (more than 2 years)
- No symptoms
- Less infectious but complications possible

### Tertiary Syphilis
- Rare with modern treatment
- Can affect the heart, blood vessels, skin, and bones

### Neurosyphilis
- Can occur at **any stage** of syphilis
- Symptoms include headache, neck stiffness, confusion, vision or hearing changes, weakness, or psychiatric symptoms

---

## Investigations

Your doctor will arrange:

- **Syphilis serology** — blood test required for all patients at risk or with clinical suspicion
- **Treponema pallidum PCR swab** — from any suspicious lesion (ulcer, rash)
- **Herpes simplex virus (HSV) PCR swab** — taken at the same time if lesions present, as HSV can co-exist
- **Other STI screening** — syphilis rarely occurs alone; full sexual health check is recommended

**Important:** Syphilis serology can be difficult to interpret. A positive or reactive result — or a strong clinical suspicion with negative serology — should always trigger specialist review.

---

## Management

> **This requires specialist input.** Syphilis is managed by sexual health services. Treatment in the community is only considered when specialist management is not possible, and only after discussion with a sexual health specialist. Always discuss with your doctor.

### Neurosyphilis — Urgent

If neurosyphilis is suspected:

- With **ocular symptoms** (vision changes): requires urgent ophthalmology review
- Without ocular symptoms: requires urgent infectious disease review

### Syphilis in Pregnancy — Urgent

All pregnant patients with reactive syphilis serology need urgent action:

- The lead maternity carer (LMC) must be informed
- Obstetric review is needed
- If **≥ 20 weeks gestation**: may need hospital admission and inpatient treatment
- If **< 20 weeks gestation**: urgent sexual health specialist advice required
- See NZSHS (New Zealand Sexual Health Society) Syphilis in Pregnancy guidelines

### Treatment (Community Setting — Only After Specialist Advice)

If treatment must be initiated in the community (after specialist discussion):

- Blood is collected at the time of treatment for baseline syphilis serology
- Patients are warned about the **Jarisch-Herxheimer reaction** — a febrile reaction that can occur within 24 hours of treatment, especially in early syphilis; usually self-limiting
- **Antibiotic treatment** is prescribed according to the stage of infection (typically intramuscular benzathine penicillin; alternatives exist for penicillin allergy — discuss with specialist)
- Syphilis is a **notifiable disease** — a notifiable infection form must be completed

### Preventing Reinfection and Further Transmission

- Advice on sexual contact during treatment
- **Contact tracing** is essential — sexual health services will arrange this; all recent sexual contacts need assessment and possible empiric treatment
- Manage sexual contacts appropriately
- Review in approximately 1 week

### Doxy-PEP (Post-Exposure Prophylaxis)

Doxycycline post-exposure prophylaxis (doxy-PEP) may be considered for gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men, or transgender women — discuss with your doctor or sexual health clinic.

### Follow-up Serology

Follow-up blood tests are needed to confirm treatment success:

- **Infectious syphilis** (primary, secondary, early latent): serology at 1, 3, 6, and 12 months
- **Late latent and tertiary syphilis**: serology at 6 and 12 months
- If serology does not decline appropriately, this may indicate treatment failure or reinfection — specialist review is needed

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor will refer to sexual health services or seek specialist advice if:

- Newly reactive syphilis serology (any new positive result)
- Any uncertainty about how to interpret serology results
- Clinical suspicion of syphilis but serology is negative
- Considering a T. pallidum PCR swab from a lesion
- Penicillin allergy or contraindication when antibiotics are needed
- Follow-up serology suggests treatment failure or reinfection
- Contact tracing has not been arranged

---

## Key Points for Patients

- Syphilis is curable with antibiotics — early diagnosis and treatment prevents serious complications
- You may not have any symptoms — testing is the only way to know
- All sexual partners from the relevant time period need to be tested and possibly treated
- Avoid sexual contact until you and your partner(s) have been assessed and treated
- After treatment, repeat blood tests are needed to confirm the infection has cleared
- Syphilis does not confer immunity — reinfection is possible
