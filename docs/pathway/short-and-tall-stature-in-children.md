# Short and Tall Stature in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment of children who are significantly shorter or taller than expected, including when further investigation or specialist referral is needed.

---

## Red Flags — See Your Doctor Promptly

The following features warrant prompt medical assessment:

- Very short or very tall stature
- Abnormal growth rate (growing too quickly or too slowly)
- Dysmorphic features (unusual physical characteristics)
- Early or late puberty

---

## Background

- A child's height depends on their genetic potential as well as general health and nutrition.
- The most common causes of short and tall stature are normal variants — familial short or tall stature, and constitutional delay or advancement of growth and development.
- Illnesses that can be hidden, like coeliac disease, poor nutrition, or being small or premature at birth, may mean children do not reach their full potential adult height.
- Although height under the 3rd percentile is an indication that growth may need closer examination, most children in this range will not have underlying disease.
- A poor growth velocity (defined as being below the 25th percentile over a minimum of 6 months) is the most sensitive indicator of an underlying growth problem. Any adverse event can cause reduced growth velocity — including psychological stress, malnutrition, or occult illness.
- A poor growth velocity is non-specific and requires thorough investigation.
- Growth velocity can be difficult to interpret in late childhood due to the variable onset of puberty, which causes growth acceleration.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your child by taking a history, performing an examination, and arranging relevant tests.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Gestational age at birth
- Birth weight
- Current medications
- Height compared to peers
- Family history, including timing of puberty in parents and parental heights

### Examination

Your doctor will:

- Measure height (using a stadiometer if available) and plot it on a growth chart appropriate for your child's age and sex
- Compare your child's height against their parents' heights using the **midparental height** calculation:
  - Boy: (father's height + mother's height + 13) ÷ 2 cm
  - Girl: (father's height − 13 + mother's height) ÷ 2 cm
  - Most children will fall within 8 cm of the midparental height when their growth is complete
- Calculate and plot height velocity (cm per year) if previous measurements are available — at least a 6-month interval between measurements is needed to account for seasonal growth variation
- Assess pubertal development using Tanner staging
- Look for dysmorphic features
- Examine the optic fundi and assess visual fields

### Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- **Chronic illness screen:** full blood count (FBC), renal function, CRP, coeliac antibodies, calcium, phosphate, liver function tests (LFTs), urinalysis
- **Hormonal screen:** thyroid function tests (free T4 and TSH)

## Management

### When specialist referral is recommended

Your doctor will consider referring your child to a paediatric endocrinology service if your child:

- Is very short — particularly if below the 3rd percentile (referral is considered from the 5th percentile)
- Is very tall — particularly if above the 97th percentile
- Is growing too quickly or too slowly compared with other children the same age, or is crossing percentiles
- Has abnormal growth with dysmorphic features
- Is experiencing early or late puberty:
  - Girls: puberty before age 8 or after age 13
  - Boys: puberty before age 9 or after age 14

### If referral is not initially needed

Your doctor will reassess your child in 6 months using the same measuring equipment. If your child is crossing centiles at that point, specialist referral will be arranged.

### What to expect at a specialist appointment

If your child is referred, it helps to bring their Plunket book and any other growth measurements you have. The specialist will typically carry out:

- A detailed clinical history
- Examination including pubertal staging
- In some cases, a bone age X-ray

Further investigations may follow at subsequent appointments, including:

- Blood tests
- Further radiology (e.g. X-rays, pelvic ultrasound, brain MRI)
- Dynamic testing (e.g. growth hormone stimulation testing)

Follow-up is usually every 3 to 6 months to assess height velocity. It may take some months to diagnose or exclude any underlying condition. Treatment, if needed, depends on the cause — for example, growth hormone treatment.
