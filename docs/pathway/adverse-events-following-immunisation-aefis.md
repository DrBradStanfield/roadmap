# Adverse Events Following Immunisation (AEFIs)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of adverse events following immunisation — any undesirable event that occurs after vaccination, whether or not it is caused by the vaccine.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

If **anaphylaxis** is suspected, do not delay treatment. Signs include:

- Sudden onset of hives, swelling, or flushing after vaccination
- Difficulty breathing, wheezing, or stridor
- Collapse, loss of consciousness, or rapidly falling blood pressure
- Severe vomiting combined with sudden pallor

Anaphylaxis requires immediate treatment — call emergency services.

---

## Background

An **adverse event following immunisation (AEFI)** is any undesirable event that occurs after vaccination. The definition is deliberately broad to encourage reporting.

- **Adverse events** may or may not be caused by the vaccine
- **Adverse reactions** are undesirable effects directly caused by the vaccine

There are five categories of AEFI:

| Type | Explanation |
|------|-------------|
| Vaccine product-related | Event caused by the inherent properties of the vaccine when given correctly |
| Programme error | Event caused by an error in vaccine preparation, handling, or administration |
| Stress-related | Event from anxiety about, or pain from, the injection itself (not the vaccine) |
| Coincidental | Event that happens after immunisation but is not caused by it — a chance association |
| Unknown | The cause cannot be determined |

It may not always be possible to determine the cause with certainty.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will determine the type of event that occurred following vaccination.

### Fainting (Syncope)

- The most common adverse event after vaccination
- Defined as sudden pallor, loss of consciousness and slumping, with a strong central pulse, recovering within 1–2 minutes
- Fainting is a stress response to vaccination
- **Important:** In infants and babies, sudden loss of consciousness with limpness, pallor, and vomiting is not typical fainting — presume anaphylaxis or a hypotonic hyporesponsive episode (HHE) and manage accordingly

### Hypotonic Hyporesponsive Episode (HHE)

- Occurs in children aged under 2 years
- Defined as sudden onset of reduced sensory awareness or loss of consciousness, with pallor or cyanosis and muscle limpness (hypotonicity)
- Most commonly occurs 3–4 hours after the first infant vaccinations, but can develop up to 48 hours later
- Typically lasts less than 30 minutes
- No tests help confirm the diagnosis of HHE
- HHE is **not** a contraindication to future vaccination — children can receive the next dose in general practice
- HHE occurs in approximately 5–8 per 10,000 patients following whole-cell pertussis immunisation; much less frequently with the acellular vaccine (used in NZ)

Atypical features that require further medical assessment include:
- Seizure-like movements during the episode
- Becoming more unwell than expected
- Episode lasting longer than 30 minutes
- Onset more than 48 hours after vaccination

### Other Events to Assess

- Anxiety or breath-holding episodes (stress-related)
- Seizures or febrile convulsions
- Local injection-site reactions (pain, swelling, redness)
- Systemic symptoms (fever, loss of appetite, feeling unwell)

### Common Vaccine Responses

Usually occur within 1–2 days of vaccination:
- Local reaction at injection site — pain, swelling, lump, redness; typically lasts 1–2 days
- Systemic symptoms — fever and non-specific symptoms such as feeling "off colour" or reduced appetite

For **live attenuated vaccines** (e.g. MMR), responses can occur 6 days to 3 weeks after vaccination and may resemble a mild form of the disease.

### Rare Vaccine Responses

- Rare responses can be specific to each vaccine
- Most serious rare responses (e.g. seizures, thrombocytopenia, HHE, persistent inconsolable screaming) do not lead to long-term problems
- Anaphylaxis, while potentially fatal, is treatable without leaving long-term effects when managed promptly

## Management

### Anaphylaxis

Immediate treatment is required. Your doctor will manage this as an emergency.

### Reporting to CARM

Your doctor is encouraged to report any concerning or serious event following immunisation to the **Centre for Adverse Reactions Monitoring (CARM)**. The following events should be reported if they occur within the specified timeframe:

| Timeframe | Events to Report |
|-----------|-----------------|
| Within 24 hours | Anaphylaxis; persistent inconsolable screaming (more than 3 hours); hypotonic hyporesponsive episode (HHE); fever greater than 40°C |
| Within 5 days | Severe local reaction; sepsis; injection-site abscess |
| Within 12 days | Seizures (including febrile seizures); encephalopathy |
| Within 3 months | Acute flaccid paralysis including Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS); brachial neuritis (usually 2–28 days after tetanus-containing vaccine); myocarditis or pericarditis after Pfizer mRNA vaccine (median onset 2 days, range 0–98 days); thrombocytopenia (usually 15–35 days after MMR); thrombosis with thrombocytopenia syndrome (TTS) up to 42 days after COVID-19 AZ viral vector vaccine |
| 1–12 months after BCG | Lymphadenitis; disseminated BCG infection; osteitis |
| No time limit | Intussusception after rotavirus vaccine; any death, hospitalisation, or other severe and unusual event thought to be possibly related to vaccination |
| Newly introduced vaccines | All suspected adverse reactions |

### Subsequent Vaccination Doses

Your doctor will advise you about managing the current adverse event and whether the same vaccine can be given in future doses. If there are concerns, your doctor may seek specialist advice from the Immunisation Advisory Centre (IMAC).

### Programme Errors

If a vaccination error occurred (wrong preparation, handling, or administration), your doctor will document, report, and review the incident.

### ACC Treatment Injury Claims

Some adverse reactions to vaccines may be covered by ACC as a treatment injury, where the injury is clearly caused by the vaccination and is not an ordinary expected consequence. Examples likely to be covered include:

- Infections (e.g. cellulitis) due to vaccination
- Anaphylaxis
- Myocarditis or pericarditis

Ordinary consequences (e.g. inflammation around the injection site) are typically excluded from cover.

If eligible, your doctor can assist with lodging an ACC treatment injury claim (with your consent).
