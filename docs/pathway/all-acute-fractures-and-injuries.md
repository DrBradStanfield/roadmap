# All Acute Fractures and Injuries

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway provides general guidance on fracture and injury management. The specific approach will depend on the nature of the injury and the clinical setting.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

Your doctor should seek urgent orthopaedic assessment if any of the following apply:

- **Pathological fracture suspected** — fracture with disproportionate force of impact, or known/suspected underlying disease (primary or metastatic malignancy, osteomyelitis, osteomalacia, Paget disease)
- **Compound (open) fracture** or wound injury — requires emergency department assessment
- **Non-accidental injury suspected in a child** — if the injury does not fit the description given the age or developmental stage of the child
- **Uncertainty about diagnosis or management** — your doctor will seek orthopaedic advice

---

## Initial Management

Your doctor may consider the following:

1. **Adequate pain relief** before X-rays or transportation — this is a priority.
2. **Immobilisation** before transportation — a splint, backslab, or sling provides significant comfort and protects the injury.
3. **Detailed history** of the injury mechanism:
   - In children: whether the injury is consistent with the described mechanism and the child's age or developmental stage
   - In all patients: whether the force of impact is proportionate to the injury
   - If the force was minimal, whether the bone may be abnormal (see Pathological and Minimal Trauma Fractures below)
4. **Joint aspiration** — if there is a joint haemarthrosis (blood in the joint), this may be performed to reduce pain.

---

## Pathological and Minimal Trauma Fractures

### Pathological Fractures

A pathological fracture occurs through abnormal bone. Possible underlying causes include:

- Primary or metastatic malignancy
- Osteomyelitis
- Osteomalacia
- Paget disease

These are red flags for acute orthopaedic assessment.

### Minimal Trauma Fractures

A minimal trauma fracture results from a force that would not normally break a healthy bone — for example, a fall from standing height or less.

Your doctor will consider **osteoporosis** as an underlying cause. Risk factors for osteoporosis include:

- Postmenopausal women
- Smokers
- Low dietary calcium intake
- Amenorrhoea

Osteoporotic fractures occur most commonly in the wrist, hip, and spine. In older adults, a fracture at any major site (except the digits, face, or skull) should prompt consideration of osteoporosis assessment.

## Follow-Up

After a fracture or injury, your doctor may consider:

1. **Support at home** — particularly if you live alone or have dependants, such as young children.
2. **Osteoporosis screening:**
   - Recommended for postmenopausal women and men aged over 65 with a fracture
   - Recommended for all minimal trauma fractures
3. **Pain relief** — ensure adequate analgesia appropriate to the injury.
   - NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen, naproxen) may reduce bone healing and are used sparingly; they can be appropriate short-term in the acute setting if tolerated.
4. **Cast removal** — hard casts require specialist equipment for removal. If you need your cast removed, contact the provider who applied it.
5. **Early mobilisation** — encouraged for most injuries (unless otherwise directed) to prevent joint stiffness. Regular movement through the full range of motion and stretching of uninjured joints above and below the fracture site is beneficial.
6. **Physiotherapy** — your doctor may refer you if:
   - The fracture was immobilised for more than 4–6 weeks
   - You are at risk of falling
   - Normal function or gait has not returned within 3–4 weeks of cast, brace, or splint removal
   - Treatment may include strapping, soft casting, bracing, strengthening, proprioception training, and education on pain management
7. **Occupational therapy** — if you are having difficulty with everyday activities such as self-care, home access, or returning to work.
8. **Work rehabilitation** — if you are having difficulty returning to or sustaining work tasks.

---

## When to See Your Doctor

You should see your doctor or seek urgent care if:

- You have severe or worsening pain that is not controlled
- You develop new numbness, tingling, or weakness in the affected limb
- The injured area becomes increasingly swollen, red, or warm
- You have concerns about how the injury is healing
- You are unsure whether a fracture or serious injury has occurred
