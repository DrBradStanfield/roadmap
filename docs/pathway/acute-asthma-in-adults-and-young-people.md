# Acute Asthma in Adults and Young People

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway is for patients aged 12 years and over. For children under 12, a separate pathway applies.

> **Always discuss acute asthma with your doctor or call emergency services (111) if your asthma is not responding to your reliever inhaler.** Severe or life-threatening asthma is a medical emergency. The information below is for educational understanding only.

---

## Red Flags — Call an Ambulance Immediately

Call 111 if any of the following are present:

- Silent chest (no wheeze audible despite respiratory effort)
- Bradycardia (slow heart rate)
- Anaphylaxis (suspected as the trigger)
- Pregnancy with severe or life-threatening asthma

---

## Severity Assessment

Start bronchodilator treatment at the same time as assessing severity. Do not delay treatment to complete assessment.

### Examination

- Conscious state
- Ability to speak in whole sentences
- Peak expiratory flow rate (PEF)
- Oxygen saturation
- Respiratory rate and effort
- Heart rate and blood pressure
- Temperature
- Chest auscultation

### Acute Asthma Severity Classification

| Feature | Mild to moderate | Severe | Life-threatening |
|---|---|---|---|
| PEF | > 50% of best or predicted | 30–50% of best or predicted | < 30% of best or predicted |
| Oxygen saturation | > 96% | 92–96% | < 92% |
| Respiratory rate | Normal | > 25 breaths/min | Feeble respiratory effort |
| Heart rate | Normal | ≥ 110 | Bradycardia or extreme tachycardia |
| Other features | No severe features; increasing symptoms | Unable to complete sentences in one breath | Unable to talk; silent chest; exhaustion*; cyanosis*; hypotension |

*Exhaustion and cyanosis are very late signs, indicating a patient at risk of imminent respiratory arrest.

> **A lack of bronchodilator response after initial treatment is a key indicator of ongoing severity and the need for hospital admission.**

### History (once patient starts to stabilise)

- Rapidity of onset of symptoms
- Current medications; how many puffs of reliever inhaler in the last 24 hours; whether a spacer has been used correctly
- Co-morbidities
- Recent asthma control (if symptoms or reliever use > twice per week, control can be improved)
- Pregnancy
- Exposure to allergens and smoking status

### Consider Other Causes of Acute Breathlessness

- Respiratory: pulmonary embolism, pneumothorax, upper airway obstruction, pneumonia
- Cardiac: acute coronary syndrome, acute heart failure, arrhythmia
- Anxiety or hyperventilation

### Risk Factors for Severe or Fatal Asthma

If any of these apply, the patient is at higher risk and lower threshold should apply for hospital admission:

**Frequent or severe exacerbations:**
- One or more exacerbations requiring oral corticosteroids in the past 12 months
- Any previous ventilation or ICU admission for asthma
- Hospital admission or emergency department visit for asthma in the last year
- Frequent emergency treatment attendances
- Precipitous asthma (onset to severe in < 4 hours)
- Raised eosinophil count
- High SABA use (> 3 inhalers per year)

**Social factors:**
- Underuse of inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) treatment
- Disconnection from regular medical care
- Socio-economic disadvantage
- Poor housing (cold, damp, mouldy, or unflued gas heaters)
- Occupational asthma

**Co-morbidities:**
- Smoking
- Psychosis, depression, or other psychiatric illness
- Alcohol or drug use
- Aspirin or NSAID sensitivity
- Anaphylaxis or food allergy

> Note: Māori and Pacific peoples aged 15–49 years are approximately three times more likely to be admitted with asthma, with a 14% readmission rate within 90 days.

## Management

> **Practice point:** Do not use combination budesonide-formoterol (AIR therapy) to treat acute severe asthma — there is insufficient evidence for this. Short-acting beta agonists (SABA, e.g. salbutamol) remain the recommended treatment for acute attacks.

---

### Life-threatening Asthma — Emergency Management

**Act immediately. Call an ambulance.**

1. Give continuous nebulised salbutamol via oxygen (6–8 litres/minute to maintain O2 > 92%).
2. Give one immediate dose of ipratropium 500 micrograms via oxygen-driven nebuliser.
3. Remain calm and do not leave the patient unattended.
4. Call an ambulance and arrange immediate transfer to the nearest emergency department. Notify the admitting officer.
5. Give a systemic corticosteroid: prednisone 40 mg orally, or hydrocortisone 100 mg intravenously (IV).
6. Prepare to assist ventilation with a bag valve mask; prepare to intubate if trained to do so.

**If respiratory arrest occurs:**
- Consider early intubation with an ET tube (rather than LMA — ventilation is difficult in severe asthma)
- ET tube size guide: 7 for small adult, 8 for average adult, 9 for larger adult
- Be aware of pneumothorax, air trapping, and breath stacking — allow sufficient expiratory time
- Consider coexistence of anaphylaxis

**While awaiting transfer (continue all of the following):**
- Keep giving salbutamol via nebuliser continuously
- Give 500 micrograms ipratropium via nebuliser up to hourly
- Give oxygen to maintain saturations 92–96%
- Establish IV access if possible
- Consider IV magnesium sulphate 1.2–2 g over 20 minutes if available

---

### Severe Asthma — Management

1. **Give salbutamol immediately** — via spacer or nebuliser with oxygen to maintain saturations > 92%:
   - MDI + spacer: 6 puffs of salbutamol 100 micrograms
   - Oxygen-driven nebuliser: salbutamol 2.5 mg

2. If PEF remains < 50% after initial treatment, **add ipratropium**:
   - MDI + spacer: 6 puffs ipratropium 20 micrograms, or
   - Nebuliser: ipratropium 500 micrograms

3. **Monitor closely and reassess severity regularly:**
   - O2 saturation (target 92–96%)
   - Heart rate
   - Peak flow
   - Titrate O2 flow to maintain saturation of 92–96%

4. **Continue salbutamol** every 20 minutes for the first hour. If more frequent doses needed, escalate to life-threatening management.

5. **Give oral prednisone 40 mg.**

6. **Arrange transfer to emergency department** if patient is not stabilising, especially if PEF remains < 50% or deteriorating.

7. **Request emergency department assessment** if stabilising but at ongoing risk of acute deterioration due to:
   - Pregnancy
   - Slow response to bronchodilator treatment
   - Acute deterioration despite being on oral corticosteroids
   - Limited monitoring capacity
   - Significant co-morbidities (e.g. diabetes)
   - Persisting dyspnoea with PEF or FEV1 < 70%
   - Risk factors for severe or fatal asthma (see above)

**Discharge criteria for severe asthma** (if patient stabilises after 1–2 hours of observation):
- No signs of severe asthma
- PEF > 70%
- No risk factors for acute deterioration

If these criteria are met, the patient may be discharged with:
- Inhaler technique check
- Specific asthma action plan for next 24 hours and the following week
- Course of oral prednisone 40 mg daily for at least 5 days
- Supply of ICS inhaler
- Consider switching to AIR therapy (budesonide + formoterol as maintenance and reliever) to reduce future exacerbation risk

---

### Mild to Moderate Asthma — Management

1. **Give 6 puffs salbutamol via MDI + spacer.**

**MDI + spacer technique:**
- Prime the spacer before first use (10 "waste" puffs). Not needed after first use or if washed with detergent.
- Shake the inhaler, then insert into spacer.
- Place lips around the mouthpiece, then press the inhaler.
- Breathe in and out normally through the mouth 6 times per puff.
- Repeat for each puff.

2. **Monitor the patient:**
   - O2 saturation (should be > 92%)
   - Heart rate
   - Peak flow

3. **Repeat salbutamol 6 puffs every 20 minutes** while PEF < 70% or as needed.

4. **Consider oral prednisone 40 mg.**

5. **Reassess severity** and continue treatment according to response:
   - Unstable or PEF < 50%: escalate to severe management, arrange transfer to emergency department
   - PEF < 70% but worsening: manage as severe asthma
   - Improving: monitor until stable, no signs of severe asthma, and PEF > 70%

**Discharge preparation:**
- Check inhaler technique
- Provide asthma action plan for next 24 hours and following week
- Consider prescribing oral prednisone 40 mg daily for at least 5 days
- Ensure supply of ICS inhaler
- Consider switching to AIR therapy (budesonide + formoterol)

---

## When to Escalate to Emergency Department

| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| Life-threatening asthma | Call 111 immediately; transfer by ambulance |
| Severe asthma not stabilising, PEF < 50% or deteriorating | Transfer to emergency department |
| Severe asthma stabilising but at risk (pregnancy, exacerbating factors, PEF/FEV1 < 70%, risk factors for fatal asthma) | Request emergency department assessment |

---

## Follow-up After Acute Asthma

Book an early follow-up appointment with the patient's GP to:

- Prevent further exacerbations:
  - Check corticosteroid inhaler technique
  - Ensure continuation of ICS inhaler
  - Develop or update a personalised asthma action plan

**Asthma action plan options:**
- AIR Asthma Action Plan (printable or interactive)
- ICS Asthma Action Plan — 4-stage (standard-dose ICS) or 3-stage (high-dose ICS)

- Identify and address barriers to accessing medical care and medication
- Schedule regular review and vaccinations
- Support smoke-free environment
- Consider pulmonary rehabilitation or respiratory physiotherapy for breathing techniques if chronic breathing pattern dysfunction is suspected

**Breathing retraining:** Poorly controlled asthma can produce chronically overactive chest wall and neck muscles. Breathing training programs, yoga, and Buteyko exercises can improve symptoms, quality of life, and reduce reliever use.
