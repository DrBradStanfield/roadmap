# Nipple Pain in Breastfeeding

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of nipple pain in breastfeeding — including common causes, self-care measures, and when medical review is needed.

---

## Red Flag — Seek Medical Review Promptly

- **Herpes simplex virus (HSV) on the nipple or areola** — presents as a cluster of painful blisters. HSV is rare but can be fatal to newborns. This requires urgent assessment.

---

## Background

Nipple pain is one of the most common reasons women stop breastfeeding. Sore nipples in the first week are very common, ranging from uncomfortable to severe. In most cases, pain in the early days settles once attachment is established.

---

## What Your Doctor Will Assess

Your doctor will:

1. **Check breastfeeding position and latch** — poor attachment is the most common cause of nipple pain.

2. **Examine your baby's mouth** — looking at tongue mobility and whether a frenulum (tongue-tie) may be limiting latch depth.

3. **Examine your breast and nipple** — looking for signs of trauma, infection, or skin conditions.

4. **Consider possible causes** (see below).

---

## Causes of Nipple Pain

### Normal Early Tenderness

Tenderness at the start of a feed is expected for the first 7 to 10 days after birth, with peak tenderness between days 3 and 6. As milk flows, the discomfort usually eases. If pain lasts through the whole feed, or nipple damage is visible, see your doctor or a lactation consultant.

### Nipple Trauma

May appear as bruising, abrasions, blisters, cracks, or fissures. Usually caused by:
- Poor positioning or latch (infant attached mainly to nipple rather than breast)
- Breast pump injury (suction too high, shield too small)

### Engorged Breasts

Swollen breasts can make it hard for the baby to latch deeply, leading to a shallow, painful latch.

### Bacterial Nipple Infection

Usually caused by *Staphylococcus aureus*. Signs include fissures, crusting, redness, and sometimes purulent discharge.

### Nipple Thrush (Candida)

Note: Recent evidence suggests Candida is rarely the sole cause of nipple pain in breastfeeding. Dermatitis is now considered a more likely explanation for many cases previously labelled as thrush.

If thrush is present, it may cause:
- Pink, shiny, flaky nipples with a reddened areola
- Burning or shooting pain, typically after feeding, but sometimes during and between feeds
- Nipples very sensitive to light touch, even from clothing
- Shooting pains deep in the breast and increased pain with let-down

Risk factors include recent antibiotic use and nipple trauma.

### Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV)

Presents as a discrete cluster of painful papular lesions on the nipple or areola. Rare, but important to identify as it can be fatal to newborns.

### Nipple Vasospasm

Intense nipple pain — sharp, burning, or stinging — associated with nipple blanching (colour change), usually occurring after breastfeeding or pumping. Caused by blood vessel spasm reducing blood flow to the nipple.

Triggers include cold exposure, nipple trauma, smoking, and caffeine.

Risk factors:
- Diabetes
- Smoking
- Low BMI
- Cold hands or feet
- Family history of Raynaud's phenomenon
- History of high milk supply

### Blocked Nipple Pore (White Spot or Bleb / Milk Blister)

A white or yellow pinhead-sized blister on the nipple causing pain during feeding. Caused by a blocked duct at the nipple surface due to an overgrowth of skin. Often resolves spontaneously during feeding.

### Nipple Eczema or Dermatitis

An itchy, red, scaly rash on the nipple or areola, with burning pain during breastfeeding. Can appear at any stage of breastfeeding.

## Management

### General Measures for Sore or Cracked Nipples

Your doctor may advise you to:

- Express a small amount of milk before feeding to soften the nipple and help the baby get a deeper latch
- Offer the least sore breast first
- Make small adjustments during feeding to deepen the latch — or unlatch and try again if it hurts
- Try a reclined feeding position with the baby prone against your body
- Try frequent short feeds rather than longer infrequent ones
- Express temporarily for 24 to 48 hours if feeding is too painful
- After feeding, dry nipples gently and leave a few drops of breast milk on them — or apply an emollient or nipple balm. Gel pads may also help
- Change breast pads frequently
- Consider nipple shields for damaged nipples — best used under guidance from a lactation consultant or LMC

**Paracetamol and ibuprofen** are both safe during breastfeeding and can help with pain from any cause (other than normal early tenderness).

Apply any topical treatments to the nipple immediately after a feed.

### Bacterial Infection

Your doctor may consider:
- **Flucloxacillin** 1 g orally three times a day for 5 days (oral antibiotics are preferred over topical treatment to reduce risk of antibiotic resistance)

### Nipple Thrush

If your baby has oral thrush at the same time:
- Your doctor may consider **nystatin drops** 1 mL four times a day for one week, then once a day for one week (for the baby)

If there is uncertainty about whether thrush is the cause, your doctor will likely refer you for a specialist opinion — as dermatitis is now considered more likely than candida in many cases.

### Herpes Simplex Virus (HSV)

Your doctor may consider:
- **Valaciclovir** 1 g orally twice a day for 7 days (safe in breastfeeding), or
- **Aciclovir** 400 mg orally five times a day for 7 days

*Note: Doses of both medications must be reduced in kidney (renal) impairment — your doctor will check this.*

You will need to:
- Stop feeding from the affected breast until all lesions are healed
- Discard any milk expressed from the affected side
- Continue feeding from the unaffected side
- Keep all lesions covered
- Wash hands thoroughly

### Nipple Eczema or Dermatitis

Your doctor may consider:
- **Mometasone 0.1% (Elocon)** or **methylprednisolone aceponate 0.1% (Advantan)** applied sparingly to the nipple immediately after a feed, for 7 days (ointments are preferred)
- Avoiding soap, body washes, and nipple products
- Moisturising with sorbolene or purified lanolin
- Checking for secondary bacterial infection

### Nipple Vasospasm

Your doctor may advise you to:
- Keep your body warm and avoid exposing nipples to cold
- Apply warm compresses for 5 minutes immediately after feeds
- Use breast warmers or a polar fleece around the breasts
- Avoid caffeine and nicotine

If pain persists despite regular paracetamol and ibuprofen, your doctor may consider:
- **Nifedipine** 30 mg orally once a day for two weeks (to relax blood vessels)
- **Magnesium** 300 mg tablets (one to two per day) or **fish-oil capsules** — evidence is limited but some women find them helpful
- Checking thyroid function and iron studies for contributing abnormalities

### Blocked Nipple Pore (White Spot or Bleb)

Most blebs are painless and resolve with feeding without treatment. If persistent and painful:
- Massage olive oil into the nipple to help soften and break up the blockage
- Gently wash the nipple with a face washer in the shower daily
- Clean breasts after feeding
- Avoid poorly fitting bras
- Good attachment reduces pain with feeding

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you to a lactation consultant or breastfeeding medicine specialist if:
- Diagnosis is uncertain
- Possible tongue-tie causing attachment difficulties
- Nipple vasospasm is present
- Nipple shields are needed
- You are unsure about correct attachment or positioning
- Pain continues through the entire feed or visible nipple damage is present
- Symptoms are not settling
