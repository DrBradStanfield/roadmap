# Abdominal Ultrasound

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers when an abdominal ultrasound scan is considered appropriate — including situations requiring emergency care, and those where your doctor may arrange a scan.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Do not wait for an ultrasound if you have any of the following. Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department:

- **Suspected acute cholecystitis or cholangitis** — severe persistent right upper quadrant or upper abdominal pain, especially with fever and/or jaundice. May also cause itch, pale stools, low blood pressure, or confusion.
- **Acute abdomen or suspected appendicitis** — severe abdominal pain requiring urgent surgical assessment.
- **Suspected ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)** — sudden severe abdominal or back pain, especially in older patients with known aneurysm.
- **Suspected testicular torsion** — sudden severe testicular pain.
- **Painful jaundice** — jaundice combined with significant abdominal pain.

Arranging imaging should never delay emergency hospital assessment in these situations.

---

## When Your Doctor May Consider an Abdominal Ultrasound

### Adults

Your doctor may consider arranging an abdominal ultrasound in the following situations:

**Abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA)**
- A newly suspected or known AAA found on examination or previous imaging.
- Surveillance of a known AAA (typically on a defined schedule).

**Palpable abdominal mass**
- A non-pulsatile upper abdominal mass consistent with an enlarged liver (hepatomegaly) on examination.
- Note: for other types of abdominal mass, your doctor may discuss whether a CT scan is more appropriate.

**Abnormal liver function tests (LFTs)**
- ALP more than twice the upper limit of normal and GGT above normal — your doctor may mark this as urgent (within 2 weeks).
- On follow-up testing 3 or more months later, if ALP and GGT remain above normal.
- ALT above 120 units/L that has not improved with conservative measures (such as weight loss, lifestyle changes, or medication adjustment) in patients with risk factors like diabetes, obesity, or certain medications — and where there are no features of concern suggesting a more urgent cause.
- Isolated raised GGT more than 5 times the upper limit of normal, which persists after 3 months.

**Biliary colic or gallbladder symptoms**
- Suspected biliary colic or acute cholecystitis where symptoms have settled with pain relief, and both ALP and GGT are above normal, and no previous imaging of the bile ducts has been done — typically arranged as urgent (within 2 weeks).
- Recurrent right upper quadrant or epigastric (upper abdominal) pain not thought to be due to heartburn or dyspepsia, and no previous imaging has been done.
- Suspected resolved biliary colic or cholecystitis in a patient suitable for gallbladder surgery (cholecystectomy), and no previous imaging has been done.

**Hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) surveillance**
- Ongoing surveillance for liver cancer as recommended by a specialist or as part of hepatitis B or hepatitis C management.
- Suspected liver cancer.

**Jaundice without obvious cause**
- Painless jaundice without a clear explanation — your doctor may arrange this urgently (within 48 hours).

### Children (Aged 14 and Under)

**Red flags — emergency assessment required:**
- Acute abdominal pain including suspected appendicitis — urgent paediatric assessment is needed; do not delay by arranging imaging.
- Suspected pyloric stenosis (typically presents in young infants with forceful vomiting) — urgent paediatric assessment.

**When your doctor may consider ultrasound:**
- Recurrent right upper quadrant pain with suspected gallbladder pathology — typically arranged as urgent (within 2 weeks).
- Recurrent abdominal pain — three or more episodes over at least 3 months, severe enough to affect daily activities such as school attendance, and after common causes like constipation have been excluded.

---

## Situations Where Ultrasound Is Generally Not the First Choice

Your doctor may direct you to a different investigation or pathway for:

- **Suspected colorectal (bowel) cancer** — usually assessed differently.
- **Dyspepsia (indigestion) or heartburn** — managed through a separate pathway.
- **Incidental imaging findings** — reviewed according to specific guidance for incidental findings.
- **Bloating with concerning features** (e.g., weight loss, reduced appetite, abnormal vaginal bleeding) — your doctor will discuss whether CT or another scan is more appropriate.
- **Unexplained weight loss, persistent fatigue, or vague abdominal pain with suspicion of underlying cancer** — CT imaging of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis may be more appropriate.
- **Kidney or urinary tract conditions** — a renal ultrasound is typically used instead.
- **Acute pancreatitis or symptomatic chronic pancreatitis** — emergency or specialist surgical assessment is needed.

---

## What Ultrasound Does Not Cover

The following are generally not publicly funded by ultrasound and your doctor may discuss whether private imaging is appropriate:

- Hernia assessment
- Follow-up of previously diagnosed gallstones in patients with no symptoms
- Isolated bloating with no other concerning features
- Screening for AAA or fatty liver in people without symptoms or specific risk factors
