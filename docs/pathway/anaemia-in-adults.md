# Anaemia in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of anaemia in adults — identifying the cause and guiding appropriate investigation and treatment.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Attention

See your doctor urgently if you have anaemia with any of:

- Weight loss, fevers, bone pain, and night sweats
- Unexplained change in bowel habit
- Abnormalities in other blood-cell lines (white cells or platelets)
- Severe anaemia with haemodynamic instability (fast heart rate, low blood pressure, collapse)

---

## Background

Anaemia means haemoglobin is below the normal reference range. It is often picked up as an incidental finding on a blood test and may cause no symptoms.

Key facts:
- Anaemia can be caused by iron, folate, or B12 deficiency; renal insufficiency; anaemia of chronic inflammation; or bone marrow disease.
- 10% of people over 65 living in the community have anaemia. Prevalence rises rapidly after age 50.
- Most anaemia is mild — only 3% of women and 1.5% of men have haemoglobin below 110 g/L.
- In older people, about one third have nutritional deficiency, one third have anaemia of chronic inflammation or renal disease, and the remainder are unexplained.
- Most anaemia is managed in general practice.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history and perform an examination, then arrange blood tests to identify the cause and severity.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Symptoms of anaemia — fatigue, lethargy, shortness of breath
- Symptoms that may suggest malignancy — fevers, night sweats, weight loss, bone pain
- Diet, alcohol consumption, menstruation
- Change in bowel habit
- Underlying conditions (kidney disease, liver disease, inflammatory or autoimmune disease)
- Previous blood transfusions
- Medications — particularly NSAIDs, aspirin (cause blood loss), metformin, proton pump inhibitors, valproate (cause B12 deficiency), cytotoxics (cause aplastic anaemia)
- Family history and ethnicity (relevant to thalassaemia, sickle cell anaemia, G6PD deficiency)

### Examination

Your doctor will look for:

**Severity signs:**
- Pallor of skin, mucous membranes, and nails
- Signs of cardiac stress — fast heart rate, low blood pressure, heart murmur, crackles in the lungs

**Possible cause signs:**
- Abnormal bleeding or bruising
- Jaundice
- Enlarged lymph nodes
- Enlarged liver or spleen
- Abdominal mass
- Rectal examination for rectal mass or melaena

### Blood Tests

Your doctor will arrange:

- **Full blood count (FBC)** — assesses severity and whether other blood cell lines are affected
- **Ferritin** — the key test for iron deficiency; can be falsely normal when inflammation is present (ferritin is an acute phase protein)
- **B12 and folate** — tested when indicated (see below)
- **Blood film** — shows blood cell shape and helps identify the likely cause; must be specifically requested
- **Reticulocyte count** — indicates whether the bone marrow is actively producing red cells
- **Mean cell volume (MCV)** — classifies anaemia as microcytic, normocytic, or macrocytic, which guides the diagnosis

---

## Diagnostic Thresholds

**Anaemia is defined as:**
- Male: haemoglobin < 130 g/L
- Female: haemoglobin < 115 g/L (including in pregnancy)

**Iron deficiency is defined as:**
- Ferritin < 20 micrograms/L (adults, including pregnant women)
- Ferritin < 100 micrograms/L in patients with chronic inflammatory conditions (including heart failure)

---

## Approach by Blood Test Pattern

Your doctor will use the reticulocyte count and MCV to narrow the cause:

### Elevated Reticulocytes (increased red cell destruction or acute loss)
- Haemolysis — the doctor will determine if congenital (e.g. hereditary spherocytosis, thalassaemia, sickle cell) or acquired (e.g. autoimmune). A haemolytic screen will be arranged.
- Acute blood loss

### Macrocytic Anaemia (MCV > 100)
Consider:
- Alcohol excess
- B12 or folate deficiency
- Hypothyroidism
- Bone marrow disease — primary bone marrow failure, myelodysplasia, multiple myeloma
- Secondary cancers

### Microcytic Anaemia (MCV < 80)
Consider:
- **Iron deficiency** — most common cause
- **Anaemia of chronic inflammation** (previously called anaemia of chronic disease):
  - Caused by any systemic inflammatory condition — malignancy, infection, autoimmune disease
  - Typically shows normal or elevated ferritin with reduced serum iron; elevated CRP; low reticulocyte count
  - A soluble transferrin receptor test can help identify co-existing iron deficiency
  - Can also present as normocytic
- If neither iron deficiency nor chronic inflammation, consider **haemoglobinopathy** (e.g. thalassaemia) — a haemoglobinopathy screen will be arranged

### Normocytic Anaemia (MCV 80–100)
Consider:
- Recent haemorrhage
- Renal failure
- Thyroid or other endocrine disorder
- Early iron deficiency
- Anaemia of chronic inflammation
- Bone marrow malignancy (multiple myeloma, secondary cancers)

### Cause Not Obvious
Additional tests your doctor may arrange:
- Serum iron and soluble transferrin receptor
- Kidney and liver function
- Thyroid function
- Tests for multiple myeloma: serum protein electrophoresis (SPEP), immunoglobulin levels, serum free light chains, serum calcium

## When to Test B12

Your doctor will consider B12 testing if you have:

- Unexplained anaemia or macrocytosis
- Unexplained neurological symptoms — tingling, weakness, memory problems, neuropathy
- Gastrointestinal disorders — Crohn's disease, coeliac disease, previous gastric surgery, glossitis
- Long-term vegan or vegetarian diet
- Long-term use of metformin, proton pump inhibitors, or H2-receptor antagonists
- Age over 75
- Autoimmune conditions — thyroid disease, type 1 diabetes, coeliac disease
- 6 months after starting B12 replacement (to confirm response)
- Pregnancy or lactation with suspected deficiency

---

## When to Test Folate

Folate testing is appropriate when there is clinical suspicion of:

- Malnutrition or malabsorption (e.g. inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease, alcoholism)
- Unexplained anaemia, macrocytosis, or megaloblastic changes on blood film
- Use of drugs affecting folate metabolism (some antiepileptics, methotrexate, sulfasalazine, certain chemotherapy agents)
- Haemodialysis or previous bariatric surgery
- To re-check after 1–3 months of folate replacement

If folate deficiency is suspected but does not meet these criteria, empirical folic acid treatment is often more cost-effective than testing.

---

## Management

Management depends on the identified cause:

- **Iron deficiency** (with or without co-existing chronic inflammation): follow the Iron Deficiency in Adults pathway — this will typically involve oral iron supplementation, or IV iron infusion if oral iron is unsuitable
- **B12 deficiency**: B12 supplementation (oral or injection depending on cause)
- **Folate deficiency**: folic acid supplementation
- **Anaemia of chronic inflammation**: treat the underlying inflammatory condition; specialist input may be needed
- **Thalassaemia or haemoglobinopathy**: specialist haematology management
- **Severe anaemia**: your doctor may arrange a blood transfusion
- **No identifiable cause**: monitoring until symptomatic or a treatable cause is found; about one third of anaemia in older adults is unexplained
- Haematology specialist advice can be sought by your doctor at any stage

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will refer to haematology if anaemia is associated with:

- Blood film findings suggesting haematological disease — leukaemia, myelodysplasia, myeloma
- Abnormalities in other blood cell lines — low platelets (thrombocytopenia) or low neutrophils (neutropenia)
- Evidence of haemolysis — elevated reticulocytes, elevated bilirubin, elevated LDH, absent haptoglobin, positive direct antiglobulin test
