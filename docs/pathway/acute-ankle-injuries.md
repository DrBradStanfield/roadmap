# Acute Ankle Injuries

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial assessment of acute ankle injuries — helping determine whether imaging is needed and identifying the type of injury.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department if you have:

- Obvious deformity or bone visible through the skin
- Complete inability to bear any weight with severe pain
- Significant neurovascular compromise (loss of sensation, absent pulses, or pale/cold foot)
- Suspected high-energy injury (e.g. fall from height, motor vehicle accident)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Mechanism of injury — e.g. crossing uneven ground, sudden change of direction, excessive twisting inward (inversion) or outward (eversion), jumping from a height
- Whether there was an audible sound at the time of injury (e.g. a pop)
- Ability to weight bear immediately after the injury and/or continue activity
- Location of pain
- Any previous ankle injuries

Physical examination typically includes:

- Checking gait for any limp
- Looking for swelling, bruising, or joint effusion
- Palpating the ankle and foot — specifically the malleolar regions, navicular bone, the front of the ankle (anterior tibiofibular ligament), the full length of the fibula, and the base of the 5th metatarsal
- Checking range of motion and strength
- Assessing the Achilles tendon
- A neurovascular check — including pulses (posterior tibial artery, dorsalis pedis artery), capillary return, sensation, and ankle reflexes
- Looking for any coexisting injuries (e.g. knee or foot on the same side)

## Ankle Anatomy (Background)

The ankle can be thought of as a ring structure made up of the medial malleolus, medial ligament, lateral ligaments, lateral malleolus, and tibiofibular ligaments. If the ring is disrupted at 2 or more sites — whether by fracture or ligament rupture — the injury is considered unstable and may require surgery.

Two joints allow movement at the ankle:

- **Tibiotalar (true ankle) joint** — allows the foot to flex up and down (dorsiflexion and plantar flexion). Formed by the distal tibia (medial wall), distal fibula (lateral wall), and talus.
- **Subtalar joint** (between talus and calcaneus) — allows inversion and eversion of the foot.

## Imaging

Your doctor will decide whether an X-ray is needed. They may use the **Ottawa Ankle Rules** — a validated tool to identify who needs imaging:

- X-ray is recommended if there is pain near the malleolus AND inability to bear weight for 4 steps, OR tenderness at the back edge or tip of either malleolus
- X-ray is also recommended if there is pain in the midfoot AND inability to bear weight, OR tenderness over the navicular or base of 5th metatarsal

Children under 12 and elderly patients are more likely to have a fracture (rather than a sprain) and are often X-rayed routinely.

If X-rays are taken, your doctor will assess:

- **AP view** — used to assess the medial clear space; if widened more than 3 mm, this suggests rupture of the medial deltoid ligament
- **Mortise view** — used to assess the tibiofibular joint and ankle congruency; widening of the tibiofibular joint greater than 6 mm or loss of fibula-tibia overlap suggests syndesmosis disruption (diastasis); increased medial clear space with lateral shift of the talus may suggest a high fibular fracture
- **Lateral view** — used to assess the posterior malleolus

Your doctor will also check for less obvious fractures — for example, if there is tenderness over the proximal fibula, a full-length lower leg X-ray may be requested to look for a high fibula fracture. When one fracture is seen, a second is always looked for.

---

## Management

Management depends on the diagnosis reached after history, examination, and imaging. Your doctor may consider:

- **Ankle sprain** — most common; managed with rest, ice, compression, elevation, and physiotherapy-guided rehabilitation
- **Ankle fracture** — management depends on fracture type and stability; may range from a boot or cast through to surgical fixation
- **Achilles tendon injury** — assessment and management specific to injury severity

Once your doctor has determined the type and severity of your injury, they will advise on weight bearing, immobilisation, and rehabilitation.
