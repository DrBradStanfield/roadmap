# Physical Activity — Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: December 2023.*

> This is general information. Your doctor can help you find the right starting point and check that exercise is safe for you.

This pathway covers how doctors talk with you about physical activity. They look at where you are starting from and what might be getting in the way, then help you set targets.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will explore your current situation, including:

- **Current activity levels:** how much time in the past week you spent on activities such as swimming, jogging, aerobics, football, tennis, gym workouts, cycling (including cycling to work and in your leisure time), walking (including walking to work, shopping, walking the dog, walking for pleasure, walking up stairs), housework, childcare, gardening, washing the car, or DIY work.
- **Readiness and motivation:** how important the change is to you and how confident you feel about making it. Your doctor may use a tool called the Readiness Ruler.
- **Barriers** to being physically active, which may include:
  - Physical health conditions (e.g. asthma, arthritis, diabetes, injury, heart disease, osteoporosis, ME/CFS)
  - Mental health-related factors (e.g. anxiety, depression, cognitive impairment)
  - Age-related factors (e.g. falls risk)
  - Personal, social, environmental, or cultural factors (e.g. isolation, low income, lack of motivation, time, confidence, or energy)

---

## Contraindications and Precautions

Your doctor will consider whether exercise is safe for you right now.

**Exercise is generally not appropriate if you have:**

- Unstable cardiovascular disease (e.g. recent heart attack, unstable angina, breathlessness at rest, pericarditis, myocarditis, endocarditis, symptomatic aortic stenosis, cardiomyopathy, unstable or acute heart failure, uncontrolled rapid heart rate)
- Fever
- Acute pulmonary embolism or pulmonary infarction
- Any acute severe illness
- Serious musculoskeletal injury or problem
- Severely impaired cognitive functioning

**Exercise requires caution if you have:**

- Uncontrolled or poorly controlled asthma
- COPD: you need to be stable before training, and your oxygen saturation should be above 88–90%
- Uncontrolled diabetes
- Hypertension: if your resting systolic blood pressure is 180 or higher, or your resting diastolic blood pressure is 100 or higher, medication should be taken before regular physical activity, and heavy weights strength training should be restricted because it can create particularly high pressures
- Osteoporosis: avoid activities with a high risk of falling. Movements that twist or bend the spine to the end of their range may need to be modified, especially if you are at high risk of fracture
- Unexplained dizzy spells

## Management

### Benefits of Being Active

Your doctor may explain that being active can help you to:

- Feel better and have more energy
- Feel more relaxed and less stressed
- Improve your mood
- Sleep better
- Manage your weight
- Live independently for longer
- Improve posture, strength, and balance
- Prevent or manage conditions such as heart disease, high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, many cancers, arthritis, anxiety, depression, and dementia
- Keep your bones and muscles strong and your joints flexible
- Keep your bowels regular

### Activity Targets

**Initial goal: build up to at least one of:**

- **2.5 hours of moderate-intensity activity** spread throughout the week (e.g. 30 minutes on 5 days), OR
- **1.25 hours of vigorous-intensity activity** spread throughout the week (e.g. 15 minutes on 5 days), OR
- An equivalent combination of both

It may help to break exercise into smaller chunks, for example 10 minutes at a time.

**Moderate-intensity activity** noticeably increases your heart rate and breathing. You may sweat, but you can still carry on a conversation. You can talk, but you cannot sing.

Examples: brisk walking, stair climbing, actively playing with children, cycling on level ground, strenuous gardening (e.g. carrying loads in a wheelbarrow, digging), dancing, vacuuming, washing the car, general building tasks (e.g. roofing, painting), carrying or moving moderate loads (less than 20 kg).

**Vigorous-intensity activity** increases your heart rate and breathing further. You cannot do these activities and talk at the same time.

Examples: hill walking, running, fast swimming, cycling faster than 16 km/h, rope skipping, sports (rugby, football, netball, tennis, hockey), heavy digging, chopping wood.

**Muscle-strengthening activities** (twice a week) can give extra health benefits when combined with moderate or vigorous activity. In these activities your muscles work or resist against a force or weight. Many sports combine all of these elements. Your doctor may suggest input from a suitably skilled instructor.

Examples: carrying young children or heavy shopping, sawing, cycling uphill, push-ups, sit-ups, squats, weight training, resistance bands, explosive sports (e.g. lifting weights, sprinting, team sports).

**Long-term goal:** For extra health benefits, including losing weight, aim for 5 hours of moderate or 2.5 hours of vigorous activity spread throughout the week.

### Adults Aged 65 and Older

Your doctor may advise:

- Carry out some form of physical activity regardless of age, health status, or ability
- Move daily in as many ways as possible
- If currently inactive, start gently and slowly build up to:
  - 30 minutes of moderate-intensity activity 5 days a week
  - 3 sessions of flexibility and balance activities per week
  - 2 sessions of muscle-strengthening activities per week
- Continue an already active lifestyle if still capable and safe to do so

### Reducing Sedentary Behaviour

Your doctor may encourage you to:

- Sit less and move more
- Break up long periods of sitting
- Build activity into your daily routine

The Ministry of Health recommends being active on most days, preferably every day. Any activity is better than none.

### If You Have Barriers to Exercise

Your doctor may give advice on overcoming barriers, and may consider referring you to:

- Physical activity support (Green Prescription) or healthy lifestyle support
- Hydrotherapy, for example if you have difficulty weight-bearing on land, or weakness, pain, or joint dysfunction affecting mobility. In hydrotherapy, a physiotherapist gives you exercises in a specially designed pool.

Exercising in water may be easier than on land because of buoyancy. Water-based exercise can help with pain, joint mobility, and overall fitness. If water-based exercise suits you, you may also manage your own exercise in a public or home swimming pool.

Your doctor may also suggest a subsidised gym membership.
