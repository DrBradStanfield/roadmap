# Physical Activity — Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers how doctors approach physical activity counselling — assessing where you're starting from, what might be getting in the way, and what targets to work towards.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will explore your current situation including:

- **Current activity levels** — how much time in the past week you spent on activities such as swimming, jogging, aerobics, football, tennis, gym workouts, cycling (including commuting), walking (including walking to work, shopping, walking the dog), housework, childcare, gardening, washing the car, or DIY work.
- **Readiness and motivation** — your interest in making changes and confidence in doing so.
- **Barriers** to being physically active, which may include:
  - Physical health conditions (e.g. asthma, arthritis, diabetes, injury, heart disease, osteoporosis, ME/CFS)
  - Mental health-related factors (e.g. anxiety, depression, cognitive impairment)
  - Age-related factors (e.g. falls risk)
  - Personal, social, environmental, or cultural factors (e.g. isolation, low income, lack of motivation, time, confidence, or energy)

---

## Contraindications and Precautions

Your doctor will consider whether exercise is safe for you right now.

**Exercise is generally not appropriate if you have:**

- Unstable cardiovascular disease (e.g. recent heart attack, unstable angina, breathlessness at rest, pericarditis, myocarditis, endocarditis, symptomatic aortic stenosis, cardiomyopathy, unstable or acute heart failure, uncontrolled rapid heart rate)
- Fever
- Acute pulmonary embolism or pulmonary infarction
- Any acute severe illness
- Serious musculoskeletal injury or problem
- Severely impaired cognitive functioning

**Exercise requires caution if you have:**

- Uncontrolled or poorly controlled asthma
- COPD — you need to be stable with oxygen saturation above 88–90% before starting
- Uncontrolled diabetes
- Hypertension — if your resting systolic blood pressure is 180 or higher, or diastolic 100 or higher, medication should be taken before exercising, and heavy weights training should be restricted
- Osteoporosis — avoid high fall-risk activities; movements involving extreme twisting or spinal flexion may need modification
- Unexplained dizzy spells

## Management

### Benefits of Being Active

Your doctor may explain that being active can help you to:

- Feel better and have more energy
- Feel more relaxed and less stressed
- Improve your mood
- Sleep better
- Manage your weight
- Live independently for longer
- Improve posture, strength, and balance
- Prevent or manage conditions such as heart disease, high blood pressure, type 2 diabetes, many cancers, arthritis, anxiety, depression, and dementia
- Keep bones, muscles, and joints strong and flexible
- Keep your bowels regular

### Activity Targets

**Initial goal — build up to at least one of:**

- **2.5 hours of moderate-intensity activity** spread throughout the week (e.g. 30 minutes on 5 days), OR
- **1.25 hours of vigorous-intensity activity** spread throughout the week (e.g. 15 minutes on 5 days), OR
- An equivalent combination of both

It can help to break activity into smaller chunks — for example, 10 minutes at a time still counts.

**Moderate-intensity activity** noticeably increases your heart rate and breathing. You can still talk, but you cannot sing.

Examples: brisk walking, stair climbing, actively playing with children, cycling on level ground, dancing, strenuous gardening (e.g. digging, carrying loads), vacuuming, washing the car, general building tasks, carrying loads under 20 kg.

**Vigorous-intensity activity** increases your heart rate and breathing significantly. You cannot talk comfortably while doing these activities.

Examples: hill walking, running, fast swimming, cycling faster than 16 km/h, rope skipping, sports (rugby, football, netball, tennis, hockey), heavy digging, chopping wood.

**Muscle-strengthening activities** (twice a week) provide extra health benefits when combined with moderate or vigorous activity.

Examples: carrying young children or heavy shopping, sawing, cycling uphill, push-ups, sit-ups, squats, weight training, resistance bands, explosive sports (sprinting, team sports).

**Long-term goal:** For extra health benefits including weight management, aim for 5 hours of moderate or 2.5 hours of vigorous activity spread throughout the week.

### Adults Aged 65 and Older

Your doctor may advise:

- Carry out some form of physical activity regardless of age, health status, or ability
- Move daily in as many ways as possible
- If currently inactive, start gently and slowly build up to:
  - 30 minutes of moderate-intensity activity 5 days a week
  - 3 sessions of flexibility and balance activities per week
  - 2 sessions of muscle-strengthening activities per week
- Continue an already active lifestyle if still capable and safe to do so

### Reducing Sedentary Behaviour

Your doctor may encourage you to:

- Sit less and move more
- Break up long periods of sitting
- Build activity into your daily routine

Any activity is better than none.

### If You Have Barriers to Exercise

Your doctor may consider referring you to:

- Physical activity support (Green Prescription) or healthy lifestyle programmes
- Hydrotherapy — if you have difficulty weight-bearing on land, or weakness, pain, or joint dysfunction affecting mobility. Water-based exercise uses buoyancy to make movement easier, and can help with pain, joint mobility, and overall fitness.
