# Plagiocephaly

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of positional plagiocephaly — flat or misshapen head shape in infants.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

Contact your doctor promptly or go to the emergency department if your baby shows signs of raised intracranial pressure:

- Severe acute irritability
- Change in pitch of cry
- Increasing vomiting
- Unusual drowsiness
- Bulging fontanelle (soft spot on top of head)
- "Sunset" appearance to the eyes (irises pushed downward)

These features are rare but require urgent assessment.

---

## Background

- **Positional (deformational) plagiocephaly** refers to a misshapen, flat, or asymmetrical head shape that develops from external pressure on the skull.
- **Brachycephaly** is symmetrical flattening of the back of the head.
- Mild to moderate plagiocephaly generally resolves over time with repositioning strategies.
- Early detection and management improves outcomes in severe cases.
- **Congenital torticollis** (tightness of the neck muscle) is a common contributing factor and can be associated with hip dysplasia.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess:

**History:**
- Risk factors including prematurity, breech or multiple birth, restricted intrauterine environment, and persistent sleeping position
- Any developmental concerns
- Features that might suggest raised intracranial pressure (see Red Flags above)

**Examination:**
- Skull shape viewed from above, front, and behind — positional plagiocephaly typically gives the skull a parallelogram shape from above
- Severity is graded as:
  - **Mild:** Slight flattening of posterior skull on one side, with flattening of the opposite forehead
  - **Moderate:** Posterior skull and forehead flattening with slight forward ear shift
  - **Severe:** Posterior skull and forehead changes with significant ear shift and eye socket (orbit) asymmetry
- Head circumference measured and plotted on percentile charts
- Neck assessment for **torticollis** — head tilt, limited rotation, or a firm swelling along the sternocleidomastoid (SCM) muscle. 90% of congenital muscular torticollis cases resolve without treatment.
- Eye examination including red reflex — eye problems can present as torticollis

**Investigations:**
- Cranial ultrasound is **not** required for plagiocephaly
- Skull X-rays are **not** helpful
- CT scan is only considered in consultation with paediatric services

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following based on severity and findings:

**If red flags of raised intracranial pressure are present:**
- Urgent neurosurgery assessment will be arranged

**If craniosynostosis (premature fusion of skull bones) is suspected:**
- Non-urgent neurosurgery assessment is arranged
- Craniosynostosis affects approximately 1 in 2,000–2,500 births and produces distinctive skull deformity patterns; it is different from positional plagiocephaly

**If there is a tight neck muscle (sternocleidomastoid) or limited head/neck movement:**
- Your doctor may refer to plastic surgery or physiotherapy for assessment

**If developmental delay is present alongside plagiocephaly:**
- Paediatric assessment may be arranged

**For positional plagiocephaly without the above concerns:**

Your doctor will provide reassurance and the following advice:

- **Sleeping position:** Keep your baby on their back to sleep (for safety), but alternate which end of the cot their head is at, so they naturally turn to different sides
- **Cot orientation:** Turn the cot so your baby turns their head toward the door on their less-favoured side
- **Tummy time:** Place your baby on their stomach while awake and supervised — this is essential for both plagiocephaly and neck muscle tightness
- **Feeding:** Alternate which arm you use to hold your baby during feeds
- **Carrying:** Vary your holding position to reduce prolonged pressure on the same area of the skull
- **Mobile placement:** Hang a mobile so your baby looks toward their preferred side to encourage gentle neck movement

**Outlook:**
- Once your baby can sit or roll independently, head shape usually stabilises and improves gradually
- As the skull grows over months and years, flattening improves
- **Helmet therapy is not recommended in most cases** and is not publicly funded

**Moderate or severe cases:**
- Your doctor may refer to physiotherapy for additional assessment and guidance
- Follow-up appointments will be arranged to monitor progress
