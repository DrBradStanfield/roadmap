# Land Contamination

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of potential health risks from living on or near contaminated land, including former orchards, timber treatment sites, landfills, and industrial land.

> **Note (November 2025):** Several coloured sand products used by children and in early childhood settings have been found to contain asbestos. If a child was exposed to these sands, no immediate medical management is required — avoid or eliminate further exposure. See Health New Zealand for the list of recalled products.

---

## Background

Contamination can come from many sources, including former orchards and market gardens, landfills, timber treatment sites, and other industrial sites where potentially harmful chemicals were used, stored, or disposed of. It can also come from asbestos released during demolition or renovation, lead from old houses with lead paint, and ash from burning treated timber.

Known and suspected contaminated sites — called Hazardous Activities and Industries sites (HAIL) — may appear on property land information memoranda (LIM) or council records.

Receiving notification that your land may contain contaminants can be worrying, but for most old contamination situations the human health risk is low.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess exposure risk by considering:

**What contaminants may be present, based on former land use:**

| Former Land Use | Likely Contaminants |
| --- | --- |
| Orchards and market gardens (pre-1975) | Organochlorine pesticides (primarily DDT), lead arsenate |
| Timber treatment sites | Copper-chrome-arsenic (CCA) — arsenic is the main concern; pentachlorophenol (PCP) |
| Oil/gas/petroleum storage, service stations, vehicle workshops | Hydrocarbons — benzene and toluene (recent contamination), polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons or PAH (older contamination) |
| Landfills | Variable — depends on what was landfilled |

**How significant any identified contaminant may be:**

| Contaminant | Potential Health Risk |
| --- | --- |
| Arsenic | Does not degrade. Long-term low-level exposure may be linked to internal cancers (e.g., bladder, stomach) |
| DDT/DDE/DDD | Associated with preterm birth. Degrades over time — current concentrations at most NZ horticultural sites are not thought to cause ill-health |
| Lead | Main concern is neurodevelopmental effects in children. In adults, may affect blood pressure and kidney function. Blood lead levels ≥ 0.24 micromole/L require notification |
| Pentachlorophenol (PCP) | Associated with cancers (mainly from dioxin impurities). Not used since 1988 — current residues are expected to be insignificant |
| Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAH) | Associated with increased risk of various cancers. Degrades and loses toxicity over time |

**Your doctor will also ask about:**

- The primary route of exposure is ingestion of soil or home-grown produce (including soil attached to vegetables). Skin contact is a minor consideration.
- Timing and duration — the longer ago the contaminating activity occurred, the less bioavailable the contaminants generally are.
- Whether other family members are at risk, especially young children or pregnant women.
- If the contaminating activity occurred within 3 years of residential use, a wider range of contaminants (e.g., endosulfan, chlorpyrifos) may be considered.

---

## Investigations

- If exposure to lead (from contaminated land or other sources such as lead paint) is suspected, your doctor may arrange a **blood lead level** test.
- For most other contaminants, blood or urine tests are generally not useful — current clinical tests have limited value in this setting.

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following:

1. **Reassurance** — for most old land contamination, human exposure and health risk are low. With the exception of arsenic, there is little documented evidence of health effects from most individual contaminants in non-occupational exposures.

2. **Information about your property** — encourage seeking specific information about potential contaminants. Having as much detail as possible is important, as investigating possible harm can be difficult.

3. **Advice on minimising risk**, particularly for young children and pregnant women:
   - Normal daily activities on grassed or paved properties that do not disturb soil are very low risk.
   - Take care when disturbing soil — wear garden gloves, wash hands, keep soil away from children's mouths.
   - Wash vegetables thoroughly before eating.
   - Do not eat eggs from free-range chickens unless confident the site is uncontaminated.
   - If activities regularly disturb the soil and the site is on the Listed Land Use Register, consider having site testing done.

4. **Discussion with treating specialists** — if you already have an existing condition (e.g., cancer), your doctor may discuss the relevance of any contaminant exposure with your specialist.

5. **Specialist referral** — currently there are no specialists available for referral if significant exposure is found. Community and Public Health services are considering what further testing may be useful.
