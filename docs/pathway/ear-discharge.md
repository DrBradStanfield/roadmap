# Ear Discharge

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of ear discharge in adults and children — identifying the cause and determining appropriate treatment.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if:

- Cellulitis of the pinna (outer ear) and surrounding skin
- Mastoiditis — pinna pushed forward with fever above 38°C
- Signs of intracranial infection — fever and headache
- Facial palsy
- Severe pain

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will examine the ear with an otoscope and consider the likely cause based on the nature of the discharge.

### Conditions That Can Cause Ear Discharge

| Condition | Typical Features |
|-----------|-----------------|
| **Otitis externa** | Ear canal red, swollen, debris present. Variable discharge (bacterial, fungal, or allergic) |
| **Acute otitis media with perforated eardrum** | Severe ear pain, often significantly relieved when a whitish to yellow discharge begins |
| **Chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM) or cholesteatoma** | Past history of ear infections, perforated eardrum, recurrent or persistent discharge lasting more than 6 weeks. Not usually painful |
| **Discharging tympanostomy tube or grommets** | History of grommet insertion; discharge from the ear |
| **Foreign body in ear** | History of object in ear canal |
| **Skull fracture** | Clear, thin to bloody discharge with history of head injury |

If discharge is not responding to first-line treatment, your doctor may take a swab for microscopy and culture to guide antibiotic choice. This is particularly useful for recurrent discharge.

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following depending on the cause:

### Otitis Externa

Managed according to the otitis externa pathway — involves ear canal cleaning and topical drops.

### Acute Otitis Media

Managed according to the acute otitis media pathway.

### Discharging Tympanostomy Tubes or Grommets

Your doctor may:

- Advise **dry mopping** every 6 hours using tissue spears (twisted tissue paper) to keep the canal clear
- Refer for early **microsuction** (the preferred method for cleaning the ear canal; do not syringe with water)
- Prescribe **steroid and antibiotic ear drops**, which may include:
  - **Ciproxin HC** (ciprofloxacin with hydrocortisone) — 5 drops twice daily for 10 days. Not funded.
  - **Sofradex or Otodex** — partially funded. Recommended for a maximum of 2 days due to a small risk of ototoxicity (inner ear damage, approximately 1 in 1,000 to 1 in 10,000) if there is a direct pathway to the middle ear
  - **Locorten-Vioform** — funded. Also recommended for a maximum of 2 days for the same reason. Note: can stain hair, clothes, and linen
  - **Kenacomb** — too viscous to be effective; if prescribed, 2 days maximum only due to ototoxicity risk
  - For chronic perforations, non-ototoxic drops such as Ciproxin HC are preferred by local specialists
- Consider adding **oral antibiotics** if fever or systemic illness is present
- **Review after 1 week** — if no improvement, referral to an ear nurse specialist or ENT is considered

For recurrent grommet discharge lasting 8 weeks or more, your doctor will refer to ENT/otolaryngology for further assessment.

### Chronic Suppurative Otitis Media (CSOM)

Your doctor may:

- Clean the external ear canal — microsuction is the best method; cotton wool on a probe can gently mop thin secretions; do not syringe with water
- Advise **dry mopping** every 6 hours with tissue spears
- Prescribe **antibiotic and steroid ear drops**, used 3 to 4 times daily for 2 to 3 days after discharge has settled
  - **Ciprofloxacin drops** are considered most effective for CSOM. Ciprofloxacin is funded for second-line treatment of CSOM when endorsed accordingly
  - Ototoxic drops carry a small risk (1:1,000 to 1:10,000) of inner ear damage when used with a perforated eardrum or patent ventilation tube — your doctor will discuss the risks and benefits
- Guide treatment using swab culture results if available
- Use **Locacorten-Vioform** if fungal overgrowth is identified
- Refer to ENT if CSOM does not respond to treatment (e.g., due to aural polyps, cholesteatoma, or abnormal eardrum)

### How to Use Ear Drops (Patient Instructions)

Your doctor or nurse may advise:

1. Mop ear secretions with twisted tissue paper (tissue spears) or cotton wool before inserting drops
2. Lie with the affected ear pointing upward
3. Instil the prescribed number of drops without the dropper touching the ear
4. Apply gentle pressure to the tragus (the small flap in front of the ear canal), pressing several times in a pumping action to help the drops penetrate deeply
5. Stay in position for 3 minutes
6. Do not plug the canal after the drops

**Keeping the ear dry:** Use cotton wool coated in Vaseline in the entrance of the ear canal during water exposure, wear ear plugs, and use a shower cap when showering.

## When to Seek Specialist Care

Your doctor will consider urgent ENT referral if:

- Any red flags are present (see above)
- Otitis externa with severe pain and/or canal swelling at first presentation
- Non-responsive otitis externa
- Suspected cholesteatoma
- CSOM not responding to treatment

Non-urgent ENT referral may be considered for:

- Grommet discharge continuing for 8 or more weeks
- Aural polyps
- Myringitis (inflammation of the eardrum)
- Abnormal eardrum with persistent discharge
- Tympanic membrane perforation with frequent or bothersome discharge
- Significant hearing impairment affecting use of hearing aids
