# Epilepsy in Children and Young People

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the ongoing management of epilepsy in children and young people aged under 15 years with an established diagnosis. Initial diagnosis and seizure assessment are managed by a paediatrician; this guidance supports shared primary care follow-up.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Attention

Contact a doctor promptly if:

- New onset of developmental regression
- New onset of focal neurological symptoms or signs
- Rash after starting or increasing an anti-seizure medication (ASM)

---

## Background

### What is epilepsy?

Epilepsy is a neurological condition defined by any of:

- At least 2 unprovoked (or reflex) epileptic seizures more than 24 hours apart
- One unprovoked seizure with a 60% or greater probability of further seizures
- Diagnosis of a recognised epilepsy syndrome

The cause can be genetic, structural, infectious, immunological, metabolic, or unknown.

### Epilepsy syndromes

An epilepsy syndrome diagnosis helps guide prognosis, choice of anti-seizure medication (ASM), and understanding of the underlying cause. Syndromes are defined based on age of seizure onset, seizure types, developmental progression, neurological examination, EEG, and neuroimaging.

## Ongoing Management

Your doctor will share ongoing care with the child's paediatrician. In primary care, this typically involves:

### Annual Review (Minimum)

Your doctor will schedule at least one annual review in addition to specialist appointments. More frequent reviews may be needed if:

- The child is having frequent or poorly controlled seizures
- There is an underlying medical condition
- There are other concerns about development or behaviour

### What Your Doctor Will Check

**Seizure control:**
- Ask about any seizures since the last visit — type, frequency, duration

**Medication side effects:**
- Your doctor will check for side effects of anti-seizure medications (ASMs)

**Emergency planning:**
- Ensure all caregivers (including teachers) understand the first aid emergency plan for seizures

**Missed doses and sick days:**
- Your doctor will check whether you know what to do if a dose is missed or the child is unwell

**Mood and behaviour:**
- Sensitively ask about any changes in mood or behaviour

**Growth and development:**
- Review growth and developmental progress at each visit

**Psychosocial wellbeing:**
- Ask about the possibility of bullying or social isolation
- Check whether the child and family have adequate support

**Management plan:**
- Update the child's personal written management plan

## Medication Guidance

### Sick Days

Anti-seizure medications (ASMs) must be continued even when a child is unwell.

- If a child vomits within 1 hour of taking a dose, give a single replacement dose
- If the child vomits again within 1 hour of the replacement dose, do not give another dose — contact a healthcare professional for advice

### Blood Tests

Routine blood tests are not needed. Your doctor may consider testing if:

- The child is on phenytoin and doses are being adjusted, or there are concerns about subtherapeutic levels
- There are symptoms or signs suggesting medication side effects (e.g., bruising with sodium valproate)
- Breakthrough seizures are occurring with signs of non-compliance

If drug levels are required, blood should be taken immediately before the next scheduled dose.

### Common Anti-Seizure Medications Used in Children

Medications your doctor may consider or that a specialist may have prescribed include:

- Carbamazepine
- Clobazam
- Ethosuximide
- Lamotrigine
- Levetiracetam
- Sodium valproate
- Phenytoin
- Phenobarbitone

Your doctor can advise on monitoring, missed dose guidance, and side effects for each medication.

## Potential Co-Morbidities

Children with epilepsy have higher rates of certain co-existing conditions. Your doctor will screen for:

- Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD)
- Autism spectrum disorder (ASD)
- Mood disorders
- Learning disabilities or intellectual disability
- Cerebral palsy or other motor disorders
- Movement disorders

---

## Managing Specific Concerns

### Rash After Starting an Anti-Seizure Medication

A drug-induced rash typically appears 2 to 8 weeks after starting or increasing the dose of an ASM. It is most commonly associated with:

- Carbamazepine
- Phenytoin
- Lamotrigine
- Phenobarbitone

The rash is typically maculopapular and can be associated with swollen lymph nodes and enlarged liver or spleen. It can be difficult to distinguish from a viral rash. In severe cases it can be life-threatening.

**If your doctor suspects the rash is medication-related, they will seek urgent paediatric advice.**

### Behavioural Change or Drowsiness

Your doctor will consider whether any change in behaviour is related to a recent change in ASM or dose:

- If unrelated to medication, other causes will be investigated
- If related to medication, your doctor may consider slowing the dose increase or returning to the previous dose

### Breakthrough Seizures or Increasing Seizure Frequency

Your doctor will check whether medication is being taken as prescribed:

- If compliance is adequate, your doctor will review the paediatrician's treatment escalation plan — or seek specialist advice if none exists
- If the child has started having a new type of seizure, urgent paediatric assessment will be arranged

### Seizures With Concerning Features

Urgent specialist review is needed for:

- More than 3 seizures
- A seizure lasting longer than 30 minutes
- New focal neurological signs
- A new seizure type
- Seizure frequency worsening despite anti-seizure medication

### Developmental Regression or Lack of Progress

If a child shows developmental regression or is not progressing as expected, your doctor will arrange urgent paediatric review.

## If the Child Menstruates

Your doctor will follow a separate pathway for epilepsy management in the context of menstruation and reproductive health considerations.
