# Pertussis (Whooping Cough)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of pertussis (whooping cough), including treatment of cases and protection of close contacts at risk of severe illness.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department if:

- Infant or child younger than 6 months with suspected pertussis
- Child with chronic heart or lung disease and suspected pertussis
- Episodes of apnoea (breathing pauses) or cyanosis (blue discolouration)

These situations require urgent assessment by a paediatric or medical team.

---

## Background

### About Pertussis

- Pertussis is a highly infectious respiratory disease caused by *Bordetella pertussis* and spread through aerosolised droplets from coughing.
- It is the most common vaccine-preventable acute respiratory disease in New Zealand. Epidemics occur every 3 to 5 years because protection decreases over time after infection or immunisation.
- Natural infection does not provide long-term protection — repeat infection is possible.
- The incubation period is usually 7 to 10 days (range 5 to 21 days).
- Untreated people are infectious for 21 days from just before cough onset.

### Why Infants Are at Greatest Risk

- Infants are at greatest risk of severe disease and hospitalisation. The disease is harder to diagnose in infants because the presentation is less typical, and other illnesses (e.g. bronchiolitis) overlap significantly.
- About 60% of infants with pertussis require hospital admission. Of those:
  - 30% are in hospital for more than 1 week.
  - 10% are admitted to the Paediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU).

### Vaccination

- Immunity takes 10 to 14 days to develop after vaccination. Protection usually lasts 5 to 10 years in children.
- The best protection for infants is maternal vaccination in the second trimester of each pregnancy.
- Vaccination after exposure does not prevent the current infection but provides protection from further exposures.

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a careful history, because pertussis can be difficult to distinguish from other respiratory infections. They will consider age, immunisation status, and previous infection.

**Characteristic symptoms your doctor will ask about:**

- Paroxysms (sudden bursts) of coughing
- Cough ending in vomiting
- Inspiratory whoop (a high-pitched sound when breathing in after a coughing fit) — more typical in younger children
- Apnoea or cyanosis (in infants younger than 12 months)

**How the illness typically progresses:**

- Starts as an insidious upper respiratory tract infection with cough.
- Paroxysmal cough develops within 1 to 2 weeks and typically lasts 1 to 2 months, gradually becoming less frequent and less severe.
- Paroxysms are often triggered by drinking, eating, movement, or distress, and can be worse at night.
- Between paroxysms, patients usually appear and examine as normal.

**In infants, the presentation is different:**

- May have cough followed by apnoea rather than a whoop.
- Gagging, gasping, cyanosis, apnoea (breathing paused more than 15 seconds), poor feeding, or seizures are more common — these can precede the paroxysmal cough stage.
- Fever is generally minimal.

**Physical examination:**

- Most children examine normally between coughing spasms.
- Your doctor will assess general appearance, vital signs, respiratory system (looking for difficulty breathing, signs of pneumonia, apnoea), and hydration status.

**Assessment of respiratory distress:**

| | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| Airway | Stridor on exertion or crying; wheeze present | Some stridor at rest; wheeze marked | Stridor at rest; wheeze severe; silent chest |
| Behaviour and feeding | Normal; talks in sentences | Some irritability; difficulty talking or feeding | Increased irritability or lethargy; looks exhausted; unable to feed |
| Accessory muscle use | Mild intercostal and suprasternal recession | Moderate recession; tracheal tug; nasal flaring | Marked intercostal and suprasternal recession |
| Other | — | May have brief apnoea | Gasping, grunting; extreme pallor, cyanosis; increasingly frequent or prolonged apnoea |

**Diagnostic testing:**

- Your doctor may collect a nasopharyngeal swab for Bordetella pertussis PCR (the preferred test). PCR is most sensitive in the first 2 weeks; it is unlikely to detect pertussis after 4 weeks.
- A negative test does not exclude pertussis — your doctor may treat on clinical suspicion without waiting for results.
- Serology is not recommended.

## Management

### Treatment of the Infection

Your doctor will discuss the need for **hospital assessment** if:

- The patient is an infant or child younger than 12 months
- There are episodes of apnoea or cyanosis, suspected pneumonia, or encephalopathy
- The child has chronic heart or lung disease
- There is, or is at risk of, severe dehydration
- There is concern about the ability to recognise or respond to deterioration

For adult patients with complicated or severe illness (e.g. episodes of cyanosis, suspected pneumonia or encephalopathy), your doctor may seek a general medicine assessment.

**Antibiotics:**

Your doctor will start antibiotics on suspicion, without waiting for test results. Antibiotics reduce the infectious period from 21 days to less than 5 days. They do not alter the course of illness unless started within a few days of symptom onset, but your doctor may still consider them to prevent spread.

Your doctor may consider the following antibiotics:

- **Azithromycin** (first-line, funded for all ages):
  - Babies younger than 6 months: azithromycin 10 mg/kg (maximum 500 mg) orally once daily for 5 days. Parents should seek review if the infant develops vomiting — macrolides in the first 6 weeks of age are associated with hypertrophic pyloric stenosis.
  - Children aged 6 months or older: azithromycin 10 mg/kg (maximum 500 mg) on day 1, then 5 mg/kg (maximum 250 mg) daily for a further 4 days.
  - Older children and adults (including pregnant or breastfeeding patients): azithromycin 500 mg day 1, then 250 mg daily for a further 4 days.
- **Trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole (co-trimoxazole)** if azithromycin is contraindicated:
  - Children aged 2 months or older: 24 mg/kg (maximum 960 mg) twice daily for 14 days. Contraindicated in infants younger than 2 months.
  - Adults: 960 mg twice daily for 14 days.
- **Erythromycin** for 14 days is a third-line alternative.

**Isolation:**

Your doctor will advise isolation until no longer infectious:

- After 2 days of azithromycin, or
- After 5 days of trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole or erythromycin, or
- 21 days after the onset of any cough without antibiotic treatment, or
- 14 days after the onset of paroxysmal cough without antibiotic treatment.

This means staying away from work, school, preschool, and childcare — and avoiding settings where there are infants, pregnant people, or others at risk.

**Supportive care:**

- No medication has been found to reduce the paroxysms.
- Your doctor will advise simple supportive measures as for a post-viral cough.
- Ensure carers of infants are aware of symptoms that need urgent clinical review, including apnoea, cyanosis, and difficulty feeding.

### Management of Close Contacts

A close contact is someone who has been within 2 metres of a case for 1 hour or more during the infectious period.

The goal is to protect infants, pregnant women, and people at risk of severe illness. Your doctor may consider **prophylactic antibiotics** (same regimen as treatment) for close contacts:

| Close contact | Recommendation |
|---|---|
| Infants younger than 6 months (regardless of immunisation status) | Should receive prophylactic antibiotics |
| Infants aged 6 to 12 months with fewer than 3 doses of pertussis-containing vaccine | Should receive prophylactic antibiotics |
| People with a health condition that may be worsened by pertussis (e.g. chronic respiratory or immunocompromising conditions) | Your doctor may consider prophylactic antibiotics on a case-by-case basis |
| People who work with or care for high-risk individuals, or live with a household infant under 12 months or pregnant person | Should receive prophylactic antibiotics if not vaccinated within the last 5 years |
| Pregnant people in their third trimester | Should receive prophylactic antibiotics |

Close contacts should be advised that if they become symptomatic, they should seek medical review and isolate until no longer infectious.

**Vaccination for close contacts:**

Your doctor may offer pertussis vaccination to any unimmunised or partially immunised close contacts, especially infants younger than 12 months and pregnant patients. Vaccination after exposure does not prevent the current infection but provides protection from further exposures.

### Prevention

Your doctor can discuss pertussis vaccination, which is the most effective way to prevent the disease.

**Funded vaccination includes:**

- Pregnant patients during every pregnancy (recommended from 16 weeks' gestation) — primarily to protect the infant.
- Infants and children according to the immunisation schedule.
- Adults with Boostrix (dTap) from age 45 years (if fewer than 4 previous tetanus doses) and from age 65 years.
- Parents or primary caregivers of infants admitted to neonatal intensive or special care baby units for more than 3 days where the mother had not received Tdap at least 14 days before birth.
- Previously unimmunised or partially immunised patients.
