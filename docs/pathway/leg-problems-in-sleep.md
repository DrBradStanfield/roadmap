# Leg Problems in Sleep (RLS, PLMS, Nocturnal Leg Cramps)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of three common nocturnal leg conditions: restless legs syndrome (RLS), periodic limb movements of sleep (PLMS), and nocturnal leg cramps.

> **Note (March 2025):** A new international guideline has been released which may alter management for RLS/PLMS. Your doctor will apply the most current evidence when advising you.

> **Always discuss with your doctor.** This information is to help you understand what your doctor may assess and consider — it is not a substitute for professional medical advice.

---

## Background

### RLS and PLMS

RLS and PLMS are part of the same spectrum of nocturnal movement disorders and are common causes of insomnia and daytime sleepiness.

- It is important to distinguish RLS and PLMS from other movements during sleep, especially the restlessness that often accompanies sleep apnoea.
- PLMS is defined by findings on a sleep study. It may be associated with other sleep disorders such as obstructive sleep apnoea and is more common in older adults.
- Periodic limb movement **disorder** is a relatively rare condition: it requires both PLMS on a sleep study AND nocturnal symptoms not explained by other causes.

### Nocturnal Leg Cramps

- Very common, affecting up to 40% of people over 50, and becoming more frequent with age.
- Roughly half of affected people experience cramps between 3 times per week to every night.
- A seasonal pattern has been reported (summer episodes approximately double winter episodes) but the reason is unknown.

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess which condition is present by taking a history, examining you, and arranging investigations.

### Diagnosing RLS

The essential diagnostic criteria for RLS are:

1. **An urge to move the legs** (sometimes also arms or other body parts), usually accompanied by uncomfortable or unpleasant sensations.
2. The urge or sensations **begin or worsen during rest or inactivity**.
3. They are **partially or totally relieved by movement** (e.g. walking, stretching), at least while active.
4. They are **worse in the evening or night** than during the day, or occur only in the evening or night.

A family history of RLS is present in approximately 40% of primary RLS cases.

### Identifying PLMS

PLMS features include:

- Disturbed sleep or daytime fatigue with repetitive flexing movements of the legs (occasionally the arms)
- Dorsiflexion and fanning of the toes
- Often disturbs a bed partner
- May impair sleep quality without necessarily waking the patient
- 80% of people with RLS will also have PLMS detected on a sleep study

### Identifying Nocturnal Leg Cramps

- Sudden, painful contractions of the calf, thigh, or foot muscles
- Usually occur during sleep or on waking from sleep
- Last for minutes
- Relieved by stretching, but pain may persist for up to 72 hours after the cramp

### Medications That May Trigger or Worsen Symptoms

Your doctor will review your medication list, as several common medicines can contribute:

- Tricyclic antidepressants
- SSRIs (e.g. fluoxetine, sertraline)
- SNRIs (e.g. venlafaxine, duloxetine)
- Antipsychotics (e.g. haloperidol, quetiapine, olanzapine)
- Anti-emetics (e.g. prochlorperazine)
- Sedating antihistamines
- Diuretics, LABAs (for leg cramps)

### Possible Underlying Causes

**For RLS and PLMS:**
- Iron deficiency (even low-normal ferritin)
- Pregnancy
- High caffeine intake
- Renal disease
- Diabetes
- Parkinson's disease
- Venous insufficiency
- Connective tissue disorders
- Multiple sclerosis
- Medications that reduce dopamine activity

**For nocturnal leg cramps:**
- Idiopathic (no identifiable cause)
- Flat feet or hypermobility
- Prolonged sitting in a cramped position, or standing on concrete
- Electrolyte or fluid abnormalities (low magnesium, low calcium, excessive sweating, dialysis)
- High alcohol intake
- Hypothyroidism
- Neurological conditions (Parkinson's disease, motor neurone disease, neuropathy)
- Pregnancy, diabetes

### Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- **Blood tests:** Full blood count, iron studies (including ferritin), renal function, fasting glucose
- **Additional tests if indicated:** Thyroid function, vitamin B12, folate, calcium, magnesium, autoantibodies

### Alternative Diagnoses to Exclude

- Peripheral vascular disease
- Myoclonic jerks (common and normal in early sleep in about one-third of people)
- Peripheral neuropathy
- Neurological disorders (multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease, ALS)
- Dystonias or nerve root compression
- REM sleep behaviour disorder
- Sleep apnoea (can cause restlessness mimicking RLS/PLMS)

## Management

### Restless Legs Syndrome (RLS) and PLMS

#### Step 1: Reassurance and Education

- Treatment is not always required for mild symptoms.
- RLS is **not** a precursor to Parkinson's disease — your doctor can reassure you of this.
- Treatment is generally considered when there is evidence of disturbed sleep (delayed sleep onset, frequent waking, unrefreshing sleep) or daytime fatigue affecting normal functioning.

#### Step 2: Address Underlying Causes

- If iron deficiency is identified (or ferritin is low-normal), your doctor may recommend iron supplementation with the aim of raising ferritin above 75 micrograms/L.
- Review and modify contributing medications where possible.

#### Step 3: Non-Pharmacological Measures

For mild or infrequent symptoms, lifestyle changes alone may be sufficient:

- Regular physical exercise and good sleep habits
- Walking, leg massage, or a warm bath before bed
- Reducing or avoiding caffeine, nicotine, and alcohol
- Compression stockings (may reduce symptoms)
- Distracting activities such as reading before bed

#### Step 4: Non-Dopaminergic Medications

If lifestyle changes are insufficient, your doctor may consider:

- **Codeine** 30–60 mg before bed
- **Clonazepam** (starting at 0.25 mg, maximum 1 mg at night) — particularly if other sleep disturbances coexist. Note: can worsen obstructive sleep apnoea and has a long half-life (risk of morning drowsiness, unsteadiness). Used short-term and intermittently due to tolerance risk.
- **Gabapentin** 100–300 mg two hours before bed, titrated up towards 900 mg/day or higher

#### Step 5: Dopaminergic Medications (Moderate–Severe Symptoms)

If symptoms are moderate to severe and not settling with the above, your doctor may trial dopaminergic therapy:

**General principles:**
- Medications are usually given 1–3 hours before bed, timed to symptom onset
- Start low, titrate slowly
- Never stop abruptly — taper over at least a month (risk of neuroleptic malignant syndrome)
- Doses used for RLS are much lower than those used for Parkinson's disease
- Common side effects: nausea, orthostatic hypotension

**Dopamine agonists (first-line for severe daily RLS):**

*Pramipexole* (tends to be better tolerated):
- Start 0.125 mg, 2–3 hours before symptoms or bedtime
- Titrate in 0.125 mg increments every 4–7 days
- Use the lowest effective dose
- If no response at 1 mg, reconsider diagnosis and consider specialist referral

*Ropinirole:*
- Start 0.25 mg, 30–60 minutes before symptoms or bedtime
- Increase by 0.25 mg weekly until relief
- Use the lowest effective dose
- If no response at 2 mg, reconsider diagnosis and consider specialist referral

> **Important:** Both pramipexole and ropinirole can cause impulse control disorders (compulsive gambling, binge eating, hypersexuality, obsessive-compulsive behaviours). Patients and partners should be informed of this risk, and your doctor will check for these symptoms at each prescription review.

**Dopamine precursors (if agonists not tolerated, for intermittent symptoms only):**

- Levodopa/carbidopa (Sinemet) or levodopa/benserazide (Madopar), starting at 50 mg/day, 1–2 hours before bed, titrated to 100–200 mg/day
- Avoid regular daily use — this increases the risk of augmentation
- Best suited for patients with intermittent rather than nightly symptoms

#### Watch for Augmentation and Rebound

These are important complications of dopaminergic therapy:

**Augmentation** (up to 80% of patients on long-term dopaminergic therapy):
- Symptoms shift to earlier in the day, become more severe, spread to other limbs
- Worsens if the dose is raised; improves on reducing or stopping the drug
- Risk factors: levodopa doses above 200 mg/day, timing doses too early before bed
- Management: lower the dopaminergic dose, consider a drug holiday, or switch drug

**Rebound:**
- Symptoms return in the early morning or late night as the drug wears off
- Related to the drug's half-life
- May be addressed with a longer-acting preparation or divided dosing

If breakthrough pain occurs while on levodopa, adding codeine 30–60 mg before bed may help.

#### When a Sleep Study Is Needed

RLS is a clinical diagnosis. Confirmation of PLMS requires a sleep study. Your doctor can discuss whether this is needed.

### Nocturnal Leg Cramps

#### Acute Relief

When a cramp occurs:
- Forcefully stretch the affected muscle (e.g. pull toes towards you for a calf cramp)
- Walk or jiggle the leg, then elevate it
- Apply ice massage to the leg
- Direct a warm or hot stream of water at the cramped area, or have a warm bath

#### Prevention — Conservative Measures (Try First)

- **Daily calf stretching before bed:** Stand about 1 metre from a wall, lean in with palms on the wall, legs straight, feet flat on the floor. Hold for 10–20 seconds, rest, repeat 3–4 times.
- If inactive during the day, try a few minutes of sedentary cycling before bed
- Keep bedding loose around feet and legs
- Reduce or avoid caffeine and alcohol
- Maintain adequate hydration

#### Address Identifiable Causes

- Correct electrolyte or fluid abnormalities
- Reduce or stop medications that precipitate cramping or dehydration (e.g. diuretics) where possible

#### Medications — What to Avoid

**Do not use quinine.** It has been associated with serious and potentially fatal blood disorders (thrombocytopenia, haemolytic uraemic syndrome, thrombotic thrombocytopenic purpura). New Zealand's Medsafe has advised against its use since 2007 — even low-dose quinine in tonic water is discouraged.

#### Medications — What May Help (Limited Evidence)

The evidence base is limited, but options that may be considered include:

- Calcium, magnesium, high-dose vitamin B6 or B12 supplements (note: some over-the-counter cramp remedies containing ginkgo may raise INR in patients on warfarin)
- Magnesium supplements (may help particularly in pregnancy)
- Iron replacement if iron-deficiency anaemia is present
- Off-label options (limited evidence): nortriptyline, diltiazem or verapamil (not nifedipine), orphenadrine, gabapentin

## When to Refer to a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you to a sleep physician or neurologist if:

- RLS or PLMS is **severe or resistant to treatment**
- You develop **augmentation or rebound** on dopaminergic therapy
- RLS or PLMS appears to be secondary to a **progressive neurological condition** (e.g. Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis) — neurology referral
- **Confirmation of PLMS by sleep study** is required — private sleep assessment
- **Persistent distressing nocturnal leg cramps** despite optimal management — sleep assessment to exclude underlying causes
