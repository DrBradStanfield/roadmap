# Tibial Shaft Fractures

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial assessment and management of tibial shaft fractures in adults and children.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department immediately if there are signs of:

- **Open fracture** — any wound near the fracture site
- **Neurovascular compromise** — absent pulses, loss of sensation, or pallor/coldness of the foot
- **Acute compartment syndrome** — increasing pain despite immobilisation, muscle tenderness, or swelling (see below)
- **Displaced fracture in a child** — requires urgent orthopaedic assessment for possible surgery

---

## Background

**In adults:**
- Often caused by direct blows (motor vehicle accidents, falls from height)
- Spiral fractures may result from violent twisting (contact sports, skiing)
- Usually associated with fractures of the fibula
- Often open due to thin overlying skin — soft tissue can be compromised even in closed fractures

**In children:**
- Distal spiral tibia fractures are common in children aged 9 months to 3 years (called **toddler's fractures**)
- Occur with minor trauma when learning to walk
- May be difficult to see on initial X-ray
- Second most common fractured bone following non-accidental (inflicted) injury

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will typically:

1. Check whether the fracture is open — any wound near the fracture site suggests an open fracture
2. Assess neurovascular status, including the popliteal artery
3. Carefully examine the ankle, knee, and hip joints
4. Arrange X-rays of the knee and ankle joints to rule out other fractures; hip X-ray if high-impact injury
5. In children: suspect tibial shaft fracture if a child limps or fails to weight-bear, or if there is warmth or swelling over the tibia
6. If uncertain, arrange follow-up X-rays in 2 weeks to look for callus formation confirming the diagnosis

### Acute Compartment Syndrome

Your doctor may assess for compartment syndrome, which usually presents within 48 hours of injury. It is caused by raised pressure within a closed fascial compartment, leading to reduced blood flow and muscle damage.

Signs your doctor will look for:
- Increasing pain despite immobilisation of the fracture
- Pain on passive movement of the toes
- Muscle tenderness and swelling
- Sensory deficit in the distribution of nerves passing through the compartment
- Weakness of toe or ankle movement
- Reduced or absent peripheral pulses (a later sign)

**Anterior compartment:** weakness of toe extension and ankle dorsiflexion, pain on passive toe flexion, numbness between the first and second toes (deep peroneal nerve)

**Posterior compartment:** weakness of toe flexion and ankle inversion, pain on passive toe extension, numbness on the sole of the foot

Your doctor will remove all casts, splints, and dressings to fully assess the limb if compartment syndrome is suspected.

---

## Management

### Initial management (all patients)

Your doctor will:
- Provide pain relief
- Cover any open wounds
- Apply a splint or backslab above the knee before transport to hospital

**Splinting principles your doctor follows:**
- Immobilise the joints above and below the fracture
- Never apply a circumferential (full) cast acutely, as this can restrict circulation
- Pad all bony prominences
- Check neurovascular function before and after splinting
- Elevate the limb; use crutches to prevent weight-bearing

### Adults

Your doctor will arrange acute orthopaedic assessment for all adults with tibial shaft fractures, unless the fracture is a very minor distal tibia fracture.

### Children

Your doctor will provide adequate pain relief and will determine whether the fracture is displaced or undisplaced:

- **Displaced fractures:** Your doctor will arrange acute orthopaedic assessment for possible surgery
- **Undisplaced spiral fractures:** Your doctor may manage with casting:
  - In babies and toddlers: above-knee split plaster of Paris (POP) cast for 4 weeks, with cast change or completion at 1 week
  - Aged 5–10 years: above-knee POP cast for 5–6 weeks (cast change at 1 week), then possibly a patella-bearing cast for a further 2 weeks
  - Older than 10 years: above-knee POP cast for 6 weeks, then a patella-bearing cast for 4 weeks if there are signs of bone healing on X-ray; admission may be considered for elevation and pain management

### Compartment syndrome

If acute compartment syndrome is suspected, your doctor will arrange urgent orthopaedic assessment for consideration of surgical decompression (fasciotomy).

### Allied health and rehabilitation

Your doctor may consider referral to:
- **Physiotherapy** — if there is a strength, range of motion, proprioceptive, or functional deficit, or if pain is a significant feature
- **Occupational therapy** — if there is difficulty with everyday activities such as self-care, household management, driving, or work
- **Work rehabilitation** — if returning to work or sustaining work tasks is a concern
