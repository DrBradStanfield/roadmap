# B-Cell Chronic Lymphocytic Leukaemia (B-CLL)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and ongoing management of confirmed B-CLL in general practice.

---

## Background

B-CLL is characterised by a progressive accumulation of monoclonal lymphocytes and requires immunophenotyping for diagnosis. Key features of early-stage B-CLL:

- Low-grade B-lymphoproliferative disorder
- Most common type of adult leukaemia
- Mainly found in those older than 50 years
- Low risk of disease progression in the 5 years after diagnosis
- Median survival of more than 12 years
- Usually progresses slowly with increasing lymphocytosis, bone marrow involvement with cytopenias, lymphadenopathy, hepatomegaly, and splenomegaly

Other complications include:

- Autoimmune haemolytic anaemia
- Immune thrombocytopenia
- Hypogammaglobulinaemia with recurrent respiratory tract infections
- Herpes zoster
- Transformation to high-grade disease

The word "leukaemia" can generate significant anxiety. It is helpful to discuss the benign nature of the disease in its early stage, the lack of treatment needed in most cases, and its generally good prognosis. Your doctor can talk this through with you.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you if you have a confirmed clonal B-cell population or confirmed B-CLL.

**History — your doctor will ask about:**
- Unexplained fever
- Unexplained weight loss (e.g., more than 10% in 6 months)
- Night sweats
- Fatigue
- Recent infections

**Examination — your doctor will check for:**
- Lymphadenopathy in axillae, groin, and neck
- Hepatomegaly and splenomegaly

**Investigations your doctor may arrange:**
- Immunophenotyping (if no previous result on record) — to confirm the diagnosis
- FBC
- Vitamin B12 and folate
- Creatinine
- Liver function tests (LFTs)
- Haemolysis screen (if anaemic), including Coomb's test
- Serum immunoglobulins
- Protein electrophoresis
- Urate
- LDH

## Referral to Haematology

Your doctor will refer you to a haematologist if any of the following are present at initial presentation or during follow-up:

- Significant symptoms — night sweats for more than 1 month without evidence of infection, significant weight loss, or extreme fatigue (after excluding other causes such as infection)
- Symptomatic lymphadenopathy or hepatosplenomegaly
- Disease found in 3 or more of 5 sites (axillae, groin, neck, liver, spleen)
- Blood test changes not otherwise explained:
  - Haemoglobin less than 100 × 10⁹/L
  - Platelets 100 × 10⁹/L or lower
  - Neutrophils less than 0.8 × 10⁹/L
- Progressive lymphocytosis of 30 × 10⁹/L or more with:
  - An increase of 50% or more over 2 months, or
  - Lymphocyte doubling time (LDT) less than 6 months (without other contributing factors such as infection or steroids)
- Immunophenotyping confirms a clonal population other than B-CLL
- Any other clinical concerns related to B-CLL

## Ongoing Monitoring (Early B-CLL)

Early B-CLL has a low risk of disease progression. Your doctor will monitor you in general practice if your disease is stable.

**Review schedule:**
- Every 6 months for the first year
- Then yearly if stable or with slow, asymptomatic progression

**At each review, your doctor will:**
- Arrange an FBC
  - Note: the rise in white blood cells matters less than the development of cytopenias, night sweats, or weight loss
  - The absolute lymphocyte count can rise above 200 × 10⁹/L — this alone is not a reason to start treatment
  - Immunophenotyping does not need to be repeated once B-CLL is confirmed
- Ask about infections, weight loss, fatigue, night sweats, and enlarged lymph nodes
- Check your weight and examine for lymphadenopathy and hepatosplenomegaly
- Screen for other malignancies — people with B-CLL have an increased risk of other cancers, particularly non-melanoma skin cancers (which can progress rapidly). Age-appropriate screening for breast, prostate, and colon cancer is recommended.

**If you develop a painful rash or any sign of infection**, contact your doctor promptly. After an infection, your lymphocyte count may temporarily rise — your doctor may repeat the FBC about 1 month after the infection has resolved.

## Vaccinations

Vaccinations are an important part of managing B-CLL. Your doctor will offer and recall you for:

- **Annual influenza vaccination**
- **Pneumococcal vaccination** — Prevenar 13 (PCV13) is preferred over Pneumovax 23 (23PPV) due to better immunogenic response
- **Shingrix (recombinant shingles vaccine)**:
  - People with B-CLL are at increased risk of shingles
  - Shingrix is safe in immunocompromised patients and is funded for adults aged 18 and over with a haematological malignancy (including CLL)
  - Given as two doses, 2 to 6 months apart
- **RSV vaccine (Arexvy)** if aged 60 years or older:
  - People with haematological malignancies are at increased risk of severe RSV infection
  - Arexvy is licensed for adults 60 and over but is currently unfunded — you may wish to discuss self-funding with your doctor

**Live vaccines are contraindicated** in B-CLL due to the risk of disseminated infection. The following must not be given:

- Zostavax (live shingles vaccine)
- Measles, mumps, rubella (MMR)
- Varicella
- BCG
- Oral typhoid
- Yellow fever
- Japanese encephalitis
- Rotavirus
