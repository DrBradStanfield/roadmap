# Fibromyalgia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: May 2024.*

Fibromyalgia is a combination of chronic pain and disabling fatigue. People with fibromyalgia experience pain differently because of dysfunctional pain processing in the central nervous system. It is a pain processing problem in the brain, not a musculoskeletal condition.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Concerning Features

**See your doctor if you have any of these.** They may point to another significant condition that needs investigating:
- Symptoms that started at an older age.
- Weight loss.
- Night pain.
- Pain focused in one place.
- Fever or sweats.
- Neurological symptoms.
- A past history of cancer.

---

## Background

- Fibromyalgia is very common, with a prevalence of around 2%. Women are affected more than men (ratio 7:1). The prevalence in Māori and European New Zealanders is similar.
- It may also come with other symptoms, including poor sleep, cognitive disturbance ("fibrofog"), headache, abdominal pain, and stiffness.
- It commonly coexists with depression and/or anxiety, which are present in 30 to 50% of patients at the time of diagnosis.
- 65% improve over time. One third improve by 30% at 3 years.

## Assessment

### History

Your doctor may ask about:
- Widespread muscle and joint pain.
- Fatigue and sleep.
- Cognitive disturbances.
- Other symptoms (e.g., headache, pins and needles, abdominal pain).
- Pain relievers and other medications you use now or have tried before.
- Any concerning features (see above).

### Examination

Examination is usually normal, with no sign of joint or muscle inflammation. There may be tender points at specific soft tissue locations, though marked point tenderness is no longer needed for diagnosis.

### Conditions to Screen For and Treat First

- **Mood disorders**, such as depression and anxiety. At least one third of patients with fibromyalgia have a mood disorder at the time of diagnosis.
- **Sleep conditions**, such as obstructive sleep apnoea and restless legs.

### Other Conditions Your Doctor May Consider

Some conditions can look like fibromyalgia or occur alongside it:
- Nervous system conditions (e.g., multiple sclerosis, myasthenia, peripheral neuropathies, motor neurone disease): suspected if there are abnormal neurological signs or visual disturbance.
- Cancer or infection: suspected if there is fever, unexplained weight loss, or swollen lymph nodes.
- Depression or anxiety.
- Other central sensitisation syndromes (e.g., irritable bowel syndrome, myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome, tension headaches).
- Other pain syndromes (e.g., myofascial pain syndrome, pelvic pain, vulvodynia).
- Functional neurological disorder.
- Sleep disorders (e.g., obstructive sleep apnoea, restless legs).
- Rheumatological conditions (e.g., connective tissue disorders, polymyalgia rheumatica, rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, Sjogren's syndrome, psoriatic arthritis, myopathies): suspected if there is early morning joint stiffness lasting longer than 30 minutes, with or without joint swelling, Raynaud's phenomenon, dry eyes, dry mouth, rashes, visual disturbance, or eye pain.
- Infections (e.g., HIV, chronic hepatitis).
- Hormone conditions (e.g., thyroid, parathyroid, Cushing's).
- Medications that can cause diffuse muscle pain (e.g., statins, isotretinoin, prolonged use of some antibiotics, bisphosphonates, varenicline, aromatase inhibitors, oral diabetes medicines).

### Investigations

Fibromyalgia does not cause abnormal blood tests or imaging. Blood tests are done to exclude other conditions.

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Full blood count and ferritin (anaemia and nutrient deficiencies).
- Vitamin B12, only if there is a reason to test (e.g., unexplained anaemia, unexplained neurological symptoms, some gut disorders, a long-term vegan or vegetarian diet, prolonged use of metformin or acid-reducing medicines, or age older than 75 years).
- Kidney function, electrolytes, and liver tests.
- Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH).
- Calcium, and possibly vitamin D. Vitamin D testing is not usually needed before or after starting vitamin D supplements.
- Creatine kinase (CK).
- CRP. It can be slightly raised (15 to 30 mg/L) with higher BMI and in older people.
- ESR, if polymyalgia rheumatica is strongly suspected but CRP is normal.
- A chest X-ray.

Autoantibody tests (such as rheumatoid factor, CCP, ANA, ENA, dsDNA, ANCA) are not needed to investigate fibromyalgia. They are considered only if there are signs of a relevant autoimmune disease. Some of these antibodies are also common in people without inflammatory disease.

Your doctor makes the diagnosis using the fibromyalgia diagnostic criteria.

## Management

Most people can be managed by their GP. Self-management and coping strategies, and staying positive, are emphasised. Many people feel relief when they receive a diagnosis. Family, carers, and colleagues may also benefit from information.

Any mood or sleep disorders are treated first.

Your doctor may use the Revised Fibromyalgia Impact Questionnaire (FIQR) to measure severity and repeat it to check how treatment is working. It rates the 4 main symptoms: pain, depression, fatigue, and sleep disturbance. Recent studies suggest drug choices can be guided by which symptoms are most prominent.

### Non-Drug Treatments

Non-drug treatments can be beneficial and are the mainstay of treatment.

**Exercise:**
- Exercise can be of significant benefit for pain and function. A gradual, step-by-step cardiovascular fitness programme is advised.
- Low impact aerobic activities (e.g., fast walking, biking, swimming) or water aerobics are the most successful.
- Strength training, water exercises, Tai Chi, and yoga may be of some benefit.
- Expect some temporary increase in pain. Your doctor may provide pain relief.
- Some people need a tailored exercise programme or physiotherapy supervision. Others may benefit from community groups or a Green Prescription.

**Stress management:**
- Pain increases stress and tension, and stress increases pain. Daily stress management can help with symptoms.
- Strategies include deep breathing, relaxation, meditation, and mindfulness. Choose one and practise it regularly.
- Apps and websites: [Breathe 2 Relax](https://healthify.nz/apps/b/breathe2relax-app), [Beyond Blue relaxation exercises](https://www.beyondblue.org.au/mental-health/wellbeing/relaxation-exercises), [CALM](https://www.calm.auckland.ac.nz).

**Good sleep habits:**
- Go to bed and get up at the same time every day. Get into bed when drowsy and ready to sleep.
- Prioritise a consistent wake-up time, to increase your chance of bright morning light.
- Avoid daytime naps if possible. If you must nap, nap before 3.00 pm and only for 30 minutes.
- Do relaxing things before bed, such as listening to music or a bath (not too hot).
- Write things down to let go and let your mind relax.
- Avoid caffeine and alcohol before bed.

**Psychological therapies:**
- There is well-supported evidence for psychological therapies, particularly cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT).

### Medications

- Non-drug therapies are the mainstay of treatment. Medicines may be used early on, alongside non-drug treatments. Their benefit tends to decline over time.
- No drug treatment works for everyone.
- Corticosteroids and opioids should not be prescribed for fibromyalgia.

Your doctor may use the table below to choose a first or second choice medicine based on your main symptoms. Each cell pairs two symptoms and lists one or two options.

| | Pain | Depression | Fatigue | Sleep |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Pain** | Pregabalin, venlafaxine | Venlafaxine, pregabalin | Amitriptyline, pregabalin | Amitriptyline, pregabalin |
| **Depression** | Pregabalin, venlafaxine | Venlafaxine | Venlafaxine, pregabalin | Pregabalin |
| **Fatigue** | Amitriptyline, pregabalin | Venlafaxine, pregabalin | Amitriptyline | Amitriptyline, pregabalin |
| **Sleep** | Amitriptyline, pregabalin | Pregabalin | Amitriptyline, pregabalin | Amitriptyline |

### Other Support

- Occupational therapy, if there are concerns about safety or managing everyday activities such as self care, housework, work, study, or leisure.
- A health coach or health improvement practitioner, if your practice has one.
- [Complex Chronic Illness Support](https://ccisupport.org.nz/): education, facilitators, group support, advocacy, and online support for people affected by fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, long COVID, and POTS. You can self-refer.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you if:
- **Rheumatology:** the diagnosis is uncertain and there is marked disability or concern.
- **Mental health services:** there is significant depression or another psychological condition alongside fibromyalgia.
- **Specialised pain service:** severe disability and pain affect your quality of life or work, and/or there is no improvement at 3 months despite the treatment above.
