# Medications for Osteoporosis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the medications used to prevent fractures in osteoporosis. Choosing the right treatment depends on your bone density, fracture history, kidney function, and other health factors — your doctor will determine what is appropriate for you.

---

## Supplements

Before or alongside drug therapy, your doctor may recommend:

- **Calcium** — see your doctor about whether supplementation is needed
- **Vitamin D** — deficiency must be corrected before starting bisphosphonate infusions

---

## Bisphosphonates (First-Line)

Bisphosphonates are the first-line drug treatment for osteoporosis. They work by slowing bone breakdown.

### Who should not take oral bisphosphonates

Your doctor will check whether any of these apply before prescribing:

- Conditions that delay food moving through the oesophagus (e.g., stricture, achalasia)
- Inability to remain upright for at least 30 minutes after taking the tablet
- Low calcium levels (hypocalcaemia)
- Significant kidney impairment (eGFR ≤ 35 mL/min/1.73m²) — lower doses or IV forms may be needed

### Monitoring with P1NP

If you are on an oral bisphosphonate, your doctor will measure a blood marker called **P1NP** at 6 months:

- A level **below 35 microgram/L** means the medication is working adequately
- A level **at or above 35 microgram/L** suggests poor absorption or missed doses — your doctor may consider switching to an IV form

P1NP monitoring is **not needed** with zoledronic acid infusions, as absorption and compliance issues do not apply.

### Safety considerations for all bisphosphonates

- **Osteonecrosis of the jaw (ONJ):** Very rare. Good dental hygiene is important while on these medications.
- **Atypical femur fractures:** Rare case reports with long-term use. Tell your doctor about any thigh, hip, or groin pain. These fractures start as stress fractures and can progress — risk rises with duration of use and falls sharply within 1–2 years of stopping.
- **Drug holidays:** If you require bisphosphonates for more than 5 years, your doctor may recommend a planned break. See your doctor for guidance.

---

### Alendronate

- **Dose:** 70 mg orally, once a week
- Taken on an empty stomach, at least 30 minutes before breakfast, with a full glass of water
- Remain upright (standing or sitting) for at least 30 minutes after taking, and until after breakfast — this reduces the risk of severe oesophageal irritation
- P1NP monitored at 6 months (target < 35 microgram/L)

---

### Risedronate

- **Dose:** 35 mg orally, once a week
- Same instructions as alendronate: empty stomach, upright for 30 minutes, with plenty of water
- Similar fracture risk reduction to alendronate; considered more effective than etidronate
- If you develop swallowing difficulty or pain, stop the medication and contact your doctor
- P1NP monitored at 6 months (target < 35 microgram/L)

---

### Etidronate

- Largely superseded by newer bisphosphonates due to side-effects, compliance issues, and reduced effectiveness
- P1NP monitored at 6 months (target < 35 microgram/L)

---

### Zoledronic Acid Infusion

Zoledronic acid is given as an **intravenous (IV) infusion** — an option if you cannot tolerate oral bisphosphonates due to gut side-effects, or cannot remain upright for 30 minutes.

**Dosing schedule:**
- If you have had a recent low-trauma hip fracture, the first dose is given at least 2 weeks after hip fracture repair
- The first 3 doses are given at intervals of 18–24 months

**Side-effects:**
- Flu-like symptoms (muscle aches, fever) are common within 24–72 hours of the infusion — paracetamol or ibuprofen can help and symptoms usually lessen with each subsequent infusion
- Uveitis (eye inflammation) occurs in about 1% of patients
- Very rare: osteonecrosis of the jaw

**Kidney safety:**
- Good hydration before and after infusion is important
- Contraindicated if eGFR < 35 mL/min/1.73 m²
- Dose reduction or slower infusion may be considered for eGFR 35–50 mL/min/1.73 m²
- Vitamin D deficiency must be corrected before the infusion

No P1NP monitoring needed.

## Oestrogen Therapy (Menopausal Hormone Therapy)

- As effective as bisphosphonates for fracture prevention
- May be appropriate as first-line treatment for women within 10 years of menopause, particularly if menopausal symptoms are also present
- Benefits are lost quickly if oestrogen therapy is stopped
- Your doctor will discuss risks and benefits with you — see the Menopause Hormone Therapy pathway for full detail

**Contraindications include:**
- Past or current heart attack or stroke
- Breast cancer or hormone-sensitive tumours
- Liver tumours
- Past blood clots (venous thromboembolism) or inherited clotting disorders
- Severe liver disease

---

## Raloxifene

*Note: Raloxifene (Evista) is being discontinued — stock expected to run out by December 2025. If you are currently on raloxifene, discuss alternatives with your doctor.*

- For post-menopausal osteoporosis only
- **Dose:** 60 mg orally once a day
- Less effective than oestrogen or alendronate at preventing fractures — does **not** prevent hip fractures
- Side-effects include hot flushes and leg cramps
- Reduces breast cancer incidence by 60–70%
- Requires Pharmac special authority funding criteria to be met

---

## Teriparatide

Teriparatide is a fragment of parathyroid hormone that stimulates new bone formation — it is a bone-building rather than bone-preserving drug.

**Who it is for:**
- T-score ≤ -3, with a history of 2 or more low-trauma fractures, and at least 1 new symptomatic fracture, after 1 year of bisphosphonate treatment

**Before starting, your doctor will check:**
- Serum calcium (hypercalcaemia is a contraindication)
- Kidney function (eGFR < 35 mL/min/1.73 m² is a contraindication)

**Dosing:**
- 20 micrograms injected under the skin once a day, using a pre-filled pen device
- Up to 18 months subsidised (course can last up to 24 months)
- Requires Pharmac special authority approval

Your doctor may seek specialist advice before prescribing for the first time.

---

## Denosumab

Denosumab is a monoclonal antibody that blocks bone breakdown. It is injected every 6 months.

**Important safety information:**

- **Do not stop denosumab without planning ahead.** Bone loss accelerates very rapidly after stopping, and clusters of spinal fractures have been reported within months of discontinuation. Before starting, your doctor will discuss how long you will use it and what medication will follow when you stop.
- Compliance with every-6-month injections is crucial.

**Funding:**
- Funded in New Zealand under Pharmac special authority criteria — your doctor will assess eligibility. Specialist input may be needed to support the application.
