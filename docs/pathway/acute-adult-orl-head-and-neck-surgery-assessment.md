# Acute Adult ORL Head and Neck Surgery Assessment

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers acute presentations involving the ear, nose, throat, head, and neck in patients aged 15 years and older. These conditions often require urgent specialist assessment — your doctor will determine whether you need to be seen by an ORL (otolaryngology/ENT) surgeon.

---

## When Urgent ORL Assessment Is Needed

Your doctor may arrange urgent ORL head and neck surgery assessment for any of the following:

- **Upper airway obstruction** — any difficulty breathing due to an obstructed airway is a medical emergency
- **Bleeding** from the head, neck, or upper airway — including post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage and bleeding from throat or oropharyngeal cancers
- **Foreign body** in the upper aerodigestive tract (e.g., a bone or coin stuck in the throat or oesophagus)
- **Severe head and neck infection** with pain, fever, or swelling — e.g., peritonsillar abscess, parapharyngeal abscess
- **Complications of sinus or ear (otological) disease**
- **Otitis externa with signs of complicated infection** (see below)
- **Nasal fractures** — ideally assessed within 1 week of injury to avoid displaced healing
- **Epistaxis (nosebleed)** not responding to standard treatment, or posterior epistaxis
- **Complications of recent ORL or head and neck surgery** — including haematoma, seroma, bleeding, or epistaxis

## Signs of Complicated Ear Infection (Otitis Externa)

These signs indicate the infection has spread beyond the ear canal and urgent assessment is needed:

### Ramsay Hunt Syndrome

Ramsay Hunt syndrome is a herpes zoster (shingles) infection of the facial nerve (VII cranial nerve).

- Vesicles and scabs may appear on the face, ear (pinna), ear canal, throat, or upper neck
- Facial palsy (weakness or paralysis of one side of the face) is common, and tends to have a worse outlook than Bell's palsy:
  - Without treatment and with complete palsy — only about 10% make a full recovery
  - Without treatment and with partial palsy — about 68% make a full recovery
- Maximum weakness usually develops within 1 week, but nerve damage can continue for up to 2 weeks after the palsy starts
- Other cranial nerves may also be affected (e.g., VIII — hearing and balance, IX and X — swallowing)
- Swallowing difficulties may occur
- Potential complications include permanent facial paralysis, ongoing nerve pain (postherpetic neuralgia), and hearing loss

### Lateral Skull Base Osteomyelitis (Malignant Otitis Externa)

This is an invasive form of otitis externa where infection spreads into the deep soft tissue and bone, which can lead to osteomyelitis and nerve damage. It is more common in older patients, particularly those with diabetes or a weakened immune system.

Signs include:
- Ear pain (otalgia) that seems out of proportion to what is found on examination
- Fever, feeling generally unwell, headache, swollen lymph nodes (lymphadenopathy), or jaw stiffness (trismus) — though any of these may be absent
- Ear discharge (otorrhoea) may be present
- The ear canal may show severe swelling, or more subtle signs such as a small area of granulation tissue on the floor of the canal
- Cranial nerves VII through XII may be involved
- A raised CRP (C-reactive protein) blood test can help distinguish invasive from non-invasive otitis externa

### Other Signs of Complicated Ear Infection

- **Mastoiditis** — fever, tenderness or swelling over the mastoid bone (behind the ear), feeling unwell
- **Complicated cholesteatoma** — signs suggesting the cyst has spread intracranially, such as facial palsy, meningitis, or abscess
- **Intracranial infection** — fever combined with headache or neurological symptoms
- **Cellulitis** spreading beyond the ear canal in a patient who is systemically unwell

## Conditions Managed by Other Specialties

The following presentations are not typically managed by ORL head and neck surgery — your doctor will direct you to the appropriate service:

- **Non-bony food stuck in the oesophagus** — managed by gastroenterology
- **Suspected Bell's palsy (facial palsy without another cause)** — managed through the facial palsy pathway
- **Suspected meningitis** (even if related to a sinus or ear condition) — requires acute general medicine assessment
- **Traumatic ear (pinna) amputation** — managed by plastic surgery
- **Dental or odontogenic (tooth-related) infections** — see toothache/dental pathway
- **Facial trauma** (other than an isolated nasal fracture) — managed by oral and maxillofacial surgery
- **Mandibular (jaw) fractures** — managed by oral and maxillofacial surgery
- **Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disorders** — managed through the TMD pathway

---

## What to Expect

If your doctor determines you need urgent ORL assessment, they will contact the on-call ORL registrar and arrange for you to be seen at hospital. Depending on the severity, you may be admitted directly.

For airway emergencies, call 111 immediately — do not wait for a GP appointment.
