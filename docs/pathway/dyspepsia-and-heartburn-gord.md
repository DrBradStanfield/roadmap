# Dyspepsia and Heartburn / GORD

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: July 2021.*

Heartburn is a burning sensation rising from the upper abdomen (epigastrium) towards the neck. It is usually caused by reflux of acid into the oesophagus. Dyspepsia is pain or discomfort in the upper abdomen, which may be episodic, recurrent, or chronic. Dyspepsia may exist with or without heartburn, whereas gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) is characterised mainly by heartburn.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department if you have any of these red flags:**
- Difficulty swallowing
- Unexplained weight loss with gut symptoms
- Unexplained iron deficiency anaemia
- Vomiting blood (haematemesis) or passing black, tarry stools (melaena)
- A lump in the abdomen (palpable abdominal mass)
- Persistent or protracted vomiting

The guideline advises acute hospital gastroenterology assessment for all of these red flags.

---

## Background

- Most dyspepsia is functional. Symptoms may or may not be linked to food.
- Dyspepsia without GORD is probably due to abnormal motor and sensory function of the gut, and can be affected by psychosocial factors.
- In GORD, complications mainly occur only in high-grade GORD and are rare. Examples are narrowing (strictures), bleeding, and Barrett's oesophagus.
- Surgery can provide appropriate long-term management in certain patients.

### Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori)

- *H. pylori* is a bacteria usually found in the stomach. It is present in about 50% of the global population.
- Most infections cause no symptoms, but chronic infection may cause dyspepsia and peptic ulcer disease. It can also increase the risk of stomach (gastric) cancer.
- Infection mostly occurs in childhood and does not clear on its own. Rates vary by region and ethnicity, so the country or region you come from predicts your risk of infection as an adult.

## Assessment

### Symptoms

**GORD:**
- Heartburn
- Regurgitation of stomach contents, including acid and/or food
- Unexplained chest pain
- Atypical asthma
- Unexplained cough or hoarseness
- Erosion of dental enamel

**Dyspepsia:**
- Pain in the upper abdomen (epigastric pain)
- Fullness after meals, bloating, belching, feeling full early (early satiety)
- Loss of appetite (anorexia), nausea and vomiting
- Heartburn

**Lifestyle factors that can make symptoms worse:** bending, alcohol, chocolate, spicy food, fatty food, smoking, and stress.

### What your doctor may ask and check

- How long you have had symptoms, their timing, and how severe the heartburn is.
- Red flags (see above).
- Diet, alcohol, smoking, and body mass index (BMI).
- Medicines, including those bought over the counter, that can cause heartburn or dyspepsia:
  - Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), including COX-2 inhibitors
  - Bisphosphonates
  - Aspirin, clopidogrel, and other anticoagulants
  - Corticosteroids
- An abdominal examination, checking for tenderness in the upper abdomen, a mass, or enlarged lymph nodes.

### Risk factors for more serious disease

Your doctor will look more carefully for serious (organic) disease if you have:
- New dyspepsia and are older than 50 years, or older than 45 years if you are Māori, Pacific, or Asian. These groups have a higher rate of gastric cancer, due to a higher rate of *H. pylori* infection as well as genetic predisposition.
- Severe or persistent dyspepsia despite adequate treatment.
- Previous peptic ulcer disease, particularly if complicated.
- Use of NSAIDs, including aspirin and over-the-counter use.

**Factors that lower the threshold for gastroscopy** (a camera test of the stomach):
- First presentation of unexplained and persistent symptoms at age 50 years or older, or 40 years or older for Māori, Pacific, or Asian descent.
- Family history of gastric cancer with onset before age 50 years.
- Severe or persistent dyspepsia despite adequate treatment.
- Previous peptic ulcer disease, particularly if complicated.
- Coughing spells or aspiration at night.

### Other conditions your doctor may consider

Functional symptoms, peptic ulcer disease, upper gut cancers (including pancreatic and gallbladder), hiatus hernia, gallstones, coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, gastroparesis, diabetes, an atypical presentation of heart disease (ischaemic heart disease), and achalasia. Doctors take particular care to rule out organic disease in people older than 45 years.

### Investigations

**Your doctor may consider:**
- **Blood tests:** not usually required. Your doctor may consider FBC, ferritin, CRP, LFTs, kidney function, and coeliac serology if there are red flags, significant risk factors, or abnormal examination findings.
- **Abdominal ultrasound:** if gallstones are possible, or if there is a lump in the abdomen. If you are older than 50 years and have a lump, your doctor may seek radiology advice about a CT scan of the abdomen.
- **H. pylori testing** if you have any of these risk factors:
  - Past history of peptic ulcer
  - Family history of gastric cancer
  - Dyspepsia and you are older than 60 years
  - Dyspepsia and high-risk ethnicity (greater than 30% *H. pylori* prevalence), such as Māori, Pacific, Asian, or African
  - Dyspepsia and you come from an area of high *H. pylori* prevalence (greater than 30%), such as South Auckland, Porirua, the East Cape, or low and middle income countries, including Asia

The rate in New Zealand-born Europeans younger than 50 years is very low.

**About the H. pylori test:**
- The faecal (stool) antigen test is the preferred test and is funded in New Zealand. It shows active infection, so it suits both diagnosis and follow-up testing.
- It needs a fresh stool sample, which must reach the lab within 48 hours or be frozen.
- Blood tests for *H. pylori* (serology) are not recommended, because they cannot tell current from past infection.
- Your doctor will make sure you have stopped PPI treatment for 2 weeks before testing and have not taken antibiotics within the last month. Recent PPIs or antibiotics can cause false negative results.

## Management

Dyspepsia and heartburn are common. Management is an individually tailored combination of lifestyle changes and medicines.

### Lifestyle measures

- Raise the head of your bed.
- Change your diet: smaller meals, less fatty food, not eating before bed, and avoiding caffeine and chocolate. A trial of a low FODMAP diet may help.
- Reduce weight.
- Stop smoking.
- Limit alcohol.
- Reduce stress.

### Medicines that can make symptoms worse

- Your doctor may stop aggravating medicines where possible (NSAIDs, bisphosphonates, aspirin, clopidogrel and other anticoagulants, corticosteroids).
- If you take or are considering NSAIDs, your doctor will follow specific guidance on NSAIDs and dyspepsia.
- Your doctor may consider stopping oral bisphosphonates and using other medicines for osteoporosis.

### Step-down PPI regimen

Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) reduce stomach acid. Your doctor may use a step-down plan:
1. Start a PPI at the usual dose. Double the dose if there is no response after 4 weeks. Continue at this dose for 8 weeks.
2. If there is an adequate response, halve the dose and continue for 4 to 12 weeks.
3. Stop the PPI, start an H2-receptor antagonist, and continue for 4 to 12 weeks.
4. Stop the H2-receptor antagonist. Use an antacid as needed.

### Functional dyspepsia

- Most people respond well to reassurance and an individually tailored mix of lifestyle changes and medicines.
- **Your doctor may consider** a PPI using the step-down regimen. If symptoms persist, your doctor may test for *H. pylori*.
- If *H. pylori* is negative and symptoms persist, it is treated as functional dyspepsia.
- **Dysmotility-type symptoms** (discomfort, bloating, early fullness, nausea rather than pain): your doctor may try a prokinetic medicine such as domperidone 10 mg three times a day first. H2-receptor antagonists come next. They have a better response rate than PPIs in this group.
  - If you respond, treatment may continue as needed.
  - If not, your doctor may test for *H. pylori* or refer you for gastroscopy.
- People with chronic dyspepsia should be reviewed at least once a year. Your doctor will ask about your symptoms, examine you, encourage lifestyle measures, and review your medicines, including over-the-counter ones.

### Heartburn or GORD

- Your doctor will advise on lifestyle measures and stop aggravating medicines where possible.
- A PPI is started using the step-down regimen. Antacids and prokinetics are useful additions to acid suppression.
- If there is no response, your doctor may trial a double-dose PPI and monitor over 3 months.
  - If there is still no response, your doctor may refer you for gastroscopy.
  - If you respond, step-down therapy continues, with the aim of stopping by 3 to 6 months.
- Symptoms come back in approximately 80% of patients:
  - **If symptoms return more than 1 month later,** your doctor will check for red flags. If there are none, step-down therapy and lifestyle measures restart, whatever the severity or frequency of symptoms. The risk of complications is low.
  - **If symptoms return within 1 month,** your doctor may refer you for gastroscopy, considering whether symptoms are severe and frequent, whether you have had no gastroscopy in the past 5 years, and whether there are red flags, risk factors, or symptoms suggesting complications.
- If a previous gastroscopy confirmed low-grade GORD, symptoms are managed without a repeat gastroscopy, unless there are new red flags or risk factors.
- If symptoms come back mildly and infrequently, lifestyle measures and the lowest effective dose of medicine are used, with ongoing monitoring for warning signs.
- **Surgery:** some people with persistent severe GORD may be considered for laparoscopic fundoplication (keyhole anti-reflux surgery). It is considered if:
  - Reflux symptoms are not controlled by maximal medical treatment.
  - You cannot tolerate medical treatment.
  - You are young, controlled on medicines, but do not want lifelong PPI treatment.

### H. pylori treatment

- Everyone who tests positive for *H. pylori* should be treated, even without symptoms.
- Taking every dose matters for successful eradication. Treatment is 80 to 90% effective.
- Māori, Pacific, and Asian patients more often have *H. pylori* resistant to clarithromycin, and are more likely to fail eradication therapy.
- Eradication reduces the return of stomach and duodenal ulcers and the risk of bleeding. It also causes most localised MALT lymphomas to regress.

**June 2026 update:** based on international evidence, New Zealand's national antibiotic guidelines now recommend double the PPI dose in the 14-day eradication regimen. For omeprazole, this means moving from 20 mg twice a day to 40 mg twice a day.

**First treatment: 14-day triple therapy.** Your doctor may prescribe, for 14 days:
- A PPI: omeprazole 40 mg twice a day, lansoprazole 30 mg twice a day, or pantoprazole 40 mg twice a day.
- Plus amoxicillin 1000 mg twice daily, or metronidazole 400 mg twice daily.
- Plus clarithromycin 500 mg twice daily.

Points your doctor will consider:
- Metronidazole replaces amoxicillin if you are allergic to penicillin.
- If you have ever had metronidazole for any infection, the combination is a PPI, amoxicillin and clarithromycin.
- If you have ever had a macrolide antibiotic for any infection, the combination is a PPI, amoxicillin and metronidazole.
- If there was a known bleed, perforation or large ulcer, the PPI continues for an extra 3 weeks.
- Complete the full course. Your doctor will warn you about side effects.
- Stopping smoking matters, because smoking significantly reduces clearance rates.
- Do not drink alcohol while taking metronidazole.

**Re-testing:** everyone is re-tested 4 to 6 weeks after finishing treatment, whether or not symptoms remain, using the faecal antigen test.

**Second treatment (if the re-test is positive).** A second course is always given: 2 weeks of four medicines:
- A PPI: omeprazole 40 mg twice a day, lansoprazole 30 mg twice a day, or pantoprazole 40 mg twice a day.
- Tripotassium dicitratobismuthate (an unapproved medicine) 120 mg four times daily.
- Tetracycline 500 mg four times daily.
- Metronidazole 400 mg three times a day.

The same advice applies: finish the course, stop smoking, and avoid alcohol with metronidazole. If eradication fails after 2 attempts, the chance of success is low, and your doctor may refer you for gastroscopy.

### Dyspepsia and heartburn in pregnancy

GORD is common in pregnancy. Heartburn and reflux have been shown to worsen nausea and vomiting, so managing them may help improve those symptoms.
- **First:** lifestyle and diet changes. Raise the head of the bed. Eat smaller meals, reduce fatty food, avoid eating before bed, and consider a trial of a low FODMAP diet. Reduce weight, stop smoking, limit alcohol, and avoid triggers.
- **If symptoms persist:** antacids or alginates. Alginates are particularly useful when heartburn is the main symptom. Antacids containing aluminium or calcium are safe. Avoid antacids containing sodium bicarbonate and magnesium trisilicate.
- **If symptoms are more severe or persist:** your doctor may consider an acid-suppressing medicine such as omeprazole. As with any medicine in pregnancy, especially in the first trimester, the aim is the minimum effective dose for the shortest possible time.

### Long-term PPI use

- Long-term PPIs may be needed for severe GORD or frequent recurrences, for stomach protection while taking NSAIDs, or for complicated disease or Barrett's oesophagus.
- PPIs have been linked with low magnesium (hypomagnesaemia), lower vitamin B12 levels, and very infrequent cases of a skin condition, subacute cutaneous lupus erythematosus (SCLE).
- PPIs should be used for the right reasons, at the lowest effective dose, for the shortest time. Your doctor will review regularly whether you still need long-term treatment.
- Rebound acid and prolonged dyspepsia may occur after stopping long-term treatment. Reducing or stopping through the step-down regimen helps.
- If reducing is not possible, your doctor may reconsider the diagnosis, look for red flags, or consider a specialist opinion.

## When to See a Specialist

- **Emergency department:** any red flag (see above).
- **Non-urgent gastroenterology assessment,** often for gastroscopy, may be arranged for:
  - Risk factors for significant organic disease, or a positive coeliac blood test.
  - *H. pylori* that persists after 2 attempts at eradication.
  - GORD that does not respond to a double-dose PPI over 3 months.
  - Dysmotility-type symptoms that do not respond to treatment.
  - GORD symptoms that return within 1 month of step-down therapy (see above).
  - Persistent severe GORD, if you would like to be considered for fundoplication surgery.
