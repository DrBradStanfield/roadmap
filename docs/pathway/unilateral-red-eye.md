# Unilateral Red Eye

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to a red eye on one side — identifying the likely cause and determining how urgently it needs to be assessed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

See your doctor urgently (same day) or go to an emergency department if you have a red eye with any of the following:

- Significant reduction in vision
- Severe or deep aching pain in the eye
- Photophobia (sensitivity to light)
- Nausea or vomiting alongside eye pain
- Halos around light sources
- A white spot visible on the cornea (the clear front of the eye)
- Pupil that looks irregular or does not react normally to light
- Known contact lens wearer with pain or discharge
- Recent trauma to the eye (especially with organic material such as soil or plant matter)
- History of shingles (herpes zoster) affecting the forehead or nose

These features can indicate sight-threatening conditions that require prompt treatment.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Onset and duration of the redness
- Whether vision has changed
- Pain — its character (aching, sharp, gritty), severity, and whether it wakes you at night
- Photophobia or tearing
- Discharge (watery, mucous, or purulent)
- Itch (suggests an allergic cause)
- Contact lens use
- History of eye conditions, previous cold sores on the eye, shingles, or connective tissue diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, lupus)
- Hayfever, eczema, or asthma

Physical examination typically includes:

- Visual acuity (reading chart)
- Pattern of redness — diffuse, localised, or around the edge of the cornea (limbus)
- Corneal examination — clarity, white spots, fluorescein dye uptake for ulcers or abrasions
- Pupil size, shape, and reactivity
- Presence and character of discharge

## Possible Causes

### Causes With Reduced Vision (Higher Urgency)

#### Bacterial Keratitis (Corneal Infection)

- A white area is visible on the cornea with a torch or ophthalmoscope
- Fluorescein dye uptake on the cornea, with marked redness
- Significant pain and photophobia
- Most common in contact lens wearers or after trauma involving organic material

#### Iritis / Uveitis

- Redness concentrated around the edge of the cornea (limbus)
- Photophobia and constant deep pain
- Pupil may be poorly reactive or distorted
- Vision mildly to moderately reduced
- In severe cases, a white layer (hypopyon) may be visible at the bottom of the iris
- Usually one eye, but can occasionally affect both

#### Acute Angle Closure Glaucoma

- Severe deep aching pain with sudden reduction in vision
- Halos around lights
- Nausea and vomiting are common
- More common in older patients and those of Asian ancestry
- Rarely affects both eyes simultaneously

#### Herpes Simplex Keratitis

- Dendritic (branching) corneal ulcer visible with fluorescein dye
- May have a past history of a "cold-sore infection" of the eye

#### Herpes Zoster (Shingles) Affecting the Eye

- Usually in older patients
- May cause iritis or inflammatory keratitis — without a corneal ulcer
- Shingles rash in the V1 dermatome (forehead, nose tip) 10 days to many months earlier
- No corneal ulcer typically present

#### Scleritis

- Severe, deep aching pain — often bad enough to wake the patient at night
- Marked redness of the eyeball (not the eyelids), either diffuse or localised to one area
- Violaceous (purple-red) hue to the scleral redness, better seen in natural light
- Common in patients with connective tissue diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis)
- Usually one eye, but occasionally both

#### Other Corneal Causes

- **Corneal abrasion:** Fluorescein uptake only — cornea otherwise clear, no white spots
- **Marginal keratitis:** Small white spots at the peripheral cornea with mild discomfort
- **Exposure keratopathy:** Poor blink reflex, associated with Bell's palsy
- **Neurotrophic keratopathy:** Reduced corneal sensation — common after previous herpes simplex or zoster infection

---

### Causes Without Reduced Vision (Lower Urgency)

#### Conjunctivitis (Infective)

- Prominent discharge (watery, mucous, or purulent)
- Visual acuity and corneal examination normal

#### Allergic Conjunctivitis

- History of hayfever, eczema, or asthma
- Significant itch with mild redness
- Seasonal pattern is common in New Zealand
- Visual acuity and corneal/iris examination normal
- Can also be triggered by prolonged use of preservative-containing eye drops

#### Subconjunctival Haemorrhage

- Bright red patch on the white of the eye — can be quite striking in appearance
- Often follows coughing, sneezing, or minor blunt trauma
- Mild irritation only, with no effect on vision
- Self-limiting — no specific treatment required

#### Episcleritis

- Redness without significant discomfort or discharge
- Pain is not prominent (not bad enough to disrupt sleep)
- If there is deep aching pain, scleritis should be assumed until proven otherwise

## Management

Many cases of non-specific unilateral red eye have no identifiable cause and are either self-limiting or helped by general symptomatic treatments such as lubricating eye drops.

Your doctor may consider the following, depending on the cause:

- **Bacterial keratitis:** Urgent ophthalmology referral; intensive topical antibiotic drops (e.g., ciprofloxacin, chloramphenicol) — the choice and frequency depend on severity
- **Iritis/uveitis:** Ophthalmology assessment; your doctor may start topical steroids (e.g., prednisolone eye drops) and cycloplegic drops (e.g., cyclopentolate) to reduce pain and prevent complications
- **Acute angle closure glaucoma:** Emergency ophthalmology assessment; treatment aims to rapidly lower eye pressure
- **Herpes simplex keratitis:** Antiviral eye ointment or drops (e.g., aciclovir); your doctor will avoid topical steroids unless supervised by ophthalmology
- **Herpes zoster affecting the eye:** Oral antiviral treatment (e.g., aciclovir, valaciclovir) and ophthalmology assessment
- **Scleritis:** Ophthalmology assessment; oral non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (e.g., ibuprofen, indomethacin) and/or systemic steroids may be used
- **Corneal abrasion:** Lubricating drops, short-term topical analgesia, and protective padding if needed; most heal within 1–3 days
- **Allergic conjunctivitis:** Antihistamine eye drops (e.g., olopatadine, ketotifen), lubricating drops, and avoidance of triggers; preservative-free drops preferred for frequent use
- **Infective conjunctivitis:** Often self-limiting; your doctor may prescribe topical antibiotic drops (e.g., chloramphenicol) if bacterial cause is suspected
- **Subconjunctival haemorrhage:** Reassurance and lubricating drops; resolves spontaneously over 1–2 weeks
- **Episcleritis:** Usually self-limiting; topical NSAIDs or lubricating drops may help

Always follow your doctor's specific advice — the treatment approach depends on the exact diagnosis and severity.
