# Polymyalgia Rheumatica (PMR)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

PMR is an inflammatory rheumatic condition in older adults causing pain and stiffness in the neck, shoulders, and pelvic girdle. It is a clinical diagnosis with no definitive test and is primarily managed in primary care with prednisone.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Giant-cell arteritis (temporal arteritis)** — if you or a patient has any of the following, contact a doctor urgently:

- New or changed headaches
- Scalp tenderness
- Jaw pain when chewing (jaw claudication)
- Visual symptoms (blurred vision, double vision, sudden vision loss)

About 15% of people with PMR will develop giant-cell arteritis (GCA) at some point. Your doctor will specifically screen for GCA before and during treatment.

---

## Background

Key facts about PMR:

- It is rare under age 50; the average age of onset is 70 years.
- It is a **clinical diagnosis** — there is no single diagnostic test.
- PMR and giant-cell arteritis (GCA/temporal arteritis) are closely linked: PMR occurs in 40–60% of patients with GCA.
- Symptoms are usually symmetrical and have typically been present for 2 weeks or longer.

---

## Symptoms

Typical symptoms include:

- Pain in the neck, shoulders, pelvic girdle, buttocks, and thighs
- Pain on both active and passive joint movements
- **Morning stiffness lasting more than 45 minutes** — a key feature
- Systemic symptoms: reduced appetite, weight loss, low-grade fever; fatigue in about one third of patients
- Mild distal symptoms (wrists, hands, knees) in about half of patients

---

## Assessment and Investigations

Your doctor will:

1. Take a detailed history of symptoms and duration.
2. Screen specifically for giant-cell arteritis symptoms (headache, scalp tenderness, jaw claudication, visual changes).
3. Consider other conditions that can mimic PMR (see below).
4. Arrange blood tests and a chest X-ray before starting treatment.

### Conditions that can mimic PMR

Your doctor will consider and rule out:

- **Rheumatoid arthritis** — more gradual onset, wrist and MCP joint involvement, positive rheumatoid factor or anti-CCP
- Other arthritides (e.g. psoriatic arthritis)
- **Malignancy** (e.g. lymphoma, myeloma) — look for lymphadenopathy, abnormal protein
- **Hypothyroidism**
- **Parkinson disease**
- **Inflammatory myopathy (polymyositis)** — proximal muscle weakness, elevated creatine kinase
- **Drug-induced myalgia** (e.g. statins)
- **Fibromyalgia** — tender points, normal CRP

### Blood tests

- **CRP** — nearly always > 5 mg/L in PMR; less than 5% of patients have a normal CRP
- **ESR** — if CRP is normal but PMR is still suspected, ESR is nearly always raised (normal in only 5–10% of PMR)
- **FBC** — may show normocytic anaemia and elevated platelets
- **LFTs, electrolytes, creatinine, urate, calcium, phosphate**
- **Creatine kinase, TSH, serum protein electrophoresis (SPE)**
- **Rheumatoid factor and anti-CCP antibody**
- **Urine microscopy** for cell count and protein (not routinely for culture)

### Imaging

- **Chest X-ray** — required for all patients before starting steroids, to exclude pulmonary pathology, chronic infection, or malignancy as alternative causes
- Additional targeted imaging if there are features suggesting malignancy

## Treatment

### When to refer to rheumatology first

Your doctor will seek rheumatology advice (rather than starting treatment directly) if:

- You are under 50 years old
- The diagnosis is uncertain

### Starting prednisone

PMR is generally managed in primary care. Your doctor will:

- Start **prednisone 15–20 mg/day** for the first month
- Doses above 20 mg do not provide additional benefit and may make it harder to taper later
- Expect a **dramatic response within days** — this is consistent with PMR; if no response, the diagnosis should be reconsidered

**Before starting, your doctor will discuss:**

- Risks relevant to your age and health (especially if you have diabetes, high blood pressure, or liver conditions)
- Drug interactions: NSAIDs (GI bleed risk), warfarin (enhanced anticoagulation), diabetic medications (antagonism), digoxin/diuretics/beta-2 agonists (hypokalaemia), and vaccines (impaired response)

### Steroid side effects and protective measures

Long-term prednisone requires protective measures your doctor will arrange:

- **Vitamin D supplementation** and advice on adequate calcium intake — for all patients
- **Bone density scan and bisphosphonates** — considered for osteoporosis prevention
- **Do not stop prednisone abruptly** — your doctor will guide tapering
- Increased infection risk — contact your doctor if you become acutely unwell, as your dose may need to be temporarily increased
- Patient education leaflet: Prednisone (oral steroids)

**Long-term side effects to monitor:**
- Insomnia, weight gain
- Skin thinning, easy bruising, delayed wound healing
- Gastrointestinal ulceration
- High blood pressure
- Muscle weakness
- Immunosuppression
- New-onset diabetes or worsened blood glucose control
- Psychiatric disturbances (mania, delirium, depression) — especially at doses above 40 mg
- Reactivation of latent infections

---

## Monitoring During Treatment

Your doctor will monitor by **clinical response, not by CRP or ESR levels**.

At each review, your doctor will ask about:

- Return of PMR symptoms (relapse)
- Symptoms of giant-cell arteritis (new headaches, scalp tenderness, visual changes)
- Long-term steroid side effects
- Blood pressure and HbA1c monitoring as appropriate

---

## Tapering Prednisone

Most patients will require prednisone for **2 to 3 years**. There is no single ideal regimen — your doctor will tailor this to you.

Once symptoms have completely resolved for 2–4 weeks, gradual reduction begins:

1. Reduce by **2.5 mg every 2–4 weeks** until reaching 10 mg/day
2. Then reduce by **1 mg every 2–4 weeks**
3. Some patients can reduce more rapidly (e.g. 1 mg every 2–3 weeks) if clinically stable

**Important:** If symptoms return during tapering, your doctor will increase back to the previous effective dose and restart the taper from there.

---

## Relapse and Treatment-Resistant PMR

- Clinical symptoms are the primary indicator of relapse — not blood tests alone
- If symptoms return during tapering, increase back to the previous steroid dose
- If the dose **cannot be reduced below 10 mg/day** despite repeated attempts, your doctor will request rheumatology assessment for consideration of **methotrexate or leflunomide** as steroid-sparing agents

---

## When Rheumatology Input Is Needed

Your doctor will seek rheumatology advice or refer if:

- You are under 50 years old
- The diagnosis is uncertain
- The steroid dose cannot be reduced as expected
- The response to prednisone is not clear-cut
- Continued relapses requiring prednisone ≥ 10 mg/day (for consideration of methotrexate or leflunomide)
- Any concerns about starting prednisone given co-morbidities or drug interactions

---

## Mobility and Function

At every review, your doctor will consider your mobility and independence. Referrals may be arranged for:

**Occupational therapy:**
- Home safety assessment and functioning
- Equipment and housing modification needs
- Adaptive equipment funding and prescription

**Physiotherapy:**
- Joint splinting and protection
- Walking aid assessment
- Symptom management education
- Joint care techniques
