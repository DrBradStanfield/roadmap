# Irritated and Dry Eyes

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: April 2024.*

"Dry eye" describes mild, chronic eye irritation with many possible causes. Lubricant drops can ease the irritation, gritty feeling, or dryness.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Background

- Irritated and dry eyes are common. Symptoms may start suddenly or may have been present for months.
- Keratoconjunctivitis sicca, also known as dry-eye syndrome, happens when the tear film is lacking or does not work properly.
- A lack of tears is often age-related. It can also be due to:
  - contact lens use.
  - medicines with anticholinergic effects.
  - diabetes.
  - autoimmune diseases, such as Sjögren's syndrome.
  - blockage of the tear glands from scarring of the eye surface, such as after eye burns or pemphigoid.
  - damage to the tear glands, such as from sarcoidosis, a tumour, or scarring after radiation.
- A tear film that does not work properly can be due to:
  - contact lens use.
  - environmental factors, such as low humidity.
  - blepharitis.
  - altered eyelid position, such as ectropion.
  - eyelids that do not close fully.
  - blinking less often, such as in Parkinson's disease.

---

## Assessment

### What your doctor may ask about

- **Contact lens use.**
- **Symptoms:**
  - dryness, grittiness, irritation, or pain.
  - red eyes.
  - scratching or burning.
  - a feeling of something under the eyelid or behind the eye.
  - heavy watering of the eyes in wind or air-conditioning.
  - blurred vision.
- **Lifestyle factors:** a dry climate, allergen exposure, smoking, and eye strain from long periods of screen use or reading.
- **Medical conditions:**
  - autoimmune disorders, such as Sjögren's syndrome (dry mouth), rheumatoid arthritis, and systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).
  - Parkinson's disease.
  - diabetes.
  - vitamin A deficiency.
  - menopause and post-menopause.
- **Medicines,** including anti-arrhythmics, anticholinergics, antihistamines, antipsychotics, antispasmodics, beta blockers, chemotherapy, diuretics, oral contraceptives, anti-glaucoma eye drops, and tricyclic antidepressants.
- **Other eye conditions,** such as ectropion, blepharitis, damage or scarring from a tumour, radiation, eye burns or laser eye surgery, and tear gland infiltration from sarcoidosis or a tumour.
- **Drops you have already tried,** how often you used them, and whether they helped.

### Examination

Your doctor may:
- **Check your vision** in each eye with a vision chart, wearing your distance glasses if you use them. If vision is below normal, they may test each eye through a pinhole. If vision improves with the pinhole and there is no cataract, your glasses may need reviewing.
- **Examine your eyelids** for a VII nerve palsy or marked ectropion.
- **Examine the cornea with fluorescein**, a dye that shows up under blue light, to look for staining, scarring, or ulcers. In dry eye syndrome, the dye may show small spots of damage, especially on the lower part of the cornea. If ectropion is present, your doctor may check for signs of a corneal infection (infective keratitis).

### Common causes to consider

- Blepharitis
- Ectropion and entropion
- Allergic conjunctivitis
- Infective conjunctivitis

---

## Management

### Treating the cause

If a cause can be found, such as blepharitis, ectropion or entropion, or allergic or infective conjunctivitis, your doctor may treat that condition.

### Starting lubricant drops

If you do not already use lubricant drops, your doctor may suggest **lubricant drops 6 times during the day, and an ointment at night, for 2 weeks.**

Funded options include:
- hypromellose 0.3% with dextran 0.1% eye drops.
- polyvinyl alcohol 1.4% or 3% eye drops.

Hypromellose 0.5% eye drops are partially funded.

Points to know:
- Preservative-free drops are preferred. Preservatives can worsen irritation.
- Ointment can blur vision, so use it at night.
- Gels give longer relief than drops, and blur vision less than ointments.

### If you already use lubricant drops

Your doctor may intensify treatment:
- Change to **preservative-free lubricant drops**, such as hyaluronate sodium, macrogol-400 with propylene glycol, or carbomer.
- For a further 2-week trial, use the preservative-free drops more often and add a **night-time lubricating ointment**, such as paraffin liquid with lanolin, or retinol palmitate.
- Consider **thicker lubricating drops**, such as carbomer or macrogol-400 with propylene glycol gel.

### Lifestyle, medical conditions and medicines

Your doctor may help you change lifestyle factors (dry climate, allergen exposure, smoking, screen or reading eye strain), treat related medical conditions, and stop medicines that may be contributing, if appropriate. Do not stop a medicine without talking to your doctor first.

### Follow-up

Your doctor may review you in **2 weeks**:
- **If improving:** continue the lubricant drops and lifestyle changes for a further 2 to 4 weeks. For long-term use, a preservative-free option may be considered, as preservatives can worsen irritation.
- **If not improving, with marked corneal changes or reduced vision of no other cause:** your doctor may consider referral to an eye specialist (ophthalmologist). Punctal occlusion or serum tears may be offered.
- **If not improving, without marked corneal changes or reduced vision:** your doctor may intensify treatment, as above, for a further 2 to 4 weeks.

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you for a non-urgent ophthalmology assessment if you have:
- marked corneal changes.
- reduced vision not due to other causes.
- a VII nerve palsy with marked corneal changes from the eye being exposed. Corrective surgery may be offered in some cases.
- no improvement after 6 weeks of treatment, including intensive therapy.

If there is no improvement after 6 weeks, but no marked corneal changes or unexplained reduced vision, an optometrist is another option. Optometrists often assess and manage these symptoms, and can arrange an ophthalmology assessment if needed.
