# Obsessive Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of OCD in primary care, including when specialist referral is needed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Help

If you or someone you know is at risk of suicide or self-harm, seek emergency help immediately (call 111 or go to your nearest emergency department).

---

## Background

OCD affects around 2% of the population.

People with OCD:

- Often cannot stop worrying, and their worries frequently compel them to behave in certain ways over and over again.
- Have **obsessions** and/or **compulsions**:
  - **Obsessions** are recurrent, persistent intrusive thoughts, impulses, or images that are usually resisted by the person and are recognised as coming from their own mind. A useful acronym is **RODSIR**: Recurrent, Own, Distressing, Senseless, Intrusive, Resist.
  - **Compulsions** are repetitive, stereotyped behaviours performed in response to an obsession, to prevent discomfort or a dreaded event. The person generally recognises their behaviour is excessive or unreasonable.

People with OCD typically spend more than one hour a day on obsessions or compulsions, and these cause significant distress or impair daily life (school, work, relationships, home life).

OCD is part of a group of related conditions that includes trichotillomania, hoarding disorder, excoriation disorder, and body dysmorphic disorder.

## Assessment

Your doctor will conduct a structured assessment. This involves:

### Screening for OCD

Many people with OCD feel shame and may not present with classic symptoms. Your doctor may consider OCD in any mental health presentation — including anxiety, depression, eating disorders, body dysmorphic disorder, alcohol or substance misuse, or some chronic physical problems (e.g., hand dermatitis).

### History — Obsessions

Your doctor may ask about:

- Unpleasant thoughts or images that keep coming into your head and are hard to get rid of.
- Distressing thoughts (e.g., about violence, contaminating others, causing accidents) that you cannot stop despite wanting to.
- Whether you try to resist these thoughts.
- What would happen if you did not act on your compulsion.
- The reasoning behind the obsessions.

*If the thoughts appear delusional, your doctor will assess for psychosis.*

### History — Compulsions

Compulsions can be external (e.g., cleaning, checking, ordering) or internal (e.g., praying, mental checking, trying to "undo" thoughts).

Your doctor may ask about:

- Repeating actions over and over (e.g., checking locks, washing hands, checking the stove is off).
- Counting things or placing objects in a specific order.
- Mantras, sayings, or words that must be said mentally or aloud.
- Rituals or routines that must be followed every day.
- What would happen if these things were not done.
- How much time per day is spent on compulsions.

### Impact and Risk

Your doctor will assess:

- How much daily activities are slowed (e.g., taking a long time to complete tasks).
- Whether OCD contributes to anxiety, suicidal thoughts, or inability to leave home, work, or care for others.
- Physical consequences (e.g., hand dermatitis from excessive hand-washing).

### Other Relevant History

Your doctor will also ask about:

- **Mental health history:** Previous conditions (especially psychosis, anorexia, anxiety, depression), prior treatments, hospital admissions, and substance use.
- **Medical conditions** that can cause obsessions, compulsions, or anxiety, including hyperthyroidism, sleep disorders, cardiovascular disease, COPD, diabetes, dementia, chronic pain, anaemia, vestibular disorders, seizure disorders, perimenopause, and others.
- **Medications and substances** that may cause these symptoms, including corticosteroids, anticonvulsants, stimulants (e.g., ADHD medications, theophylline), bronchodilators, antihypertensives (e.g., captopril), NSAIDs, levothyroxine, antiparkinsonians, benzodiazepine withdrawal, some antibiotics (e.g., penicillin), and caffeine.
- **Psychosocial situation:** Living environment, relationships, financial situation, legal issues, recent stress, trauma history, and support network.

### Risk Factors for OCD

Your doctor may consider risk factors including:

- Family history of OCD.
- Age of onset — two peaks: between 8–12 years and in the late teens to early 20s.
- OCD is more common in females but can be more severe in males.
- Coexisting psychiatric disorders — 90% of people with OCD have at least one (mood disorders, anxiety, ADHD, tics, substance use disorders, and others).

*Note: OCD and autism spectrum disorder (ASD) can coexist and may be difficult to distinguish. The key difference is whether the behaviour causes enjoyment (ASD special interest) or distress (OCD obsession).*

### Rating Scale

Your doctor may use the **Yale-Brown OCD Scale (Y-BOCS)** to document symptom severity and functional impairment at baseline and follow-up.

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following, depending on your situation:

### General Approach

- Work collaboratively with you to explore symptoms and treatment options.
- Involve family and carers (with your consent).
- Communicate a hopeful expectation of recovery.
- Arrange regular follow-up — this is key to good outcomes regardless of treatment type.

### Education and Self-Help

Your doctor may provide information about OCD and suggest:

- **Healthy lifestyle** as an adjunct to treatment: regular physical activity, healthy eating, good sleep habits, reducing caffeine and alcohol.
- **Online self-help resources:**
  - [Just a Thought — OCD (CBT course)](https://www.justathought.co.nz/ocd)
  - [This Way Up — OCD Program (CBT course)](https://thiswayup.org.au/programs/ocd-program/)
  - [Centre for Clinical Interventions — Anxiety Module](https://www.cci.health.wa.gov.au/Resources/Looking-After-Yourself/Anxiety)
  - [OCD Challenge](https://www.ocdchallenge.com/)

### Psychological Therapy

Psychological therapy is first-line treatment for OCD, particularly for mild OCD. Your doctor may discuss or refer you for:

- **Exposure and response prevention (ERP)** — a type of CBT that involves graded, structured exposure to anxiety-provoking situations while resisting compulsions. Sessions typically last 45 minutes to 2 hours.
- **Cognitive therapy** — focuses on how obsessions are interpreted.

Referral options may include self-directed eTherapy (see above), brief intervention through your primary health organisation (PHO), or private psychology/counselling services.

### Pharmacological Treatment

Medications may be considered for adults (aged ≥ 18) with moderate to severe OCD when:

- There is severe distress and significant functional impairment.
- Psychological therapy has not helped, is unavailable, or the patient declines.
- Exposure and response prevention would cause unbearable anxiety.

**First-line medications:**

High-dose selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) or clomipramine are the usual first-line treatment for moderate to severe OCD. Note: SSRI doses needed for OCD are typically higher than those used for depression, and a response may not be seen for **6 to 12 weeks**.

Your doctor may consider:

| Medication | Starting dose (days 1–4) | Increased full dose |
|---|---|---|
| Fluoxetine | 10 mg | 20 mg per day |
| Sertraline | 25 mg | 50 mg per day |
| Paroxetine | 10 mg | 20 mg per day |

*Important notes your doctor will consider:*

- Fluoxetine and sertraline are preferred first-line SSRIs.
- Paroxetine is useful but has a higher risk of discontinuation syndrome.
- Escitalopram and citalopram should generally be avoided at OCD doses due to cardiac risks.
- In women of childbearing age, sertraline or escitalopram are preferred.
- SSRIs increase bleeding risk — a gastroprotective drug may be added if taking NSAIDs or aspirin.
- Psychiatric team may use significantly higher doses (e.g., 80–120 mg fluoxetine, up to 400 mg sertraline) — primary care clinicians will not start these higher doses before specialist assessment.
- All SSRIs must be gradually tapered when stopping.

**If there is an adequate response:** Continue for at least 2 years, or until a course of ERP has been successfully completed.

**If response is inadequate after 6–12 weeks:** Your doctor will seek specialist mental health advice. A higher dose, longer trial, or addition of a second-line medication may be considered (e.g., adding an antipsychotic such as aripiprazole, risperidone, haloperidol, or olanzapine — on specialist recommendation only).

### When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor may refer for specialist assessment if you are:

- **Under 18** with moderate to severe OCD, poor response to psychological therapy, or diagnostic uncertainty.
- **18 or over** with moderate to severe impairment in functioning, poor response to psychological therapy and first-line medication, significant psychiatric or physical co-morbidities, or diagnostic uncertainty.
- Acutely unwell or at high risk of suicide — in which case urgent mental health assessment will be arranged.
