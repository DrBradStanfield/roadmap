# Foreign Bodies Ingested by Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach when a child has swallowed a foreign body — assessing what was ingested, whether it is dangerous, and what needs to happen next.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately if any of the following are suspected:

- Oesophageal obstruction (drooling, difficulty swallowing or feeding, vomiting, chest discomfort)
- Known or suspected disc or button battery ingestion
- Multiple magnets swallowed, or magnets swallowed together with metal objects
- Sharp or large objects (more than 5 cm long and/or 2 cm wide)
- Lead-based objects

**If any red flag is present, do not wait for imaging — go directly to emergency care.**

---

## Background

- Oesophageal foreign bodies, especially button batteries, require urgent removal.
- Most foreign bodies that reach the stomach pass on their own without causing problems. However:
  - Children with pre-existing gastrointestinal (GI) abnormalities, previous GI surgery, or narrowings (stenoses) are at higher risk of complications.
  - Large objects (more than 5 cm long and/or 2 cm wide) can become stuck at the pylorus (exit of the stomach).
  - A magnet swallowed with metal, or multiple magnets, can cause serious and potentially life-threatening complications.
  - Lead-based objects that do not move through the stomach can cause acute lead absorption into the body.
- Children may present with a known history of swallowing something, or later with symptoms that turn out to be caused by a foreign body.
- Most affected children are younger than 3 years.
- Coins are the most common non-food item swallowed by children.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Type, number, and size of the object(s) seen or suspected to have been swallowed
- What it is made of (plastic, metal, lead-based, battery)
- Symptoms suggesting oesophageal obstruction — difficulty swallowing or feeding, drooling, vomiting, chest discomfort
- Whether a battery was swallowed, and if so, what exact type (button/disc versus regular battery)
- Whether magnets were swallowed, and if so, whether multiple magnets or magnets with metal objects
- Relevant medical history — any gastrointestinal abnormalities, narrowings, or previous GI surgery (e.g., repaired oesophageal atresia or previous fundoplication)

Physical examination typically includes:

- Gastrointestinal — checking for neck swelling, excessive drooling, mouth injuries, tender abdomen, or signs of bowel obstruction or peritonitis
- Airways — checking for crepitus, focal wheeze, reduced breath sounds, or signs of respiratory distress (if found, an inhaled foreign body may also need to be considered)

Your doctor may consider X-ray imaging in some cases:

- If any red flag is present, imaging is not arranged first — the child is referred for emergency care immediately
- If no red flags are present, your doctor will seek specialist paediatric surgery advice to decide whether imaging is needed
- If imaging is recommended, it typically covers the neck, chest, and abdomen

---

## Management

Management outside hospital is supportive and observation only.

### Emergency referral — red flags present

Your doctor will arrange immediate emergency assessment if:

- The foreign body is lodged in the oropharynx or upper airway (above the oesophagus) and cannot be safely removed in the clinic — the child is kept nil by mouth
- Any red flag is present (see above) or there are pre-existing GI abnormalities — the child is kept nil by mouth

### Button batteries — special management

Button battery ingestion is a medical emergency. Your doctor will:

- Arrange acute paediatric surgery assessment immediately
- Keep the child nil by mouth if being referred to hospital
- If the battery was swallowed within the last 12 hours, the child is older than 1 year, and the child can swallow, your doctor may advise giving **honey 10 mL every 10 minutes, up to 6 doses** — note this should not delay transfer to hospital

Serious injury can occur within 4 hours of button battery ingestion.

### Magnets — special management

If multiple magnets or magnets together with metal have been swallowed:

- Your doctor will seek acute paediatric surgery assessment and keep the child nil by mouth if referred
- Any metallic objects (including clothing) that could be attracted to the magnets should be removed

### Asymptomatic children — low-risk object, no red flags

If the child looks well, is pain-free, can eat and drink, and has no GI or respiratory symptoms, and the object is considered low risk, your doctor will reassure the family that the foreign body will pass within weeks. Advice given includes:

- Surveillance of the child's faeces is not required
- Routine repeat X-rays are not necessary
- Return for reassessment if any new symptoms develop

### If new symptoms develop after initial assessment

If the child returns with any new concerning symptoms, your doctor will seek paediatric surgery advice. Concerning symptoms include:

- Chest, epigastric, or abdominal pain
- Cough
- Decreased appetite
- Nausea or vomiting
- Haematemesis (vomiting blood)
- Diarrhoea
- Fever
