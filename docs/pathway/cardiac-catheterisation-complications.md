# Cardiac Catheterisation Complications

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the management of common complications that can occur after cardiac catheterisation, including coronary angiography, balloon angioplasty, stenting, and trans-aortic valve implantation (TAVI).

---

## When to Seek Urgent Help

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately if:

- There is a significant bleed at the access site that does not stop with sustained pressure
- There are signs of limb ischaemia after femoral access: white, cold, painful leg with absent or reduced pulses

Contact your doctor or seek urgent medical review if:

- The access site becomes red, tender, swollen, and warm (signs of infection)
- There is a sudden increase in swelling, or swelling associated with pain or numbness
- Pain at the access site is increasing, or has not improved after 72 hours
- You develop chest pain after the procedure (may need clinical and laboratory assessment)
- You have any other unexpected or worsening symptoms after the procedure

---

## Background

Cardiac catheterisation is used in diagnostic coronary angiography and during interventional treatments such as balloon angioplasty, stenting, or trans-aortic valve implantation (TAVI).

The catheter is usually inserted via the radial artery (in the wrist) or the femoral artery (in the groin). TAVI uses a large-bore femoral catheter.

The risk of death from cardiac catheterisation is approximately 0.1%.

---

## Complications and Management

### Contrast Reaction

Allergic reactions can occur to contrast media, local anaesthetic, heparin, or protamine.

A urticarial rash and itching appearing several hours after the procedure is most likely due to a delayed hypersensitivity reaction to contrast media.

**Delayed hypersensitivity reactions to contrast media** can occur from 1 hour to 1 week after intravascular contrast. They are usually skin reactions:

- Maculopapular rash (most common)
- Angio-oedema, urticaria, and erythema (less common)
- Bronchospasm and laryngeal oedema are not typically associated with delayed hypersensitivity reactions

Your doctor will assess the severity of the reaction. Treatment is as for an allergic reaction, typically with antihistamines. The reaction should be recorded in your medical history as an adverse reaction to contrast media.

---

### Clopidogrel Allergy

A cutaneous allergic reaction — usually a maculopapular rash — can occur with clopidogrel, which is often prescribed after stenting procedures.

**Do not stop clopidogrel** without medical advice. Stopping antiplatelet medication can increase the risk of stent thrombosis if a stent has been placed.

Your doctor will manage the rash with antihistamines. If there is insufficient improvement, your doctor may seek specialist cardiology advice about alternative antiplatelet medications.

---

### Pain at the Catheter Insertion Site

Some discomfort at the access site is normal for up to 72 hours after the procedure, especially with femoral artery access. Pain should be improving by 72 hours compared to day one.

Your doctor may recommend:

- Regular paracetamol for pain relief
- A physical examination if the procedure involved a large-bore femoral catheter (such as TAVI)
- Cardiology review if pain is increasing or has not resolved after 72 hours

---

### Swelling

Some firmness (induration) around the access site is expected.

Your doctor will assess swelling carefully. If you have mild swelling with associated pain or numbness of the thigh or forearm, your doctor may arrange an ultrasound to check for complications:

- **Pseudo-aneurysm** — a bleed into the arterial wall that may feel like a pulsatile (pulsing) lump. Usually appears within days of the procedure.
- **Arteriovenous fistula** — an abnormal connection between an artery and a vein created by the needle puncture.

If either complication is suspected or confirmed, your doctor will arrange further assessment and treatment, which may include specialist referral.

Seek urgent medical attention if there is a sudden increase in swelling, or swelling with pain or numbness.

---

### Bleeding

A mild ooze at the access site is possible for the first 24 to 48 hours.

- For a small bleed: apply firm pressure and observe for one hour.
- For a significant bleed: apply sustained pressure, elevate the arm if the bleed is from a radial artery site, and call an ambulance for emergency department assessment.

Do not stop antiplatelet therapy (such as aspirin or clopidogrel) without discussing with your cardiologist, as this can increase the risk of stent thrombosis if a stent was placed.

---

### Bruising

Bruising around the access site is normal and may last one to two weeks. It will slowly resolve on its own.

---

### Infection

Infection is rare. The risk may be slightly higher when a vascular closure device has been used, especially after TAVI.

Signs of infection include the access site becoming red, tender, swollen, and warm, with or without fever.

If infection is suspected, seek urgent medical review. Your doctor will arrange urgent specialist cardiology assessment.

---

### Arterial Thrombosis

Arterial thrombosis (clotting of the artery used for access) is rare.

- **Radial artery access:** Thrombosis usually does not cause symptoms because the ulnar artery provides sufficient blood supply to the hand. An absent radial pulse after the procedure is not clinically significant unless you have symptoms.
- **Femoral artery access:** Your doctor will look for signs of limb ischaemia — a white, cold, painful leg with absent or reduced pulses. If present, urgent vascular surgery assessment is needed.

---

### Other Complications

Other serious complications are rare. A summary of timing:

| Complication | Typical time frame after procedure |
|---|---|
| Arterial embolism or stroke (stroke risk approximately 0.4%) | Minutes to hours |
| Acute kidney injury | Usually peaks by day 3, starts to recover by day 5 |
| Stent thrombosis — presents like a heart attack | Within 30 days |
| Non-access site bleeding | Any time |
| Medication side-effects | Any time |
| Thrombophlebitis or vasculitis (rare) | Days |
| Late stent thrombosis (rare) — consider if antiplatelet therapy has recently stopped | 30 days to 1 year |

Troponin levels are often elevated after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) and do not necessarily indicate a heart attack. However, if you develop chest pain after the procedure, your doctor will carry out a clinical and laboratory assessment and may seek cardiology advice.
