# Tuberculosis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers suspected tuberculosis (TB) in adults and children, and management of people who have been in contact with a confirmed case. See also: Latent Tuberculosis (LTBI screening).

---

## Important — Always Discuss with Your Doctor

TB is a **notifiable disease** — your doctor is legally required to report suspected cases to the Medical Officer of Health. Diagnosis, treatment initiation, and ongoing management require specialist involvement. This information is to help you understand the process, not to replace medical assessment.

---

## What Is Tuberculosis?

Tuberculosis is a bacterial infection caused by *Mycobacterium tuberculosis*, spread person-to-person by inhaling airborne droplets from someone with infectious TB.

TB infection can result in two different states:

**Latent TB infection (LTBI)**
- The immune system contains the infection — no symptoms, not infectious.
- Can reactivate with immune suppression or age.
- Up to one-third of the world's population may have latent TB.
- 90% of people with LTBI never develop active disease, as long as they don't become significantly immunosuppressed.

**Active TB disease**
- Symptoms present (though 1 in 3 people with active pulmonary TB have no symptoms at all).
- Pulmonary and laryngeal TB are highly infectious.
- Active TB is notifiable.

**Who is infectious?**
- Adults with pulmonary or laryngeal TB are highly infectious.
- Children under 10 with pulmonary TB are generally not infectious (cannot cough forcefully enough to spread aerosols).
- Extrapulmonary TB (e.g., lymph node, bone) does not usually pose a public health risk unless there is also pulmonary involvement.

After starting TB treatment, infectivity falls significantly — most patients are not infectious after 2 weeks of treatment.

---

## Symptoms of Active TB

### Pulmonary TB symptoms
- Persistent cough for more than 3 weeks, with or without sputum
- Malaise and fatigue
- Anorexia and weight loss
- Haemoptysis (coughing blood — may occur very late)
- Fever and night sweats

### Extrapulmonary TB symptoms
- Persistent lymphadenopathy (neck, armpit, groin)
- Symptoms relating to the affected site — e.g. abdominal pain (abdominal TB), vaginal bleeding (uterine TB)

Note: 1 in 3 people with active pulmonary TB have no symptoms and are diagnosed incidentally on chest X-ray.

---

## Who Is at Higher Risk?

### Increased risk of exposure
- Household contacts of people with pulmonary or laryngeal TB
- People who have migrated from, or recently travelled to, a high TB incidence country (e.g. India, Philippines, parts of Africa)
- Healthcare workers
- Household contacts (especially children) of people at increased risk of TB

Note: it generally takes many hours of close contact with an infectious person for TB to be transmitted.

### Increased risk of serious or disseminated disease
- Infants and young children
- Elderly people
- People who are immunocompromised — due to medications (e.g. biologics, cancer treatment), disease (e.g. diabetes, kidney failure, HIV), or social factors (e.g. malnutrition, heavy alcohol use)

---

## Diagnosis

Your doctor will consider TB if you have relevant symptoms, risk factors, or an abnormal chest X-ray (including immigration chest X-rays).

### If TB is strongly suspected
Your doctor may proceed directly to management without further investigations — particularly if a chest X-ray is clearly suggestive of active TB, or if TB has already been found on a pathology sample.

### If TB is possible but not certain
Your doctor may arrange:

**Chest X-ray** (both posterior-anterior and lateral views)

Chest X-ray patterns that suggest higher risk include:
- Patchy consolidation or infiltration
- Definite infiltration, consolidation, or cavitation
- Scarring or calcification in a pattern typical of TB

Lower-risk patterns (suggesting old rather than active TB) include:
- Calcified lymph nodes or pleura with normal lung tissue
- Minor apical pleural thickening
- Single small calcified granuloma

**Sputum tests**
- Microscopy for acid-fast bacilli (AFB) and mycobacterial culture.
- Definitive diagnosis requires identifying *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* on culture.
- Sensitivity is 75–80%; culture can take up to 6 weeks or longer.
- Samples should ideally be collected outdoors, away from other people.

**For suspected extrapulmonary TB**
- Referral to the relevant specialist for appropriate investigations (e.g. fine needle aspiration of a lymph node, biopsy).

**What is NOT used for active TB diagnosis**
- Tuberculin skin test (TST / Mantoux) and IGRA (Quantiferon Gold) test for latent TB infection — not for diagnosing active TB disease.

## Management

### Initial steps (your doctor will)

1. **Refer appropriately:**
   - Children with any suspected TB → urgent paediatric assessment
   - Symptomatic adults with suspected pulmonary TB → urgent respiratory assessment
   - Adults with suspected extrapulmonary TB and normal chest X-ray → infectious diseases assessment
   - Asymptomatic adults with higher-risk chest X-ray changes → acute respiratory assessment
   - Asymptomatic adults with lower-risk chest X-ray changes → non-acute respiratory assessment

2. **Advise on infection control:**
   - Isolation requirements are discussed with the respiratory specialist.
   - You will be advised about cough hygiene (covering your mouth, using tissues, disposing carefully).
   - Infectivity drops quickly once treatment starts — most people are not infectious after 2 weeks.

3. **Notify the Medical Officer of Health** — this is a legal requirement for suspected active TB, done without waiting for test results.

### Ongoing treatment

- TB drug treatment is **initiated and monitored by specialists** (respiratory or infectious diseases), not by your GP.
- All TB prescriptions — including repeats — must come from your specialist, not your GP.
- Each patient with active TB is assigned a public health nurse (PHN) case manager who helps coordinate treatment and contact tracing.

**Drug interactions to be aware of:**
- Rifampicin (one of the main TB drugs) is a strong enzyme inducer and interacts with many other medications, including oral contraceptives. Women of childbearing age will need alternative or additional contraception during TB treatment.

**Common side effects of TB medications:**
- Rash
- Gastrointestinal upset
- Drug-induced hepatitis
- Gout
- Visual disturbance
- DRESS syndrome (drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms) — uncommon but serious

Contact your specialist, PHN case manager, or public health team if you experience side effects.

### After completing treatment

Your doctor will monitor you for signs of TB recurrence for up to 2 years after completing treatment. The risk is low for drug-susceptible TB, but some people have lasting effects from the disease or treatment, such as:
- Peripheral neuropathy (from some TB medications)
- Bronchiectasis
- Chronic pulmonary aspergillosis
