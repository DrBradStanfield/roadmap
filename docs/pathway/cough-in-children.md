# Cough in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of cough in children, including identifying serious causes and when emergency care is needed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Take your child to the emergency department or call an ambulance if:

- Cough started from birth (neonatal onset)
- Infant has episodes of stopping breathing (apnoea) or turning blue (cyanosis) during coughing
- Infant's cough is triggered by feeding
- Child choked on food or a small object while playing

---

## Background

Coughs are common in children and are usually caused by a respiratory tract infection that resolves within 1 to 3 weeks. Young children can have 4 to 10 respiratory infections a year, which may appear to parents as one long illness.

A daily cough lasting 4 weeks or more is considered chronic and requires assessment for underlying lung disease.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a careful history asking about:

**Cough features:**
- Onset (sudden, or following a viral illness), duration, and whether it is persistent or recurrent
- Type of cough: dry, moist, whooping, barking, or paroxysmal
- Pattern and timing: worse at night, in cold air, or occurring in paroxysms with vomiting
- Triggers: feeding, lying down, exercise, allergens, smoke, stress

**Related factors:**
- Association with food or fluid intake
- Failure to thrive or poor weight gain
- Symptoms of rhinitis, wheeze, asthma, or atopic conditions
- Other symptoms: difficulty swallowing, coughing up blood (haemoptysis), night sweats, poor growth
- Exercise tolerance
- Past medical history including hospital admissions, antibiotic and inhaler use
- Perinatal factors: prematurity, low birth weight, respiratory distress syndrome
- Tobacco smoke exposure and home environment (mould, carpets, proximity to farms)
- Family history of asthma
- Immunisation status

**Physical examination** typically includes:
- General appearance, alertness, hydration status
- Vital signs: temperature, heart rate, breathing rate, oxygen saturation
- Height, weight, and head circumference plotted on a growth chart
- Listening to the lungs: added sounds, stridor, wheeze, crackles, reduced air entry
- Signs of underlying pathology: finger clubbing, cyanosis, abnormal heart sounds, enlarged lymph nodes or organs

## Possible Causes

### Asthma

Wheezing is the main sign of asthma. Most children with asthma have both wheeze and cough. A child with cough alone (without wheeze) rarely has asthma as the cause, though "cough-predominant asthma" does occur.

### Cough Receptor Hypersensitivity / Allergy

Many children with chronic unexplained cough have an oversensitive cough reflex. Triggers can include:
- ACE inhibitor medications (these can cause cough — it can take 3 months to resolve after stopping)
- Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD)
- Upper airway disorders or eosinophilic airway disease
- Cigarette smoke exposure

### Croup

A barking cough, often worse at night, typically in young children following a viral illness.

### Inhaled Foreign Body

Consider if a well child has a very sudden onset of cough, choking, or wheeze — especially if there was a choking episode. Other symptoms can include fever, stridor, and chest discomfort. Aspiration is more common in children with developmental delay or swallowing problems.

### Pertussis (Whooping Cough)

Paroxysmal coughing episodes, sometimes with a "whoop" sound, vomiting, or apnoea. Can occur even in vaccinated children.

### Post-Viral Cough

Persistent dry cough after a virus can last more than 3 weeks. A persistent moist (wet) cough lasting 4 weeks or more after a simple viral illness is unusual and warrants further assessment.

### Psychogenic (Habit) Cough

- A dry, harsh, often honking repetitive cough, or throat clearing
- Usually follows an upper respiratory tract infection that acted as an initial irritant
- Tends to decrease during enjoyable activities and is absent during sleep
- The child may appear indifferent to the cough
- Consider tic disorder (including Tourette's syndrome) in the differential

### Tracheomalacia or Bronchomalacia

Floppiness of the airway cartilage causing airway collapse during coughing, crying, or feeding. Features include:
- A brassy cough
- Stridor or wheeze
- Frequent noisy coughing and lower airway infections
- Reduced exercise tolerance
- More severe: choking during feeding, apnoea, or blue episodes

### Tuberculosis (TB)

Children with TB are usually identified through contact tracing after an adult case. Refugee children are screened on arrival. Most children develop latent TB and are treated with isoniazid. Children under 5 years, and especially infants, are at high risk of active disease and are managed more aggressively.

### Wet (Moist) Cough

A wet cough lasting 4 weeks or more requires assessment for:
- Protracted bacterial bronchitis
- Bronchiectasis
- Cystic fibrosis

A wet cough lasting less than 4 weeks is more likely to be due to a respiratory infection, bronchiolitis (in infants under 12 months), or pneumonia.

## Management

Your doctor will manage the cough based on the identified or most likely cause:

- **Red flags or severely unwell child:** Emergency paediatric medical assessment
- **Asthma:** Bronchodilators and/or inhaled corticosteroids — your doctor will follow the appropriate asthma pathway
- **Cough receptor hypersensitivity / allergy:** Your doctor may consider identifying and managing triggers (e.g., stopping ACE inhibitors, treating GORD, advising on smoke exposure)
- **Croup:** Your doctor may consider oral corticosteroids (e.g., dexamethasone); infants under 12 months should not receive oral steroids for cough and wheeze — croup is the only exception
- **Inhaled foreign body:** Your doctor will arrange urgent further assessment
- **Pertussis (whooping cough):** Your doctor may consider antibiotics (e.g., azithromycin) and will arrange notification of public health authorities
- **Post-viral cough:** If no concerning features, your doctor will provide reassurance and explain the expected course; no specific treatment is usually needed
- **Psychogenic (habit) cough:** Early diagnosis avoids unnecessary investigations. Treatment involves explaining the cycle of irritation, encouraging cough suppression (e.g., sipping tepid water), and parental reinforcement of successful efforts
- **Tracheomalacia or bronchomalacia:** Your doctor will refer for non-acute paediatric assessment; if the child is unwell or very young, paediatric advice will be sought about the need for admission
- **Tuberculosis:** Emergency paediatric assessment; notification of the Medical Officer of Health is legally required for suspected TB
- **Persistent wet cough (≥ 4 weeks):** Your doctor will follow the persistent wet cough pathway, which may include investigations for bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, or protracted bacterial bronchitis

**General advice your doctor may give:**

- Cough and cold mixtures have no proven benefit in children and carry safety risks — they are not recommended
- Simple home remedies may help: water sips, honey sips, honey lozenges (do not give honey to children under 12 months)
- Plunket's home cough mixture (for children 12 months to 6 years, with no signs of chest infection): 2 tbsp fresh lemon/orange juice, 2 tbsp honey, 1 tbsp glycerine, 1 tsp olive oil — give half to 1 teaspoon 2–3 times daily; store in fridge
- Frequent hand washing by all family members during respiratory virus season
- If a child under 5 has significant respiratory illness, your doctor may set a recall for funded flu vaccination until age 5
- Household members who smoke will be encouraged to consider smoking cessation
