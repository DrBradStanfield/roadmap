# Dyspnoea (Shortness of Breath)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of dyspnoea (shortness of breath) in adults, including identifying life-threatening causes and guiding next steps.

> **Note:** This information is for general guidance only. Always discuss your symptoms with your doctor, who will assess your individual situation and determine the most appropriate investigations and management.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department if you have:

- Acute shortness of breath developing over hours or days
- Associated low oxygen levels or chest pain
- Associated weight loss and/or general deterioration
- Acute shortness of breath at rest
- Signs of severe respiratory distress (unable to speak in sentences, altered consciousness)
- Confusion
- New or unexplained oxygen saturation below 92%
- Rapidly developing symptoms
- Stridor (high-pitched breathing noise)

---

## Background

Dyspnoea (shortness of breath) is a subjective experience of breathing discomfort where a person feels they cannot breathe fast or deeply enough. It can be:

- **Acute**: develops over minutes, hours, or days
- **Chronic**: develops over weeks or months

Dyspnoea is common — 30% of people older than 40 years report breathlessness when walking briskly on flat ground. It is commonly caused by respiratory or cardiac disease, but around one third of patients have multiple causes including deconditioning, obesity, anxiety, and anaemia on top of an underlying condition.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history including:

**Triggers and associated symptoms**
- What triggers breathlessness (e.g. exercise, pollutants, anxiety)
- Chest pain (especially exertional, central, pleuritic, or pain relieved by leaning forward)
- Palpitations and syncope
- Cough and sputum production
- Coughing up blood (haemoptysis)
- Fever, wheeze, or stridor
- Whether breathlessness at rest improves with exertion (may suggest breathing pattern disorder)
- Weight loss, muscle weakness, difficulty swallowing, or orthopnoea (breathlessness when lying flat)

**Past history and risk factors**
- Previous respiratory or cardiac conditions
- Thoracic radiotherapy or surgery
- Anxiety or panic disorders
- Thyroid disease, blood clots in the lungs, or cancer
- Pregnancy (current or recent)
- Recent illness causing deconditioning, including COVID-19
- Smoking history (including vaping and second-hand smoke)
- Risk factors for pulmonary embolism (PE):
    - Recent surgery (within 4 weeks)
    - Prolonged immobilisation
    - Oestrogen therapy (contraceptive pill or HRT)
    - Active or metastatic cancer
    - Unilateral leg swelling
    - Pregnancy or postpartum (within 6 weeks)
    - Thrombophilia
- Occupational exposures (dust, fumes, asbestos, solvents, mould)
- Medications (inhalers, diuretics, methotrexate, amiodarone, nitrofurantoin, immunotherapy, oral corticosteroids, digoxin, calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers, topiramate)

**Severity assessment — MMRC Dyspnoea Scale**

Your doctor may use the Modified Medical Research Council (MMRC) scale to grade breathlessness:

| Grade | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 0 | Only breathless with strenuous exercise |
| 1 | Short of breath when hurrying on flat ground or walking up a slight hill |
| 2 | Walks slower than others of the same age on flat ground due to breathlessness, or has to stop for breath when walking at own pace |
| 3 | Stops for breath after about 100 metres or after a few minutes on flat ground |
| 4 | Too breathless to leave the house, or breathless when dressing |

**Physical examination** typically includes:
- Vital signs: heart rate, blood pressure (lying and standing), oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, temperature
- General examination: body weight, signs of anaemia, thyroid enlargement, leg swelling (DVT), abdominal examination
- Respiratory examination: upper airway, lymph nodes, cyanosis, finger clubbing, tracheal position, chest wall, breath sounds, wheeze, crepitations
- Cardiac examination: signs of heart failure, arrhythmia, pulmonary hypertension, valve disease
- Neurological examination if neuromuscular cause suspected

## Possible Causes

### Life-Threatening Causes (Require Emergency Assessment)

- Acute coronary syndrome
- Acute exacerbation of heart failure
- Acute valvular insufficiency
- Anaphylaxis
- Aortic dissection
- Serious arrhythmias
- Epiglottitis
- Foreign body aspiration
- Lung cancer and associated complications
- Pericardial tamponade
- Pneumothorax (symptoms range from mild breathlessness with chest pain to cardiovascular collapse; signs include reduced chest movement, diminished breath sounds, surgical emphysema, tracheal deviation)
- Pulmonary embolism
- Severe acute asthma or COPD exacerbation
- Severe respiratory infection (including COVID-19 or pneumonia)
- Upper airway obstruction

### Other Causes

**Respiratory**
COPD, asthma, infection, interstitial lung disease, pleural effusion, pneumothorax, malignancy, bronchiectasis, chest wall deformity. Upper airway obstruction (especially with history of cancer or prolonged intubation).

**Post-COVID**
Breathing pattern disorder, deconditioning, myocarditis, chronic pulmonary thromboembolic disease after COVID-19 infection. COVID-19 may also reveal underlying COPD.

**Cardiac**
Heart failure, ischaemic heart disease, arrhythmias, valvular heart disease, pericardial disease.

**Vascular**
Pulmonary embolism, pulmonary hypertension.

**Obesity and deconditioning / anxiety / hyperventilation**
Very common causes or contributors. Considered diagnoses of exclusion.

**Haematological**
Anaemia (unlikely to cause dyspnoea if haemoglobin > 110 g/L unless there has been a rapid recent drop).

**ENT causes**
Laryngeal obstruction, thyroid enlargement compressing the trachea.

**Endocrine**
Diabetic ketoacidosis, thyrotoxicosis.

**Neurological/neuromuscular**
Multiple sclerosis, motor neurone disease, myasthenia gravis, Guillain-Barré syndrome.

**Breathing pattern disorders**
Chronic or recurrent conditions where a disruption in normal breathing pattern results in breathlessness and other symptoms not fully explained by disease. Common features include:
- Breathlessness at rest that improves with exercise
- Chest pain at rest but not on exertion
- Frequent sighing and yawning
- Tingling in hands and fingers (not always associated with anxiety)
- On examination: fast breathing rate at rest, chest (rather than diaphragmatic) breathing, high oxygen saturation

Can coexist with COPD or heart disease.

## Investigations

Your doctor will arrange tests based on history and examination findings:

**Blood tests**
- Full blood count (FBC), electrolytes, kidney function, blood glucose, iron studies, thyroid function (TSH)
- D-dimer (to help exclude pulmonary embolism; used with Wells' criteria)
- NT-proBNP (to help rule out heart failure):
    - Less than 35 pmol/L makes heart failure unlikely
    - Higher thresholds apply depending on age (50–75 years: >138 pmol/L; >75 years: >279 pmol/L suggests heart failure)
    - Results can be affected by obesity, hypothyroidism, kidney disease, and AF

**Spirometry** (breathing test to assess lung function)
- Essential for diagnosing COPD
- Has some contraindications (active infection, recent surgery, recent heart attack, unstable conditions)

**Chest X-ray**

**ECG** (heart tracing — checks for ischaemia or arrhythmia)

**Echocardiogram** (heart ultrasound — checks valves and heart function)

**Holter monitor** (24-hour heart rhythm recording — if arrhythmia suspected)

## Management

Management depends on the underlying cause. Your doctor will guide treatment based on diagnosis. Conditions with their own specific pathways include:

- Anaemia, anaphylaxis, angina, anxiety, asthma, atrial fibrillation, bronchiectasis, chest pain, community-acquired pneumonia, COPD, COVID-19, heart failure, heart murmurs, interstitial lung disease, long COVID, lung cancer, palpitations, pulmonary embolism, thyroid disease, obesity

### Strategies to Relieve Breathlessness

Your doctor or physiotherapist may teach you specific techniques:

**Activity pacing**
- Plan activities in advance; arrange them for your best time of day
- Break tasks into smaller steps with rest periods in between

**Pursed lips breathing**
- Breathe in gently through the nose, breathe out slowly through the mouth with lips pursed
- Expiration should be longer than inspiration (e.g. 2 seconds in, 4 seconds out)
- Can be used at rest and during activity

**"Blow as you go" (exhalation with effort)**
- Exhale during the effort phase of an activity (e.g. when pushing up from a chair)
- Avoid holding your breath during exertion

**Forward-leaning position**
- Lean forward and support your arms (on a table, walker, or knees)
- This position allows breathing muscles to work more effectively

**Relaxation techniques**
- Passive relaxation (progressively relaxing muscles from toes upward)
- Progressive muscle relaxation
- Meditation or visualisation

**Walking aids**
- Using a walking frame can significantly reduce breathlessness during movement

### Additional Support

- **Pulmonary rehabilitation**: structured exercise programme to improve fitness and stamina — especially useful for COPD
- **Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)**: can reduce alarm response to breathlessness
- **Physiotherapy**: for breathing pattern disorders (Dysfunctional Breathing Clinic)
- **Nutritional support**: chronically breathless patients are often malnourished and weak
- **Home oxygen**: if you meet the criteria, your doctor will assess this
- **Palliative care support**: if breathlessness is related to an incurable condition

---

## When to See Your Doctor Urgently

Contact your doctor promptly (same day or next available) if:

- Breathlessness is new or unexplained
- There is no clear treatable cause after initial assessment
- Symptoms are not improving with treatment
- Investigations are abnormal
- Breathlessness is associated with suspected interstitial lung disease (cough or breathlessness for over 3 months, or crackling sounds on breathing)

Your doctor may refer you to a specialist (respiratory, cardiology, or general medicine) if the cause remains unclear after initial assessment and investigations.
