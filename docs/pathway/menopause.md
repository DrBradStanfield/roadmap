# Menopause

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: January 2025.*

This pathway covers perimenopause and menopause in women aged 40 years and older. Menopause is usually associated with symptoms of low estrogen.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Red Flags

**See your doctor if you have:**
- Any bleeding after menopause (postmenopausal bleeding). This is a red flag and needs assessment.
- Bleeding around the time of menopause that is abnormally painful, long, heavy, or frequent.

---

## Background

- Natural menopause is considered to have occurred after 12 consecutive months without a period, if there is no other obvious cause and no medical intervention.
- The average age of menopause is 51 years. It may be younger in women from Asia and Africa.
- Perimenopause is the transition from the start of menopausal symptoms to the final period. It lasts between 4 and 8 years, on average. Ovarian function fluctuates during this time.
- Up to 75% of women experience vasomotor symptoms (hot flushes or night sweats), usually lasting about 5 years.
- Thinning of the vaginal and urinary tissues (urogenital atrophy) occurs in all untreated postmenopausal women, although not all women have significant symptoms.
- There is no age limit when symptoms stop. At least 10% of women have bothersome symptoms 10 years after their final period. About 16% of women aged 85 years still have hot flushes or night sweats.
- Menopause is usually diagnosed from symptoms in women of the usual age. If there is doubt, it can be confirmed by persistently raised follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and low estradiol.
- Types of menopause:
  - **Premature ovarian insufficiency:** menopause younger than 40 years. It may be spontaneous (e.g. genetic, or linked to autoimmune disease) or caused by treatment such as chemotherapy or radiotherapy.
  - **Early menopause:** menopause younger than 45 years.
  - **Surgical menopause:** menopause after both ovaries are removed.

### Common symptoms

- Early perimenopause: changes in cycle length and bleeding pattern, heavy periods, breast swelling and tenderness, headaches, and worse premenstrual symptoms.
- Hot flushes and night sweats.
- Sleep disturbance and fatigue.
- Muscle or joint pains.
- Mood changes (irritability, anxiety, and depression).
- Brain fog.
- Crawling feelings under the skin (formication).
- New facial hair, dry skin, and hair changes.
- Vaginal dryness and fragile skin.
- Low libido and painful intercourse (often not reported).
- Urinary frequency and recurrent urinary tract infections.

## Assessment

Menopause is a clinical diagnosis. If you are younger than 40 years, your doctor will assess for other causes of missed periods (amenorrhoea).

### History

Your doctor may ask about:
- Your periods:
  - regular cycles suggest premenopause.
  - irregular cycles, or no periods for less than 12 months, suggest perimenopause.
  - no periods for more than 12 months suggests menopause.
  - bleeding after sex.
- Past or current hormone use, contraception needs, cervical and breast screening, and gynaecological surgery.
- Your symptoms. A symptom score sheet may help.
- Personal history of breast or endometrial cancer, cardiovascular disease, high blood pressure, diabetes, liver disease, stroke or TIA, blood clots, or osteoporosis.
- Family history of breast cancer, blood clots, osteoporosis, or early menopause.
- Smoking, alcohol, physical activity, diet, and social factors.

If you are younger than 45 years, or have no menopausal symptoms, your doctor may look for other causes of missed or infrequent periods, such as:
- pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- hormonal contraception.
- weight loss, eating disorders, excessive exercise, stress, or significant illness.
- polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS).
- thyroid problems.
- pituitary disease, including high prolactin.
- scarring inside the uterus after a procedure.
- medications, e.g. GnRH agonists.

Early menopause without symptoms is still possible.

### Examination

- Height, weight, and blood pressure.
- Your doctor may consider breast and pelvic examinations.

### Blood tests

Hormone blood tests (e.g. FSH, LH, AMH, estradiol, testosterone) are not routinely recommended if you have symptoms and are older than 45 years. The results are unlikely to change management.

- A single normal result does not rule out perimenopause, because hormone levels can fluctuate daily.
- Results are hard to interpret while using some hormonal contraception (e.g. the combined pill or Depo-Provera).
- Testosterone testing is not used to assess menopause.

Your doctor may consider hormone tests if:
- **Premature ovarian insufficiency is suspected (younger than 40 years).** Diagnosis needs:
  - at least 4 months of no periods or very infrequent periods.
  - high FSH (more than 25 units/L). In women with typical symptoms, a single high FSH may be enough.
  - other causes ruled out.
- **Early menopause is suspected (aged 40 to 44 years).** FSH more than 25 units/L with no periods, or on day 2 to 6 of the cycle, strongly suggests early menopause.
- **You are deciding whether to stop progestogen-only contraception** (see Contraception below).

If the diagnosis is uncertain, FSH may be repeated in 4 to 6 weeks. Some hormonal treatments can lower FSH, so your doctor may ask you to stop them for at least 2 to 6 weeks before the test and use non-hormonal contraception.

### Midlife health check

Your doctor may use the visit to:
- Make sure your cervical screening and mammogram are up to date.
- Assess cardiovascular risk, including blood pressure, weight, and blood tests (e.g. lipids, HbA1c).
- Assess osteoporosis risk, including a bone density scan if indicated.

## Management

> **Practice point:** Menopause hormone therapy (MHT) is generally effective and safe. It remains the most effective treatment for hot flushes, night sweats, and genitourinary symptoms, and it has been shown to prevent bone loss and fracture. For women within 10 years of their final period or before age 60 years, without contraindications, the benefits outweigh the risks.

If you have early menopause (aged between 40 and 45 years), your doctor may offer MHT unless it is unsuitable, until age 50 years (or longer if needed). MHT reduces symptoms and preserves bone density.

### Lifestyle

- **Smoking:** stopping can reduce hot flushes and improve sleep.
- **Alcohol:** may trigger hot flushes, and cutting down may shorten them. Lower-risk drinking for women is no more than 2 standard drinks a day and no more than 10 standard drinks a week, with at least two alcohol-free days a week.
- **Weight:** weight loss in women with overweight or obesity has been shown to help reduce hot flushes. A healthy diet and regular exercise help regardless of weight.
- **Diet:** eating well during menopause can protect bone density, reduce the risk of heart disease, and improve sleep, mood, and insulin sensitivity. See [The Mediterranean diet](https://www.healthinfo.org.nz/patientinfo/624308.pdf).
- **Exercise:**
  - Aim for 30 minutes of moderate activity on most days (a weekly total of 2.5 hours).
  - At least 2 muscle-strengthening sessions per week, at a gym or at home with weights, body weight, or resistance bands.
  - Regular balance and flexibility activities, e.g. tai chi, yoga, pilates, dancing.
  - Sprint interval training can increase lean mass, reduce fat mass, and improve fitness in postmenopausal women with overweight. After a warm-up, push as hard as possible for 20 to 30 seconds, then recover for at least 2 minutes. Work up to a maximum of 5, 30 second sprints.
  - Vigorous exercise can trigger a hot flush in some women, so choose intensity and timing that suit you.
- **Bone health:** weight-bearing, resistance, and balance exercise; eating well; and adequate sunlight, with vitamin D for those at risk.
  - Sunlight on the skin is the main source of vitamin D for most people. Sunscreen still allows vitamin D to form, but glass does not.
  - Skin is less efficient at making vitamin D after age 65 years. People with dark skin need 3 to 4 times more sun exposure for the same benefit.
  - Balance enough sun for vitamin D against the risk of skin cancer.

### Behavioural approaches for hot flushes

Apart from CBT and hypnotherapy, there is limited evidence that these reduce hot flushes. They may still improve comfort and make symptoms easier to tolerate.

- Avoid triggers, e.g. alcohol, caffeine, spicy foods.
- Layer clothing, reduce bed covers, and use fans or other cooling measures.
- Good sleep habits.
- Relaxation, mindfulness, and slow breathing.
- **Cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT):** has been shown to improve sleep and mood and reduce how much hot flushes bother you. Some studies show fewer hot flushes.
- **Hypnotherapy** (with a therapist or through an app): effective in reducing hot flushes and improving sleep.

### Menopause hormone therapy (MHT)

If you have moderate to severe symptoms that affect your quality of life, especially hot flushes, your doctor can help you decide whether to start MHT.

**Who it suits:**
- Your doctor may consider MHT if you are younger than 60 years with moderate to severe symptoms.
- There is a lack of evidence for MHT to prevent heart disease or dementia in women aged 50 years or older without symptoms.
- Starting MHT at age 60 years or older is less favourable. Baseline risks of heart disease, stroke, blood clots (VTE), and dementia are higher and may be increased by MHT.
- If MHT was started younger, it may be continued beyond age 60 years after weighing individual risks and benefits. Your doctor may consider the lowest effective dose and a non-oral route.

**MHT is not used if you have:**
- Pregnancy.
- Undiagnosed abnormal vaginal bleeding.
- Suspected or active breast, endometrial, or hormone-dependent cancer.
- A past history of breast cancer.
- An active blood clot (thromboembolic disorder).
- A heart attack or stroke in the last 6 weeks.
- Uncontrolled or untreated cardiovascular disease.
- Severe active liver disease.
- Porphyria.

If you have had ovarian or endometrial cancer, your doctor will seek advice from your specialist.

**Cautions (MHT through the skin may be used):**
- Previous heart disease or stroke.
- Previous blood clots (VTE).
- Diabetes.
- Gallbladder or liver disease (increased risk of gallbladder inflammation, which may be lower with MHT through the skin).
- Migraine with aura.
- High triglycerides.
- Uterine fibroids: MHT is safe for most women, and low-dose estrogen through the skin is unlikely to make them grow. If you have a fibroid larger than 5 cm and develop symptoms after starting MHT, your doctor may arrange a pelvic ultrasound.

Systemic MHT may increase stress urinary incontinence. Low-dose vaginal estrogen helps urinary symptoms (e.g. recurrent UTIs, overactive bladder, urge incontinence).

### Non-hormonal treatments

If MHT is not suitable, or you prefer not to use it, your doctor may consider a trial of:

**SSRIs or SNRIs**
- Paroxetine, fluoxetine, citalopram, escitalopram, and venlafaxine have all been shown to help hot flushes and menopause-related mood symptoms.
- Relief is rapid, often within 4 weeks.
- Doses are generally lower than for depression, e.g. paroxetine 10 to 20 mg, escitalopram 10 to 20 mg, citalopram 10 to 20 mg, or venlafaxine 75 mg slow release.
- Paroxetine and fluoxetine are avoided if you take tamoxifen.
- These are reduced slowly when stopping, to prevent withdrawal symptoms.
- Depression and anxiety can be hard to tell apart from menopause. Symptoms may improve with MHT, SSRIs, or both.

**Gabapentin**
- As effective as low-dose estrogen for hot flushes, at 300 to 900 mg per day.
- Usually started at 100 mg once a day for one week, then increased as needed up to 300 mg three times a day. Hot flushes usually ease within days of reaching an adequate dose.
- If sleep problems and night sweats are the main symptoms, it may be started at 100 mg at night and increased by 100 mg each week until symptoms ease (up to 900 mg per night).
- It also eases anxiety. Side effects include headache, dizziness, and sleepiness.
- Use for menopausal symptoms is an unapproved indication.

**Oxybutynin**
- Shown to be effective for moderate to severe hot flushes. It can reduce hot flushes by 30 to 70%. Full effect may take up to 4 to 6 weeks.
- Usually started at 2.5 mg at night for five nights, then 2.5 mg twice a day if side effects allow. It can be increased to 5 mg twice a day if needed and tolerated.
- Side effects include constipation, dry mouth, and difficulty passing urine.
- Avoided in older people taking many medicines, and in those with glaucoma, urinary retention, or heart disease.

**Stellate ganglion blockade**
- A nerve block in the neck, given under X-ray guidance. It has been shown to relieve severe hot flushes. Benefit peaks at 2 months, and lasts up to 6 months in some women.
- Generally for severe, debilitating hot flushes when other treatments are unsuitable or have not worked.
- It is not available in the public health system; a private menopause specialist can assess whether it is an option.

**Complementary treatments**
- There is insufficient evidence to support black cohosh for hot flushes.
- Given mixed evidence, soy foods, soy extracts, and the soy metabolite equol are not recommended.

## Contraception Around Menopause

MHT is not a contraceptive. If pregnancy is possible, contraception is still needed:
- If younger than 50 years: for two years after your final period.
- If aged 50 years or older: for one year after your final period.
- All women can stop contraception at age 55 years, whether or not they are still bleeding.

**Options your doctor may discuss:**
- **Combined oral contraceptive pill:** only for women younger than 50 years, and not together with MHT. It may help irregular bleeding and hot flushes. Contraindications include smoking, high blood pressure, migraine, blood clots, cardiovascular risk, and a BMI of 35 or greater. A low-dose pill (20 micrograms ethinylestradiol) is preferred, although 30 micrograms may be equally safe. From age 50 years, estrogen-containing contraception is avoided.
- **52 mg levonorgestrel IUD (Mirena):**
  - Provides contraception, treats heavy periods, and can supply the progestogen part of MHT.
  - Having no periods does not show menopause in users of this device.
  - If aged 50 years or older with no periods for at least 12 months, your doctor may check a single FSH level. If it is 30 units/L or fewer, contraception continues and FSH is repeated in 12 months. If it is more than 30 units/L, contraception may stop after a further 12 months without periods.
  - If fitted at or after age 45 years, it can stay in as contraception for 8 years, or until menopause if you have no periods. It is removed at age 55 years.
  - If used as the progestogen part of MHT, it is replaced after 5 years.
- **Copper IUD:** if fitted at age 40 years or older (300 mm2 or more of copper), it can stay in until 1 year after your last period if that happens at age 50 years or older, or 2 years after if younger than 50 years.
- **Progestogen-only implant or pill:** can be continued to age 50 years and older. If aged 50 to 55 years with no periods for at least 1 year, FSH may help decide when to stop, as above. Contraception can stop without an FSH test at age 55 years.
- **Depo-Provera injection:** avoided after age 50 years because of possible effects on bone density and cardiovascular risk.
- **Barrier methods.**

## Vaginal and Urinary Symptoms (Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause)

Symptoms include vaginal dryness, irritation, itching, pain with sex, light bleeding, pain passing urine, recurrent urinary infections, and a persistent, smelly discharge. Your doctor will first rule out other causes, such as infection, cervical problems, or lichen sclerosus.

**Vaginal estrogen**
- Effective treatment. It can help recurrent UTIs and urinary incontinence and makes pelvic floor exercises work better.
- Not used with active endometrial cancer or undiagnosed vaginal bleeding.
- Considered safe after a previous blood clot, and for survivors of endometrial, cervical, or ovarian cancer.
- After estrogen-dependent breast cancer, if symptoms persist despite non-hormonal treatment, low-dose vaginal estrogen can be prescribed, even with aromatase inhibitors. There is a lack of evidence that it harms breast cancer outcomes.
- Absorption into the body is thought to be low, and no extra progestogen is needed.
- Early side effects: irritation and stinging (a graduated start can help); rarely, mild breast pain or vaginal bleeding.
- A typical regimen is estriol vaginal cream 1 mg/g, one applicator at bedtime every night for two weeks, then twice a week.
- If the applicator is uncomfortable, the cream can be applied with a finger, starting with small amounts.
- See your doctor about any ongoing bleeding.

**Skin care**
- Avoid soaps and detergents on the area, perfumed products, scented pads or liners, scratching, and staying in wet swimwear.
- Wash once or twice daily with warm water, a soap substitute, or a pH-balanced cleanser.
- Keep cool, especially at night, and wear loose, natural fibre underwear.

**Other measures**
- Pelvic floor exercises can help control symptoms. Pelvic floor physiotherapy can guide you.
- Regular sexual activity can help keep vaginal tissue healthy.
- If you prefer not to use hormones, mild symptoms can respond to:
  - vaginal moisturisers, used about 3 times a week (some are not compatible with condoms, so check the label).
  - water-based or silicone-based lubricants at the time of sex. These are compatible with condoms. Oil-based lubricants (e.g. petroleum gel) can cause condoms to fail.

## Sexual Concerns

- Your doctor may consider relationship and psychosocial factors, and make sure vaginal symptoms are well managed.
- MHT and vaginal estrogen improve lubrication, blood flow, and sensation. They do not improve desire, arousal, or orgasm apart from their effect on vaginal symptoms.

**Testosterone therapy**
- Shown to help postmenopausal women with recent onset of low sexual desire that causes distress. A short questionnaire can help identify this.
- There is insufficient data to support testosterone for low desire before menopause, depression or low well-being, bone loss, weight gain, or long-standing low desire.
- Products designed for men are not recommended, as they may cause masculinising effects (virilisation). Testosterone gel and oral testosterone are not recommended.
- A testosterone cream designed for women (10 mg/mL) is licensed in Australia and New Zealand but not funded.
- Not used with known or suspected breast cancer or androgen-dependent cancer, nephrotic syndrome, a history of blood clots or high calcium, pregnancy or breastfeeding, or in women of reproductive age without adequate contraception. Avoided with severe acne or excess hair growth.
- Before starting, your doctor may optimise MHT first (estrogen through the skin may raise free testosterone) and check baseline total testosterone and SHBG.
- The usual dose is 0.5 mL (5 mg) daily, or a lower 0.25 mL (2.5 mg) if SHBG is low. It is applied to the lower torso or outer thigh. Applying it to the arms risks transfer to children or pets.
- The most common side effects are acne and increased hair growth. At 5 mg/day, studies have not shown clitoral enlargement, male pattern baldness, or voice deepening. Higher doses increase these risks.
- **Monitoring:** testosterone is rechecked after 3 to 6 weeks, and the dose lowered (e.g. 3 to 4 mg/day) if it is high. Response is reviewed at 3 months; improvement can take up to 8 to 12 weeks. Treatment stops if there is no benefit after 6 months.
- There is no long-term safety data beyond 24 months. If continued, your doctor may check for androgen excess every 6 to 12 months, test testosterone every 6 months, and monitor blood pressure, FBC, LFTs, HbA1c, and lipids.

**Tibolone**
- A medicine with estrogen-like, progestogen-like, and androgen-like effects. An alternative to estrogen and progestogen after menopause when better libido is wanted.
- Eases hot flushes, may help mood, treats thinning of the vaginal and urinary tissues, and prevents bone loss. It does not stimulate the lining of the uterus.
- Side effects include headache, dizziness, nausea, abdominal pain, swollen feet, and itching.
- Not used after breast cancer, blood clots, active cardiovascular disease, or stroke.
- Not funded.

## Other Health Changes

- Weight gain (average 4 kg), with fat around the abdomen, even in slim women.
- Insulin resistance and increased risk of type 2 diabetes.
- Increased cardiovascular risk.
- Bone loss. Your doctor may assess osteoporosis risk, including a bone density scan if indicated.

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may seek specialist input if:
- You have had ovarian or endometrial cancer and are considering MHT (advice from your specialist).
- Menopause is severe or complicated (endocrinology or gynaecology advice, or a private menopause specialist).
- Stellate ganglion blockade is an option for severe hot flushes (private menopause specialist).
- You have vaginal or urinary symptoms and need pelvic floor exercise guidance (pelvic floor physiotherapy).

## Key Concepts for Patients

- Menopause is usually diagnosed from your symptoms and age. Hormone blood tests are not routinely needed if you have symptoms and are older than 45 years.
- Any bleeding after menopause needs to be checked by your doctor.
- MHT is the most effective treatment for hot flushes and vaginal symptoms. For women within 10 years of their final period or before age 60 years, without contraindications, the benefits outweigh the risks.
- Non-hormonal medicines, CBT, and lifestyle changes are options if MHT is not suitable or not wanted.
- MHT is not a contraceptive. If pregnancy is possible, contraception is needed for one to two years after your final period, depending on your age.
- Always discuss any new symptoms or medication questions with your doctor before making changes.
