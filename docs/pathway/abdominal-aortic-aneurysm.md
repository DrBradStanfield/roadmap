# Abdominal Aortic Aneurysm

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA) — a widening of the main artery running through the abdomen.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department immediately if you have:

- New back, flank, abdominal, or groin pain with associated tenderness — this may indicate dissection or rupture, which is a life-threatening emergency

---

## Background

- Most AAAs are detected as an incidental finding on examination or imaging — most patients have no symptoms.
- Most people die *with* an AAA rather than *because of* one.
- The normal diameter of the abdominal aorta is 20 mm. An aneurysm is defined as a diameter greater than 30 mm.
- **Size is the most important predictor of rupture risk.** The risk increases markedly once the diameter exceeds 55 mm in men, or 50 mm or more in women. Rate of expansion and sex also influence risk.
- Radiology reports may include up to three measurements (anterior-posterior diameter, transverse diameter, and length). The anterior-posterior (AP) diameter is the primary measurement that guides management.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- **Review risk factors.** AAA is more common in people who are older (especially over 65), male, smokers, have high blood pressure, have existing vascular disease, have a first-degree relative with AAA, or are Māori.
- **Take a history** — asking about new onset back, abdominal, loin, or groin pain, and assessing cardiovascular risk factors.
- **Examine the abdomen** — feeling for a pulsatile mass; if associated tenderness is present, this raises concern for imminent rupture.
- **Measure pulse and blood pressure.**
- **Arrange blood tests** — including full blood count, renal function, lipids, and HbA1c.
- **Consider abdominal ultrasound** if a non-acute AAA is suspected. Ultrasound is not appropriate if acute AAA is suspected — emergency vascular surgery assessment is needed instead.

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following approaches:

### Emergency management

If there are any signs or symptoms of dissection or rupture, immediate emergency vascular surgery assessment is needed.

### Risk factor reduction

People with an AAA are at high risk of cardiovascular disease. Your doctor will work with you to reduce all modifiable risk factors:

- **Smoking cessation** — smoking increases the rate of aneurysm growth by 20–25%. Quitting is one of the most important steps.
- **Blood pressure control** — rigorous management is recommended; no particular anti-hypertensive drug is preferred over others.
- **Lipid management** — statins are recommended because AAA patients have increased cardiovascular risk, and statins reduce overall mortality.
- **Antiplatelet therapy** — if the AAA is thought to be due to atherosclerotic disease and there are no contraindications, your doctor may start low-dose aspirin (e.g., 100 mg daily).
- **Diabetes management** — aggressive control of blood glucose is recommended.
- **Physical activity** — there is no specific evidence that physical activity increases the risk of AAA rupture.

### Surveillance

For non-acute AAA, your doctor may refer you to a vascular surgeon for further assessment. The vascular service will:

- Discuss the implications of an AAA diagnosis with you.
- Assess suitability for surgical repair.
- Arrange ongoing surveillance scans at appropriate intervals based on the size of the aneurysm.

Typical ultrasound surveillance intervals (endorsed by the Vascular Society of New Zealand) are guided by aneurysm diameter and patient characteristics. As a general guide, smaller aneurysms are monitored less frequently (every 1–5 years), while larger aneurysms require more frequent review (every 6 months or specialist-directed). Your doctor or specialist will advise the recommended interval for your situation.

Your doctor may also provide advice about driving following an AAA diagnosis, as this may be affected depending on aneurysm size.
