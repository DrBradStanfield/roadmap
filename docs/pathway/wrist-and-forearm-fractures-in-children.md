# Wrist and Forearm Fractures in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of wrist and forearm fractures in children, including how urgency is determined and how different fracture types are treated.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Your child should be assessed urgently if any of the following are present:

- Open fracture (bone visible through skin or skin wound overlying fracture)
- Fracture involving growth plates (physeal injuries)
- Compartment syndrome in forearm fracture

---

## Background

Wrist and forearm fractures in children commonly occur after a fall. They typically present as a painful wrist or forearm, with or without visible deformity. Young children may be unable or unwilling to move the affected arm.

---

## Assessment

Your healthcare provider will examine the injured arm for:

- Deformity or swelling
- Skin trauma (a wound may indicate an open fracture)
- **Neurovascular status** of the hand — checking nerve function, pulses, and blood flow distal to the injury (assessed before and after any treatment)
- **Compartment syndrome** in forearm fractures — signs include excessive pain, a tense compartment, and pain on finger extension
- Point tenderness — an older child with a painful wrist may have a scaphoid fracture; your doctor will feel for tenderness in the anatomical snuff box and compare both sides
- Any associated injuries, particularly at the elbow

> **Practice point:** The elbow should always be examined for associated injuries.

A fracture may not be clinically obvious on examination alone.

### X-ray

If an X-ray is indicated, your healthcare provider will arrange appropriate pain relief and splinting beforehand. X-ray views will depend on the location of symptoms and may include:

- Wrist or forearm (AP and lateral views)
- Elbow views, if elbow symptoms are present
- Scaphoid views, if scaphoid fracture is suspected

### Fracture Types on X-ray

Your doctor will assess the X-ray to identify the fracture type:

| Fracture Type | Description |
|---|---|
| **Buckle (torus) fracture** | Compression injury (e.g., fall on outstretched hand). One or both cortices show a wrinkle or buckle; cortices remain intact. Most common in children. |
| **Greenstick fracture** | Partial fracture — one side of the bone is broken, the other is bent. Your doctor will assess cortical involvement and angulation. |
| **Complete fracture** | Both cortices broken through. |
| **Growth plate (physeal) fracture** | Involves the growth plate — requires urgent specialist assessment. |
| **Scaphoid fracture** | May not be visible on initial X-ray; suspected if tenderness in the anatomical snuff box. |

### Non-Accidental Injury

Your healthcare provider will always consider non-accidental injury (NAI) as part of the assessment and will report as appropriate.

---

## Management

### All Fractures

Adequate pain relief is provided to all children with fractures.

### When Urgent Specialist Assessment Is Needed

Your healthcare provider will arrange urgent paediatric orthopaedic assessment if:

- Open fracture
- The limb appears deformed
- Compartment syndrome in a forearm fracture
- Fracture angulation on X-ray is ≥ 10°
- Fracture involves growth plates (physeal injuries)
- Monteggia fracture-dislocation is found
- Galeazzi fracture-dislocation is found

> **Important:** If urgent orthopaedic assessment is required, your child will be kept fasted (nothing to eat, drink, or chew) until they are seen.

### Ulna Fractures — Monteggia Fracture-Dislocation

If your child has a ulna fracture, your doctor will look carefully for a Monteggia fracture-dislocation — dislocation of the radial head at the elbow combined with the ulna fracture. This can be easily missed on X-ray and is most common in children aged 4 to 10 years. Reduction is always required. If found, urgent specialist assessment will be arranged.

### Isolated Radius Fractures — Galeazzi Fracture-Dislocation

If your child has an isolated radius fracture, your doctor will check for a Galeazzi fracture-dislocation — a fracture of the distal radius with disruption of the distal radioulnar joint (DRUJ) at the wrist. This can be difficult to recognise and always requires specialist referral for reduction.

---

## Management by Fracture Type

### Buckle (Torus) Fracture

- A removable wrist splint is applied for 3 weeks. Splints are as effective as casts and better tolerated.
- If a splint is unavailable, a backslab or soft cast may be used instead.
- **Routine follow-up X-rays are not needed** for children treated with a wrist splint.
- Good function usually returns within 6 weeks.
- Return to sport: sports such as soccer can often continue (using the splint for another 2–3 weeks). Contact sports and rough play that might apply force to the upper extremity should be avoided for 6 weeks. Your healthcare provider can advise on return to sport for your child's specific situation.

### Greenstick Fractures (Non-Growth-Plate Injuries)

Treatment depends on angulation and cortical involvement:

- **Angulation < 10°, single cortex:** Below-elbow plaster for 3–5 weeks (duration depends on severity and age; younger children generally need less time).
- **Angulation < 10°, both cortices of radius involved:** Below-elbow cast for 4–5 weeks.

A review X-ray at 1 week is arranged to check angulation:
- If angulation worsens, manipulation may be required.
- If the cast becomes loose, it may be replaced at 2 weeks.
- A broad arm sling is advised.

### Fractures Requiring a Full Cast

If a full cast is applied, your healthcare provider will ensure:

- The cast is moulded properly and fits well.
- Your child can freely move all fingers and thumb at the knuckle (MCP) joints — unless treating a scaphoid fracture, where the knuckle is immobilised and only the thumb's fingertip joint is kept mobile.

Fracture clinic follow-up is arranged at 1 week for fractures managed outside of hospital (except buckle/torus and greenstick fractures which are managed as above).
