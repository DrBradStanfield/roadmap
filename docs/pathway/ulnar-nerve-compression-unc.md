# Ulnar Nerve Compression (UNC)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of ulnar nerve compression at the elbow (cubital tunnel syndrome), the most common site of entrapment.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Medical Attention Promptly

See your doctor urgently if you have:

- Progressive neurological signs (worsening weakness or loss of sensation)
- Muscle wasting in the hand

These features may require specialist referral for surgical assessment.

---

## Background

Ulnar nerve compression (UNC) is the second most common entrapment neuropathy. It is often called **Cubital Tunnel Syndrome**, as the cubital tunnel at the elbow is the most common site.

- Frequently bilateral (can affect both arms)
- Most commonly occurs at the elbow (cubital tunnel)
- Less frequently at the wrist (Guyon's canal, where the nerve passes the pisiform and hamate bones)
- Compression can also occur at the thoracic outlet (T1 or C8 nerve roots)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a thorough history and perform an examination. This section describes what that typically involves.

### History

**Risk factors your doctor will ask about:**

- Male sex (5 times more likely than female)
- Diabetes
- Bony deformities of the elbow
- Smoking
- Increasing age

**Symptoms (depend on location of compression):**

For compression at the elbow:

- Numbness and tingling in the little and ring fingers, often at night
- Pain in the medial (inner) elbow
- Grip weakness or loss of hand dexterity

**Possible causes your doctor will consider:**

At the elbow:
- Soft tissue masses
- Distal humerus fracture
- Lacerations
- Nerve traction or friction from prolonged elbow flexion
- Elbow joint degeneration

At the wrist:
- Ganglion cyst
- Tumour
- Aneurysm
- Lacerations
- Fractured hook of hamate
- Repetitive trauma (e.g., use of power tools, pushing a manual wheelchair)

### Examination

Your doctor will look for:

**Sensory deficits:**
- Reduced sensation or tingling in the ulnar side of the ring finger and the whole little finger
- Reduced sensation on the ulnar side of the hand (both back and palm), up to the wrist

**Motor deficits:**
- Wasting of the small muscles of the hand (intrinsic and hypothenar muscles)
- Clawing of the ring and little fingers — more evident in distal lesions
- Special tests your doctor may perform:
  - **Froment's sign** — difficulty holding paper between thumb and index finger (weak adductor pollicis); the person compensates by flexing the thumb at the IP joint
  - **Wartenberg's sign** — the little finger adopts an outward (abducted) posture due to intrinsic muscle weakness
  - **"Making a wish" sign** — unable to cross the index finger over the middle finger

**Provocation tests:**

The most sensitive provocation test is the **combined pressure flexion test**:
- 30 seconds of elbow flexion with firm pressure on the ulnar nerve at the elbow
- Positive if this causes pain or tingling in the little and ring fingers

Note: The sensitivity and specificity of provocation tests are not high. They are used as part of a complete clinical assessment.

## Differential Diagnoses

Your doctor will consider other conditions that can cause similar symptoms:

- **C8 or T1 nerve root lesion** — neck and arm pain, upper motor neurone signs, sensation changes above the hand, weakness in muscles not supplied by the ulnar nerve
- **Thoracic outlet syndrome** — vascular abnormalities, fatigue with arm raised overhead
- **Brachial plexus abnormalities** — involvement of multiple nerves
- **Motor neurone disease**
- **Pancoast tumour** — cancer in the superior sulcus of the lung causing shoulder and arm pain in C8, T1, and T2 distributions
- **Medial epicondylitis** (golfer's elbow)

---

## Investigations

**Diagnosis is usually clinical** — based on characteristic symptoms and signs.

Your doctor may consider:

- **X-ray** — if a bony lesion or underlying joint changes are suspected
- **Nerve conduction studies** — not usually helpful in primary care; may be arranged by a specialist if there is diagnostic uncertainty
- **Ultrasound** — may be useful if a local soft tissue lesion is suspected (note: not publicly funded)

---

## Management

Your doctor will guide management based on the severity of your symptoms.

### Immediate Referral

If you have progressive neurological signs or muscle wasting, your doctor will refer you for specialist (hand surgery) assessment as a priority.

### Self-Management

Your doctor will likely advise:

- **Modify activities:** use a headset for the phone, use the other hand for driving, avoid leaning on elbows
- **Avoid precipitating activities:** prolonged elbow flexion, bending the elbow while sleeping
- **Elbow positioning overnight:** keep the elbow extended — a soft pad or rolled towel placed in the front of the elbow (antecubital fossa) can help maintain extension during sleep

### Splinting

For mild, intermittent, or moderate symptoms without muscle wasting, your doctor may consider splinting:

- Aims to hold the elbow in partial extension, reducing stretch on the nerve
- **Night-time splinting** alone has been shown to be beneficial, as nocturnal elbow flexion aggravates symptoms
- Daytime splinting may be added if night-time splinting alone is insufficient
- May also help patients who have not improved after decompression surgery

### Hand Therapy

Your doctor may refer you to a hand therapist who can offer:

- Graded exercises and rehabilitation monitoring
- Customised or off-the-shelf splints
- Guidance to reduce or delay the need for surgical intervention

### Surgery

Your doctor may consider referring you for hand surgery assessment if:

- Conservative treatment has failed
- Symptoms are moderate to severe
- Symptoms are progressive, especially motor (weakness) symptoms
- Paraesthesia (tingling/numbness) is persistent

Surgical options include **ulnar nerve decompression** or **ulnar nerve transposition** (open or endoscopic, usually under general anaesthetic, sometimes under a regional nerve block).

When a neurological deficit is present, surgery can prevent progression but may not result in complete recovery of the deficit.
