# New-onset Seizures in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the first presentation of seizures or a new type of seizure in children and young people younger than 15 years.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately if:

- Child is aged under 2 years
- Prolonged QT interval on ECG
- Focal neurological symptoms or signs
- Regression of development
- Any injury without adequate explanation or identified event

---

## Background

Up to 5% of children experience a seizure of some kind during childhood, but only 0.5 to 1% have a diagnosis of epilepsy.

Epileptic and non-epileptic seizures present with similar symptoms and signs but have different underlying mechanisms. In epileptic seizures, symptoms are caused by abnormal electrical activity in the brain; in non-epileptic seizures, there is no abnormal electrical activity.

The occurrence of an epileptic seizure does not mean the child has epilepsy. Causes of epileptic seizures include:

- **Acute symptomatic seizure** — caused by an acute insult to the brain:
  - Metabolic states (e.g. hypoglycaemia, hypoxia)
  - Traumatic brain injury
  - Infection of the brain (e.g. encephalitis, meningitis)
  - Toxins (including drugs and alcohol)
- Febrile seizures
- Single epileptic seizures
- Epilepsy

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess the child in a structured way. The key steps are:

### If the Child is Actively Seizing

- Move the child away from objects that may cause harm
- Perform a jaw thrust if concerned about the airway — do not attempt to open the mouth
- Time the seizure
- Ask someone to video the seizure if possible
- Check temperature, pulse, and blood glucose
- Proceed directly to acute seizure management (see below)

### History (When Stable)

Your doctor will take a detailed history and review any video recordings, as this is often the key to diagnosis.

**About the seizure itself:**

- Was it during sleep, on waking, or while the child was awake?
- What were they doing at the time?
- Were there any triggers (fever, head bump, sleep deprivation, emotional upset, unusual circumstances)?
- Any exposure to drugs, toxins, or alcohol (accidental or intentional)?
- Did the child experience an aura (e.g. tingling, numbness, visual changes, funny smells, rising sensation in the belly, fear)?
- Was awareness impaired?
- Was there loss of consciousness, falling, or loss of bladder/bowel control?
- Were there movements — stiffening, jerking, tremor, rhythmic clonic jerking, vocalisations?
- Which body parts and which side were involved? Did it start in one place and spread?
- Were there autonomic features (nausea, pallor, flushing, pupil changes)?
- Were there automatisms (chewing, swallowing, picking)?
- How long did the seizure last?
- How quickly did the child recover? Were there symptoms after (drowsiness, headache, focal weakness)?

**Other history:**

- Any previous similar events (may not have been recognised as seizures at the time)
- Past medical history including delivery, neonatal events, and developmental concerns
- Family history — particularly sudden deaths at a young age, drownings, events triggered by exercise or fright (may indicate cardiac syncope), arrhythmic disorders, or epilepsy

### Examination

Your doctor will typically:

- Watch the child's gait and movements
- Look for focal neurological signs
- Check for signs of meningism
- Perform fundoscopy
- Measure blood pressure
- Check for unexplained injuries (and consider non-accidental injury if injury is unexplained)
- Measure head circumference
- Assess whether development is consistent with the child's age
- Examine the skin for neurocutaneous stigmas:
  - Café au lait spots (neurofibromatosis)
  - Angiofibromas or ash leaf macules (tuberous sclerosis)
  - Trigeminal port wine stain (Sturge-Weber syndrome)

### Investigations

- **ECG** — to rule out a cardiac cause (e.g. long QT syndrome) if:
  - The child collapsed or had syncope
  - The event was associated with exercise
  - The child had a tonic or tonic-clonic seizure
  - There is significant family history (e.g. sudden death at a young age or arrhythmic disorders)
- **Blood tests** — only if indicated by history or examination (e.g. features suggesting a metabolic disorder). Most children do not need blood tests in primary care prior to referral.

## Distinguishing Epileptic from Non-epileptic Seizures

Your doctor will consider whether the event is more likely to be an epileptic or non-epileptic seizure.

### Common Non-epileptic Mimickers in Children

| Condition | Typical age | Key features |
|-----------|-------------|--------------|
| Syncope (fainting) | Any age, often teens | Triggered by prolonged standing, heat, emotional stress, micturition, or cough. Fading vision and sound, pale and clammy. Brief loss of consciousness (usually < 30 seconds), then quickly recovers when laid flat. |
| Cardiac syncope | Any age | Due to structural heart disease, arrhythmia, or channelopathies (e.g. long QT syndrome). Sudden loss of consciousness triggered by exercise, swimming, emotional stress, or noise. Palpitations may be reported. Check for family history of sudden death in young relatives. |
| Breath-holding spells | Toddlers | Triggered by pain or strong emotional upset. Child cries or gasps, stops breathing, changes colour (cyanosis or pallor), then briefly loses consciousness. May be associated with iron deficiency. |
| Benign neonatal sleep myoclonus | Neonates | Repetitive jerks of limbs, trunk, or whole body occurring only during sleep; stop abruptly when the child wakes. |
| Night terrors | 3 to 12 years | Episodes of waking inconsolably distressed for up to 45 minutes, eyes open but inattentive. Little or no memory of the event. Usually one event per night, once a week. May be mistaken for frontal lobe seizures. |
| Psychogenic non-epileptic seizures (PNES) | Adolescence, especially girls | Semi-purposeful movements, forced eye closure, tongue protrusion, pelvic thrusting, resistance to examination, lack of injuries. Rapid recovery even after long events. Often associated with underlying trauma or mental illness. |
| Tics | Childhood | Vary in movements; child has awareness of an urge to perform them and may be able to suppress them. |
| Migraine | Variable | Visual aura of migraine is slower to develop and longer lasting than the rapid-onset, short-duration, coloured circular visual hallucinations of occipital seizures. First episode of hemiplegic migraine may be mistaken for a focal seizure. Headache usually present. |

### Types of Epileptic Seizures

**Focal seizures** — originate in one hemisphere, may spread to both sides:
- Focal aware seizures
- Focal impaired-awareness seizures
- Focal to bilateral tonic-clonic seizures
- Symptoms vary by brain area affected: motor (jerks, stiffening), somatosensory (paraesthesiae, auditory/visual), psychic (déjà vu), or autonomic (nausea, pallor, heart rate or pupil changes)

**Generalised seizures** — originate in bilateral hemispheric networks:
- Absence
- Myoclonic
- Tonic-clonic
- Atonic
- Tonic
- Epileptic spasms

**Unknown onset** — how the seizure started is unknown; typically observed as tonic-clonic.

## Management

### Acute Seizure Management

Your doctor may consider the following steps during an active seizure:

**1. Keep the child safe:**
- Move them away from objects that may cause harm
- Perform a jaw thrust if concerned about the airway — do not attempt to open the mouth
- Time the seizure
- Ask someone to video the event if possible
- Check temperature, pulse, and blood glucose

**2. Manage hypoglycaemia if present:**

If blood glucose is low (less than 4 mmol/L for patients with diabetes, less than 3.3 mmol/L for all other patients):

- *If unconscious, seizing, or unable to take anything orally:* Give 2 mL/kg of 10% glucose by slow IV injection (over 5 minutes) or by intraosseous access. If glucagon is available and can be given more quickly, it may be used (500 micrograms for children ≤25 kg; 1 mg for children >25 kg). Repeat blood glucose within 10 minutes.
- *If conscious:* Give 15 g of fast-acting carbohydrate (e.g. 3–4 glucose tablets, 150 mL fruit juice, 150 mL regular soft drink, 1 tablespoon of sugar or jam). Retest blood glucose after 10 minutes; repeat carbohydrate if still below 4 mmol/L.

**3. If the seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes — rescue medication:**

Call an ambulance immediately. Your doctor may administer rescue benzodiazepine medication:

*Buccal or intranasal midazolam* (drug of choice):
- Under 3 months: 0.5 mg/kg (maximum 2.5 mg)
- 3 to 11 months: 2.5 mg
- 1 to 4 years: 5 mg
- 5 to 9 years: 7.5 mg
- 10 to 18 years: 10 mg (maximum dose)

*Alternative — rectal diazepam:*
- Neonate: 1.25 to 2.5 mg
- 1 month to 2 years: 5 mg
- 2 to 12 years: 5 to 10 mg
- 12 to 18 years: 10 to 20 mg
- Note: delayed respiratory depression may occur after rectal administration.

If still seizing 1 minute after the first dose: administer oxygen via face mask to keep oxygen saturation at least 92%, and attempt intravenous access while waiting for the ambulance.

A second dose of rescue medication may be considered if still seizing after 5 minutes and no prior doses have been given at home or by paramedics.

If intravenous access has been obtained: IV midazolam 0.15 mg/kg (maximum 10 mg).

### Post-seizure Management

**1. Recovery:**

Once the seizure subsides, your doctor will:
- Place the child in the left lateral (recovery) position
- Measure blood pressure and pulse
- Recheck blood glucose
- Assess level of alertness and mental state using the Modified Paediatric Glasgow Coma Scale
- Monitor neurological observations (GCS, pupil size and reactivity, limb movements, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, heart rate, blood pressure, temperature) every 15 minutes until the child has recovered from the postictal period

**2. Reassurance for families:**

Your doctor may reassure you that:
- Short seizures do not damage the brain
- Children do not die during short epileptic seizures
- The incidence of a serious underlying cause (e.g. brain tumour) is low
- The risk of a further seizure is approximately 30% over the next two years; the highest-risk period is immediately after the seizure and then over the following 6 to 12 months
- Long-term anti-seizure medication is not usually indicated after a first epileptic seizure

**3. Features of concern requiring urgent paediatric assessment:**

Your doctor will arrange urgent hospital assessment if any of the following are present:
- Child aged under 2 years
- Seizure lasted longer than 5 minutes
- Recurrent tonic and/or clonic seizures within 24 hours
- Seizure with any focal activity
- Recurrent focal impaired-awareness seizures within 24 hours
- Extremely frequent absence, myoclonic, or focal aware seizures
- Child is unwell with features suggesting metabolic abnormality (e.g. hypoglycaemia, electrolyte disturbance), toxins or drugs, anoxia, acute cerebral pathology, or encephalopathic features (e.g. epileptic spasms, altered mental state)
- Abnormal ECG, particularly prolonged QT interval
- Persistent new-onset focal neurological symptoms or signs after the seizure has resolved
- Concern about unexplained injury or the child's safety

**4. Rescue medication to take home:**

Your doctor may provide:
- A single plastic ampoule of buccal midazolam 15 mg/3 mL for use at home if the child has another seizure lasting more than 5 minutes
- An emergency medicine plan explaining when and how to use it
- Education on how to administer midazolam correctly

**5. Non-epileptic seizures:**

If the presentation is a non-epileptic seizure with a simple and obvious cause, your doctor may manage this in primary care with reassurance and explanation.

If there are complex features (difficulty controlling seizures, extreme behavioural issues, sleep disorders, movement disorders, migraine, underlying illness, or uncertainty about the diagnosis), or if possible cardiac syncope is suspected, your doctor will arrange specialist paediatric review.

**6. Further investigations by a specialist (if referred):**

A paediatrician may arrange:
- Electroencephalogram (EEG) — to determine epilepsy type, diagnosis, management, and prognosis
- CT head
- MRI
- Blood, urine, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) testing for metabolic, genetic, or syndrome diagnosis

---

## Safety Advice for Families

Until further assessment, your doctor may recommend:

- Adult supervision for all bathing and swimming
- Avoiding cycling and riding scooters in traffic
- Avoiding climbing higher than 1 metre, except in a playground with safety mats
- If a future event occurs, try to video the whole child (not just the affected limb) — this helps diagnosis
