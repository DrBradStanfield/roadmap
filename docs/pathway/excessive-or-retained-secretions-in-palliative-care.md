# Excessive or Retained Secretions in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers management of excessive or retained secretions in adults in the final stages of dying.

---

## Background

Excessive or retained secretions occur when a person in the final stages of dying is no longer able to clear secretions from their airways. This leads to **noisy, bubbling breathing** caused by the build-up of saliva and secretions combined with a reduced ability to swallow.

**Important to know:**
- The person is usually unconscious or semi-conscious and is generally **unaware of the problem** — this rarely causes them distress.
- However, the sound can be **very distressing for family members** who witness it.
- Explaining this clearly to family is one of the most important parts of management.

This may be more pronounced in people with advanced respiratory disease, head or neck cancer, neurodegenerative conditions, or respiratory infections.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will listen for noisy breathing and bubbling sounds — these are the main features. The context of the underlying illness guides management.

---

## Management

### Supporting Family and Carers

Your doctor and nursing team will explain what is happening and why, provide reassurance, and support the family through this difficult time.

### Simple Comfort Measures

Family and carers can be guided to:
- **Reposition the patient** onto their side — this is often rapidly effective. Reposition every 3–4 hours.
- **Slightly elevate the head of the bed**
- **Provide frequent mouth care** every 1–2 hours to maintain oral hygiene and comfort
- **Use background music or a fan** to help diffuse the sound for those in the room

### Suctioning

Suction is generally **not helpful** unless secretions are visibly pooling in the mouth. If used, suction only within the oral cavity — going deeper can trigger the gag reflex and is distressing. Specialist advice is needed if secretions are caused by a head and neck tumour.

### Hyoscine Butylbromide (Buscopan)

If oropharyngeal secretions are the main problem, your doctor may prescribe hyoscine butylbromide:

- **Starting dose:** 20 mg as needed every hour via subcutaneous injection (maximum 120 mg in 24 hours)
- **After 6 hours:** if beneficial, convert to a continuous subcutaneous infusion of 60 mg over 24 hours (maximum 120 mg per day)

**Important to understand:** Hyoscine reduces the production of new secretions — it does **not** dry up secretions that are already present. This is why repositioning and mouth care remain important.

### If the Patient Is Distressed

If the patient shows signs of distress, your doctor will also address anxiety and breathlessness, and may consider sedation. Specialist palliative care advice will be sought.

### Motor Neurone Disease (MND)

If the patient has a neurodegenerative condition such as MND, management follows the specific guidance in the Motor Neurone Disease pathway.

---

## When to Involve a Specialist

Your doctor will seek adult palliative care support and advice for:
- Persistent symptoms not responding to initial management
- Anticipatory planning and acute management
- Arranging a joint specialist visit
- Considering hospice admission
- Secretions caused by a head and neck tumour
- A distressed patient

---

*This information is intended to support conversations with your doctor. Management of palliative symptoms requires individualised clinical judgement — always discuss your situation with your treating team.*
