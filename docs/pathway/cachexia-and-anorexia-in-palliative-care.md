# Cachexia and Anorexia in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway applies to adults in palliative care. Cachexia and anorexia in serious illness require a thoughtful, individualised approach. Always discuss management with your doctor and palliative care team.

---

## Overview

Cachexia and anorexia are common and distressing in palliative care — for both the patient and their family/whānau. Watching a person lose weight and stop eating can feel alarming, and is often interpreted as a sign of impending death.

Understanding what is happening and why helps families provide better support.

---

## What Is Cachexia?

**Cachexia** is a complex metabolic syndrome — not simply starvation. It involves:
- Ongoing loss of skeletal muscle mass that cannot be fully reversed by eating more
- Progressive functional decline
- A different mechanism from normal weight loss or starvation

Cachexia develops in stages:
- **Pre-cachexia** — early changes, some opportunity to slow progression
- **Cachexia** — established muscle loss
- **Refractory cachexia** — expected in very advanced cancer (preterminal stage, life expectancy under 3 months, poor performance status)

Cachexia is distinct from simple starvation (which can be reversed with nutrition), depression, malabsorption, or age-related muscle loss.

**Anorexia** is loss of desire to eat, resulting in reduced food intake. Anorexia and cachexia often overlap in cancer and other advanced illness, but anorexia is not always present in cachexia.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess:

### Features of cachexia
Cachexia is considered when a patient has **≥ 5% body weight loss**, or **BMI ≤ 20 kg/m²** plus three or more of:
- Decreased muscle strength
- Fatigue or reduced physical activity
- Anorexia (loss of appetite)
- Low muscle mass
- Abnormal blood tests: CRP > 5, haemoglobin < 120 g/L, serum albumin < 32 g/L

### Features of anorexia
- Lack of hunger
- Early satiety (feeling full after very little food)
- Disinterest in food
- Altered smell and taste

### Possible causes of anorexia
Your doctor will look for treatable contributing factors:
- Metabolic changes from advanced cancer
- Anxiety, depression, or stress
- Fatigue
- Nausea and vomiting
- Altered taste
- Mouth problems — thrush, dry mouth, mucositis, ill-fitting dentures
- Constipation
- Difficulty swallowing or gut obstruction
- Pain
- Medication side effects or treatments (e.g. chemotherapy)
- Strong odours (cooking smells, fungating wounds)
- Cognitive changes

## Management

Management is tailored to each person's clinical condition, treatment goals, and values.

### 1. Treat reversible causes of anorexia
If anorexia has a treatable cause (pain, nausea, constipation, oral thrush, depression), treating it may help slow the progression to cachexia. Exercise, where possible, can reduce cancer-related fatigue and help preserve muscle mass.

### 2. Dietary advice
The goal is comfort and quality of life, not maximising calories. Your doctor and dietitian may advise:

- Avoid placing pressure on the patient to eat — this creates tension and often worsens symptoms
- Small, frequent, attractively presented meals
- Allow the patient to choose what they feel like eating
- Vary food texture, temperature, and flavour
- If taste has changed, experiment with new foods (e.g. marinated meats)
- Social mealtime participation if the patient feels up to it
- A small amount of alcohol (if previously enjoyed) may reduce the feeling of fullness and add calories

If swallowing is difficult, food should be offered in an appropriate texture or consistency.

### 3. Nutritional supplements
Your doctor may prescribe oral nutritional supplements (e.g. Sustagen, Prosure, Ensure) once or twice daily. These can help slow cachexia progression but **cannot reverse it**. Nutrition plays a supporting role — not a curative one.

### 4. Pancreatic enzyme supplementation
In pancreatic insufficiency, your doctor may prescribe pancreatin to improve absorption.

### 5. Medications

**Prokinetics (e.g. domperidone)**
- Useful when early satiety or nausea is prominent
- Particularly indicated in gastroparesis from advanced cancer
- Contraindicated in gut obstruction
- Dose: domperidone 10 mg three times a day, 30 minutes before meals
- Trial for 1 week — stop if no benefit
- Side effects: abdominal cramps, diarrhoea, rare oculogyric crisis

**Corticosteroids (e.g. dexamethasone)**
- Can improve appetite and energy — but do not improve nutritional status or reverse cachexia
- Most appropriate when life expectancy is weeks to a couple of months
- Dose: dexamethasone 2 to 4 mg per day — use lowest effective dose
- Trial for 1 week — stop if no benefit
- Recommended for **no more than 2 weeks** without specialist palliative care review
- With prolonged use, appetite benefit wanes and muscle catabolism risk increases
- Side effects: oral thrush, muscle weakness (proximal myopathy), fluid retention, skin fragility, stomach upset
- Note: in palliative care, some doses may be used in ways not officially approved by the medicines regulator

**Megestrol acetate**
- Seek palliative care or oncology advice before prescribing — not commonly used by local specialists
- May have a role in selected patients

**Antidepressants (e.g. mirtazapine)**
- If depression is contributing to anorexia, treating it may improve appetite
- Mirtazapine 15 to 45 mg at night has appetite-stimulating and weight gain effects as secondary benefits

### 6. Family and whānau support

The family/whānau may feel distressed watching a person not eat. They may feel their food is being rejected. The patient may feel pressured to eat, which makes things worse.

Your doctor and care team will help explain:
- **Cachexia is part of the illness process, not starvation** — it cannot be reversed by eating more
- Pressure to eat usually causes more distress, not better outcomes
- Other ways to express care and love that do not involve food
- Cultural beliefs about food and nourishment deserve acknowledgement and respectful discussion

Increased food intake does not improve prognosis, but preserving quality time and reducing distress does.

### 7. Review of other medications
Your doctor will review whether regular medications (blood pressure treatment, diabetes medications) can be reduced or stopped, as goals of care shift toward comfort.

## When Specialist Advice Is Needed

Seek palliative care support and advice for:
- Guidance if dexamethasone has been used for more than 2 weeks
- Anticipatory planning and management of acute changes
- Arranging a joint visit from a palliative care specialist
- Consideration of enteral feeding (via gastrostomy) in selected patients:
  - Patients having palliative radiotherapy to the head and neck who cannot swallow due to side effects
  - Patients with neurological conditions who have lost safe swallowing
  - Note: **enteral feeding is not appropriate for patients who are dying, or in established metabolic cachexia**
- Consideration of hospice admission

Seek palliative care or oncology advice before starting megestrol acetate.

---

## Working with Your Doctor

Cachexia and anorexia are among the most difficult aspects of serious illness — for patients and families alike. Your doctor and palliative care team will work with you to maximise comfort, support the family, and focus on quality of life rather than caloric targets. Honest, compassionate conversation about what is happening and what to expect is central to good care.
