# Non-Melanoma Skin Cancer

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) — the two most common non-melanoma skin cancers. It covers recognition, assessment, treatment options, and when specialist referral is needed.

---

## Background

The most common non-melanoma skin cancers are basal cell carcinoma (BCC) and squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), also called keratinocyte cancer.

**Basal cell carcinoma (BCC):**
- The most common skin cancer, accounting for approximately two-thirds of all skin cancers.
- Locally invasive but metastasis is very rare.
- Patients with BCC often develop multiple primary tumours over time.

**Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC):**
- The second most common skin cancer, accounting for approximately 28% of all skin cancers.
- Derived from epidermal keratinocytes.
- Can metastasise.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess for suspicious symptoms and risk factors.

**Suspicious symptoms** — lesion is:
- Changing (growing in size, changing shape or colour)
- Not healing, or bleeding
- Thickened or indurated
- Painful or tender to touch

**Risk factors:**
- Personal and family history of skin cancer
- History of significant sun exposure
- Immunosuppression
- Light/fair skin, blue or green eyes, blonde or red hair
- Older age
- Male sex

A full-body skin check is recommended, even if the lesion the patient has noticed is not concerning. Your doctor will examine and document all lesions of concern, including under dermoscopy, noting site, size, colour, margin, surface texture, and signs of ulceration, bleeding, or crusting.

### How Your Doctor Recognises Each Type

**Basal cell carcinoma (BCC)** can appear as:
- A non-healing ulcer with visible small red dilated blood vessels (telangiectasia)
- A slowly growing papule, nodule, or plaque
- Skin-coloured, pink, or pigmented
- Spontaneous bleeding

There are several presentations: nodular (pearly, telangiectatic), superficial (erythematous), pigmented, morphoeic (pale scar-like), and basosquamous (features of both BCC and SCC).

**Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC):**
- Well-differentiated SCC is usually a papule or nodule with abnormal keratin production.
- Poorly differentiated SCC may be an eroded papule or nodule without distinguishing features.
- SCC in situ (Bowen's disease) appears as a slowly expanding red patch.

**Amelanotic melanoma** — if any suspicion, your doctor will follow the Melanoma pathway:
- Often not recognised as melanoma due to absence of pigmentation.
- Any lesion that is **e**levated, **f**irm, and **g**rowing (EFG rule) over 4 to 6 weeks should be treated as suspicious for melanoma, even if non-pigmented.

If SCC is suspected, your doctor will also examine regional lymph nodes, especially for head and neck lesions.

## Management

Most low-risk BCCs or SCCs can be managed in general practice and do not require specialist referral. Your doctor will choose the approach based on the lesion type, location, size, and your individual circumstances.

### Biopsy and Excision

- For all non-pigmented lesions likely to be malignant, **excision biopsy with 4 mm margins** is recommended.
- A skin biopsy (rather than excision) may be considered if the diagnosis is uncertain, the lesion is in a high-risk location (e.g., near the eye), or surgery is not appropriate given the patient's age or health.

Management then depends on histology results.

### After Confirmed SCC

- **Excision biopsy with adequate margins (≥ 4 mm):** routine follow-up.
- **Excision biopsy with inadequate margins (< 4 mm):** referral for re-excision.
- **SCC in situ (Bowen's disease):** non-surgical treatment is an option (see below).
- **SCC confirmed on biopsy:** excise with 4 mm margins.
- **High-risk SCC features:** referral to specialist (see Referral section).

### After Confirmed BCC

- **Excision biopsy with adequate margins (≥ 4 mm):** routine follow-up.
- **Excision biopsy with inadequate margins (< 4 mm):** referral for re-excision.
- **Superficial BCC confirmed on biopsy:** non-surgical treatment may be appropriate.
- **BCC with high-risk features:** referral if cannot be managed in general practice.

### Non-Surgical Treatment Options

Non-surgical treatment may be considered for superficial BCC or SCC in situ when surgery is not possible or appropriate. These are not suitable for all lesions — your doctor will assess whether they are appropriate.

**Cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen):**
- Freezes the lesion using a double freeze-thaw technique.
- Suitable for small superficial lesions on covered areas of the trunk and limbs.
- Best avoided for lesions on the head and neck, or lower legs.
- Results in a blister that crusts and heals over several weeks; may leave a permanent white mark.
- Not always successful — careful follow-up is required.

**Curettage and cautery:**
- Suitable for lesions on the trunk or limbs (excluding lower legs) when the operator is experienced.

**Fluorouracil (Efudix) cream** — for SCC in situ, particularly multiple or widespread lesions:
- Cure rate 67–92% (versus ~95% with excision).
- Applied twice daily for 10–30 days to one area at a time (maximum 500 cm²).
- Contraindicated in pregnancy, breastfeeding, and DPD enzyme deficiency.
- Causes inflammation and skin erosion (expected response).
- Avoid sunlight during treatment; do not use sunscreen on treated areas.
- Review at 2 weeks and again at 2–3 months after completion.
- If lesions persist at 3 months, biopsy or excision is required. Repeat treatment is contraindicated.

**Imiquimod cream** — for superficial BCC:
- Cure rate 84% at 3 years (versus 98% with excision).
- Applied at bedtime, 5 times per week for 6 weeks.
- Contraindicated in recurrent/invasive/nodular BCC; lesion within 1 cm of hairline, eyes, ears, nose, or lips; immunosuppressed patients; pregnancy and breastfeeding.
- Expected response: inflammation (often worse in week 2), then skin erosion, then healing.
- Pause treatment if excessive inflammation occurs — the paused time is added to the total 6-week course.
- Stop immediately and seek review if systemic symptoms occur (fever, fatigue, flu-like symptoms, nausea, diarrhoea, muscle pain).
- Review at 2 weeks into course and at 3 months after completion. If lesions persist at 3 months, biopsy or excision is required. Repeat treatment is contraindicated.

**Combination of gentle cryotherapy followed by imiquimod** may also be used in selected cases.

### High-Risk Features

Certain features indicate higher risk and influence whether referral is needed.

**High-risk features of BCC:**

*Clinical:*
- Tumours on head or neck
- Tumours > 20 mm on non-head and neck sites
- Recurrent lesions
- Lesions with poorly defined borders
- Lesions on previous radiation therapy or burn sites
- Lesions in immunocompromised patients

*Pathological:*
- Morphoeic, sclerosing, or mixed infiltrative
- Micronodular
- Basosquamous (keratinising)
- Perineural invasion

**High-risk features of SCC:**
- Poorly differentiated SCC
- Spindle cell carcinoma
- Acantholytic SCC
- Adenosquamous tumours
- Neurotropic tumours

**Risk of recurrence:**

*SCC:* 18% risk of a subsequent SCC within 3 years. Additional high-risk features for local recurrence or metastasis: > 2 cm diameter, location on lip or ear, poorly differentiated, > 4 mm thick, recurrent SCC, perineural invasion (up to 50% risk of regional recurrence).

*BCC:* 44% of patients develop a second BCC within 3 years; 66% of local recurrences arise within 3 years of initial treatment. Metastasis is rare.

### Suspected Metastatic Disease

If metastatic disease is suspected at any stage, urgent specialist referral is needed.

## Long-Term Follow-Up

Your doctor will arrange follow-up based on your specific situation. In general:

- After non-surgical treatment: review at 3 months to confirm clearance, then at least annual skin checks.
- After poorly differentiated SCC, SCC of the lip or ear, or in immunosuppressed patients: review at 3 months, then 6-monthly skin checks for life (including examination of the relevant lymph node drainage region).
- All other patients: annual skin checks for life.

**Additional measures your doctor may consider:**

- **Nicotinamide** (vitamin B3), 500 mg twice daily (over-the-counter): reduces rates of new BCC and SCC in high-risk patients by 23% after 1 year versus placebo, and actinic keratoses by 11%.
- **Acitretin:** a more established option for prevention in high-risk patients; specialist input is usually sought first.
- Patients with chronic immunosuppression (organ transplant recipients, CLL, HIV, long-term immunosuppressants) may need specialist monitoring.

**Skin check appointments include:**
- History and symptom review.
- Examination of the original site, all lymph nodes, and a total body check for new lesions.
- Suspicion of subcutaneous lesions or dermal nodules, particularly in the draining lymph node region of the original cancer.

## Patient Education

Your doctor will advise you on:

- The importance of regular skin checks — your risk of a new primary skin cancer is elevated.
- Skin self-examination techniques.
- **Sun-smart behaviour:**
  - Avoid direct sunlight during peak UV radiation hours.
  - Slip, Slop, Slap, and Wrap.
  - Apply sunscreen at least 20 minutes before going outside and every 2 hours thereafter.
  - Avoid artificial UV exposure (e.g., sunbeds).
- **Medications that can increase sunburn risk:** tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, sulfonamides, NSAIDs, frusemide, hydrochlorothiazide, isotretinoin, acitretin, sulfonylureas, phenothiazines, imatinib, hydroxychloroquine, amiodarone, diltiazem, enalapril, quinine-containing medications.
- First-degree family members have an increased risk and should be aware.

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will refer to a skin cancer specialist if:

- Any suspicion of metastatic disease (urgent referral).
- Excision biopsy shows SCC or BCC with inadequate margins (< 4 mm).
- High-risk SCC features (poorly differentiated, spindle cell, acantholytic, adenosquamous, neurotropic).
- High-risk BCC features that cannot be managed in general practice.
- Biopsy or excision is not available in the general practice setting.
- Negative biopsy but clinical suspicion persists.
- The patient requires closer monitoring (e.g., chronic immunosuppression).

*Note: All referrals require good quality digital photographs and/or a biopsy report.*
