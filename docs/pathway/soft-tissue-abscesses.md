# Soft Tissue Abscesses

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers soft tissue abscesses in the trunk and limbs for patients of all ages. The primary treatment is incision and drainage of the pus, with antibiotics only if there is associated cellulitis.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if any of the following apply:

- Suspected necrotising fasciitis (severe pain disproportionate to appearance, rapidly spreading)
- Systemically unwell (signs of sepsis) or immunocompromised
- Aged under 3 months
- Abscess involving the pilonidal, head and neck, or perianal regions, or located over a joint
- Large red area or rapidly progressive redness

---

## Background

An abscess is a collection of pus within a cavity formed by tissue breakdown. In the skin and soft tissues this most commonly results from bacterial infection — typically *Staphylococcus aureus*. The definitive treatment is drainage; antibiotics alone are insufficient for a formed abscess.

Related conditions covered by separate pathways include Bartholin's cyst or abscess, mastitis and breast abscess, and recurrent staphylococcal infection.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will look for signs that confirm an abscess is present:

- Fluctuance (a palpable fluid-filled cavity) — note this may not be obvious if the abscess is deep to the fascia
- Draining pus
- Yellow or white centre in an acutely inflamed lesion

Your doctor will ask about:

- Whether there was a pre-existing lesion at the site (e.g., sebaceous cyst, pilonidal sinus, lipoma)
- Conditions or medications that may indicate immunocompromise

Physical examination will include:

- Checking for fever, lymphadenopathy, and signs of sepsis
- In children, vital signs are assessed against age-appropriate norms (heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure, oxygen saturations, conscious state)
- In adults, concerning sepsis features include systolic blood pressure below 90 mmHg, heart rate above 130 bpm, temperature above 38°C or below 36°C, respiratory rate above 25 breaths/minute, new oxygen requirement, or acute confusion
- Assessment of the abscess location, including proximity to joints, pilonidal, head and neck, or perianal regions

Your doctor will also consider other diagnoses:

- **Necrotising fasciitis** — a surgical emergency. Suspect if there is severe pain disproportionate to examination findings, rapidly advancing soft tissue involvement, signs of sepsis, necrotic or bullous skin changes, or crepitus
- **Boil (furuncle)** — a deep bacterial folliculitis; some resolve spontaneously, others need incision and drainage
- **Deep vein thrombosis (DVT)**
- **Lymphoedema** (may be a contributing factor)

Investigations are not usually needed for a simple abscess. A wound swab may be taken if the abscess is not improving despite antibiotics, there is concern about antibiotic resistance, or there are recurrent presentations.

## Management

### When will your doctor refer?

Your doctor will seek specialist advice or refer to hospital if:

- Any red flag is present (see above)
- The abscess is in a location requiring specialist input (e.g., pilonidal, head and neck, perianal, over a joint, or facial)
- There is swelling or redness around a joint in children
- Inpatient management is needed (e.g., a young child who cannot tolerate the procedure in the community)
- There is no improvement after 24 to 72 hours on oral antibiotics
- Oral antibiotics are not being tolerated

### Drainage

For uncomplicated abscesses managed in primary care, your doctor will:

- Incise and drain the abscess if it is superficial, away from joints and nerves, and situated distal to the clavicle
- Consider a wick to keep the incision open if needed — packing of the abscess cavity is not routinely recommended
- Review the following day to re-dress the wound

If the abscess is already draining spontaneously, non-surgical management with dressings or poultices may be appropriate — ensuring there is an adequate drainage opening.

Non-fluctuant lesions (no pus collection yet) are managed as for cellulitis.

### Antibiotics

Antibiotics are **not** routinely required after drainage of an uncomplicated abscess. The surrounding redness usually settles within 24 to 48 hours with drainage alone.

If there is associated cellulitis, your doctor may consider oral antibiotics:

**In adults:**
- Flucloxacillin 1 g three times a day for 5 days
- If mild penicillin allergy (e.g., rash): cefalexin 1 g three times a day for 5 days
- If severe penicillin allergy (e.g., anaphylaxis): trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole 960 mg twice a day for 5 days
- If known recent MRSA: treat based on known sensitivities; if unsure, trial a beta-lactam first (a history of previous MRSA does not necessarily mean current MRSA)

**In children:**
- If no penicillin allergy: cefalexin 25 mg/kg three times a day (maximum 1 g/dose) for 5 days, or flucloxacillin 25 mg/kg four times a day (maximum 1 g/dose) for 5 days
- If penicillin allergy: trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole 24 mg/kg twice a day for 5 days (maximum 960 mg/dose)
- If known current MRSA: trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole as above (or per sensitivities)

MRSA risk factors include frequent courses of antibiotics, infection not responding to standard treatment, family history of MRSA, recent healthcare exposure in a high-risk setting, crowded living conditions, or open/broken skin.

### Follow-up

Your doctor will review in 24 to 48 hours depending on clinical concern:

- If redness does not settle after drainage of an uncomplicated abscess, check swab results and consider a course of oral antibiotics
- If not improving or not tolerating oral antibiotics, specialist advice will be sought
- If ongoing dressings are needed beyond 48 hours, community nursing assessment may be requested
- Recurrent or multiple abscesses are managed according to the recurrent staphylococcal infection pathway; if multiple or recurrent lesions persist, infectious diseases assessment (adults) or paediatric assessment (children) may be requested
