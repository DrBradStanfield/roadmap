# Herpes Zoster (Shingles)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of shingles (herpes zoster) in adults and children — from diagnosis through to antiviral treatment and patient advice.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

Contact your doctor promptly or seek urgent care if you have any of the following:

- Rash on or around the eye (ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve) — this requires urgent ophthalmology or specialist input
- Known immunosuppression or signs of acute severe systemic infection
- Shingles affecting multiple body areas (multi-dermatomal) or spreading widely (disseminated shingles)

---

## Background

### What Is Shingles?

Shingles (herpes zoster) is a painful blistering rash caused by reactivation of the varicella zoster virus (VZV) — the same virus that causes chickenpox.

During the original chickenpox infection (usually in childhood), VZV travels to cranial nerve and spinal dorsal root ganglion cells, where it remains dormant for years. It can then reactivate later in life, travelling down sensory nerves to produce the characteristic shingles rash.

- Shingles can occur at any age but is much more common in adults, particularly older people.
- About one in three people will develop shingles in their lifetime.
- Shingles is more common and more severe in people with impaired immunity.
- People with active shingles are infectious — mainly through direct contact with blister fluid, and less commonly through airborne spread — and can cause chickenpox in non-immune contacts.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you based on your history, symptoms, and examination findings.

### Typical Presentation

The first symptom of shingles is usually **pain** — often before the rash appears.

**Pain:**
- Can be severe
- Follows the distribution of one or more sensory nerves, often near the spine where those nerves emerge
- May be localised or spread over a wider area
- Often accompanied by feeling unwell, fever, and headache

**Rash:**
- Appears in the painful area of skin within 1–3 days of pain onset (sometimes up to 10 days)
- Starts as closely grouped red spots, which become raised, then blister, then crust over
- Follows a band-like distribution along the nerve supply of one or more neighbouring spinal nerves
- Can affect any area with a sensory nerve supply, including the mouth, ears, and genital area

**Recovery:**
- Children and young adults: typically 2–3 weeks in uncomplicated cases
- Older adults: typically 3–4 weeks

### Examination Findings

Your doctor will look for:

- **Rash or vesicles** in a dermatomal distribution
- **Enlarged or tender lymph nodes** in the area draining the affected region
- **Rash in the mouth, ear, or on the eardrum** — may suggest Ramsay Hunt syndrome (see below)
- **Eye involvement** — conjunctivitis, corneal changes, or rash around the eye suggest herpes zoster ophthalmicus
- **Signs of disseminated infection** — encephalitis, meningitis, pneumonia, or other serious complications
- **Secondary bacterial infection** — redness, warmth, increased pain, or discharge beyond what is expected

#### Ramsay Hunt Syndrome

Shingles infection of the seventh cranial nerve (facial nerve). Features include:

- Vesicles on the face, pinna, ear canal, pharynx, or upper neck
- Facial palsy — which tends to have a worse outlook than Bell's palsy
- Possible involvement of other cranial nerves (e.g. VIII, IX, X), including swallowing difficulty or hearing problems
- Potential for permanent facial paralysis, postherpetic neuralgia, and hearing loss if untreated

### Investigations

Laboratory tests are not routinely needed. If the diagnosis is uncertain (atypical features), your doctor may take a swab from a blister for PCR testing to confirm the virus.

If there is concern about kidney function (renal impairment), your doctor may check a creatinine level before prescribing antivirals, as dose adjustment is required.

## Management

Your doctor will tailor treatment based on your age, severity, and any risk factors.

### Red Flag Management

- **Eye involvement:** Managed via the Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus pathway — specialist review is needed.
- **Immunosuppression, multi-dermatomal, or disseminated shingles:** Your doctor will seek infectious diseases advice. Some patients require intravenous aciclovir in hospital.
- **Oncology patients:** Your doctor will seek acute oncology advice.

### Analgesia

Your doctor may recommend:

- Paracetamol as a first step for pain relief
- Additional analgesia based on pain severity, following standard acute pain management principles

### Oral Antiviral Medication

Antivirals work best when started **within 72 hours of rash onset**. Starting earlier maximises the potential to:

- Reduce acute pain
- Shorten the duration of the rash
- Reduce infectiousness
- Lower the risk of eye complications (if the eye is involved)
- Reduce the risk of shingles-associated stroke

*Note: Evidence on whether antivirals reduce postherpetic neuralgia (nerve pain lasting after the rash) is mixed.*

**Your doctor may consider starting antivirals within 7 days of rash onset if you:**
- Are aged over 50
- Have an unusual rash location (neck, limbs, or perineum)
- Have moderate or severe pain
- Have a moderate or severe rash

**Your doctor may consider antivirals regardless of timing if you have:**
- Eye involvement
- Immunocompromised status

**Adult antiviral options:**

*Valaciclovir* (preferred — better absorption, three-times-daily dosing):
- 1 g orally three times a day for 7 days
- Dose reduced in renal impairment
- If immunocompromised: continue for at least 7 days, then 2 days beyond crusting of lesions
- Maintain good hydration throughout

*Aciclovir* (alternative):
- 800 mg orally five times a day for 7 days
- Dose reduced in renal impairment
- If immunocompromised: continue for at least 7 days, then 2 days beyond crusting of lesions
- Maintain good hydration throughout

**Children:**

*Valaciclovir (aged 2–18 years):*
- 20 mg/kg (maximum 1 g) orally three times a day for 5–7 days
- If immunocompromised: continue 2 days after lesions have crusted

*Aciclovir:*
- Aged 1 month to 2 years: 100 mg orally five times a day for 5 days (double dose if immunocompromised)
- Aged 2–18 years: 200 mg orally five times a day for 5 days (double dose if immunocompromised)

> **Important note on renal impairment:** Aciclovir is 80% cleared by the kidneys. Accumulation can cause neurotoxicity (lethargy, confusion, agitation, hallucinations). Dose reduction and adequate hydration are essential in patients with reduced kidney function.

### Steroids

The role of steroids in shingles is controversial — they are likely to be of minimal or no benefit for most people, and may carry risks.

Your doctor may consider a short course of steroids if:
- Acute nerve inflammation (neuritis) is present
- Ramsay Hunt syndrome is diagnosed (facial palsy with ear pain)

If appropriate, your doctor may prescribe prednisone — ideally started within 3 days of symptom onset — with the dose and duration tailored to your circumstances.

### Antibiotics

Antibiotics are **not** routinely needed for shingles. Your doctor may prescribe them only if there is evidence of a secondary bacterial skin infection, typically with flucloxacillin (or erythromycin if allergic to penicillin).

Do not use topical antibiotics on the rash.

### Topical Preparations

Topical treatments have limited evidence but may provide comfort:

- **Povidone iodine ointment** (with a light cover to protect clothing) — may have soothing and antimicrobial effects
- **Calamine cream or lotion** — calamine lotion may be more drying than cream

Wash hands after applying anything to the rash, as blister fluid is contagious.

### If You Are Pregnant

Shingles during pregnancy does not pose the same risk to the baby as chickenpox. Treatment is the same as for non-pregnant patients.

If you are pregnant and non-immune to chickenpox and have been exposed to someone with active shingles, your doctor will seek infectious diseases advice.

### Patient Advice — What to Do at Home

- Keep the rash area clean and dry
- Cover the rash with a non-adhesive, absorbent dressing and a protective ointment (such as white soft paraffin) to prevent secondary infection and reduce spread
- Wash hands frequently, especially after touching the rash

**Infection control:**
- Shingles is less contagious than chickenpox, but the blister fluid can cause chickenpox in people who are not immune
- The risk of spreading to others is very low if the rash is kept covered until all blisters have crusted over
- Avoid contact with people who are non-immune to chickenpox and at higher risk — including pregnant women, infants, and immunocompromised individuals — until your blisters have crusted

### Follow-up

- If pain persists after the rash resolves, this may be postherpetic neuralgia — discuss with your doctor.
- Your doctor may also discuss herpes zoster vaccination (Shingrix) to reduce the risk of shingles in the future.
