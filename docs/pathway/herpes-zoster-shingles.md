# Herpes Zoster (Shingles)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: May 2024.*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of shingles (herpes zoster) in adults and children, from diagnosis through to antiviral treatment and patient advice.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

See your doctor the same day if you have shingles and any of the following:

- Rash on or around the eye (ophthalmic branch of the trigeminal nerve)
- Known immunosuppression or signs of acute severe systemic infection
- Shingles affecting multiple body areas (multi-dermatomal) or spreading widely (disseminated shingles)

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department if you have shingles and become confused or very drowsy, have a severe headache with a stiff neck, develop new weakness on one side of the body, or become very unwell with fever, shivering or a racing heart.**

---

## Background

### What Is Shingles?

Shingles (herpes zoster) is a painful blistering rash caused by reactivation of the varicella zoster virus (VZV), the same virus that causes chickenpox.

During the original chickenpox infection (usually in childhood), VZV stays dormant in cranial nerve and spinal dorsal root ganglion cells, usually of sensory nerves. It stays there for years. It can then reactivate and travel down the nerves to the skin to produce the shingles rash.

- Anyone who has had chickenpox may later develop shingles.
- Shingles can occur in childhood but is much more common in adults, especially older people.
- About one third of people are estimated to develop shingles in their lifetime.
- Shingles is more common and more severe in people with impaired immunity.
- People with active shingles are infectious, mainly through direct contact with shingles blisters and less commonly through breathing in airborne virus. They can cause chickenpox in non-immune contacts.
- Chickenpox (VZV) vaccination is highly effective in preventing chickenpox and so reduces the risk of shingles. Because it is a live-attenuated vaccine, the vaccine virus can occasionally reactivate and cause shingles.

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you based on your history, symptoms, and examination findings.

### Typical Presentation

The first symptom of shingles is usually **pain**, often before the rash appears.

**Pain:**
- Can be severe
- Is in the area of one or more sensory nerves, often close to the spine where those nerves emerge
- May be localised to one spot or spread over a larger area
- You usually feel unwell, and may have a fever and headache

**Rash:**
- Appears in the painful area of skin within 1–3 days of pain onset (sometimes up to 10 days)
- Starts as closely grouped red spots, which become raised in a continuous band on the skin supplied by one, or sometimes more, neighbouring spinal nerves
- New spots keep appearing for several days, each blistering or filling with pus, then crusting over
- Can affect any area with a sensory nerve supply, including the mouth, ears, and genital area

**Recovery:**
- Pain and feeling unwell ease gradually as the rash disappears
- Children and young adults: usually 2–3 weeks in uncomplicated cases
- Older adults: may take 3–4 weeks

### Examination Findings

Your doctor will check whether you are immunosuppressed, which can increase the risk of severe infection or complications. They will look for:

- **Rash or vesicles** in a dermatomal distribution
- **Enlarged or tender lymph nodes** in the area draining the affected region
- **Blisters in the mouth, outer ear, ear canal, or on the eardrum**: may suggest Ramsay Hunt syndrome (see below)
- **Eye involvement**: conjunctivitis, corneal changes, or rash around the eye suggest herpes zoster ophthalmicus
- **Features of disseminated shingles**: encephalitis, meningitis, myelitis, pneumonia, or weakness on one side of the body (hemiparesis)
- **Signs of secondary bacterial infection**: this can be severe and is usually caused by *Staphylococcus aureus* or *Streptococcus pyogenes*

#### Ramsay Hunt Syndrome

Shingles infection of the seventh cranial nerve (facial nerve). Features include:

- Blisters and scabs on the face, outer ear (pinna), ear canal, throat (pharynx), or upper neck
- Facial palsy is common and has a worse outlook than Bell's palsy. Untreated, 10% of people with complete palsy recover completely, compared with 68% of people with partial palsy.
- The palsy is usually at its worst within 1 week, but there is evidence that late nerve damage (denervation) can occur up to 14 days after the palsy starts
- Other cranial nerves may also be involved (e.g. VIII, IX, X), and swallowing may be impaired
- Complications may include permanent facial paralysis, postherpetic neuralgia, and hearing loss

### Investigations

Laboratory tests are not routinely needed. If there are atypical features, your doctor may take a swab from a blister for PCR testing to confirm the diagnosis.

If severe kidney impairment (renal impairment) is suspected, your doctor may check a creatinine level before prescribing valaciclovir tablets, as the dose will need to be reduced.

## Management

Your doctor will tailor treatment based on your age, severity, and any risk factors.

### Red Flag Management

- **Eye involvement:** Your doctor will follow the herpes zoster ophthalmicus pathway.
- **Immunosuppression, severe systemic infection, multi-dermatomal, or disseminated shingles:** Your doctor will seek infectious diseases advice. Some patients are admitted to hospital for intravenous aciclovir.
- **Oncology patients:** Your doctor will seek acute oncology advice.

### Analgesia

Your doctor may recommend rest and:

- Paracetamol as a first step for pain relief
- Further pain relief following acute pain guidance for adults or children

### Oral Antiviral Medication

Antivirals give the most benefit when started **within 72 hours of rash onset**. Possible benefits:

- Reduce acute pain
- Shorten the duration of the rash
- Reduce viral shedding and infectiousness
- Lower the risk of eye complications (if the eye is involved)
- Reduce the risk of shingles-associated stroke

*Note: Because the evidence is contradictory, it is unclear whether antivirals reduce how often postherpetic neuralgia (nerve pain lasting after the rash) occurs, how severe it is, or how long it lasts.*

**Your doctor may consider starting antivirals within 7 days of rash onset if you:**
- Are aged over 50 years
- Have an atypical rash, e.g. shingles affecting the neck, limbs, or perineum
- Have moderate or severe pain
- Have a moderate or severe rash

**Your doctor may consider antivirals regardless of how long you have had the rash if you have:**
- Eye involvement
- Immunocompromised status

**Adult antiviral options:**

Valaciclovir has advantages over aciclovir: better absorption and three-times-daily dosing.

*Valaciclovir:*
- 1 g orally three times a day for 7 days
- Dose reduced in renal impairment (creatinine clearance less than 50 mL/min)
- If immunocompromised: continue for at least 7 days, then 2 days beyond crusting of lesions
- Maintain good hydration throughout

*Aciclovir:*
- 800 mg orally five times a day for 7 days
- Dose reduced in renal impairment (creatinine clearance less than 50 mL/min)
- If immunocompromised: continue for at least 7 days, then 2 days beyond crusting of lesions
- Maintain good hydration throughout

**Children:**

*Valaciclovir (aged 2–18 years):*
- 20 mg/kg (maximum 1 g) orally three times a day for 5–7 days
- If immunocompromised: continue 2 days after lesions have crusted

*Aciclovir:*
- Aged 1 month to 2 years: 100 mg orally five times a day for 5 days (double dose if immunocompromised or if absorption is impaired)
- Aged 2–18 years: 200 mg orally five times a day for 5 days (double dose if immunocompromised or if absorption is impaired)

> **Important note on renal impairment:** Aciclovir (also the active form of valaciclovir) is 80% cleared by the kidneys. Doses of both should be reduced in renal impairment to stop the drug building up, which can cause neurotoxicity. Your doctor will make sure you are well hydrated and watch for lethargy, confusion, agitation, and hallucinations. They will seek specialist advice on the dose if you are immunocompromised and have renal impairment.

### Steroids

The role of steroids in shingles is controversial. They are likely to be of minimal or no benefit, and potentially harmful.

Your doctor may consider steroids if:
- Acute nerve inflammation (neuritis) is present
- Ramsay Hunt syndrome is diagnosed (facial palsy with ear pain)

If appropriate, your doctor may prescribe a short course of prednisone, ideally started within 3 days of symptom onset. For example, 60 mg of prednisone for 5 days, then reduced over 5 days. A lower dose may suit some people.

### Antibiotics

Antibiotics are **not** routinely needed for shingles. Your doctor may prescribe them only if there is evidence of a secondary bacterial infection, typically with flucloxacillin (or erythromycin if allergic to penicillin). If intravenous antibiotics are being considered, your doctor will seek infectious diseases advice first.

Do not use topical antibiotics on the rash.

### Topical Preparations

Topical treatments have limited evidence but may provide comfort:

- **Povidone iodine ointment** (with a light cover to avoid discolouring clothing): may have soothing and antimicrobial effects
- **Calamine cream or lotion**: calamine lotion may be more drying than cream

Wash hands after applying anything to the rash, as blister fluid is contagious.

### If You Are Pregnant

Shingles during pregnancy does not pose the same risk of complications to the baby as chickenpox. Treatment is the same as for non-pregnant patients.

If you are pregnant and non-immune to chickenpox and have been exposed to someone with shingles (for example a household contact), your doctor will seek infectious diseases advice.

### Patient Advice — What to Do at Home

- Keep the rash area clean and dry
- Cover the rash with a non-adhesive, absorbent dressing and a protective ointment (such as white soft paraffin) to prevent secondary infection and reduce spread
- Wash hands frequently, especially after touching the rash

**Infection control:**
- Shingles is less contagious than chickenpox, and you are only potentially contagious after the rash appears
- The blister fluid can still cause chickenpox in people who are not immune, including infants and especially people with impaired immunity
- The risk of spreading to others is negligible if the rash is kept covered until all blisters have crusted over
- Avoid contact with people who are non-immune to chickenpox and at risk of severe disease, including pregnant women, infants, and immunocompromised individuals, until your blisters have crusted

### Follow-up

- If pain persists after shingles, this may be postherpetic neuralgia. Discuss it with your doctor.
- Your doctor may also discuss herpes zoster vaccination (Shingrix) to reduce the risk of shingles in the future.
