# Ankle and Foot Pain in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of ankle and foot pain in adults where there is no acute injury. If the pain follows an injury, this is handled separately.

---

## Red Flag — Seek Urgent Medical Attention

- **Septic arthritis** — if a joint is acutely inflamed and hot without a history of trauma, this must be excluded urgently. Your doctor will arrange emergency orthopaedic assessment.

---

## Background

Most foot pain affects one of three anatomical zones, which helps guide diagnosis and management:

- **Forefoot** — toes and intermetatarsal spaces (phalanx and metatarsal bones)
- **Midfoot** — the part of the foot that adapts to uneven surfaces; includes navicular, cuboid, and cuneiform bones
- **Hindfoot** — includes the ankle, talus, and calcaneus

The ankle has two key joints:
- **Tibiotalar joint** (the "true" ankle joint) — allows up/down motion (dorsiflexion and plantar flexion)
- **Subtalar joint** — allows side-to-side motion (inversion and eversion)

Common underlying causes include foot structural abnormalities, toenail and skin problems, toe deformities, and chronic conditions such as diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, rheumatoid arthritis, and gout.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- **Pain** — type, location, duration, timing (e.g. at night, with activity, at rest), associated symptoms (numbness, tingling, swelling, radiation, pain in other joints), and impact on daily activities, work, and exercise
- **Exacerbating factors** — uneven ground, instability, activity type, weight gain
- **Footwear** — type of shoes worn, recent change in shoes, wear pattern on the sole
- **Relevant history** — previous foot or ankle injuries or surgery, precipitating events
- **Co-morbidities** — diabetes, gout, peripheral vascular disease, peripheral neuropathy, back pain, inflammatory bowel disease, skin conditions, smoking, sexual history (reactive arthritis)
- **Risk factors** — older age, obesity, high-impact activities (running, jumping), occupations requiring prolonged standing or walking
- **Treatments already tried** — analgesia, orthotics, injections, physiotherapy, walking aids

Physical examination will assess the foot and ankle while standing, walking, and lying down:

- **Standing:** Toe alignment, arch position (a normal dropped arch reconstitutes on tiptoes; failure to do so suggests tibialis posterior rupture), Achilles tendon alignment and thickening, hindfoot alignment, ability to squat and perform single-leg heel raise
- **Walking:** Gait pattern — heel strike, foot flat, heel off, toe off; any limp or abnormalities such as marked pronation, supination, or abnormal heel alignment
- **Lying supine:** Swelling, skin integrity (discolouration, calluses, ulcers), nail integrity, foot deformity (flat foot, high arch, hammer toes, bunions), neurological signs, vascular status (pulses, warmth, colour), range of movement, lower limb and back problems
- **Shoes:** Type and pattern of wear

## Causes by Location

### Forefoot (toes and ball of foot)

- **Bunions (hallux valgus)** — deviation of the big toe, bony prominence at the first MTP joint
- **Hallux rigidus** — stiffness and arthritis of the first MTP joint (big toe); causes pain on slopes and rough ground, pain with dorsiflexion activities (steps, heels, jumping). Examination shows an enlarged, often erythematous joint, reduced dorsiflexion
- **Gout** — acute joint inflammation, classically at the first MTP joint
- **Metatarsalgia** — pain below the second and third metatarsals
- **Metatarsal stress fractures** — common with prolonged or unusual exercise; initially pain with exercise progressing to pain at rest. Second metatarsal shaft most common. Swelling and localised tenderness over the metatarsal shaft
- **Morton's neuroma** — burning pain caused by an interdigital neuroma, often from tight shoes; usually radiates to adjacent sides of two toes, commonly between the third and fourth metatarsals, reproduced by pressure into the web space
- **Calluses and corns** — pressure-related skin thickening

### Midfoot

- **Tibialis posterior tendinopathy (flat foot deformity)** — more common in women aged 40+; associated with diabetes, obesity, steroid use; presents with medial hindfoot swelling and pain, progressive loss of arch, inability to single-leg heel raise with the heel staying valgus
- **Medial arch pain** — may involve navicular stress fractures or Lisfranc joint complex disruption
- **Ganglia** — localised dorsal midfoot swelling
- **Lateral midfoot pain** — cuboid or base of fifth metatarsal involvement
- **Midfoot osteoarthritis** — dorsal foot pain, stiffness

### Hindfoot (heel and ankle)

- **Plantar fasciitis** — pain at the insertion of the plantar fascia on the calcaneus (underside of heel)
- **Achilles tendinopathy** — pain over the Achilles tendon and calcaneus
- **Tarsal coalition** — congenital fusion of tarsal bones; presents as painful stiff foot in adolescence, often with recurrent ankle sprains and flat foot; limited subtalar movement on examination, arch does not reconstitute on tiptoes
- **Peroneal tendon disorders** — lateral ankle pain
- **Tibialis posterior tendinopathy at ankle level** — medial ankle pain, swelling about the medial hindfoot
- **Hindfoot osteoarthritis** — subtalar or talonavicular joint OA; pain with weight-bearing, stiffness, night pain (less common than in hip/knee OA), impingement on dorsiflexion; antalgic stiff gait on examination

## Investigations

Your doctor may arrange investigations depending on the most likely cause:

**Blood tests** (if inflammatory or systemic cause suspected):
- FBC, CRP, urate, creatinine, LFTs
- Rheumatoid factor (RhF) and anti-CCP antibodies (CCP Ab) — if both positive, specificity for rheumatoid arthritis is 97%
- Anti-nuclear antibodies (ANA)

**Imaging:**
- **X-ray foot** urgently (within 2 weeks) if red flags are present (persistent deep pain unrelated to activity, unexplained night pain)
- **X-ray foot with weight-bearing views** if undiagnosed pain for more than 4 weeks
- **X-ray foot** if multiple joint swelling for more than 6 weeks and inflammatory arthritis is suspected
- **X-ray** of the affected joint if osteoarthritis with significant functional impairment (when orthopaedic assessment is being requested)
- **Bone scan or CT** for metatarsal stress fractures if doubt remains — initial X-rays are often normal; callus may appear after 2–3 weeks
- **Ultrasound** (unfunded) if Morton's neuroma diagnosis is uncertain

## Management

Management depends on the identified diagnosis. Your doctor may consider the following:

### Septic Arthritis
If suspected, urgent orthopaedic assessment will be arranged.

### Gout
Managed according to the gout pathway.

### Positive CCP Ab (possible rheumatoid arthritis)
Rheumatology referral will be requested. If the patient smokes, this is an added risk factor for rheumatoid arthritis (both prevalence and severity).

### Forefoot Conditions

**Hallux rigidus:**
- Stiff-soled shoes with a broad toe box or rocker sole
- Podiatry for orthotics to prevent hyperextension of the big toe
- Modification of daily activities
- NSAIDs or intra-articular steroid injection if significant synovitis
- Physiotherapy or podiatry assessment if pain disrupts daily activities and conservative measures fail
- Orthopaedic assessment if ongoing problems for longer than 6 months
- Surgery options (if conservative treatment fails): cheilectomy (removal of osteophytes), osteotomies, arthrodesis of the first MTP joint

**Metatarsalgia:**
- Managed conservatively; podiatry for pressure offloading

**Morton's neuroma:**
- Broad-toed shoes
- Podiatry assessment for pressure reduction
- Steroid injection
- Surgery if conservative treatment fails

**Calluses:**
- Podiatry management

### Midfoot Conditions

**Tibialis posterior tendinopathy (flat foot):**
- Physiotherapy for eccentric exercises
- Podiatry for orthotics (medial arch support, medial heel wedge)
- NSAIDs if tolerated
- Orthopaedic assessment if there is progression of pain with hindfoot deformity on weight-bearing (but foot still flexible), increasing medial hindfoot pain, and subfibular region tenderness

**Midfoot osteoarthritis:**
- Conservative management; orthopaedic assessment if significant functional impairment

### Hindfoot Conditions

**Tarsal coalition:**
- Podiatry for moulded longitudinal arch support
- Avoid exacerbating activities
- NSAIDs if tolerated
- Orthopaedic assessment if symptoms persist or deteriorate
- Surgery: coalition excision in adolescents; arthrodesis if fixed deformity with degenerative changes (typically in patients older than 20 years)

**Peroneal tendon disorders:**
- NSAIDs if tolerated
- Activity modification
- Physiotherapy for eccentric exercises
- Podiatry for orthotics and lateral forefoot padding
- Update footwear if significant lateral shoe wear
- Orthopaedic assessment if no improvement within 2 months, or if significant hindfoot weakness
- Surgery options (if conservative treatment fails): debridement and repair, hindfoot realignment osteotomies, tendon transfer

**Hindfoot osteoarthritis:**
- NSAIDs (night pain with synovitis may be responsive)
- Conservative management; orthopaedic assessment if significant functional impairment

**Plantar fasciitis and Achilles tendinopathy:**
- Managed according to specific guidelines for each condition

### Chronic Pain
If all management options have been tried unsuccessfully, your doctor may consider referral for specialist chronic pain assessment and management.

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you if:

- **Urgent orthopaedic:** Septic arthritis is suspected
- **Rheumatology:** Positive CCP Ab (possible rheumatoid arthritis)
- **Orthopaedic assessment:** Pain threatening weight-bearing status where non-operative options have been unsuccessful; ongoing hallux rigidus problems; peroneal tendon disorders not improving after 2 months; tarsal coalition with persistent or worsening symptoms; tibialis posterior tendinopathy with progressive deformity
- **Physiotherapy:** Eccentric exercise programmes for tendinopathies
- **Podiatry:** Orthotics, footwear advice, callus and nail management
- **Chronic pain specialist:** All management options exhausted
