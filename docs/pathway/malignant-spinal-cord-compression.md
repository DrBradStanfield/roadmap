# Malignant Spinal Cord Compression

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways | Category: Palliative Care*

This pathway covers adult patients with an established diagnosis of malignancy who may have malignant spinal cord compression (MSCC). This is an oncological emergency requiring urgent medical assessment.

> **Important:** Spinal cord compression is an oncological emergency. If you or a patient may have this condition, contact a doctor immediately — do not wait.

---

## Background

Malignant spinal cord compression (MSCC) occurs when cancer spreads to bones or soft tissues around the spinal cord.

Key facts:
- Relatively uncommon but potentially catastrophic — late diagnosis causes permanent loss of function and significant morbidity.
- Most common in prostate, lung, myeloma, and breast cancer, but can occur with any malignancy, especially with bone involvement.
- MSCC can be the initial presentation of cancer — 1 in 5 patients presenting with MSCC are not previously known to have malignancy.
- Thoracic cord compression is most common, but any spinal level or multiple sites can be affected.
- In cauda equina compression, bowel or bladder dysfunction may be the only symptom and the person may be pain-free.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

The following symptoms in a cancer patient require urgent medical review:

- New or increasing back/radicular pain with known spinal metastases
- Bilateral radicular pain (pain radiating down both legs/arms)
- Unexplained back pain, especially pain that worsens lying in bed
- Bowel and/or bladder dysfunction (new incontinence, retention, or constipation)
- New difficulty mobilising in a known cancer patient

---

## Assessment

A doctor will assess by:

**History — asking about:**
- Back pain (localised or radiating). Escalating pain in bed, or radiculopathy, are often early signs (may precede other symptoms by weeks). Note: sites of pain and level of compression do not always correlate.
- Lower limb or occasionally upper limb weakness
- Sensory disturbance (numbness or tingling)
- Bowel and bladder dysfunction (incontinence, retention, constipation)
- New difficulty mobilising
- History of spinal involvement with new or increasing back pain (note: previous X-rays and bone scans can be misleading)

**Neurological examination — looking for:**
- Sensory level
- Motor weakness
- Palpable bladder
- Loss of anal sphincter tone

Note: A normal neurological examination does not exclude evolving cord compression.

---

## Management

Management decisions should be made promptly by a doctor in consultation with oncology or palliative care.

### When active treatment is appropriate

1. **Seek radiation oncology advice immediately** — do not fax or email urgent requests. Contact the on-call radiation oncology registrar urgently via hospital switchboard.

2. **Give dexamethasone immediately:**
   - 16 mg orally (or IV/subcutaneously if unable to take oral medication)
   - If given subcutaneously: administer very slowly (can sting); give as 2 separate boluses at separate sites
   - If dexamethasone unavailable: give prednisone 100 mg immediately, then switch to daily dexamethasone
   - Steroids are short-term — a plan for withdrawal will be made based on investigation results
   - In palliative care, doses and routes may be unapproved

3. **Urgent imaging:**
   - MRI of the whole spine is the investigation of choice
   - CT is used if MRI is contraindicated (pacemaker, ferromagnetic metal implants, or foreign bodies)

4. **If multiple myeloma** with associated spinal cord compression, hypercalcaemia, or renal failure: request acute haematology assessment.

### When active treatment is not appropriate

If the patient is imminently dying or active treatment is not appropriate, seek adult palliative care support and advice for symptom management and comfort care.

---

## Information for Patients and Families

This is a serious medical emergency. If you have cancer and develop any of the red flags above (especially new back pain, leg weakness, or changes in bowel/bladder function), contact your doctor or go to an emergency department immediately. Early treatment significantly improves outcomes.
