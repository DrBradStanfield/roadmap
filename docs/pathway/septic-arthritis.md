# Septic Arthritis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway is for adults with suspected septic arthritis (a bacterial joint infection). For children, a separate pathway applies.

> **Suspected septic arthritis is a medical emergency. Always discuss with your doctor immediately.** Unrecognised or inadequately treated septic arthritis can cause permanent joint damage or death in up to 11% of patients. The information below explains how doctors assess and manage this condition.

---

## What Is Septic Arthritis?

Septic arthritis is a dangerous and destructive form of acute arthritis usually caused by bacteria spreading through the bloodstream to a joint (haematogenous spread). It can also occur after:

- A penetrating injury to the joint
- Joint aspiration, intra-articular steroid injection, arthroscopy, or joint surgery
- Spread of infection from adjacent bone (osteomyelitis)

**Most common pathogen:** *Staphylococcus aureus*

Other causes include:
- Gonorrhoea (a rare but important cause in sexually active adults — often with less joint damage)
- Mycobacterium species (rare)

---

## Red Flag

**Acute rheumatic fever** — an important condition that must be excluded in patients presenting with acute joint pain or swelling.

---

## Recognising Septic Arthritis — Key Features

> Your doctor will assess for these features. This helps explain what they are looking for.

**Classic presentation:**
- Rapid onset (hours to a day) of a swollen, tender, exquisitely painful joint
- Difficulty moving the affected joint
- Usually a single large joint — most commonly the **knee** (>50%), followed by wrist, ankle, hip, or shoulder
- More than one joint is involved in about 20% of cases
- Systemic symptoms: fever, malaise, feeling unwell
- Redness around the joint

**Important:** 40–60% of patients with septic arthritis may have no fever, especially older patients. A low-grade fever can also occur with gout, making distinction difficult.

---

## Who Is at Higher Risk?

Your doctor will check for predisposing factors:

- Penetrating injury to the skin
- Recent joint surgery
- Past history of septic arthritis
- **Prosthetic (artificial) joint** — infected prosthetic joints have fewer signs and are harder to diagnose
- Previous intra-articular corticosteroid injection
- Intravenous drug use
- Skin infection or cutaneous ulcers
- Diabetes mellitus
- Rheumatoid arthritis
- Other pre-existing joint disease (gout, osteoarthritis)
- Immunosuppression
- Chronic kidney disease
- Age > 80 years
- Alcoholism
- Multiple risk factors substantially increase the risk

---

## Differential Diagnosis — What Else Could It Be?

Septic arthritis can be difficult to distinguish from other causes of acute joint pain. Your doctor will consider:

| Condition | Key distinguishing features |
|---|---|
| **Gout** | Previous/recurrent episodes; low-grade fever possible; tophi; normal or raised urate |
| **Rheumatic fever** | Important to exclude; usually younger patients; history of strep infection |
| **Reactive arthritis** | Infection elsewhere (e.g. gonorrhoea, chlamydia, gastroenteritis); extra-articular features |
| **Inflammatory arthritis** | Pain worse after rest; symmetric joint involvement; extra-articular features (nodules, eye irritation, rash, bowel symptoms) |
| **Osteoarthritis** | Pain worse with activity; chronic course; older patient |
| **Mechanical injury** | Sudden onset following activity |
| **Gonococcal arthritis** | Sexually active adult; may have urethral/cervical discharge; less articular damage |
| **Sarcoidosis** | Skin, eye, or lung involvement |

**Note:** Gonorrhoea can spread haematogenously into a joint to cause true septic arthritis — it is not only reactive.

---

## What Happens During Assessment

### History

Your doctor will ask about:
- Onset and character of pain (sudden vs gradual; hours vs days)
- Which joint(s) are affected
- Fever, sweats, malaise
- Previous joint problems, surgery, or infections
- Risk factors (see above)
- Sexual history (if gonococcal arthritis is a possibility)
- Recent infections elsewhere (skin, urinary tract, respiratory)

### Examination

Your doctor will check:
- Temperature, pulse, blood pressure
- The affected joint: swelling, redness (erythema), effusion (fluid), nodules, range of movement, pain on movement
- Surrounding joints, skin, and eyes

### Investigations

**If septic arthritis or infected prosthetic joint is the most likely diagnosis:**

> **Do not perform joint aspiration or investigations in the community. Refer immediately for acute orthopaedic assessment.** Delay increases the risk of permanent joint damage.

**If septic arthritis is considered unlikely:**

Your doctor may arrange:
- **Blood tests:** FBC (full blood count), CRP, ESR, blood cultures. The lab request form should note "suspected osteoarticular infection" for ESR to be processed correctly.
- **Plain X-ray:** Not usually diagnostic of septic arthritis, but helps assess for osteoarthritis, inflammatory arthritis, or gout. Used for non-acute assessment only.
- **Joint aspiration:** May be considered if the doctor is competent to perform it, diagnosis is uncertain, but septic arthritis is unlikely. Fluid is sent for: cell count, crystals, Gram stain and culture, ZN stain and mycobacterial culture.

---

## Management

> **Your doctor manages this — not something to manage at home.**

1. **If septic arthritis is suspected:** Request urgent orthopaedic assessment.
2. **If septic arthritis is suspected:** Make the patient nil by mouth (NBM) while awaiting orthopaedic review — surgical washout of the joint is often required.

Treatment in hospital typically involves:
- Joint washout (arthroscopic or open surgical irrigation)
- Intravenous antibiotics (choice depends on likely organism and local resistance patterns)
- Prolonged antibiotic course (often weeks)
- Physiotherapy for joint rehabilitation

---

## When to Refer Urgently

**Request acute orthopaedic assessment immediately if:**
- Septic arthritis is the most likely diagnosis
- An infected prosthetic joint is suspected

Septic arthritis is treated in hospital. This is not a condition managed in primary care.
