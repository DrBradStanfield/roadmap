# Blood or Body Fluid Exposures

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers non-sexual blood and body fluid exposures, including needlestick injury, in both occupational and non-occupational settings.

**This is a time-sensitive situation. Always discuss with your doctor as soon as possible — HIV PEP must be started within 72 hours of exposure, and ideally within 2–4 hours.**

---

## Key Terms

- **Recipient** — the injured or exposed person.
- **Source** — the person whose blood or body fluid caused the exposure.
- **Unknown source** — exposure where the source's identity is unknown or consent is declined.

---

## Background

Blood or body fluid exposure (BBFE) may occur through percutaneous (skin-penetrating), mucous membrane, or non-intact skin contact.

The majority of exposures are low risk. Seroconversion to HBV, HCV, or HIV following an accidental community needlestick injury is uncommon, and most patients will not require post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP).

Healthcare workers are generally at higher risk, depending on the type of injury and body fluid involved.

---

## Immediate First Aid

Do this immediately, before anything else:

**For needlestick, sharp injury, bite, or scratch:**
- Allow the wound to bleed freely
- Wash with soap and running water
- Do not squeeze or rub the wound

**For splash to eye, nose, or mouth:**
- Rinse thoroughly with water or saline for at least 30 seconds, repeated several times

**For splash on skin:**
- Wash with soap and water; avoid skin trauma

Remove contaminated clothing and shower if needed.

---

## Risk Assessment

Your doctor will assess risk based on both the **type of body fluid** and the **features of the exposure**.

### Body Fluid Risk Level

**Infectious** (evidence of blood-borne virus transmission):
- Blood
- Visibly bloody body fluids

**Potentially infectious** (risk unknown):
- Amniotic fluid, cerebrospinal fluid, pericardial fluid, peritoneal fluid, pleural fluid, synovial fluid
- Vaginal secretions or semen
- Saliva in dentistry (likely blood-contaminated)
- Tissue fluid from burns or skin lesions

**Not infectious** (unless visibly blood-stained):
- Nasal secretions, saliva (non-dental), sputum, stool, sweat, tears, urine, vomit

### Features of Exposure

**Higher risk:**
- Hollow-bore needle recently removed from a blood vessel
- Deep injury causing bleeding
- Visible blood or body fluid on the device
- High viral load in the source (known HIV, active HBV or HCV)

**Lower risk:**
- Superficial injury with no visible bleeding
- Mucosal splash (eye or mouth)
- Needle not used on a blood vessel (e.g., suturing needle, subcutaneous injection)
- Old discarded sharps
- No visible blood on the sharp
- Sharp passed through gloves or clothing
- Human bite with no visible blood

**No risk:**
- Skin not breached
- Body fluid on intact skin
- Sharp not previously used on a patient

---

## Transmission Risk Figures

When the source is **confirmed** to be infected, average transmission risk per exposure:
- **HIV:** 0.3% percutaneous; 0.1% mucocutaneous
- **HCV:** 0.5–1.8%
- **HBV:** 6–30% (higher if source is HBeAg positive); non-immune recipients are at greatest risk

---

## Management by Exposure Type

### Exposures with Negligible Risk of BBV Transmission

No further action needed for:
- Skin not breached
- Contact of body fluid with intact skin
- Sharp not used on a patient
- Exposure to non-infectious body fluids (not visibly blood-stained)

Your doctor will explain that PEP is not indicated and testing is not required. Opportunistic screening for blood-borne viruses may still be considered.

### Exposures with Potential BBV Risk

Your doctor will arrange blood tests for both the source (if available) and the recipient.

**Recipient tests:**
- Hepatitis B surface antibody (HBsAb); if non-immune or unvaccinated, also HBsAg, HBcAb, and LFT (ALT)
- HCV antibody (with reflex PCR if positive); or HCV RNA if previously HCV Ab positive
- HIV serology (Ag/Ab)

**Source tests (if available):**
- Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg); if positive, also HBeAg and HBV DNA
- HCV antibody or HCV RNA
- HIV serology (Ag/Ab)

Results should be followed up within 24 hours.

---

## Possible HIV Exposure — PEP

HIV post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) must be given **within 72 hours** of exposure, and ideally within 2–4 hours.

### PEP is Never Recommended if:

- More than 72 hours have passed since exposure
- Exposure was only: oral intercourse; body fluid on intact skin; bite or saliva on skin or mucous membrane
- Needlestick from a discarded needle in the community (e.g., park, beach) from an unknown source
- The recipient has been taking PrEP as prescribed (if poorly adherent, PEP may still be considered)
- The source has been taking PrEP adherently
- The source is HIV positive but on treatment with an undetectable viral load

If PEP is not excluded, your doctor will seek urgent advice from an infectious diseases specialist.

---

## Possible Hepatitis B Exposure — HBIG

Your doctor will manage hepatitis B exposure based on the recipient's immune status and source's HBsAg result:

| Recipient HBsAb | Source HBsAg | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Negative | Negative | Start HBV vaccination |
| Positive (>10 IU/L) | Positive | No action |
| Negative | Positive | HBIG 400 units within 72 hours + start HBV vaccination |
| Previously positive, now negative (<10 IU/L) (evidence sighted) | Positive | No action |
| Previously positive (no evidence) or unknown response, now negative | Positive | HBIG 400 units within 72 hours + start HBV vaccination |
| Positive (>10 IU/L) | Unknown | No action |
| Negative | Unknown | HBIG 400 units within 72 hours (if high-risk source) + start HBV vaccination |

**Notes:**
- If the recipient has completed ≥3 HBV vaccine doses at recommended intervals and is not immunocompromised: if they have ever had a positive HBsAb, they are considered to have lifelong protection even if currently undetectable.
- HBIG must be given as soon as possible and within 72 hours of exposure.

---

## Possible Hepatitis C Exposure

There is no effective post-exposure prophylaxis for HCV.

Early treatment of any subsequent HCV infection has a 98% cure rate. Your doctor will arrange follow-up HCV testing and, if infection is confirmed, manage via the Chronic Hepatitis C pathway.

---

## Preventive Behaviours During Follow-Up

Until seronegative status is confirmed on follow-up testing, your doctor will advise:
- Safe sexual behaviour (HCV sexual transmission risk is very low; highest risk is receptive anal sex, especially if HIV positive)
- Safe injecting behaviour (e.g., New Zealand Needle Exchange Programme)
- Avoid sharing personal hygiene items (toothbrushes, razors)
- Avoid blood or tissue donation

---

## Follow-Up Testing Schedule

| Source status | Recipient follow-up testing |
|---|---|
| HIV positive or unknown | HIV serology (Ag/Ab) at 6 and 12 weeks |
| HCV viral load detectable or unknown | HCV viral load + LFT (ALT) at 6 and 12 weeks; HCV Ab at 12 and 24 weeks |
| HBsAg positive or unknown + recipient non-immune | LFT (ALT) at 6 and 12 weeks; HBsAg at 12 and 24 weeks; HBsAb at 6 weeks post-vaccination (or 4 months if simultaneous HBIG given) |

---

## Special Situations

**Community needlestick (discarded needle, unknown source):**
- Seroconversion is extremely rare in this scenario.
- HIV PEP is not recommended.

**Occupational exposure (general practice or community dental):**
- Report immediately to the infection prevention and control coordinator.
- Follow the practice's BBFE policy.
- The exposed person must not assess their own risk — another health practitioner must perform the assessment.

**Shared drug equipment:**
- Highest risk is from shared needles used to inject into a blood vessel.
- Other shared equipment (water, spoons, filters, tourniquets, swabs) also carries risk if in contact with broken skin.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Help

- **Possible HIV exposure:** Seek urgent infectious diseases assessment immediately — PEP must start within 72 hours (ideally 2–4 hours).
- **Possible hepatitis B exposure:** HBIG must be given within 72 hours — your doctor will contact the blood bank to arrange this.
- **Uncertainty about risk level:** Your doctor will seek infectious diseases advice.
