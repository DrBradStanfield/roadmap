# Pain and Bleeding in Early Pregnancy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of pain and/or bleeding in the first trimester of pregnancy.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately if:

- Suspected ectopic pregnancy
- Cervical shock (products of conception in the cervix causing cardiovascular instability)
- Haemodynamic instability (very low blood pressure, rapid heart rate, collapse)

---

## Background

- 20–40% of pregnant women experience bleeding during the first trimester. The major causes are miscarriage (10–20% of clinical pregnancies) and ectopic pregnancy (1–2%).
- Rarer causes include cervical and vaginal lesions (e.g., malignancy, cervical ectropion, polyps, infection) and uterine infection.
- Bleeding can also occur at the time of placental embedment at around 4–5 weeks.
- The uncertainty around viability, and then the grief of miscarriage, is difficult for women and their families. Checking in on how they are coping and offering counselling can help.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you in the following way:

### Step 1 — Check for clinical instability

Signs of clinical instability that require urgent care include:

- Tachycardia (heart rate > 100 beats per minute)
- Hypotension (blood pressure < 100/60) or orthostatic hypotension
- Products of conception in the cervix causing cervical shock
- Internal or external blood loss
- Severe pain or sepsis
- Ruptured ectopic pregnancy

If clinically unstable, your doctor will move directly to emergency management.

### Step 2 — History (if stable)

Your doctor will ask about:

- **Last menstrual period (LMP)** — dates, regularity, contraception used, date of positive pregnancy test
- **Pain** — severity, character, and location (note: intermittent pain does not rule out ectopic pregnancy)
- **Bleeding** — when it started, volume (number of pads per hour or day), presence of clots, abnormal discharge or odour, and whether it is vaginal, rectal, or urethral
- **Obstetric and gynaecological history** — previous pregnancies, miscarriages, ectopic pregnancies, pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), sexually transmitted infections (STIs), smear history, endometriosis
- **Contraception** — IUD and progestogen-only pill are associated with ectopic pregnancy; tubal ligation also raises risk

### Step 3 — Symptoms suggestive of ectopic pregnancy

Most common symptoms:
- Abdominal or pelvic pain
- Missed period or amenorrhoea
- Vaginal bleeding with or without clots

Other symptoms that may occur:
- Breast tenderness
- Gastrointestinal symptoms
- Dizziness, fainting, or syncope
- Shoulder tip pain
- Urinary symptoms
- Passage of tissue
- Rectal pressure or pain on defecation

### Step 4 — Physical examination

Your doctor will:

- Record temperature, pulse, and blood pressure
- Examine the abdomen for tenderness, guarding, and rebound
- Look for signs suggestive of ectopic pregnancy:
  - Abdominal or pelvic tenderness
  - Adnexal mass or tenderness
  - Cervical motion tenderness
  - Rebound tenderness or peritoneal signs
  - Pallor, abdominal distension
  - Tachycardia or hypotension
  - Shock or collapse
- If bleeding heavily or blood pressure is low, a speculum or bimanual pelvic examination may be done to check for products of conception in the cervix

### Step 5 — Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- **Pregnancy test** — if pregnancy is not already confirmed
- **Quantitative serum beta hCG** — if less than 9 weeks gestation, serial measurements at least 48 hours apart help assess viability
- **First antenatal blood tests** — full blood count (FBC), blood group and antibody screen, hepatitis B, rubella immunity, syphilis serology, HbA1c, ferritin, HIV screening
- **Midstream urine (MSU)** — dipstick and culture if any abnormalities
- **Vulvovaginal NAAT swab** — for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and trichomonas
- **High vaginal swab** — for candida and bacterial vaginosis
- **Ultrasound scan** — typically arranged if 6 weeks or more gestation. Before 6 weeks, an intrauterine pregnancy may not yet be visible (usually visible from ~5–6 weeks when beta hCG > 1500); your doctor may monitor with serial beta hCG first

## Management

Management depends on whether you are clinically stable, and on the results of your scan.

### If clinically unstable

Your doctor will:

- Resuscitate as needed (IV fluids, analgesia)
- Remove any products of conception from the cervix
- Insert a large-bore intravenous line
- Arrange emergency transport to hospital via ambulance
- Notify the on-call gynaecology team that you are coming

### If clinically stable but at high risk

Your doctor will arrange urgent gynaecology assessment and hospital transport via ambulance if you have:

- Heart rate > 100 beats per minute
- Low or dropping blood pressure
- Symptoms highly suggestive of ectopic pregnancy

### Based on scan results

#### Viable intrauterine pregnancy

- Your doctor will reassure you and continue routine antenatal care.
- If you have pain, other causes will be considered.
- You should return if bleeding worsens or persists beyond 14 days.

#### Non-viable intrauterine pregnancy (confirmed miscarriage)

Your doctor will discuss management options with you:

- **Expectant management** (watching and waiting): suitable if you are in the first trimester, haemodynamically stable, able to attend follow-ups, and live within 60 minutes of a hospital. You will be seen weekly or more frequently, advised to use simple analgesia, and asked to avoid intercourse or tampons while bleeding continues. A urine pregnancy test is repeated 3 weeks after passing the products of conception.
- **Medical or surgical management** in secondary care: all options (expectant, medical, surgical) will be offered. Medical management involves a single dose of medication to complete the miscarriage at home.

#### Intrauterine pregnancy of uncertain viability

- Your doctor may repeat blood tests and arrange a further scan.
- If still uncertain, early pregnancy assessment will be sought.
- You should seek medical attention if there is worsening bleeding or pain, and call emergency services if symptoms become severe.

#### Ectopic pregnancy

- If clinically unstable: emergency resuscitation and ambulance transport to hospital.
- If clinically stable: urgent gynaecology assessment and hospital transport via ambulance.

#### Pregnancy of unknown location (no pregnancy visible on scan)

- This could represent a complete miscarriage, an early intrauterine pregnancy, or an ectopic pregnancy.
- Your doctor will seek gynaecology advice about the appropriate next steps.

#### Retained products of conception (POC)

- If you have severe pain, signs of infection, heavy bleeding (soaking a pad in less than an hour), or prolonged bleeding beyond 14 days, your doctor will seek gynaecology advice.
- If only a small amount of retained tissue is present and you are stable, your doctor may consider expectant management and may prescribe prophylactic antibiotics such as **amoxicillin + clavulanic acid** 625 mg three times a day for one week.

#### Molar pregnancy

- If a molar pregnancy is suspected on ultrasound, early pregnancy assessment will be arranged.
- Products of conception are sent for histology.

### Anti-D immunoglobulin (for Rh-negative women)

If you are Rh-D negative and have had a sensitising event (bleeding, miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, or certain procedures), your doctor may prescribe anti-D immunoglobulin to prevent your immune system from forming antibodies that could affect future pregnancies.

- Anti-D should be given as soon as possible, always within 72 hours of the sensitising event.
- **Dose:** 250 units if less than 12 weeks gestation; 625 units if 12 weeks or more.
- Anti-D is administered as an intramuscular injection; you will need to remain on-site for 20 minutes after administration.
- Anti-D is generally not needed for a threatened miscarriage in the first trimester unless bleeding is heavy, repeated, or associated with pain, or dates are close to 12 weeks.

**Sensitising events requiring anti-D at less than 12 weeks:**
- Chorionic villus sampling
- Completed spontaneous miscarriage
- Surgical or medical abortion (10–12 weeks only)
- Ectopic pregnancy, molar pregnancy
- Heavy or repeated uterine bleeding associated with abdominal pain

**Sensitising events requiring anti-D at 12 weeks or more:**
- All vaginal bleeding (including threatened miscarriage, antepartum haemorrhage)
- Ectopic or molar pregnancy
- Invasive fetal procedures (amniocentesis, chorionic villus sampling, intrauterine blood sampling)
- External cephalic version (turning a breech baby)
- Abdominal trauma
- Medical or surgical abortion
- Intrauterine death or stillbirth

### Follow-up and complications

1. **Prolonged or heavy bleeding after miscarriage (> 2 weeks):** Your doctor may take swabs and prescribe broad-spectrum antibiotics such as **amoxicillin + clavulanic acid** 500/125 mg three times a day for one week. If bleeding is causing shock or anaemia, urgent gynaecology assessment is needed.
2. **Concerning features suggesting retained products of conception** — abdominal cramps, increasing bleeding, sudden new bleeding, or feeling unwell — warrant a repeat ultrasound scan.
3. **Persistent pelvic pain and tenderness** with or without low-grade fever may indicate endometritis from retained products of conception. Your doctor will arrange an acute pelvic ultrasound.
4. **Previous ectopic pregnancy:** Advise your doctor early in any future pregnancy so that an early scan can be arranged.
5. **Asherman syndrome** (intrauterine adhesions): If you had surgical management for miscarriage and develop sub-fertility, menstrual irregularities (including absent periods), cyclic pelvic pain, or recurrent pregnancy loss, this may be the cause. Your doctor will refer you for gynaecology assessment.

### After a miscarriage

Your doctor will suggest a review within 2 weeks to:

- Ask about symptoms (bleeding, pain)
- Check how you are feeling emotionally and offer counselling if needed
- Discuss contraception and pre-conception advice for future pregnancy planning
