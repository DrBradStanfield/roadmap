# CT Head

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers when a CT scan of the head may be appropriate, what conditions warrant urgent assessment, and important safety considerations around radiation and contrast.

---

## Important Safety Considerations

### Radiation

CT scans use ionising radiation, which carries a small risk of cancer over a lifetime. Key points:

- CT should be **avoided in pregnancy** unless the scan is clinically critical
- For patients **younger than 40 years**, alternative imaging (e.g. MRI or ultrasound) may be preferred, particularly when frequent follow-up scans are expected

### IV Contrast

IV contrast is **not commonly used** for CT head or sinus scans. When it is used, it is generally safe but can cause:

- Mild to severe allergic reactions (including anaphylaxis)
- Contrast-induced kidney impairment in susceptible patients
- Worsening of hyperthyroidism (thyrotoxicosis)

Tell your doctor if you:

- Have had a previous allergic reaction to IV contrast
- Have impaired kidney function (eGFR less than 30 mL/min)
- Take **metformin** (a diabetes medication)
- Have hyperthyroidism or thyroid problems

## When a CT Head May Be Indicated

Your doctor will assess whether a CT head is appropriate. The following are situations where it is commonly considered.

### Cognitive Impairment

A CT head may be recommended if there are features suggesting the cognitive decline could be caused by a space-occupying lesion, bleed, or fluid build-up (hydrocephalus), including:

- Rapid cognitive decline over weeks
- Focal neurological signs (weakness, speech problems, coordination)
- History of falls
- Use of anticoagulant medication (blood thinners)
- Gait (walking) problems or urinary incontinence
- History of cancers known to spread to the brain (e.g. lung, melanoma, breast)

A CT head may also help identify the **subtype of dementia** (e.g. vascular dementia, normal pressure hydrocephalus, Alzheimer's disease), which can influence treatment options. However, CT alone cannot diagnose dementia.

If the presentation suggests frontotemporal dementia (e.g. patient younger than 65 with speech disturbance or apraxia), MRI may be more appropriate than CT.

### Headache

A CT head may be indicated for a **change in headache pattern** with progressive increase in frequency or severity — particularly in patients aged 50 and older — combined with any of:

- Headache in a patient with a known cancer (especially lung, breast, or melanoma, which can spread to the brain)
- Headache made worse by coughing, sneezing, or bearing down (Valsalva manoeuvre), in a pattern not typical of migraine
- Associated persistent nausea and vomiting not typical of migraine
- Objective neurological deficit — speech disturbance, or arm or facial weakness

Headaches consistent with migraine, tension-type headache, medication overuse headache, or cluster headache do not typically require a CT head.

### Seizures

A CT head may be appropriate for:

- A **first seizure** in a patient aged 21 or older, particularly when MRI is unavailable
- A seizure in a **palliative care patient** where the result may change management

A first seizure is generally considered **lower risk** if all of the following apply:

- Single seizure, unprovoked or provoked by a known substance (e.g. alcohol or drugs)
- The patient has returned to normal health
- No suggestion of intracranial pathology
- None of the following higher-risk features are present:
  - Recent head injury
  - Age older than 40
  - Fever or systemic symptoms
  - History of anticoagulant use or malignancy
  - New focal neurological deficit
  - Focal (partial) seizure
  - Persistent altered mental state
  - Persistent headache

If higher-risk features are present, your doctor will seek specialist neurology advice rather than ordering a CT directly.

### Focal Neurological Signs and TIA

- **Focal neurological signs** (new weakness, speech problems, visual changes): your doctor may seek neurology advice and arrange a CT head if recommended, typically within 48 hours
- **Suspected TIA (transient ischaemic attack):** a CT head within 48 hours may be arranged, particularly if the episode occurred within the last 7 days and specialist review is not immediately available

## Conditions Requiring Emergency or Specialist Assessment

Some presentations should not wait for an outpatient CT head — your doctor will arrange immediate care:

- **Suspected subarachnoid haemorrhage** (sudden severe "thunderclap" headache) — emergency neurosurgery assessment
- **Suspected stroke** — follow the stroke pathway; do not wait for a community CT
- **High-risk TIA** — same-day specialist assessment (features include ABCD2 score ≥ 4, anticoagulant use, atrial fibrillation, known carotid stenosis, crescendo TIA, unilateral arm weakness, or neck/facial pain suggesting arterial dissection)
- **Drowsiness or decreased level of consciousness** — acute secondary care assessment
- **Suspected prolactinoma** — referral for secondary care assessment (MRI is typically preferred over CT)
