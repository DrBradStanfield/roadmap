# Febrile Illness in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of fever in children — determining severity and guiding appropriate care.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department if your child:

- Is aged **less than 6 weeks** (any illness, any temperature)
- Has a **fever above 38°C and is aged less than 3 months**

---

## Background

A fever is defined as a temperature above 38°C. However, a child's temperature fluctuates during illness, and measuring temperature is prone to inaccuracy regardless of the method used. How well the child appears is always more important than the exact number on the thermometer.

Most febrile illness in children is caused by viruses. The younger a baby is, the higher the risk of a serious bacterial infection such as bacteraemia or bacterial meningitis.

- The most common bacterial cause in children under 5 years is a **urinary tract infection (UTI)**
- Less common but serious bacterial causes include: sepsis, pneumonia, meningitis, bone, joint, or soft tissue infections, and Kawasaki disease
- There is no reliable relationship between the height of a fever and the likelihood of a serious bacterial illness

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will check vital signs and be alert to the subdued child with abnormal physiological findings.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Duration and type of symptoms — how long the fever has lasted, other symptoms such as respiratory, gastrointestinal, or skin changes
- Alertness, activity level, and response to social cues
- Feeding and number of wet nappies
- Risk factors including premature birth, poor nutrition, underlying or chronic medical conditions, not being immunised, or exposure to tobacco smoke or cold and damp housing

### Examination

Your doctor will examine for:

- Deterioration or reduced level of consciousness
- Poor perfusion (pale, mottled, ashen, or blue skin)
- Petechial or purpuric rash
- Cerebral irritation
- Bulging fontanelle in an infant

Baseline vital signs recorded include: temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, and capillary refill time (CRT).

### Illness Severity

Your doctor will classify the illness as mild, moderate, or severe.

**Severe illness — features include:**

- Child looks seriously unwell
- Pale, mottled, ashen, or blue skin
- Reduced consciousness, confusion, difficult to wake, or very irritable
- Purpuric or petechial rash
- Capillary refill time more than 3 seconds, cold extremities
- Severely fast heart rate or breathing rate for age (see tachycardia/tachypnoea tables below)
- Weak, high-pitched, or continuous cry
- Respiratory distress (grunting, significant chest indrawing)
- Bile-stained vomiting (assume bowel obstruction — call an ambulance)
- Fever above 38°C in a child aged less than 3 months

**Heart rate — severe vs moderate thresholds by age:**

| Age (years) | Severe (beats/min) | Moderate (beats/min) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| < 1 | ≥ 160 | 150–159 |
| 1–2 | ≥ 150 | 140–149 |
| 3–4 | ≥ 140 | 130–139 |
| 5 | ≥ 130 | 120–129 |
| 6–7 | ≥ 120 | 110–119 |
| 8–11 | ≥ 115 | 105–114 |
| ≥ 12 | ≥ 130 | 91–130 |

**Breathing rate — severe vs moderate thresholds by age:**

| Age (years) | Severe (breaths/min) | Moderate (breaths/min) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| < 1 | ≥ 60 | 50–59 |
| 1–2 | ≥ 50 | 40–49 |
| 3–4 | ≥ 40 | 35–39 |
| 5 | ≥ 29 | 27–28 |
| 6–7 | ≥ 27 | 24–26 |
| 8–11 | ≥ 25 | 22–24 |
| ≥ 12 | ≥ 25 | 21–24 |

*Thresholds sourced from NICE – Sepsis: Recognition, Diagnosis and Early Management*

**Moderate illness — features include:**

- Parent or caregiver concerned the child is behaving differently
- Not responding normally to social cues
- Very sleepy or decreased activity, but wakes with stimulation
- Rigors or temperature above 39°C
- Moderately fast heart rate or breathing rate for age (see tables above)
- Nasal flaring
- Dry mucous membranes or poor feeding
- Reduced urine output
- Capillary refill time 2–3 seconds
- Oxygen saturation below 92% on air, or accessory muscle use
- Known cardiac, respiratory, or neurological disability

**Mild illness — features include:**

- Normal colour of skin, lips, and tongue
- Alert, content, or smiling
- Strong, normal cry or not crying
- Moist mucous membranes and normal skin and eyes
- Responds normally to social cues

### Investigations Your Doctor May Consider

**Urine sample** — your doctor may collect urine if:

- Your child is under 2 years with unexplained fever lasting more than 1 day
- Your child has had a previous urinary tract infection

Bag testing has a high false positive rate and is used only as a screening tool if a clean catch is not possible.

**Chest X-ray** — your doctor may consider this if:

- Atypical presentation or diagnostic uncertainty
- Child is aged less than 3 months
- Child is severely unwell
- History suggesting underlying respiratory disease
- Complications such as a pleural effusion are suspected
- Persisting fast breathing, particularly when the fever comes down

## Management

### Sepsis

If sepsis is suspected — a confirmed or suspected infection where the child appears very unwell or has features of severe tachycardia, tachypnoea, or poor perfusion — your doctor will manage this as a medical emergency. Early recognition and treatment are vital.

### Severe Illness

Your doctor will:

1. Call an ambulance for urgent transfer to hospital
2. Administer oxygen to maintain oxygen saturations above 94%
3. If meningococcal disease is suspected, begin treatment while waiting for the ambulance

### Moderate Illness

Your doctor may:

1. Seek paediatric advice if:
   - Fever continues after 5 days
   - Your child has co-morbidities or is immunocompromised
   - Suspected urinary tract infection in an infant under 3 months
   - Concern about your ability to manage at home or return for review
   - No improvement or deterioration after 24 hours
2. Offer **paracetamol** (e.g., Pamol) if the child is distressed — this can be given regardless of the height of the fever
3. Arrange extended monitoring of heart rate, breathing rate, capillary refill, and fluid intake and output
4. Review after 2 hours:
   - If improved — provide home care advice and arrange a follow-up review the next day
   - If no change or worsening — seek paediatric advice

**Return immediately to your doctor or emergency department if your child:**

- Develops a non-blanching rash
- Has a seizure
- Develops breathing difficulties
- Has persistent vomiting
- Becomes more drowsy than usual
- Becomes less well than at their first visit

### Mild Illness

Your doctor may:

1. Reassure and provide home care advice if the child appears content
2. Offer **paracetamol** (e.g., Pamol) if the child is distressed — regardless of the exact temperature
3. Seek paediatric advice if fever has lasted more than 5 days or the child has known co-morbidities

**Return immediately to your doctor or emergency department if your child:**

- Develops a non-blanching rash
- Has a seizure
- Develops breathing difficulties
- Has persistent vomiting
- Becomes more drowsy than usual
- Becomes less well than at their first visit
