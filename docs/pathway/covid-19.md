# COVID-19

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers community management of suspected or confirmed COVID-19 in adults and children — including severity assessment, antiviral eligibility, symptom management, and isolation guidance.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care (call 111)

**Adults — call an ambulance if any of the following:**

- New shortness of breath at rest or on minimal movement, or unable to talk in full sentences
- Sudden onset of shortness of breath with or without chest pain
- Oxygen saturation 93% or less, or 3% or more drop from baseline
- Unable to stand, or new onset of multiple faints or falls
- New confusion or abnormally drowsy
- Severe dehydration — minimal fluid intake with little or no urine output
- Rapid deterioration
- Severe chest pain
- Significant coughing up of blood (small streaks of blood are common and not usually concerning)

**Children — call an ambulance if any of the following:**

- Severe respiratory distress
- Oxygen saturation less than 92% (or a drop from baseline of 3% or more in a child with pre-existing low oxygen levels)
- Fast breathing:
  - Under 2 months: more than 60 breaths per minute
  - 2–11 months: more than 50 breaths per minute
  - 1–5 years: more than 40 breaths per minute
  - 6 years or older: more than 30 breaths per minute
- Inability to breastfeed or drink
- Poor urine output
- Lethargy or loss of consciousness
- Convulsions
- Fever above 38°C in an infant younger than 6 weeks

---

## Contact Your Doctor or Healthline (0800 611 116) if:

**Adults:**
- New or worsening shortness of breath
- Symptoms getting worse overall
- Getting worse after a period of improvement
- Severe dehydration symptoms: very dry mouth, passing very little urine, feeling very light-headed

**Children:**
- Fever persisting for 5 or more days (may indicate bacterial infection or rare complications)
- Drinking less than half of usual fluids
- Frequent vomiting or diarrhoea
- Oxygen saturation 92–96%

## Background

COVID-19 is caused by the SARS-CoV-2 virus (currently the Omicron variant in New Zealand). It is spread mainly through the respiratory route — aerosols and droplets. Transmission from surfaces is possible but uncommon.

Most people in New Zealand now have mild to moderate illness due to widespread vaccination and prior exposure. Severe illness can still occur, particularly in unvaccinated people and those with risk factors (see below).

COVID-19 most commonly affects the respiratory tract but can also affect the heart, gut, nervous system, and other organs — so symptoms can be varied.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess severity and risk of complications. Severity is classified as:

**Adults:**

| Severity | Features |
|----------|----------|
| Mild | No breathlessness; able to maintain hydration; symptoms include fatigue, sore throat, headache, muscle aches, fever, runny nose, cough |
| Moderate | Some breathlessness (not severe); oxygen saturation above 93%; concern about hydration; new postural dizziness |
| Severe | Any red flag listed above — hospital assessment indicated |

**Children:**

| Severity | Features |
|----------|----------|
| Mild | Mild cough or upper respiratory symptoms; no breathlessness; mild gut symptoms; oxygen saturation above 96% |
| Moderate | Moderate breathing difficulty; drinking less than half of usual; frequent vomiting/diarrhoea; oxygen saturation 92–96% |
| Severe | Any red flag listed above — hospital assessment indicated |

## Risk Factors for Complications

Your doctor will consider the following when deciding on monitoring and management:

**Medical risk factors (adults):**
- Pregnant, or within 6 weeks of giving birth
- Immunocompromised
- Active cancer
- Severe co-morbidities (e.g., severe respiratory, heart, liver, kidney, or neurological disease; sickle cell disease; diabetes)
- Mental health or addiction concerns
- Frailty
- Morbid obesity

**Personal risk factors (adults):**
- Māori or Pacific peoples
- Older age

**Social factors** that may affect safe self-monitoring:
- Social or geographical isolation
- Lack of transport
- Unreliable phone or internet connection
- Housing insecurity
- No suitable caregiver
- Disability support required
- Language or health literacy barriers

**Children at higher risk:**
- Infants younger than 1 month
- Premature birth (under 37 weeks) in children younger than 2 years
- Immunocompromised
- Severe or complex medical conditions (e.g., neurodisability)
- Diabetes
- Severe obesity (BMI at or above the 95th percentile)

## COVID-19 Antivirals — Eligibility

From 1 September 2025, funded antiviral treatment is available if the patient:

- Meets Pharmac access criteria, **and**
- Has symptoms, **and**
- Is within 5–7 days of symptom onset (depending on the agent)

Eligibility now includes:
- People aged 65 years or older
- People aged 50 years or older considered at high risk of hospitalisation or death

Your doctor will assess whether antivirals are appropriate and prescribe if eligible. Time matters — treatment must start early.

---

## Testing

- **Rapid antigen test (RAT):** The standard test for COVID-19. Self-testing is encouraged if symptomatic.
- If a RAT is negative but symptoms continue, repeat testing after 24 and 48 hours.
- If reinfection is unlikely (within 28 days of a previous infection), testing is generally not needed.
- PCR testing is no longer routinely funded or processed in primary care.

## Management

### Symptom Management

Your doctor may advise:

| Symptom | What may help | Seek help if |
|---------|--------------|-------------|
| Fever | Paracetamol as directed on the label | Fever lasting 5+ days in children (possible bacterial infection or Kawasaki disease / PIMS-TS) |
| Cough, sore throat, runny nose | Paracetamol; adults may use standard cold and flu medications | Shortness of breath, severe cough, or coughing up significant blood — call 111 |
| Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea | Frequent small sips of fluid; oral rehydration solutions (e.g., Hydrolyte) | Unable to drink; more than 4 episodes of diarrhoea per day; significantly reduced urine output; dizziness or fainting |
| Headache, body aches | Paracetamol or ibuprofen as directed | Severe persistent headache not responding to treatment |
| Shortness of breath | Positioning to assist breathing | Severe shortness of breath or difficulty breathing — call 111 |
| Chest pain (in children or young adults with no cardiac risk factors) | Ibuprofen as directed, if mild and associated with general body aches, no palpitations, and not otherwise unwell | Chest pain lasting more than 10 minutes, especially with breathlessness, palpitations, sweating, or collapse — call 111 |
| Rash | Antihistamines for itch; emollients for dry skin; topical corticosteroids if known eczema | Non-blanching rash, or patient is unwell — possible alternative diagnosis (e.g., meningitis); rash appearing after day 14 in an unwell child (consider PIMS-TS) |
| Conjunctivitis | Saline drops; antibiotic drops if yellow discharge | Child with conjunctivitis who is also unwell with a rash — may need assessment for PIMS-TS |
| Dizziness, fainting, lethargy | Stay hydrated; seek urgent medical assessment | Associated with chest pain, palpitations, possible seizure, unconsciousness lasting more than 30 seconds, or persistent drowsiness — call 111 |

### Isolation Guidance

- **Recommended isolation period:** 5 days from day 0 (day 0 is the day symptoms started or the day of the positive test, whichever came first).
- If symptoms resolve by day 5, normal activities can resume on day 6.
- If still symptomatic on day 6, remain at home until recovered.
- Wearing a mask is recommended until the end of day 10 when visiting healthcare facilities or people who are at risk of severe illness, as some people remain infectious for up to 10 days.

**Household contacts:**
- Do not need to test if they have no symptoms.
- Can continue usual activities but are encouraged to wear a mask for the first 5 days of the case's illness when in crowded indoor spaces, visiting vulnerable people, or using public transport.
- Should test if symptoms develop and isolate if positive.

### Medications — Special Situations

If you take regular medications, your doctor may review the following:

- **ACE inhibitors or angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs):** Dose may be reduced or paused if at risk of dehydration.
- **Diabetes medications (including insulin):** Your doctor will advise on sick-day management.
- **Clozapine:** Levels may increase during acute COVID-19 infection — monitoring is important.
- **Oral menopause hormone therapy:** May be paused or switched to a transdermal (patch/gel) preparation.
- **Immunosuppressants:** Do not stop or adjust without specialist advice.
- **Depot injections (contraception or mental health):** Discuss alternatives with your doctor if a scheduled injection is due.

### Special Populations

**Immunocompromised patients:** May remain infectious for longer than usual. Your doctor may involve a specialist.

**Aged residential care residents:** May present with atypical symptoms (e.g., unexplained fever, worsening of existing conditions, increased confusion). Facility-specific protocols apply.

**Patients with existing chronic conditions:** Your doctor may activate a chronic disease action plan (e.g., asthma, COPD, heart failure) and review goals of care.

## Persistent Symptoms After COVID-19

If symptoms persist after COVID-19, your doctor will consider the following possibilities:

- **Gradual recovery (most common):** Ongoing but slowly improving symptoms. No further testing or isolation needed. Review if still unwell after 6 weeks.
- **Unrelated illness:** New acute-onset respiratory or gut symptoms up to day 29 are common and usually represent a different infection. Stay home if unwell.
- **Reinfection:** New acute COVID-19 symptoms after day 29. Test and self-isolate; treat as a new COVID-19 infection including considering antivirals.
- **Rebound infection:** Initial recovery followed by relapse within 30 days. Occurs in up to 10% of cases — even in those who did not take antivirals. Isolate until 24 hours after symptoms resolve; further antivirals are not recommended.
- **Persistent infection:** Only considered in severely immunocompromised patients with a persistently positive test after day 20. Specialist involvement required.
- **Long COVID:** Consider if symptoms persist beyond 6 weeks. Discuss with your doctor.

## Paediatric Inflammatory Multisystem Syndrome (PIMS-TS)

A rare but serious complication occurring 2–6 weeks after COVID-19 (which may have been mild or asymptomatic). It presents with:

- Persistent fever for more than 3 days
- Inflammation and single or multi-organ involvement
- Features may include vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, rash, conjunctivitis, headache, confusion, neck swelling, swollen hands and feet

PIMS-TS shares features with Kawasaki disease. Any concern should prompt urgent medical review.
