# Pulmonary Embolism (PE)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

PE is a blood clot in the lungs. It can present subtly and is easily missed — a high index of suspicion is important. This pathway covers how your doctor assesses and manages suspected PE.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately if:

- Pleuritic chest pain with dyspnoea (shortness of breath), low oxygen levels, or fast heart rate
- Haemodynamic instability (collapse, very low blood pressure, shock)
- High clinical suspicion of PE on clinical grounds

---

## Assessment

PE may present with very few clinical signs or symptoms.

### Symptoms Your Doctor Will Ask About

Suspect PE if there is unexplained:
- Pleuritic chest pain (pain that worsens with breathing in)
- Shortness of breath
- Low oxygen levels (hypoxia)
- Fast heart rate (tachycardia)
- Coughing up blood (haemoptysis)
- Pleural effusion (fluid around the lung)

### Risk Factors for PE

Your doctor will assess for risk factors. Major risk factors include:

- Recent surgery (within 4 weeks)
- Immobilisation — lying in bed for more than 48 hours, or a limb in plaster or external fixator
- Oestrogen therapy — combined oral contraceptive pill or hormone replacement therapy (HRT)
- Active or metastatic cancer
- Unilateral leg swelling
- Thrombophilia (blood clotting disorder)
- Pregnancy or within 6 weeks of giving birth

Minor risk factors include:

- Seated travel for longer than 8 hours
- Myeloproliferative disorder
- Previous pulmonary embolism

### Examination

Your doctor will check:
- Blood pressure, heart rate, jugular venous pressure (JVP), and oxygen saturation
- PE is more likely if oxygen saturation is below 95% or heart rate is above 94 beats per minute
- Signs of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in the legs

An **ECG** will be arranged.

---

## Probability Assessment: Wells' Score

If the diagnosis is uncertain, your doctor will calculate a **Wells' score** to estimate the probability of PE.

| Clinical Feature | Points |
|---|---|
| Clinical signs and symptoms of DVT | 3 |
| PE is the most likely diagnosis (or equally likely) | 3 |
| Heart rate > 100 beats per minute | 1.5 |
| Immobilisation ≥ 3 days or surgery in the previous 4 weeks | 1.5 |
| Previous DVT or PE | 1.5 |
| Haemoptysis | 1 |
| Malignancy (receiving treatment, or treated in past 6 months, or palliative) | 1 |

**Score interpretation:**
- **> 6:** High probability
- **2–6:** Moderate probability
- **< 2:** Low probability

---

## D-Dimer Testing

D-dimer is a blood test used to help rule out PE. It is only useful when pre-test probability is low or moderate — a positive D-dimer does not confirm PE.

### Age-Adjusted D-Dimer Thresholds

For laboratory D-dimer, your doctor uses age-adjusted cut-off values:

| Age (years) | D-dimer cut-off | Positive if ≥ |
|---|---|---|
| < 50 | 500 microgram/L | 500 |
| 51–60 | 600 microgram/L | 600 |
| 61–70 | 700 microgram/L | 700 |
| 71–80 | 800 microgram/L | 800 |
| 81–90 | 900 microgram/L | 900 |
| > 91 | 1000 microgram/L | 1000 |

*Note: Age-adjusted ranges apply to laboratory D-dimer only, not point-of-care tests.*

---

## PERC Rule (Low-Risk Patients)

For patients with **low Wells' score**, your doctor may apply the **Pulmonary Embolism Rule-out Criteria (PERC)**. If the answer to **all** PERC criteria is **no**, no further investigation for PE is needed.

The 8 PERC criteria are:
1. Age ≥ 50
2. Heart rate ≥ 100 beats per minute
3. Oxygen saturation < 95%
4. Unilateral leg swelling
5. Haemoptysis
6. Recent surgery or trauma (within 4 weeks requiring hospitalisation)
7. Prior DVT or PE
8. Exogenous oestrogen use

If **any** criterion is yes, a D-dimer test is needed.

*PERC has been validated in emergency departments in over 8,000 patients and is endorsed for use in primary care.*

## Management

### By Probability

- **High probability of PE** (Wells' > 6, or acutely unwell, or haemodynamically unstable): Your doctor will request urgent hospital assessment. An ambulance with IV access, oxygen, and analgesia will be arranged.

- **Moderate probability of PE** (Wells' 2–6, or low Wells' with ≥ 1 PERC criterion):
  - D-dimer arranged urgently and reviewed same day
  - If D-dimer is **positive** → urgent hospital assessment
  - If D-dimer is **negative** → clinically significant PE is excluded

- **Low probability of PE** (Wells' < 2 and all PERC criteria negative, or low Wells' with ≥ 1 PERC criterion and negative D-dimer): Your doctor will manage for the most likely alternative cause.

### After a Diagnosed PE

If you have been diagnosed with PE and started on anticoagulation (blood thinners such as warfarin or a direct oral anticoagulant/DOAC) during a hospital admission, your doctor will continue monitoring you for the treatment duration decided at discharge.

**Duration of anticoagulation:**

- **Provoked PE** (identifiable, reversible risk factor): usually 3 to 6 months
- **Unprovoked PE** or **recurrent PE/DVT**: lifelong anticoagulation is often considered, particularly if the risk of recurrent clot outweighs the risk of bleeding from treatment

If there is any uncertainty about how long to continue anticoagulation, your doctor will seek haematology specialist advice.

---

## When Your Doctor Will Refer

Your doctor will request acute hospital assessment if:
- PE probability is high
- You are acutely unwell or haemodynamically unstable
- PE is possible and D-dimer is positive

Haematology advice will be sought if the duration of anticoagulation is unclear.
