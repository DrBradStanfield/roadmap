# Carotid Artery Stenosis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the management of abnormal carotid ultrasound or CT angiogram (CTA) results, and incidental carotid artery stenosis identified outside hospital.

---

## Background

Carotid artery stenosis can be symptomatic or asymptomatic. Significant stenosis may present as a transient ischaemic attack (TIA) or stroke. Management of asymptomatic carotid artery stenosis is still evolving and remains an area of active research.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance immediately if you have any of the following (these may indicate a TIA or stroke):

- Sudden transient loss of vision in one eye (amaurosis fugax)
- Sudden difficulty speaking or understanding speech (dysphasia)
- Sudden weakness or numbness on one side of the body or face

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will determine whether carotid artery stenosis is symptomatic or asymptomatic, as this determines the management pathway.

### Symptomatic carotid artery stenosis

Symptoms suggesting the carotid artery is the source include:

- Monocular blindness — typically brief, transient loss of vision in one eye (amaurosis fugax), usually lasting less than 10 minutes
- Dysphasia (difficulty speaking or understanding)
- Unilateral motor or sensory symptoms affecting the face and limbs

If these symptoms are present, your doctor will follow the TIA or Stroke pathway.

### Vertebrobasilar symptoms (different artery territory — carotid imaging not indicated)

- Cortical blindness
- Double vision (diplopia)
- Isolated homonymous hemianopia or quadrantanopia
- Bilateral motor or sensory symptoms affecting face and/or limbs
- Ataxia (loss of coordination)

### Asymptomatic carotid artery stenosis

Asymptomatic stenosis may be discovered incidentally on imaging or as a carotid bruit on examination. A carotid bruit is a poor predictor of stenosis severity but does indicate generalised cardiovascular risk — both cardiovascular and cerebrovascular disease.

Your doctor will not routinely request carotid imaging for asymptomatic patients and will focus on cardiovascular risk reduction.

---

## Investigations

Your doctor may arrange the following if you have had a TIA or minor stroke:

- **CT Head with CT Angiogram (CTA)** — specialists increasingly use this as a single combined procedure rather than two separate tests
- **Carotid ultrasound** — may be arranged instead of or alongside CTA

If imaging shows carotid stenosis greater than 50%, your doctor will discuss the result immediately with a stroke specialist.

---

## Management

### Symptomatic carotid artery stenosis

Your doctor may consider:

- **Urgent stroke specialist review** — if imaging shows more than 50% stenosis, you have had a TIA or minor stroke, and you are fit for surgery
- **Carotid endarterectomy (CEA)** — a surgical procedure to remove the narrowing. Your doctor will assess suitability based on your overall health and comorbidities. Some CEAs are performed under local anaesthetic, and there are very few contraindications. The complication rate (mainly stroke and death) is less than 3% at experienced centres.
- **Vascular surgery advice** — if there is complete occlusion (surgery is not beneficial for complete occlusion) or if imaging results are unclear
- **Cardiovascular risk reduction** — in all patients, regardless of whether surgery is planned. This includes:
  - Antiplatelet therapy (e.g., aspirin) or other antiplatelet agents
  - Antihypertensive medications
  - Statin therapy
  - Smoking cessation support

### Asymptomatic carotid artery stenosis

Your doctor will not refer to vascular surgery for asymptomatic stenosis. Management focuses on reducing your overall cardiovascular risk:

- Antiplatelet therapy (e.g., aspirin) or other antiplatelet agents
- Antihypertensive medications
- Statin therapy
- Smoking cessation support
