# Trigger Finger

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of trigger finger — a finger that becomes locked after flexion due to tendon or tendon sheath inflammation.

---

## Background

A trigger finger is a finger that becomes "locked" after it has been flexed. The cause is often unclear but is thought to be due to inflammation causing swelling of a tendon or tendon sheath.

Possible contributing factors include:
- Pre-existing conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, amyloidosis, diabetes, or carpal tunnel syndrome
- Repetitive hand activity, especially extensive gripping or use of tools that press on the palm

Most patients do not have any underlying conditions.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history and examine the affected finger, checking for:

- Difficulty straightening the finger without pulling on it
- A click heard when the finger is pulled straight
- Whether one or more fingers are affected
- A nodule at the base of the finger or thumb (may be tender on palpation; easier to detect with active flexion and extension)

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following steps, depending on severity:

### 1. Conservative Treatment (First-line)

Most cases will settle with rest:

- **Activity modification** — reducing gripping to allow inflammation to settle
- **Hand therapy / splinting** — if the patient is unable to rest appropriately (e.g., manual worker), a splint and exercise programme may be recommended

### 2. Steroid Injection (if not settled after 4–6 weeks, or earlier for severe locking)

If a single digit is involved and conservative treatment has not worked, your doctor may consider a corticosteroid injection:

- **Injection:** 10 mg triamcinolone with 0.5 mL plain lignocaine into the palmar surface of the hand, at the A1 pulley (base of the finger) and point of maximal tenderness or palpable nodule
- A finger splint may help rest the finger for a few days after the injection
- This treatment works in up to 75% of cases
- Pain relief may be effective for up to 3 months

Note: Radiology is not a prerequisite when a reasonable clinical diagnosis has been made and there are no signs of flexor sheath infection.

### 3. Referral for Hand Surgery Assessment

Your doctor may refer for surgical assessment if:

- Non-surgical treatment is not successful
- There is recurrence of triggering after successful treatment
- Multiple digits are involved

### 4. ACC Referral

If the condition is work-related, your doctor may consider an ACC referral.
