# Non-Fatal Strangulation and/or Suffocation

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of people who have experienced strangulation (pressure to the neck) and/or suffocation (obstruction to the upper airway). It outlines what a GP or primary care doctor will do during an assessment.

---

## Background

Strangulation (sometimes called choking) and suffocation are common forms of intimidation, particularly in family and intimate partner violence (IPV). They are now a separate criminal charge under New Zealand's Crimes Act 1961.

People who have been strangled face a high risk of serious injury. The risk of future homicide increases significantly for people previously strangled by their partners — by an estimated 800%.

Māori are overrepresented as victims of physical and/or sexual IPV. From a Māori perspective, all forms of violence are a violation of mana and tapu.

Many victims have minimal visible symptoms and signs following the event — only around 50% of strangulation cases show visible findings on initial examination.

---

## When to Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department if:

- Reduced level of consciousness, confusion, or a compromised airway
- Possible or actual loss of consciousness at the time of the event
- Current symptoms related to a possible or actual loss of consciousness
- Significant neck pain, difficulty swallowing (dysphagia), or difficulty speaking (dysarthria)
- Any other injuries or acute medical conditions requiring urgent attention

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you carefully and sensitively. Patients may be withdrawn or irritable, or have difficulty recalling events — this is common, and direct questions may be asked to help build a complete picture.

Your doctor will:

- Create a confidential and safe space for discussion
- Explain the limits of confidentiality (information may need to be shared if there is a serious and imminent risk of harm)
- Inform you that any further action — such as making a police report or seeking counselling — is your choice and can be taken whenever you are ready
- Use a professional interpreter if needed rather than a friend or relative

**History your doctor will take:**

- How the pressure to the neck or obstruction to the airway occurred (e.g. one hand, two hands, forearm, object, face or nose covered)
- Whether you could breathe properly
- How long the pressure or obstruction lasted
- How many times it occurred during the incident
- Whether you experienced: neck pain, headache, voice changes, hearing changes, vision changes, difficulty swallowing or talking, loss of consciousness, feeling faint, memory gaps, confusion, or loss of bladder or bowel control
- Any attempted self-protection (hitting, scratching, biting)

**Physical examination:**

Your doctor will examine you in good lighting. They will look for:

- Hoarse or raspy voice, cough
- Difficulty or pain on swallowing
- Redness or swelling of the neck
- Pinpoint bruises above the neck (scalp, face, eyelids, around the eyes, inside the mouth, ears)
- Scratches or grazes
- Bruising around the neck, under the chin, or behind the ears
- Any other injuries

**Documentation:**

Accurate documentation is important — police or courts may request medical records if charges are considered. Your doctor will record the time and date of the examination. With your consent, photographs may be taken if secure storage is available; if police are involved, police photography is recommended.

**If you are pregnant:**

Your doctor will also consider the wellbeing of the baby, as the fetus could be affected by reduced oxygen. They may check for fetal movements and heartbeat, and seek obstetric advice about whether further monitoring is needed.

**Safety assessment:**

Your doctor will consider your safety, particularly if this event occurred in the context of intimate partner violence or family harm. They may ask questions such as:

- Where is the person who harmed you now?
- Are you afraid to go home?
- Has the violence escalated in frequency or severity?
- Have children witnessed violence or been harmed?
- Does the person who harmed you make threats of homicide or suicide?
- Is there alcohol or substance use involved?

---

## Management

Management depends on your individual situation.

**Your doctor may:**

- Refer you to the Emergency Department if there are any concerns about consciousness, airway, or serious injury
- Record your injuries carefully with body diagrams and/or photographs (with your consent)
- Complete ACC documentation (event code for non-fatal strangulation/suffocation: NOS SN47z — Asphyxiation or strangulation)
- Check whether your home environment is safe
- Provide you with written information on managing strangulation and/or suffocation injuries
- If strangulation or suffocation occurred alongside a sexual assault, refer you to appropriate sexual assault services
- Refer you to community support services, and discuss any concerns about family safety
- Share clinical information with police or other agencies if necessary to prevent serious future harm — they will discuss this with you first wherever possible

**Regarding your privacy and legal proceedings:**

If police request a copy of your clinical notes, your doctor will seek your consent first. Your doctor is not expected to act as a forensic expert — any report they provide will be a factual account, not a specialist forensic opinion.
