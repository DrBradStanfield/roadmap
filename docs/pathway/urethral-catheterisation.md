# Urethral Catheterisation

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: August 2019.*

This pathway covers when a catheter may be passed through the urethra to drain the bladder, the alternatives, how it is inserted, and how it is cared for, changed, and removed.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any treatment decisions with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department if urethral trauma is possible.** This may follow a straddle injury (fall, kick, or cycling) or a fractured pelvis (car accident, fall, or crush), with:
- blood at the tip of the penis
- lower abdominal pain
- inability to pass urine
- or a collection of blood (haematoma) in the perineum, the area between the legs.

If urethral trauma is possible, a catheter should not be passed through the urethra. Hospital urology assessment is needed.

If a catheter cannot be inserted at the general practice or an urgent care clinic, emergency department assessment may be arranged.

---

## Background

A urethral catheter may be needed for:
- Acute urinary retention, for example with benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) or blood clots.
- Chronic retention, if it is linked to impaired kidney function or infection.
- A failed trial of void (a trial without the catheter).
- Incontinence. This is assessed carefully in light of the person's social situation. It may be useful for people who are terminally ill.

A suprapubic catheter (inserted through the lower abdomen) may be needed instead:
- After failed urethral catheterisation.
- For long-term management of a neuropathic bladder.
- After acute urethral trauma.

---

## Assessment

### Risk factors where specialist input may be needed

- Urethroplasty or radical prostatectomy in the past 6 weeks.
- Urethral trauma in the past 4 weeks.
- A known urethral stricture.
- A history of difficult catheterisation.
- Recent vaginal surgery.
- Spinal cord injury, which needs special consideration.

If you currently have a symptomatic urinary tract infection (UTI), your doctor may treat it with appropriate antibiotics.

### Alternatives to a permanent catheter

Before a permanent urethral catheter, your doctor may consider:

- **Intermittent catheterisation:** a catheter is inserted and removed several times a day to empty the bladder. It is an effective option for incomplete bladder emptying due to idiopathic or neurogenic bladder dysfunction. It suits people with adequate cognitive function, mobility, motivation, and manual dexterity.
- **Condom drainage** (also called urodomes, uridomes, or external sheaths): a convenient, non-invasive option for males with incontinence. A urology assessment with a kidney ultrasound should first confirm complete emptying and low pressure drainage, to reduce the risk of UTI and upper tract damage.
- **Suprapubic catheter:** may be considered for people who are sexually active or may become so, and for males and females who are likely to need a catheter long term.

## Management

### Where the catheter is inserted

- At the general practice, if the equipment and expertise are available.
- Otherwise at an urgent care clinic.
- If insertion fails in either setting, emergency department assessment may be arranged.

### Antibiotic prophylaxis

Antibiotics are not routinely given to prevent infection. Your doctor may prescribe a single dose before or at the time of catheter insertion or change if:
- you have had a symptomatic UTI or sepsis after a previous catheter change, or
- there was trauma during catheterisation, meaning frank blood in the urine afterwards or 2 or more attempts.

Options are a single dose of either:
- trimethoprim 300 mg, or
- ciprofloxacin 500 mg orally.

### What the procedure involves

- The procedure is explained and informed consent is obtained. Consent can be verbal and recorded in your notes.
- A chaperone, good lighting, and privacy are considered.
- Sterile technique and infection prevention steps are used, including hand hygiene.
- An antiseptic local anaesthetic lubricating gel (lidocaine with chlorhexidine) is placed into the urethra first.
- The catheter type depends on the reason for catheterisation. If you have a latex allergy, a silicone Foley catheter is used.
- The catheter is not forced if resistance is felt.
- Once urine drains, the catheter is advanced fully and its balloon is filled with sterile water. The volume of urine drained is recorded.
- In males, the foreskin is fully replaced afterwards.
- Your doctor may consider sending a urine sample for further tests.

If there is blood at the catheter tip after a failed attempt, this suggests a false passage. No further attempts are made and urology advice is sought. Moderate to heavy blood in the urine with possible clots may need a 3-way catheter and urgent urology advice.

### Catheter removal or change

Your doctor will make a clear plan for when the catheter is removed or changed:
- A trial of void is not done if the catheter was placed for urinary retention with obstructive uropathy and kidney failure.
- After acute retention in someone with prior BPH symptoms, your doctor may start an alpha-1-blocker and arrange catheter removal and a trial of void after 2 to 3 days.
- If retention was triggered by an acute event (such as constipation, medical illness, or surgery), the catheter may be removed after 3 to 5 days or once you have recovered.
- After trauma or a difficult catheterisation, the catheter may be left in place for up to 6 weeks.
- For long-term use, the first catheter change is planned for 6 weeks. Later changes depend on individual needs. Catheters may be left in for up to 3 months.
- For chronic urinary retention, or if a transurethral resection of the prostate (TURP) is planned, the catheter stays in until you are seen in a urology clinic.
- If the catheter is removed too early, your doctor will consider whether a trial of void is appropriate or whether it should be reinserted.
- If you need antibiotic prophylaxis, a supply may be organised for future catheter changes.

### Alpha-1-blockers after retention

Alpha blockers work by relaxing smooth muscle.
- **Doxazosin:** the usual dose is 2 mg at night for 7 days, then doubled every 2 weeks up to 8 mg at night. A lower starting dose, increased over several weeks, is used if you are elderly or have low blood pressure.
- **Tamsulosin** may be considered if a non-selective alpha blocker is not tolerated or not suitable. It is a selective alpha-1A-blocker and causes less postural hypotension (a drop in blood pressure on standing).
- Postural hypotension is more likely if you already take blood pressure medicine. That medicine may need to be reduced.

## Common Catheter Problems

### Leakage around the catheter

Leakage may be caused by catheter blockage, bladder spasm, or straining when opening the bowels.

**Blockage and encrustation:**
- Minerals and other material (such as mucus, protein, and bacteria) can build up on the catheter.
- This is common. It causes recurrent blockage in 40 to 50% of people with a long-term catheter. It is often made worse by *Proteus* bacteria, infection, and alkaline urine.
- Flushing the catheter may be tried, and you may be encouraged to drink more fluids.
- A bladder washout with warmed sterile saline may help.
- A larger bore catheter may be used, for example a silicone Foley 16 Fr.
- If blockages happen often, a pattern may be found (for example, near the end of a catheter's lifespan), and planned catheter changes may be made more frequent.

**Bladder spasm:** your doctor may consider an antispasmodic such as oxybutynin, used with caution in older people.

**Constipation** is treated.

### Catheter-related urinary tract infection

- A UTI in someone with a catheter is treated if there is fever, pain, or the person is systemically unwell. The catheter does not need to be changed.
- With an indwelling catheter, bacteria in the urine become almost inevitable: 5% per day, 50% by 1 week, and almost all by 1 month.
- If there are no symptoms, there is no reason for concern or treatment.
- A change in the look or smell of the urine is not a reason for antibiotics.
- Over time, a "biofilm" develops on the catheter. Antibiotics will not clear an infection while the catheter stays in, but they do increase antibiotic resistance.
- When a bacterium is identified, your doctor may check local antibiotic susceptibility patterns.

### Blood in the urine or new urinary symptoms

If you have an indwelling catheter and develop blood in the urine or a marked change in urinary symptoms, see your doctor. Other urological problems, including bladder cancer, may need to be considered.

### Ongoing care

Community nursing services may arrange catheter changes, catheter care, and catheter supplies.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may seek urology advice or arrange a urology assessment if:
- urethral trauma is possible (hospital assessment)
- you have had a urethroplasty or radical prostatectomy in the past 6 weeks, before a urethral catheter is inserted
- a suprapubic catheter is needed
- a 3-way catheter is needed for heavier bleeding with possible clots
- catheter insertion is unsuccessful
- phimosis (a tight foreskin) prevents catheterisation
- it is unclear when to try removing the catheter.

After recent vaginal surgery, your doctor may seek gynaecology or urology advice.
