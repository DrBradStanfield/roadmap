# Bronchiectasis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Bronchiectasis is a chronic lung condition where the airways (bronchi) become permanently widened and damaged. This leads to a cycle of infection, inflammation, and further airway damage. This pathway explains how your doctor diagnoses and manages it.

---

## About Bronchiectasis

- Bronchiectasis causes a **chronic productive cough**, repeated bacterial lung infections, airway inflammation, and flare-ups (exacerbations).
- It is **underdiagnosed** — delays in diagnosis are common because the symptoms overlap with asthma, COPD, sinusitis, reflux, and common respiratory infections.
- It is associated with reduced quality of life, increased hospital admissions, and — if poorly managed — increased mortality.
- It can affect adults and children of any age.

---

## What Causes Bronchiectasis?

In up to **50% of cases no cause is found** (idiopathic). In about **40% the cause is a past infection**, especially in childhood:

- Bacterial or viral pneumonia
- Whooping cough (pertussis)
- Tuberculosis
- Adenovirus or measles

It can also be associated with:

- Asthma and allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis (ABPA)
- Cystic fibrosis
- COPD
- Gastro-oesophageal reflux and aspiration
- Sinusitis
- Inflammatory bowel disease
- Rheumatoid arthritis or other connective tissue conditions
- Immune deficiency (especially low immunoglobulin levels)
- Congenital conditions (e.g. Kartagener's syndrome, mucociliary dysfunction)
- Past airway blockage (e.g. inhaled foreign body, especially in children)

## How Doctors Diagnose It

Your doctor will consider bronchiectasis if you have:

- **Chronic cough** — especially with daily sputum (phlegm) production, recurrent infection, and bacteria found in sputum cultures; symptoms persisting over many years; no history of smoking
- **Frequent antibiotic courses** for recurrent chest infections
- **COPD** with frequent or prolonged flare-ups
- **Unexplained haemoptysis** (coughing up blood, usually blood-streaked sputum)
- **Finger clubbing** (a sign of chronic lung disease)
- Māori or Pasifika background with persistent cough and sputum (higher risk population)

### Investigations Your Doctor May Arrange

- **Blood tests:**
  - Immunoglobulins (IgG, IgA, IgM) — to check for immune deficiency
  - Total IgE and Aspergillus serology (IgE and IgG) — to check for ABPA
- **Chest X-ray** — often abnormal but not usually diagnostic. A normal chest X-ray does **not** rule out bronchiectasis; it is mainly used to exclude other causes (e.g. cancer, tuberculosis).
- **Spirometry (breathing test):**
  - FEV1 below 50% predicted indicates severe disease and poor prognosis.
  - Results may be normal, obstructive, or restrictive.
- **Sputum sample** — taken when you are clinically stable (not during a flare-up), to identify bacteria at baseline. Three samples are also sent for mycobacterial culture (to check for non-tuberculous mycobacteria).
- **High resolution CT (HRCT) chest** — the gold standard test to confirm the diagnosis. This requires discussion with a respiratory specialist before it can be requested by your GP.

## Long-Term Management

Your doctor will tailor management to the underlying cause. Key principles:

### Sputum Clearance

Learning to clear sputum effectively is central to managing bronchiectasis. Your doctor may refer you to a **respiratory physiotherapist** for education and airway clearance techniques.

### Exercise and Rehabilitation

**Pulmonary rehabilitation** — a supervised exercise and education programme — improves quality of life, exercise capacity, and reduces cough and sputum volumes.

### Vaccinations

- **Influenza (flu) vaccine** — recommended every year.
- **Pneumococcal vaccine** — recommended (note: not publicly funded for adults with bronchiectasis; discuss with your doctor).

### Smoking

If you smoke, stopping is important. Your doctor can help with smoking cessation support.

### Inhaled Medications

- **Inhaled corticosteroids (ICS) are not recommended** for bronchiectasis unless you also have asthma or ABPA.
- **Short-acting bronchodilators** (reliever inhalers) may be used if you have breathlessness and spirometry shows airflow obstruction.
- **Long-acting bronchodilators** are generally not recommended unless you also have asthma or COPD.

## Managing Flare-Ups (Acute Exacerbations)

A flare-up is defined as **more than 48 hours of worsening** in at least 3 of these symptoms beyond your usual day-to-day variation:

- Increased cough
- Increased sputum volume or the sputum becoming darker/thicker
- Breathlessness
- Fatigue
- Coughing up blood (haemoptysis)

**Important:** When you are well, you may still cough daily and produce sputum, and sputum cultures may still grow bacteria. This is **not** an indication for antibiotics when you have no other symptoms of a flare-up.

### Antibiotic Treatment of Flare-Ups

Your doctor will use your previous sputum culture results to guide antibiotic choice.

**Updated October 2025 guidance (NZ Formulary):**

For **mild to moderate exacerbations**, treat for **10 to 14 days**:

- If *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* was found in recent sputum cultures: **oral ciprofloxacin** 500 mg twice daily (750 mg twice daily if you have cystic fibrosis)
- Otherwise (no recent bacteriology or other organisms): **amoxicillin + clavulanic acid** 625 mg three times daily as first choice, or **doxycycline** as an alternative for adults and adolescents
- If penicillin-allergic and no *Pseudomonas*: **roxithromycin** 300 mg once daily

**If no clinical improvement:** your doctor will adjust antibiotics once bacteria are identified and susceptibility results are available.

**Notes on antibiotic treatment:**
- Dual antibiotic combinations are generally not needed.
- Oral steroids are **not recommended** for bronchiectasis flare-ups unless you also have asthma or another eosinophilic airway condition.
- *P. aeruginosa* may be present as a coloniser without causing infection. It does not always require treatment when you are clinically stable — but its presence is a marker of poorer prognosis.

**If you are not improving or have severe symptoms:** your doctor will arrange hospital assessment for intravenous antibiotics.

### Sputum Clearance During Flare-Ups

Continue airway clearance techniques during a flare-up. If you are struggling, your doctor can refer you to a respiratory physiotherapist.

### Long-Term Antibiotics

If you have **3 or more flare-ups per year**, your doctor will refer you to a respiratory specialist to consider long-term antibiotics (usually oral macrolides such as azithromycin).

## When to Seek Medical Help

**Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if you have:**

- Large or persistent haemoptysis (significant coughing up of blood)
- Severe breathlessness or signs of serious illness
- A flare-up not responding to oral antibiotics

**See your doctor urgently if:**

- You are having a flare-up that is not improving on antibiotics within a few days
- You are coughing up blood (any amount — your doctor needs to assess this)
- You feel significantly more unwell than usual

---

## When Your Doctor Will Refer You to a Specialist

Your doctor will refer you to a respiratory specialist if:

- You have persistent or ongoing symptoms
- Spirometry shows moderate to severe airflow obstruction
- Your quality of life is significantly affected
- You are having 3 or more flare-ups per year despite appropriate treatment
- *P. aeruginosa* is found in your sputum
- Your doctor is considering long-term antibiotic therapy
- You have bronchiectasis associated with rheumatoid arthritis, immune deficiency, or inflammatory bowel disease
- You have significant upper airway symptoms (e.g. recurrent sinusitis, nasal polyps)
- Non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) are found in your sputum

*Note: Specialist assessment is also used to confirm diagnosis (HRCT chest) and for IV antibiotic treatment of severe flare-ups that do not respond to oral antibiotics.*
