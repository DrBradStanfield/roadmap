# Contraceptive Implant

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: January 2025.*

Contraceptive implants are progestogen-containing rods inserted under the skin of the arm. They give long-acting, reversible contraception.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to See Your Doctor

**See your doctor if:**
- The insertion site becomes red, hot, painful, or oozes pus. This can be an infection, which usually occurs within the first 2 months.
- You notice the implant starting to come out of your arm.
- You can no longer feel one or both rods. Use another form of contraception until your doctor has checked.
- Your bleeding pattern becomes bothersome. Treatment may help.
- You think you may be pregnant.
- Your implant has been in place for 5 years. It is treated as expired at that point.

---

## Background

- Long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) are recommended in New Zealand as a first-line choice for all eligible patients, because they work very well and are safe.
- The implant used in New Zealand is the levonorgestrel implant (Jadelle, two rods). Each rod contains 75 mg of levonorgestrel.
- It lasts 5 years and is approximately 99.5% effective against pregnancy for most users.
- It works mainly by preventing ovulation.
- It is safe to use while breastfeeding.
- It is available on prescription.
- The etonogestrel implant (Implanon NXT, one rod) contains 68 mg etonogestrel. It is used in Australia and elsewhere and may be seen in New Zealand. It lasts 3 years.

## Assessment

Your doctor will first talk through all your contraception options. If you choose the implant, they will take a history, check for reasons it may not suit you, and give you written information to help you decide.

### When the implant may not be suitable

**Not used if:**
- You are pregnant or might be pregnant.
- You have active breast cancer, or have had it within the last 5 years.

**Your doctor will weigh the risks carefully if you have:**
- Unexplained vaginal bleeding.
- A past history of breast cancer.
- Decompensated cirrhosis.
- Benign or malignant liver tumours.
- Heart disease or stroke that develops while you are using the implant. If it was already present before starting, the benefits generally outweigh the risks.

### Medicines that can make the implant less effective

Some medicines speed up liver enzymes and can reduce how well hormonal contraception works, including the implant, the combined pill, and the progestogen-only pill. Your doctor will check your current medicines. These include:
- **Anti-seizure medicines:** carbamazepine, phenytoin, oxcarbazepine, phenobarbital, primidone, rufinamide, eslicarbazepine, topiramate.
- **Antibiotics:** rifampicin, rifabutin.
- **HIV medicines:** nevirapine, nelfinavir, tipranavir ritonavir, efavirenz, etravirine.
- **Others:** St John's wort, bosentan, modafinil, aprepitant, sugammadex.

The effect takes 28 days to wear off, even after a single dose. Griseofulvin is also checked.

If you need the levonorgestrel emergency contraceptive pill while taking one of these medicines or griseofulvin, its effect is unclear. Your doctor may consider doubling the dose to 3 mg.

### Ruling out pregnancy

You are unlikely to be pregnant at the appointment if you have no signs of pregnancy and any of these apply:
- The appointment is within 5 days of the start of a normal period.
- The appointment is within 5 days of an abortion, miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, or treatment for gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD).
- You have not had sex since your last normal period, or since giving birth, an abortion, a miscarriage, an ectopic pregnancy, or treatment for GTD.
- You have been using a reliable contraceptive method consistently and correctly.
- You gave birth less than 21 days ago.
- You are fully breastfeeding, have no periods, **and** gave birth less than 6 months ago.
- You have not had sex for more than 21 days and have a negative urine pregnancy test.

Condoms have a high failure rate (18%), so they may not count as reliable when starting a new method. A urine pregnancy test can be falsely negative if you had unprotected sex in the last 21 days.

### Other checks

- Weight and blood pressure.
- An examination if you have abnormal bleeding or symptoms of a sexually transmitted infection (STI).
- Unexplained vaginal bleeding is investigated before insertion.
- Your cervical screening should be up to date.
- STI testing may be offered, especially if you are at higher risk.
- A pregnancy test, if needed.
- Whether a current implant has expired.

## Benefits, Downsides, and Side Effects

Your doctor will also discuss the cost of insertion and removal.

### Advantages

- Over 99% effective, with little for you to do.
- Lasts 5 years ("fit and forget").
- Can be used by people who cannot take estrogen or who get estrogen side effects.
- Can be used after giving birth and while breastfeeding.
- May be useful for people with conditions that affect absorption of medicines.
- Can be used by people with a past history of stroke, transient ischaemic attack (TIA), ischaemic heart disease, peripheral vascular disease, or several heart disease risk factors (smoking, obesity, diabetes, high blood pressure, abnormal cholesterol).
- Can be used by people with migraine with aura.
- May improve acne and period pain.
- No evidence of a significant drop in bone density (unlike the medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) contraceptive injection).
- No restriction for obesity, and no evidence that it causes weight change.
- No periods (in approximately 20% of users) may be seen as an advantage.

### Disadvantages and risks

- A procedure is needed to put it in and take it out.
- It can cause irregular bleeding. About 1 in 7 people ask to have it removed for this reason.
- It may be hard to remove, especially if it was inserted deeply.
- There is a small risk of the implant moving or coming out.
- It gives no protection against STIs.
- There may be initial costs for the prescription, device, and fitting.
- Some people may see having no periods as a downside.

### Risks of the procedures

- **Insertion:** bruising and discomfort for up to 1 week, a small scar, and a possible allergic reaction or infection.
- **Removal:** bruising and discomfort for up to 1 week, a small scar, and a possible infection. If a rod cannot be easily felt, removal may not be possible and you may need to see another provider.

### Changes in bleeding

Your normal bleeding pattern is unlikely to continue. Typically:
- Less than one-quarter have regular bleeds.
- One-third have infrequent bleeding.
- One-fifth have no bleeding.
- One-quarter have prolonged or frequent bleeding.

Changed bleeding patterns are likely to stay irregular. If bleeding becomes bothersome, treatment may help, or the implant can be removed.

### Other side effects

These are uncommon and usually mild. Less than 5% of implants are removed because of hormonal side effects.
- Acne may appear, improve, or worsen, but few people stop for this reason.
- A local reaction or scarring.
- Functional ovarian cysts.
- Some people report headache, mood changes, weight gain, breast tenderness, lower libido, or abdominal pain, but no causal link has been established.

### The fifth year

Effectiveness drops slightly in the fifth year:
- 99.2% if you weigh 60 kg or less.
- 98.9% if you weigh more than 60 kg.

Early replacement is not routinely recommended, but your doctor may discuss it and offer an earlier replacement. Using the implant beyond 5 years is not recommended.

## Management

Implants should only be inserted or removed by a clinician trained to do so. If your doctor is not trained, they may arrange this with an experienced colleague.

### Before insertion

- Abnormal uterine bleeding is managed first.
- If you take a liver enzyme-inducing medicine or griseofulvin, use extra precautions or another method while taking it and for 28 days afterwards.

### Timing of insertion

Ideally, the implant goes in between days 1 and 5 of your menstrual cycle, so it works straight away. It can go in at other times if pregnancy can be reasonably ruled out, using the **quick start method**:

- **First day of your last period was 1 to 5 days ago:** the implant goes in today. It works immediately and no extra precautions are needed.
- **First day of your last period was more than 5 days ago, or you have no periods:**
  - A baseline pregnancy test is done. Even if it is negative, an early pregnancy is possible if you have had unprotected sex since your last period. The implant is not known to affect a fetus, though this cannot be guaranteed.
  - If you had unprotected sex in the last 5 days, your doctor may offer levonorgestrel emergency contraception.
  - The implant goes in today.
  - Avoid sex or use condoms for 7 days.
  - Repeat a pregnancy test in 4 weeks, even if you have had bleeding.

### Special situations

- **Switching from another method:** your doctor will advise whether extra precautions are needed.
- **Late for replacement:** the implant is treated as expired once it has been in for 5 years. If you do not want to be pregnant, it needs replacing or another method started. Your doctor may consider a pregnancy test and emergency contraception. A new implant follows the quick start method.
- **After levonorgestrel emergency contraception:** the implant can go in straight away. Avoid sex or use condoms for 7 days after insertion.
- **After a copper IUD used for emergency contraception:** if the IUD is being removed and the implant goes in at least 7 days before that, no extra precautions are needed. Otherwise, avoid sex or use condoms for 7 days after insertion. Repeat a pregnancy test 4 weeks after insertion, even if you have had bleeding.
- **After abortion, miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, or treatment for GTD:** the implant can go in at any time. If inserted fewer than 5 days after, it works immediately with no extra precautions. If 5 or more days after, the quick start method is used once pregnancy is reasonably ruled out.
- **After giving birth:** half of first periods after birth are preceded by ovulation.
  - An immediate insertion is preferably done before leaving the birthing unit.
  - If inserted less than 21 days after birth, it works immediately with no extra precautions.
  - If inserted 21 or more days after birth, the quick start method is used once pregnancy is reasonably ruled out.
  - If you are breastfeeding, the implant can go in when it suits you. Your doctor may consider waiting until your milk comes in, about 72 hours after the placenta is delivered.

### If pregnancy cannot be ruled out

- The implant is not known to affect a fetus, but this cannot be guaranteed.
- Your doctor may consider emergency contraception if you had unprotected sex within the last 5 days.

### Getting the implant

- Your doctor may prescribe the implant and a local anaesthetic.
- Take your prescription to the pharmacy at least a few days before your fitting appointment, as most pharmacies need to order implants.
- Bring the device with you to the fitting appointment.

## After Insertion

### Infection at the insertion site

Signs are redness, heat, pain, or pus. It usually occurs within the first 2 months.
- The implant does not need to come out.
- An abscess may need to be drained.
- Your doctor may give oral antibiotics for 7 to 10 days, then check that the infection has cleared.
- If it has not cleared, the implant is removed.
- The implant can partly or fully come out after an infection, so return if you notice it coming out.

### Implant coming out (expulsion)

Expulsion is rare. It usually happens within a few months of insertion or with an infection. If there is no infection, a new implant may be inserted through a new cut near the old site.
- One rod alone does not give contraceptive cover and could increase the risk of ectopic pregnancy.
- If both rods are not in place, use a different method until both rods are in place or another regular method is established.

### Irregular bleeding

Irregular bleeding is best treated early. Your doctor will first consider other causes, such as pregnancy, an STI (especially chlamydia), or a problem with the cervix or uterus, and investigate as needed.

If there is no reason you cannot use them, your doctor may consider:
- The combined oral contraceptive pill, cyclically or continuously, for 3 months (outside the product licence).
- Estradiol 1 mg daily for three weeks a month. Your doctor may consider stopping after 6 months to check for improvement. It can continue for the full 5 years if needed.
- A COX-2 inhibitor or anti-inflammatory (NSAID) taken regularly for 5 to 10 days, if suitable. For example, celecoxib 100 to 200 mg twice a day or ibuprofen 400 mg two to three times a day.
- Medroxyprogesterone acetate 10 mg a day for three out of four weeks.

These medicines can be used throughout the life of the implant and it still works very well. If bleeding does not settle, your doctor will look again for a cause and may consider removing the implant.

### A rod you cannot feel

- Use another form of contraception, and your doctor may consider emergency contraception.
- For the two-rod levonorgestrel implant, an X-ray of the arm with the insertion site marked can find the rods.
- If both rods are in a suitable position, they can stay until they are due out, and you can rely on them for contraception. Rods deeper than just under the skin can still be relied on.
- A rod buried in muscle has a higher risk of moving further. A rod that gets into the basilic vein may move to the armpit or chest, so removal under ultrasound guidance may be considered.
- If a rod cannot be seen in the arm on X-ray, further imaging is arranged. A chest X-ray is likely to be next.
- The single-rod etonogestrel implant does not show on X-ray, so other imaging is needed.
- When removal is planned for a rod that cannot be felt, ultrasound is normally used to find it and guide removal.

### Pregnancy with an implant

This is very rare. It is usually due to an early pregnancy that was not detected at the time of insertion.
- Your doctor will assess how far along the pregnancy is and rule out an ectopic pregnancy.
- If you decide to continue the pregnancy, the implant is removed. There is no medical reason to recommend an abortion because the fetus was exposed to levonorgestrel.
- If the pregnancy began before the implant was inserted or became effective, and you choose an abortion, you may keep the implant.

### A short course of an enzyme-inducing medicine

Avoid sex, or use condoms or other extra contraception, while taking the medicine and for 28 days afterwards.

---

## Removal and Return of Fertility

- Most people ovulate within 3 weeks of removal, so fertility returns quickly.
- Any hormonal side effects settle quickly after removal.
- If you do not want to become pregnant, start another method straight after removal.

## Key Concepts for Patients

- The implant is one of the most effective contraceptives and lasts 5 years.
- Your bleeding pattern will likely change. Treatment can help if it bothers you.
- Some medicines can make it less effective. Tell your doctor about everything you take, including St John's wort.
- If you cannot feel a rod or the implant starts to come out, use other contraception and see your doctor.
- Always discuss any new symptoms or medication questions with your doctor before making changes.
