# Mpox

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment, management, and prevention of mpox (monkeypox) in primary care.

---

## Background

Mpox is an illness caused by an orthopoxvirus. There are two genetically and clinically distinct clades:
- **Clade I** (subclades Ia and Ib) — endemic to Central Africa. More likely to spread via non-sexual close contact; more prevalent among women and children.
- **Clade II** (subclades IIa and IIb) — endemic to West Africa.

A global outbreak of Clade IIb began in 2022. While cases have declined, Clade IIb transmission persists globally and disproportionately affects gay, bisexual, and other men who have sex with men (GBMSM) and their sexual networks.

In 2024, the WHO declared mpox a public health emergency of international concern due to an upsurge in Clade I cases in the Democratic Republic of the Congo (DRC). Sporadic Clade I cases have appeared outside Africa, predominantly in returned travellers, with no sustained secondary transmission reported.

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess for mpox if you meet clinical and epidemiological criteria — you may have a flu-like illness with or without a rash, or a rash without prior flu-like symptoms.

### Clinical Criteria

One or more acute unexplained skin or mucosal lesion(s), or proctitis (anorectal pain, bleeding, discharge).

### Epidemiological Criteria (at least one in the 21 days before symptom onset)

- Close contact with a confirmed or probable mpox case
- Travel to an area where mpox is endemic or where there is sustained human-to-human transmission
- Intimate or sexual contact with GBMSM, or with individuals at an event linked to mpox cases (e.g. Pride events)
- Being in a priority group (see below)

### Priority Groups for Higher Vigilance

Both Clade I and II:
- GBMSM engaged in high-risk sexual activities, and their sexual contacts
- Sex workers
- Individuals with medical risk factors for severe disease:
  - Unvaccinated
  - Immunocompromised (e.g. poorly controlled HIV with CD4 < 350 cells/microlitre, or on immunosuppressive therapy — HIV-positive people with normal CD4 count are not considered immunocompromised)
  - Pregnant
  - Infants and young children
- People who routinely interact with individuals in the above groups

Clade I additionally:
- People in shared accommodation settings (e.g. prisons, homeless shelters, halls of residence, aged care or disability facilities) or larger communal/intergenerational households
- People who routinely interact closely with children (healthcare workers, carers, early childhood educators)

### Typical Presentation

- **Incubation period:** 1 to 2 weeks
- **Prodromal phase** (up to 5 days): fever, chills, myalgia, arthralgia, backache, lymphadenopathy, sore throat, exhaustion
- **Rash or lesions** (usually 1 to 3 days after fever): typically beginning on the face and spreading; may also affect palms, soles, and mucous membranes. Stages: macular → papular → vesicular → pustular. Crusting begins 14 to 21 days after rash onset; scabs fall off leaving dyspigmented scars.

**Cases are infectious** from symptom onset until all lesions have fully crusted and scabs have fallen off (typically 2 to 4 weeks). Scabs may still contain infectious virus.

### Atypical Presentations (Clade II, especially if vaccinated)

- Lesions may be limited to the anogenital area or oropharynx
- May present with proctitis (rectal pain, bleeding), urethritis (urethral pain, discharge, dysuria), or oropharyngeal symptoms (sore throat, pharyngitis, tonsillitis) without visible lesions
- Very few lesions (even a single lesion), or no progression through multiple stages
- Fever and prodromal symptoms may be absent or appear after lesions

Cases with proctitis only (no visible lesions) should be considered infectious for 21 days after diagnosis, or until symptoms resolve.

### Complications to Look For

Your doctor will examine you for complications, which may require hospitalisation:
- Sepsis
- Severe pain
- Cellulitis (e.g. genital)
- Severe proctitis
- Dehydration from vomiting and diarrhoea
- Parapharyngeal abscess
- Bronchopneumonia or pneumonitis
- Encephalitis
- Severe dysphagia from mouth lesions
- Eye complications: conjunctivitis, blepharitis, keratitis, vision loss
- Pregnancy complications: miscarriage, stillbirth, congenital mpox

### Alternative Diagnoses Your Doctor Will Consider

- Syphilis
- Measles
- Herpes zoster or simplex
- Chickenpox
- Bacterial skin infections
- Molluscum contagiosum
- Allergic reactions
- Scabies
- Hand, foot, and mouth disease
- Enterovirus

## Testing

If mpox is suspected, your doctor will collect swabs using appropriate PPE. Self-collected swabs are not appropriate — do not go to a community testing centre.

**What is tested:**
- Multiple swabs (up to 3) from different skin lesions
- Throat and/or rectal swabs if lesions are present
- Additional swabs for alternative diagnoses if strongly suspected

Your doctor will note "possible mpox" on the request and include relevant travel and contact history.

**Mpox is a notifiable disease** — your doctor is required to notify the Medical Officer of Health when mpox is suspected or confirmed.

## Management

Management of mpox is directed by your doctor. This is a notifiable condition requiring coordination with Public Health.

### Antiviral Treatment: Tecovirimat

Tecovirimat is an oral antiviral that can reduce the severity of mpox. It is an unapproved medicine in New Zealand but is fully funded if eligibility criteria are met.

**Your doctor may prescribe tecovirimat if all of the following apply:**
- Mpox is microbiologically confirmed, or clinically highly likely
- Signs of active disease are present
- You are at risk of severe disease (unvaccinated, immunocompromised, pregnant, infant/young child), OR you have lesions in high-risk areas (e.g. extensive, painful periocular or perianal lesions)

Prescribing requires approval from an infectious diseases or sexual health specialist. Prescriptions can be filled at any pharmacy.

### Supportive Care by Symptom

Your doctor will tailor treatment to your symptoms:

- **Eye symptoms (conjunctivitis):** Stop contact lenses and eye make-up until fully recovered. Lubricating eye drops for severe irritation; cold compresses for eyelid swelling. Eye pain, decreased vision, or corneal changes require urgent specialist assessment.
- **Diarrhoea:** Conservative management. Anti-motility agents (e.g. loperamide) should not be used.
- **Difficulty urinating:** Sitting in a warm bath may help. Urinary retention requires catheterisation and urology input.
- **Dyspepsia:** Proton-pump inhibitor.
- **Nausea and vomiting:** Antiemetics (e.g. ondansetron, promethazine).
- **Pain:** NSAIDs or paracetamol; opiates for severe pain.
- **Painful oral lesions:** Salt-water mouth washes 4 to 6 times daily; local anaesthetic rinse (e.g. Xylocaine Viscous or Mucosoothe) every 3 hours; ice chips.
- **Penile swelling:** Cold compresses and analgesia for mild-to-moderate swelling. Severe swelling, phimosis, or paraphimosis requires urgent urology advice.
- **Proctitis:**
  - Topical corticosteroid/anaesthetic (e.g. Proctosedyl or Ultraproct) twice daily after bowel movements
  - Stool softeners
  - Lidocaine gel as required
  - Sitz baths: 10 cm warm water, 15 minutes, 3 to 4 times daily (add salt or Epsom salts if desired; clean bath with dilute bleach after use)
- **Itching (pruritis):** Antihistamines and topical anti-itch preparations.

Mpox is usually a self-limiting illness lasting 2 to 4 weeks.

### Isolation and Transmission Prevention

**Clade I:**
- Suspected cases and symptomatic close contacts should isolate until directed by Public Health.
- Wear a surgical mask when in the same room as others, even if no oral lesions or pharyngitis are present.

**Clade II:**
- Isolation is not required, but restrictions to prevent transmission apply until no longer considered infectious.

**For both clades:**
- Avoid intimate or sexual contact with others (including intimate touching and use of sex toys)
- Cover all skin lesions when around others

**How mpox spreads** (to inform precautions):
- Direct skin-to-skin contact with rash, lesions, or scabs
- Contact with bodily fluids (saliva, nasal mucus, semen, vaginal fluids, blood)
- Contact with contaminated materials (bed linens, towels, clothing, healthcare equipment)
- Vertical transmission during pregnancy
- Animal-to-human transmission
- Respiratory transmission during prolonged face-to-face contact or aerosol-generating procedures

Public Health will follow up with and provide advice to all cases and contacts.

## Close Contact Guidance

### Clade I Close Contacts

You are a close contact if, during the infectious period, you had:
- Sexual or intimate contact (including kissing) with the case
- Direct skin or mucous membrane contact with the case
- Contact with contaminated materials without appropriate PPE
- Time in an enclosed space with a Clade I mpox case

**What to do:**
- Get post-exposure vaccination (ideally within 4 days, up to 14 days after exposure)
- Monitor for symptoms for 21 days from last exposure
- Avoid intimate or sexual contact with others for 21 days
- Avoid attending/working in high-priority settings, schools, or early childhood education centres
- Contact Public Health if symptoms develop

Isolation is generally not required.

### Clade II Close Contacts

You are a close contact if, during the infectious period, you had:
- Direct contact with skin lesions or mucous membranes through close physical, intimate, or sexual contact
- Contact with contaminated materials (bed linens, towels, clothing, healthcare equipment)
- Being within 1.5 metres of a case during aerosol-generating procedures
- A sharps injury

**What to do:**
- Get post-exposure vaccination
- Monitor for symptoms for 21 days from last exposure
- Avoid intimate or sexual contact with others for 21 days
- Contact Public Health if symptoms develop

Isolation and exclusion are generally not required.

## Mpox Vaccination

The mpox vaccine (JYNNEOS/MVA-BN/Imvamune/Imvanex) is provisionally approved in New Zealand.

**Schedule:** 0.5 mL, with a second dose recommended after a minimum of 28 days (can be given up to 2 years after the first dose).

### Pre-exposure Vaccination — Who Is Eligible

- GBMSM, transgender, or non-binary people who in the past 6 months have had:
  - A new sexually transmitted infection diagnosis
  - More than one sexual partner
  - Sex at a commercial venue
  - Sex at a large public event in an area of likely mpox transmission
- People living with HIV who are at risk of mpox exposure
- Sexual partners of people at increased risk
- Occupational risk: laboratory staff working with orthopoxviruses, healthcare workers with repeated mpox patient exposure, sex workers with at-risk clients
- People at risk during a local outbreak (as determined by a medical officer of health)
- People who anticipate any of the above circumstances

Pre-exposure vaccination is also recommended for adolescents under 18 who meet the above high-risk criteria (requires off-label consent).

The vaccine is not available purely for travel to affected areas unless you independently meet the high-risk criteria.

### Post-exposure Prophylaxis

- Ideally given within 4 days of exposure; effective up to 14 days after exposure
- If outside the 14-day window, vaccination is still offered if there is ongoing risk of exposure or if other eligibility criteria are met

**Note:** Your GP is not automatically notified by the Aotearoa Immunisation Register (AIR) when you receive the mpox vaccine. Overseas vaccinations can be retrospectively entered in AIR or your practice management system.

## When to Seek Urgent Help

Contact your doctor promptly or seek emergency care if you develop:
- Eye pain, decreased vision, or discharge with ocular lesions
- Inability to swallow or breathe normally
- Signs of severe infection (high fever, rapid deterioration)
- Urinary retention (inability to pass urine)
- Severe penile swelling
