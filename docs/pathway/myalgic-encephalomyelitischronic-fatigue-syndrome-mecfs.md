# Myalgic Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (ME/CFS)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of ME/CFS — a debilitating, chronic multisystem illness. Diagnosis and management of ME/CFS is complex and should be done with your doctor.

---

## Red Flag — See Your Doctor Urgently

See your doctor promptly if you have:

- Marked unintentional weight loss

This should be investigated to exclude other causes before attributing it to ME/CFS.

---

## Background

- ME/CFS is a debilitating, chronic multisystem disease with a wide range of symptoms.
- Diagnosis is made on clinical criteria, in the absence of alternative diagnoses.
- ME/CFS affects an estimated 2–4 per 1,000 people, with women more commonly affected than men (approximately 3:1). Many people never receive a diagnosis.
- The exact cause is unknown. Research shows pathophysiological changes in the immune system, cellular metabolism, neuroendocrine system, and cardiovascular regulation, though it is unclear whether these precede or result from the illness.
- People with ME/CFS have often experienced prejudice and disbelief, and may have lost trust in healthcare services.
- Recovery is more common in the first 2 years and becomes less likely beyond this. Prognosis is generally better for those diagnosed early and given appropriate treatment and support.

## Diagnosis

### Key Features Your Doctor Will Look For

Your doctor will take a detailed history focusing on the following key features of ME/CFS:

**1. Fatigue**
Overwhelming physical and mental fatigue and loss of stamina which substantially reduces activity level in all aspects of life.

**2. Post-Exertional Malaise (PEM)**
Worsening of symptoms after physical, mental, or emotional exertion that would not have caused a problem before the illness. Key characteristics:
- Symptoms typically worsen 12–48 hours after the activity or exposure
- PEM often causes a relapse lasting days, weeks, or even longer
- The severity and duration of symptoms are out of proportion to the inciting event
- For some patients, sensory overload (light and sound) can induce PEM

**3. Unrefreshing Sleep**
Sleep that does not restore energy; disturbances in sleep quality or rhythm are common.

**4. Cognitive Impairment ("Brain Fog")**
Problems with thinking, memory, executive function, information processing, or attention.

**5. Orthostatic Intolerance**
Worsening of symptoms when upright, including light-headedness, fainting, increased fatigue, cognitive worsening, headaches, and nausea.

### IOM 2015 Diagnostic Criteria

Your doctor may use the Institute of Medicine (IOM) 2015 Diagnostic Criteria. A diagnosis of ME/CFS requires:

**All three of:**
1. Substantial reduction in ability to engage in pre-illness levels of activity, persisting for more than 6 months, accompanied by fatigue (not caused by excessive exertion, not substantially alleviated by rest)
2. Post-exertional malaise (PEM)
3. Unrefreshing sleep

**Plus at least one of:**
- Cognitive impairment
- Orthostatic intolerance

These symptoms should be present at least half the time at moderate, substantial, or severe intensity.

### Severity

**Moderate ME/CFS:** Reduced mobility; some ability to self-care and carry out daily activities with adjustments.

**Severe ME/CFS:**
- Unable to carry out any activities, or minimal daily tasks only (e.g. brushing teeth)
- Severe cognitive difficulties
- May be dependent on a wheelchair
- Often unable to leave the house; may spend most of the time in bed
- Often extremely sensitive to light and sound

**Very Severe ME/CFS:**
- Bed-bound and dependent on care
- Needs help with personal hygiene and eating
- Very sensitive to sensory stimuli

### What Your Doctor Will Examine and Test

Your doctor will perform an examination (usually normal unless another condition is present) and may arrange baseline blood tests:

- Blood count, CRP, ferritin
- Electrolytes, kidney and liver function, calcium, magnesium, blood glucose/HbA1c
- Thyroid function
- Coeliac antibodies
- Vitamin D
- B12 (if clinically indicated)
- If associated with a viral illness: Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, HIV
- If symptoms suggest inflammation: ANA, rheumatoid factor, creatine kinase, cortisol
- ECG and chest X-ray if palpitations, chest pain, or breathlessness

Your doctor will also check for **orthostatic intolerance** by measuring lying and standing heart rate and blood pressure, and may perform a NASA Lean Test.

**NASA Lean Test:**
1. Lie down for 15 minutes; baseline heart rate and blood pressure recorded
2. Stand upright with feet 20 cm from a wall, shoulders leaning back against it
3. Heart rate and blood pressure measured every minute for 9 minutes

A significant result: heart rate increase >30 bpm above baseline sustained throughout (>40 bpm in those under 19), or systolic blood pressure drop of ≥25 mmHg.

## Management

Management of ME/CFS is a developing field. Your doctor will work with you to tailor treatment to your symptoms and severity.

### Validation

Your doctor should acknowledge that ME/CFS is a genuine, often debilitating illness. If you have previously encountered disbelief, it is reasonable to raise this.

### Pacing — Managing Post-Exertional Malaise

Pacing is the most important self-management strategy for ME/CFS. It involves identifying the threshold at which exertion triggers PEM, and keeping daily activity within that "energy envelope."

Your doctor or a health professional can help you:
- Identify which activities (physical and cognitive) trigger your symptoms
- Balance energy across social, physical, cognitive, and daily living activities with adequate rest
- Remain as active as possible without triggering PEM, and cautiously increase activity only when energy levels genuinely improve

Practical strategies:
- Keep an activity/symptom diary to track triggers and patterns
- Use tools to monitor or save energy: step counters, heart rate monitors, shower chairs
- Divide activities into smaller tasks with rest breaks
- Alternate between cognitive and physical tasks
- Notice early warning signs that rest is needed (e.g. muscle fatigue, raised resting heart rate)

### Physical Activity and Exercise

There is no evidence that exercise cures or improves ME/CFS. Symptom-contingent physical activity (staying within your energy limits) can help maintain fitness and muscle tone.

**Important:** Graded exercise therapy (GET) — a previously recommended approach — is no longer supported by current ME/CFS guidelines due to concerns about potential harm.

If you regularly exercise, watch for signs of deterioration: more frequent PEM, increased resting heart rate, longer sleep, fewer daily steps.

### Sleep

- Good sleep hygiene helps but is unlikely to fully resolve unrefreshing sleep in ME/CFS.
- Focus on a quiet, dark, appropriately temperatured sleep environment; consistent routines; avoiding caffeine and alcohol.
- Natural light exposure during the day may help with delayed sleep onset.
- Your doctor may consider low-dose tricyclic antidepressants (e.g. amitriptyline or nortriptyline 5 mg at night) for insomnia and pain.
- Melatonin (1–2 mg, one hour before bed, titrating up) may be trialled, though there is no evidence it improves sleep in ME/CFS specifically.
- Patients with ME/CFS are often more sensitive to medication side-effects; doses are started very low and increased slowly.

### Orthostatic Intolerance and POTS

**Non-medication strategies your doctor may recommend:**
- Avoid prolonged lying flat — sit upright in bed as tolerated
- Raise the head of your bed by 20 cm
- Eat small, regular meals with smaller carbohydrate portions; avoid weight loss
- Increase fluid intake — starting at 2 L/day, potentially up to 4 L/day; drink a glass of water before any orthostatic challenge (getting up, going out, showering)
- Increase salt intake if blood pressure is normal or low — up to 5 g/day may be needed
- Avoid long or hot showers; avoid prolonged standing or sitting
- Wear compression garments (ideally toe-to-waist)
- Try recumbent exercise (e.g. swimming, recumbent cycling)
- Learn counter-pressure manoeuvres

**Medication (if lifestyle measures are insufficient):**

Your doctor may consider medication if orthostatic symptoms do not improve after trying lifestyle changes. All treatments for POTS are off-label. Options include:

- **Fludrocortisone** — increases blood volume by retaining salt and water. Started at 50 micrograms daily (max 100 micrograms). Blood pressure and electrolytes checked after 2 weeks. Must not be stopped suddenly — dose tapered gradually.
- **Beta blockers** — propranolol is preferred for POTS (10 mg twice daily, increasing to three times daily if needed). Bisoprolol 1.25 mg daily if propranolol not tolerated. Not used in asthma.
- **SSRIs (e.g. citalopram)** — serotonin helps with orthostatic vasoconstriction. Started at 20 mg daily.
- **Midodrine** — causes vasoconstriction; 2.5 mg three times daily, titrating up. Maximum 30 mg/day. Blood pressure monitored.
- **Pyridostigmine bromide** — improves heart rate control; 30–60 mg three times daily. Gastrointestinal side-effects common.

Your doctor should manage these medications. Always discuss before making changes.

### Pain

- Low-dose tricyclic antidepressants (e.g. amitriptyline or nortriptyline 5 mg at night) can help with both pain and insomnia.
- **Low-dose naltrexone** is used off-label by some doctors for ME/CFS pain. It is not funded and must be sourced from a compounding pharmacy. Starting dose 0.5–1 mg daily, titrating slowly up to a typical dose of 3–5 mg daily (maximum 5 mg). Should not be used with opioids.
- Your doctor can discuss these options and what is appropriate for your situation.

### Psychological Support

Psychological support is not the same as saying your symptoms are "in your head." Counselling, mindfulness, and therapy can help with coping strategies for living with a chronic illness, and may help prevent "push-through" behaviour that triggers PEM.

If you need mental health support, ask your doctor for a referral to appropriate services or a private practitioner.

Note: The Lightning Process and similar therapies are not recommended by NICE (UK) guidelines for ME/CFS.

### Nutrition

- Eat from all four food groups daily and drink plenty of fluids.
- Choose lower glycaemic index (GI) carbohydrates (e.g. oats, wholegrain bread, legumes, basmati rice) for more sustained energy.
- Batch-cook and freeze meals for lower-energy days.
- Limit caffeine (tea, coffee, cola, energy drinks), especially from early afternoon.
- Restrictive diets (e.g. ketogenic) are not supported by evidence in ME/CFS and can cause nutritional deficiencies.

If you have concerns about your nutrition or are losing weight unintentionally, ask your doctor about a referral to a dietitian.

### Supplements and Complementary Medicine

- **Vitamin D:** People with ME/CFS — especially those who are housebound — may be deficient. Your doctor may recommend supplementation.
- **Magnesium** (at night for cramps), **co-enzyme Q10** (for muscle fatigue), and **B12 injections** are commonly used by patients, though there is no high-quality evidence supporting them.
- There is no credible evidence for hypnosis, chiropractic, homeopathy, anti-Candida drugs, or thyroid supplements (if thyroid function is normal).
- There is limited evidence that massage may be helpful.

Discuss any supplements or complementary therapies with your doctor, particularly regarding cost, effort, and potential interactions.

### Antidepressants

- Antidepressants are **not** recommended as a general treatment for ME/CFS — they have not shown significant benefit.
- If you develop depression alongside ME/CFS, your doctor may consider an SSRI (preferred, as it is less likely to worsen fatigue or orthostatic symptoms).
- Given symptom overlap with depression and fibromyalgia, a trial of antidepressants is sometimes considered where symptoms overlap significantly.
- Note that antidepressants can worsen fatigue in some ME/CFS patients and should be stopped if there is no improvement within 6–8 weeks.

### Vaccinations

Most people with ME/CFS tolerate vaccinations normally. A small number experience temporary worsening of symptoms.

- There is no evidence that any vaccination causes ME/CFS.
- Your doctor may time vaccinations for when you are relatively well, with 2–3 days of planned rest afterwards.
- For COVID-19: vaccination reduces the risk of severe illness and long COVID, which can trigger ME/CFS flares.

### Work, Education, and Daily Living

- Your doctor can support you with letters for employers or educational institutions to facilitate flexible arrangements.
- If you are struggling with daily self-care, your doctor can refer you for home occupational therapy assessment or needs assessment for community support.
- Financial support (disability allowance, supported living payment) and mobility aids (parking permits) may be available — discuss with your doctor.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you for specialist assessment if:

- There is marked unintentional weight loss or significant diagnostic uncertainty (general medicine assessment)
- You are bed-bound and need passive physiotherapy to maintain range of motion
- You need occupational therapy or needs assessment for home support
- POTS medications are being considered and your doctor wants specialist input (general medicine advice)
- Mental health support is needed beyond what primary care can provide
