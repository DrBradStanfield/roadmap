# Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the diagnosis and management of IBS — how doctors make the diagnosis, what symptoms to watch for, and the range of dietary, lifestyle, and medication treatments available.

> **Note:** This information is for educational purposes. Always discuss your symptoms and any treatment changes with your doctor or healthcare provider.

---

## Red Flags — See Your Doctor

Tell your doctor promptly if you have any of the following — these require investigation to rule out serious causes:

- Age > 50 years at first presentation of bowel symptoms
- Rectal bleeding
- Unexplained iron deficiency anaemia
- Unintentional weight loss

---

## What Is IBS?

IBS is a relapsing and remitting functional bowel disorder characterised by chronic abdominal pain and altered bowel habits without an identifiable organic cause. It affects 10–15% of the population.

The exact cause is not fully understood. Contributing factors include:
- Post-infectious changes (e.g., after Campylobacter gastroenteritis)
- Food sensitivities
- Changes in gut bacteria (altered faecal microbiota)
- Visceral hypersensitivity — the bowel is more sensitive to normal stimuli, producing more pain than in people without IBS
- Altered gut motility and intestinal transit
- Psychosocial factors and chronic stress (acute stress can also trigger flare-ups)
- Depression and anxiety

### Rome IV Diagnostic Criteria

A diagnosis of IBS requires:

**Recurrent abdominal pain at least 1 day per week in the last 3 months**, associated with 2 or more of:
- Related to defecation (e.g., pain relieved by a bowel motion)
- Associated with a change in frequency of stools
- Associated with a change in form (appearance) of stools

Symptoms must have been present for the last 3 months with onset at least 6 months before diagnosis.

The absence of the full Rome IV criteria does not exclude IBS — the symptom pattern can change over time.

### Subtypes of IBS

| Subtype | Description |
|---------|-------------|
| IBS-C (constipation-predominant) | Abnormal bowel movements are mostly constipation |
| IBS-D (diarrhoea-predominant) | Abnormal bowel movements are mostly diarrhoea |
| IBS-M (mixed) | Both constipation and diarrhoea each account for > 25% of abnormal movements |
| Unclassified IBS | Meets IBS criteria but does not fit a subtype |

## How Your Doctor Makes the Diagnosis

IBS is diagnosed on the basis of symptoms and exclusion of other causes. There is no single definitive test.

### Symptoms Your Doctor Will Ask About

- Frequency, severity, and triggers of bowel symptoms
- What makes symptoms better or worse
- Impact on quality of life
- Family history of colorectal cancer, ovarian cancer, coeliac disease, or inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)
- Previous cholecystectomy (gallbladder removal) or bowel surgery — risk factors for bile salt malabsorption
- Dietary habits, fluid intake, and known food triggers
- Medications (metformin and proton-pump inhibitors can affect bowel habit)
- Physical activity, alcohol use, recent travel

**Other common IBS symptoms include:**
- Bloating or abdominal distension
- Feeling of incomplete bowel emptying
- Urgency, straining, or mucus in stools
- Fatigue, anxiety, and depression

### Features That Suggest Something Other Than IBS

Your doctor will check for "concerning features" that are not consistent with IBS:

- Age > 50 years at first presentation
- Rectal bleeding
- Unexplained iron deficiency anaemia or weight loss
- Symptoms present for less than 6 months
- Abdominal or rectal mass felt on examination
- Progressive (worsening) abdominal pain
- Fever
- Symptoms that wake you at night
- Severe perianal pain or discharge
- Joint, skin, or eye symptoms alongside bowel symptoms
- Elevated inflammatory markers (CRP, ESR, faecal calprotectin) or low albumin on blood tests

### Examination

Your doctor will check vital signs, look for signs of anaemia, assess weight and BMI, and examine the abdomen and rectum. A gynaecological examination may be performed if a pelvic condition is suspected.

### Investigations

Results are usually normal in IBS. Tests are done to exclude other conditions:

- **Blood tests:** Full blood count, CRP, liver function, renal function, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), iron studies, coeliac serology (if diarrhoea or significant bloating)
- **Stool tests:** Culture for Giardia, Amoeba, and other enteric pathogens if diarrhoea; ova, cyst, and parasite testing if travel-related infection is possible; Clostridioides difficile PCR if relevant risk factors
- **Faecal calprotectin** (if diarrhoea present for > 6 weeks): A raised result can indicate IBD, colorectal cancer, or other inflammatory bowel conditions. A low result (< 50 mcg/g) generally excludes active IBD. Not useful if there is rectal bleeding.
- **Pelvic ultrasound**: May be considered to exclude ovarian pathology
- Abdominal ultrasound, colonoscopy, and hydrogen breath testing are **not** recommended for routine IBS assessment

## Management

In the absence of red flags or concerning features and with normal investigations, referral to a gastroenterologist is rarely required. A multidisciplinary approach — education, dietary advice, lifestyle changes, and psychological support — is the foundation of management.

### Education and Reassurance

Your doctor will acknowledge your symptoms and explain that improvement is possible. Many patients' main concern is that they may have cancer — this can often be reassured against when investigations are normal.

---

### Dietary Management

**General dietary advice:**
- Eat regular meals; eat slowly and chew food thoroughly; do not eat late at night
- Eat a variety of foods from all food groups
- Avoid or reduce: fatty foods, spicy foods, caffeine, fizzy drinks, fruit juice, sorbitol (artificial sweetener), and alcohol
- Adjust fibre intake depending on your bowel habit (increase for constipation, decrease if worsening diarrhoea or bloating)
- Limit fresh fruit to 3 portions per day
- Eat oats (e.g., porridge) to help reduce wind and bloating
- Drink at least 8 cups of water or non-caffeinated drinks per day
- Keep a **food and symptom diary** to identify trigger foods

Avoid overly restrictive diets — these can be harmful and are rarely necessary.

#### Low-FODMAP Diet

FODMAPs are fermentable carbohydrates that are poorly absorbed and rapidly fermented by gut bacteria, causing gas, bloating, and bowel disturbance:

- **F**ermentable carbohydrates, including:
- **O**ligosaccharides (fructans and galacto-oligosaccharides)
- **D**isaccharides (lactose)
- **M**onosaccharides (fructose)
- **P**olyols (sorbitol, mannitol, maltitol, xylitol, and isomalt)

A low-FODMAP diet can significantly improve abdominal symptoms. It should be implemented under the supervision of a dietitian, as it involves structured elimination and reintroduction phases.

#### Lactose Intolerance

Consider lactose intolerance if bloating, pain, flatulence, or diarrhoea consistently occur a couple of hours after consuming milk or dairy products. Small amounts of lactose are usually tolerated. Tolerance can sometimes be improved with gradual incremental increases.

Options include:
- A 4-week lactose exclusion trial, followed by a structured lactose challenge
- Formal lactose breath test (available privately)
- Lacteeze tablets when eating out (available from some pharmacies; take 1–3 tablets with a high-lactose meal)

#### Wheat Intolerance

Wheat intolerance differs from coeliac disease. In coeliac disease there is immune-mediated damage to the small bowel; in non-coeliac wheat intolerance, small bowel abnormalities are not seen, but symptoms still occur. Patients may still experience symptoms from gluten-free products. A food and symptom diary with elimination and reintroduction can help establish wheat intolerance.

---

### Lifestyle Advice

- Stop smoking
- Reduce alcohol consumption if excessive
- Regular physical activity: aim for 20–60 minutes of moderate-intensity exercise, 3–5 days per week
- Participation in group activities can help

---

### Psychosocial Support

About half of patients with IBS have significant mood symptoms that either exacerbate IBS or develop as a consequence of it.

Helpful approaches include:
- Relaxation techniques (breathing exercises, meditation, t'ai chi)
- **Gut-directed hypnotherapy** — evidence-based and effective for IBS
- **Cognitive behaviour therapy (CBT)** — particularly helpful when stress and anxiety are a major driver

---

### Complementary Treatments

#### Peppermint Oil

There is some evidence for benefit with bloating, wind, and bowel cramps. It has antispasmodic and anti-flatulent effects. It may worsen reflux.

Use enteric-coated capsules (e.g., Colpermin or Mintec) so the oil is released in the colon. Take 1 capsule three times daily, 30 minutes before a meal. Trial for 2 weeks; if helpful, continue at the lowest effective dose.

#### Probiotics

Some evidence suggests benefit, but data on specific strains is limited. *Bifidobacterium infantis* 35624 has the best supporting evidence. Not funded — can be expensive. Trial for 4 weeks at the recommended dose; if helpful, continue.

#### Herbal Supplements

Iberogast (7-herb preparation) has some supporting evidence. Phloe, slippery elm, and aloe vera are commonly used but have limited evidence. These can be trialled.

---

### Medications — Symptom-Targeted

Most patients do not need medication. When symptoms are significant, medication is targeted to the predominant symptom.

#### For Diarrhoea (IBS-D)

First, trial dietary measures: eliminate artificial sweeteners, excess caffeine, high-fat foods, alcohol, and FODMAPs.

If medications are needed:
- **Loperamide:** Start at a very low dose (as little as 0.5 mg). Can be used regularly (2–4 mg morning or evening) or prophylactically before meals out. No evidence for tolerance developing.
- **Bile acid sequestrants** (if bile salt malabsorption suspected): Colestipol (subsidised) or cholestyramine. Start with 1 sachet daily, increase by 1 sachet per week as tolerated, up to 3 sachets daily.
- **Mebeverine (Colofac) 135 mg:** Three times daily, 20–30 minutes before food. Trial for 1 week regularly, then use as needed.
- **Ondansetron** (unlicensed use; patient must consent): Start 4 mg daily, increase gradually over 3 weeks up to a maximum of 6 mg three times daily (median effective dose 4 mg/day). Particularly helpful for rectal urgency.

Avoid codeine phosphate — risk of dependence, tolerance, and drug interactions.

#### For Constipation (IBS-C)

Ensure adequate fluid and dietary soluble fibre (oats, barley, rye, pulses, fruit, vegetables).

If medications are needed:
- **First line — bulk-forming laxatives:**
  - Psyllium (e.g., Metamucil) — soluble fibre; may initially worsen bloating
  - Sterculia (e.g., Normacol) — insoluble fibre; better tolerated if bloating is a problem; not subsidised
  - Benefiber — soluble fibre; easier to take, stays clear in water
- **Second line — macrogols** (e.g., Movicol, Molaxole): Preferable to lactulose as it causes less bloating

Note: symptoms may initially worsen; advise gradual dose increase. Discontinue fibre if symptoms are persistently worse after an adequate trial.

#### For Abdominal Pain, Bloating, and Distension

- Paracetamol is preferred over NSAIDs for pain; analgesics are generally not very effective for IBS pain
- **Antispasmodics** (intermittent use; lowest effective dose):
  - Mebeverine: 1-week regular trial, then use as required
  - Hyoscine (Gastrosoothe): 10–20 mg four times daily; 1-week regular trial, then as required
  - Domperidone
  - Peppermint oil (see above)

#### Antidepressants (For All IBS Subtypes with Significant Pain)

Antidepressants are effective for IBS pain and discomfort through analgesic and motility effects — not primarily as treatment for depression or anxiety. This distinction is important to explain to patients.

- **Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs):** Avoid in IBS-C (worsen constipation). Start amitriptyline or nortriptyline (less sedating) at 5–10 mg at night. Assess after 3–4 weeks; increase slowly to 25–50 mg at night if needed. Review every 6–12 weeks. Doses used are much lower than standard antidepressant doses. *Unlicensed indication for IBS — patient must be informed.*
- **SSRIs/SNRIs:** Lower level of evidence. Consider if TCAs are ineffective or not tolerated. May be preferred in IBS-C or where anxiety predominates. Citalopram or fluoxetine 10–20 mg daily. *Unlicensed indication.*

---

### If Not Improving

Your doctor will regularly review your progress. If symptoms are not improving despite dietary, lifestyle, and medication treatment:

1. Re-assess for any concerning features, dietary adherence, and psychosocial factors
2. Consider a dietitian referral if not already done
3. Consider starting or adjusting medication
4. If symptoms remain persistent or worsening despite optimal treatment, your doctor may refer to a gastroenterologist

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will refer to a gastroenterologist (non-urgent) if:
- Rectal bleeding
- Unexplained iron deficiency anaemia
- Unintentional weight loss
- Concern about colorectal cancer
- Symptoms persistent or worsening despite optimal management

A **dietitian referral** is appropriate for:
- Guidance on low-FODMAP diet or other dietary strategies
- When dietary management alone has not led to improvement
