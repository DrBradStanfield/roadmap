# Immunoglobulin Administration

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the administration of immunoglobulin in general practice. Immunoglobulin may be indicated following specific exposures or in certain clinical situations — your doctor will determine whether it is appropriate for you.

---

## Background

Immunoglobulins (IG) are preparations made from human plasma, treated to reduce the risk of virus transmission. They provide **passive immunity** — protection that is immediate but temporary, lasting only weeks to months.

There are two types used in New Zealand:

- **Normal human immunoglobulin** — used for measles, polio, hepatitis A
- **Disease-specific immunoglobulins** — includes tetagam P (tetanus), zoster IG-VF (chickenpox/shingles), hepatitis B IG, and rabies IG

Immunoglobulin may be indicated for people who have no natural immunity to a particular infection, or who have impaired antibody production. Common situations include:

- Post-exposure prophylaxis for measles
- Unimmunised patients with tetanus-prone wounds
- Blood or body fluid exposures (e.g. hepatitis B)
- Babies born to mothers who are hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) positive
- Post-exposure varicella zoster (chickenpox) or herpes zoster
- Anti-D immunoglobulin for RhD negative women after bleeding in pregnancy

---

## Safety Precautions

Your doctor will check the specific immunoglobulin product for precautions and contraindications before administration. Key safety points include:

- **Immunoglobulin products approved for intramuscular injection must not be given intravenously** — this can cause serious adverse reactions
- Use with caution if you have isolated IgA deficiency, thrombocytopenia, coagulation disorders, or are taking anticoagulant medications
- Give with caution if you have a history of a previous systemic allergic reaction to human immunoglobulin preparations
- If platelet count is low, your doctor may administer subcutaneously rather than intramuscularly

---

## Vaccine Interactions

Your doctor will check for any interactions with vaccines:

- **Passively acquired antibodies can interfere with live vaccines.** Live vaccines include: MMR (measles, mumps, rubella), oral rotavirus, oral polio, oral typhoid, BCG (tuberculosis), varicella zoster (chickenpox, Zostavax), and yellow fever
- If immunoglobulin has been administered, **live vaccines should be deferred for at least 3 months**
- If a live vaccine has recently been given, **immunoglobulin should not be administered for at least 2 weeks**
- Inactivated (non-live) vaccines may be given at the same time as immunoglobulin (in a separate syringe), which is sometimes done for tetanus-prone wounds

---

## After Administration

After receiving immunoglobulin, your doctor may monitor you for adverse reactions:

- **Common:** Local tenderness, redness, and stiffness at the injection site — these may persist for several hours
- **Occasional:** Mild fever, malaise (feeling unwell), drowsiness, or urticaria (hives)
- **Rare:** Skin reactions, headache, dizziness, nausea, generalised allergic reactions, or convulsions. These occur more often in people with very low levels of immunoglobulin (profound hypogammaglobulinaemia)

Your doctor will also note that following immunoglobulin administration, **blood tests for antibodies may temporarily give misleading positive results** due to the passively transferred antibodies.

---

## What Your Doctor Will Do

Your doctor will manage the process of obtaining and administering immunoglobulin. This includes:

- Confirming the clinical indication and obtaining written informed consent (as immunoglobulin is a blood product)
- Contacting the **NZ Blood Service (NZBS)**, which operates a 24-hour on-call service to confirm eligibility and coordinate supply
- Requesting the immunoglobulin product through the appropriate Blood Bank
- Checking for any vaccine interactions and drug precautions
- Notifying the Medical Officer of Health if a notifiable disease is suspected

If you have questions about whether immunoglobulin is right for you, or about any aspect of the process, speak with your doctor.
