# Chronic Non-cancer Pain

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: July 2024.*

Chronic non-cancer pain (also called persistent pain) is defined as constant daily pain for 3 months or more in the last 6 months. It is a major cause of disability, affecting 1 in 5 New Zealanders. It is more common in women and in Māori and Pacific peoples.

It is a complex medical condition that affects, and is affected by, many parts of life, including social environment, emotional state, physical health and fitness, sleep, and nutrition. An early, active approach may reduce how long the pain lasts and how severe it is.

**The goal of management is to restore physical and emotional function and improve quality of life, rather than to lower the pain score. Eighty per cent of successful management is non-pharmacological; medications contribute only 20%.**

> This information is for general understanding. Your doctor will assess your specific situation and guide your management plan.

---

## Red Flags — See a Doctor Promptly

Tell your doctor straight away if you have:

- Trauma
- Constitutional symptoms: fever, chills, or weight loss
- Pain when you are older than 50 years or younger than 20 years
- Neurological deficit, such as severe or worsening numbness or weakness, or problems with bladder or bowel control

These may suggest a serious underlying cause.

If you have back pain with new problems controlling your bladder or bowel, or new weakness in your legs, call 111 or go to the emergency department.

---

## Understanding Pain Types

Your doctor may describe your pain using these terms:

**Nociceptive pain**: from tissue damage caused by trauma, an injury that has not healed, or inflammation. Examples include cuts, bruises, fractures, sprains, and arthritis.

**Neuropathic pain**: from a lesion or disease of the nervous system. It is often described as burning or shooting, sometimes with "pins and needles", numbness, or itching. Examples include diabetic neuropathy, postherpetic neuralgia, and multiple sclerosis.

**Radicular pain**: radiates in a thin line down a limb, usually more painful further down, and can be troublesome at night. Pins and needles and numbness may follow a specific nerve-root (dermatomal) pattern. On its own, without signs such as weakness or abnormal reflexes, it does not mean the nerve is damaged.

**Nociplastic pain**: altered pain processing (central sensitisation) with no clear evidence of tissue damage or disease. It is characterised by hypersensitivity in apparently normal tissue. Examples include fibromyalgia, irritable bowel syndrome, and non-specific low back pain. It is less likely to respond to pain medications.

## Assessment

Chronic pain needs a detailed assessment, which may require multiple appointments. Your doctor will ask how much the pain limits what you can do, because that drives treatment. An examination can help identify the type of pain, rule out serious causes, and reassure you. Tests should be arranged only to answer a specific question, to avoid over-investigation.

### Take a Detailed History

If time is short, your doctor may start with a brief 10-minute pain history using the **SPACE** framework:
- **Sleep:** what wakes you up, and whether sleep is refreshing
- **Pain:** location, duration, triggers, relievers, and type
- **Affect:** depression, and self-harm or suicide risk
- **Cognitive:** cognitive impairment or brain fog
- **Energy:** energy and fatigue

A fuller history also covers:
- **Pain characteristics:** nature, intensity, location, duration, onset, aggravating and relieving factors, radiation, and frequency
- **Past medical history:** previous treatments (including alternative ones) and outcomes, other conditions, and medications (including over-the-counter and herbal)
- **Performance and functioning:** impact on daily activities, work, and relationships
- **Psychosocial factors:** depression and anxiety, suicidal thoughts, substance use, and beliefs about pain
- **Sociocultural context:** spiritual and cultural beliefs about pain, problems at work, significant life events, and family support

### Check for Red Flags Suggesting Serious Cause

- **Possible fracture:** major trauma, or minor trauma in older or osteoporotic people
- **Possible tumour:** age older than 50 years or younger than 20 years, history of cancer, fever, chills or weight loss, pain worsening at night or when lying down, immunosuppression
- **Possible infection:** fever, chills or malaise, immunosuppression, recent bacterial infection, intravenous drug use
- **Possible neurological disorder:** severe or progressive changes in sensation or weakness, bladder or bowel dysfunction, or signs of nerve problems in the legs or saddle area (perineum) with low back pain

### Identify Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS) Early

CRPS requires urgent intervention and referral. It is characterised by continuing regional pain, either spontaneous or triggered, that seems out of proportion in time or degree to the usual course of any known injury or other lesion.

**Budapest criteria for CRPS diagnosis:**
1. Continuing pain disproportionate to any inciting event
2. At least 1 symptom in 3 of 4 categories: sensory (hyperalgesia/allodynia), vasomotor (temperature or colour asymmetry), sudomotor/oedema (swelling, sweating changes), motor/trophic (reduced movement, weakness, tremor, changes in hair, nails, or skin)
3. At least 1 sign at evaluation in 2 or more of the same 4 categories
4. No other diagnosis better explains the findings

*Note: Acute onset of CRPS, especially in children and adolescents, should be treated and referred urgently.*

### Identify Specific Pain Syndromes

Some conditions need specific management pathways:
- Chronic pelvic pain
- Fibromyalgia
- Headache (adults and children)
- Postherpetic neuralgia
- Temporomandibular disorder (TMD)
- Vulvodynia

## Management

### 1. CRPS — Urgent Action

If CRPS is suspected in an adult, your doctor may refer you urgently for a specialised pain assessment. If you meet the Budapest criteria, your doctor may also start a reducing (tapering) course of prednisone. In children and adolescents (younger than 16 years), CRPS may not meet the Budapest criteria. Your doctor may seek urgent paediatric advice, and prednisone is not used in this group without specialist input.

### 2. Physical Factors

**Physical activity** is essential for managing chronic pain, particularly low back pain, osteoarthritis, and neuropathic pain. Start where you are at: sudden increases in activity may cause a pain flare.

- Your doctor may suggest starting with gentle activities, such as walking in a heated pool, stretching, chair yoga, or beginner's yoga
- **Activity pacing** is a key skill, because overdoing things can set back recovery as much as underdoing them. Pick an activity that pain affects, set an amount you are confident you can do, and add a "relax" step after it. For example:
  - Sitting for 20 minutes and then moving or standing
  - Housework or gardening for 10 minutes followed by stretches
- Keep to that amount for a week, then build up slowly each week, whatever your pain levels. Avoid "boom-bust" cycles.
- Your doctor may refer you to a physiotherapist with experience in chronic pain
- There is no evidence for alternative and complementary medicine, chiropractic, or osteopathy for managing chronic pain

**Sleep:** Go to bed and get up at the same time every day. Avoid napping during the day if possible; if you must, nap before 3.00 pm and then only for 30 minutes. Wind down with relaxing activities, and avoid caffeine and alcohol before bed.

**Diet:** Your doctor may give advice on healthy eating.

### 3. Psychosocial Support

Psychological strategies can help change thoughts and beliefs about pain, and so reduce the impact pain has on your life.

**Key messages your doctor may share:**
- Chronic pain is a complex problem, and good pain management needs approaches outside of medication
- Pain is our inbuilt danger alarm, which can sometimes become sensitive and easier to trigger
- Thoughts and beliefs about pain can influence the body's response to pain signals
- Many people with chronic pain can learn to live well despite pain, and most can lead completely normal lives
- There is no instant answer or quick fix, and your doctor stays alert to any change suggesting a different cause

**Psychological strategies include:**
- [Retrain Pain](https://www.retrainpain.org/) (online course)
- [painTRAINER](https://www.paintrainer.org/login-to-paintrainer/) (cognitive behavioural therapy, free login)
- Mindfulness: Healthify He Puna Waiora, [Mindfulness and Letting Go](https://healthify.nz/hauora-wellbeing/m/mindfulness)

Your doctor may also treat anxiety, depression, or substance use if present.

**Unhelpful beliefs to address:**
- "Hurt equals harm"
- "Exercise will make pain worse"
- "When in pain, I should rest"
- "Returning to work will worsen my pain"
- "Only medical interventions can fix my pain"

**Culturally safe care:** traditional practices, such as mirimiri (massage) and rongoā Māori (traditional Māori medicine), may be included where they are consistent with evidence-based guidelines. For Māori in particular, more emphasis may go on self-management and long-term strategies.

**Work and social functioning:**
- Most people are encouraged to stay at work, or to return as early as possible (with reduced hours if needed)
- Social isolation is common and debilitating. Start small and gradually increase social contact.

### 4. Pharmacological Pain Relief

Medication is adjusted to achieve the best functional effect, rather than pain relief. If a medication is not providing a significant benefit, your doctor may stop it and explore another option.

**Long-term opioids are not recommended for chronic non-cancer pain.**

For specific medicines, your doctor may follow separate guidance on medications in chronic pain.

### 5. Self-Management and Flare-Up Planning

**Self-management principles:**
- Self-management is important
- Start with what you can manage now and build slowly
- Your doctor may help you develop a pain management plan that you own

**During a flare-up (non-pharmacological strategies should be the mainstay):**
- Identify and prevent triggers, such as overactivity or underactivity, and avoid "boom-bust" patterns
- Set realistic goals to cope with, rather than cure, the flare-up
- Use heat pads or cold compresses on the sore area
- Do gentle stretching and movement to prevent stiffness
- Breathe slowly and deeply to avoid tension and hyperventilation
- Use distraction: a hobby, or talking with someone about anything other than the pain
- Avoid long periods of rest or inactivity
- Accept the pain, and that your situation will start to improve
- Return to your doctor if you develop new or different symptoms, rather than a flare of existing ones

### 6. Specific Pain Types

Certain conditions are managed with their own specific pathways:
- Chronic pelvic pain, fibromyalgia, headache, postherpetic neuralgia, TMD, vulvodynia
- Facial neuralgia may need ORL (ear, nose, throat) or neurosurgery assessment

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may consider referring you for a specialised pain assessment if any of these apply:

### Complexity Indicators (Biomedical)
- Opioid use for more than 90 days (excluding palliative or end-of-life comfort care)
- Refractory neuropathic pain
- Red flags suggesting a serious cause
- Lying down or reclining for more than 60 minutes in total during usual waking hours

### Complexity Indicators (Psychological)
- Kessler 10 (K10) score more than 19 out of 50
- DASS-21: Depression greater than 9, Anxiety greater than 7, or Stress greater than 14
- Fear-avoidance behaviour (avoiding activity because of fear of pain or re-injury), low mood, or social withdrawal
- Expecting passive treatment, rather than active participation, or strong analgesia to be the key to managing pain
- Unhelpful beliefs about pain (see above)

### Other Reasons for Specialist Referral
- No improvement after 3 months despite appropriate medical management
- Potential benefit from an interventional pain management technique (see below)
- Suspected CRPS (urgent)

### Interventional Techniques

These may give only short-term relief, are useful in less than 10% of people with chronic non-cancer pain, and are being used less often. They include:

**Nerve blocks (targeted injections):**
- Diagnostic blocks: local anaesthetic given mainly to find the pain source. They can give false positive results.
- Therapeutic blocks: local anaesthetic, with or without steroid, may be used for temporary relief
- Lumbar epidural injections for radicular pain, more likely to help when the pain comes from disc disease rather than spinal stenosis. Epidural injections for non-radicular pain are not recommended.
- Other examples: facet joint injections, sacroiliac joint injections, and occipital nerve block

**Nerve ablation (radiofrequency rhizotomy):**
- When diagnostic injections show a facet joint is the pain source, radiofrequency rhizotomy at that site can produce longer-term relief

**Neurostimulation (spinal cord stimulator):**
- May be considered for: failed back surgery pain with radicular symptoms, CRPS of the lower limb, peripheral vascular disease pain, refractory angina
- A positive trial with an external stimulator is needed before a permanent device is implanted
- Goals are set before implantation and should be functional and objective (e.g. reduced analgesic use), not only a lower pain score

---

## Follow-Up

Your doctor may suggest seeing you 1 to 2 times a week for 4 sessions, then monthly, then every 3 months. This is costly and may not be available to everyone. Follow-up aims to:

- Review your pain management plan
- Consider involving practice nurses, or a health improvement practitioner if your practice has one
- Encourage self-management and avoid dependence
