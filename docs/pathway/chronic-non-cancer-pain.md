# Chronic Non-cancer Pain

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Chronic non-cancer pain (also called persistent pain) is defined as constant daily pain for 3 months or more in the last 6 months. It affects about 1 in 5 people and is a complex medical condition influenced by physical, psychological, social, and cultural factors.

**The goal of management is to restore function and improve quality of life — not simply to eliminate pain. Eighty per cent of effective management is non-pharmacological; medications contribute only 20%.**

> This information is for general understanding. Your doctor will assess your specific situation and guide your management plan.

---

## Red Flags — See a Doctor Promptly

Tell your doctor straight away if you have:

- Trauma or injury causing or worsening pain
- Constitutional symptoms: fever, chills, or unexplained weight loss
- Pain starting after age 50 or before age 20 (when no prior diagnosis)
- Neurological deficit: numbness, weakness, or loss of bladder/bowel control

These may indicate a serious underlying cause that needs urgent investigation.

---

## Understanding Pain Types

Your doctor may describe your pain using these terms:

**Nociceptive pain** — from tissue damage (cuts, bruises, arthritis, sprains). Usually well-localised and aching or throbbing.

**Neuropathic pain** — from a lesion or disease of the nervous system. Often burning, shooting, or "pins and needles." Examples: diabetic neuropathy, postherpetic neuralgia.

**Radicular pain** — radiates in a thin line down a limb (often from a pinched nerve root). More painful toward the periphery; can cause numbness in a specific dermatomal pattern.

**Nociplastic pain** — altered pain processing in the nervous system with no clear tissue damage. The nervous system becomes sensitised and easier to trigger. Examples: fibromyalgia, non-specific low back pain, irritable bowel syndrome. Less likely to respond to standard pain medications.

## Assessment

Your doctor will conduct a thorough assessment — this may take multiple appointments. They will:

### Take a Detailed History

A brief assessment uses the **SPACE** framework:
- **Sleep** — what wakes you up, whether sleep is refreshing
- **Pain** — location, duration, triggers, relievers, type
- **Affect** — depression and anxiety screening
- **Cognitive** — brain fog or concentration difficulties
- **Energy** — fatigue levels

A fuller history also covers:
- **Pain characteristics** — nature, intensity, location, onset, aggravating/relieving factors, radiation
- **Past medical history** — previous treatments and outcomes, other conditions, medications
- **Performance and functioning** — impact on daily activities, work, relationships
- **Psychosocial factors** — mental health (depression, anxiety), substance use, beliefs about pain, work and financial stress
- **Sociocultural context** — cultural beliefs about pain, family and social support

### Check for Red Flags Suggesting Serious Cause

- **Possible fracture:** major trauma, or minor trauma in older or osteoporotic people
- **Possible tumour:** age >50 or <20, history of cancer, weight loss, pain worsening at night
- **Possible infection:** fever, chills, immunosuppression, intravenous drug use
- **Possible neurological disorder:** progressive weakness, bladder/bowel dysfunction, neurological deficit

### Identify Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS) Early

CRPS requires **urgent referral**. It is characterised by regional pain disproportionate to any injury, with associated changes in sensation, skin colour/temperature, sweating, or movement.

**Budapest criteria for CRPS diagnosis — all four must be met:**
1. Continuing pain disproportionate to any inciting event
2. At least 1 symptom in 3 of 4 categories: sensory (hyperalgesia/allodynia), vasomotor (temperature or colour asymmetry), sudomotor/oedema, motor/trophic changes
3. At least 1 sign at evaluation in 2 or more of the same 4 categories
4. No other diagnosis better explains the findings

*Note: Acute CRPS in children and adolescents must be treated and referred urgently.*

### Identify Specific Pain Syndromes

Some conditions need specific management pathways:
- Chronic pelvic pain
- Fibromyalgia
- Headache (adults and children)
- Postherpetic neuralgia
- Temporomandibular disorder (TMD)
- Vulvodynia

## Management

### 1. CRPS — Urgent Action

If CRPS is suspected, your doctor will refer urgently to a pain specialist. In adults who meet the Budapest criteria, a prednisone taper is typically started. Children and adolescents need urgent specialist input.

### 2. Physical Factors

**Physical activity** is essential for managing chronic pain — particularly for low back pain, osteoarthritis, and neuropathic pain.

- Start gradually — gentle activities like walking, swimming, stretching, or chair yoga
- Use **activity pacing**: identify an activity, establish a comfortable amount, add a rest element, maintain that level for a week, then slowly increase
- Avoid "boom-bust" patterns (doing too much on good days, collapsing on bad days)
- Physiotherapy with a therapist experienced in chronic pain can help

**Sleep:** Maintain regular sleep/wake times; avoid napping after 3pm; limit caffeine and alcohol before bed; use relaxation techniques.

**Diet:** A healthy balanced diet supports overall wellbeing.

### 3. Psychosocial Support

Psychological approaches are a core part of pain management, not an add-on.

**Key messages your doctor may share:**
- Chronic pain is a complex problem requiring approaches beyond medication
- Pain is the body's danger alarm — in chronic pain, that alarm can become oversensitive even without ongoing harm
- Thoughts and beliefs about pain influence how the body responds to pain signals
- Many people with chronic pain learn to live well and lead full lives
- There is no quick fix; gradual improvement through active participation is the goal

**Psychological strategies include:**
- Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) for chronic pain
- Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT)
- Mindfulness
- Online resources such as Retrain Pain

**Unhelpful beliefs to address:**
- "Hurt equals harm" — pain does not always mean damage is occurring
- "Exercise will make it worse" — appropriate activity generally improves chronic pain
- "I need to rest until the pain is gone" — prolonged inactivity worsens chronic pain
- "Only a medical procedure can fix this" — most chronic pain improves with active self-management

**Work and social functioning:**
- Staying at or returning to work (even with reduced hours) is usually beneficial
- Social connection matters — isolation worsens chronic pain

### 4. Pharmacological Pain Relief

Medications are adjusted to improve function, not just reduce pain scores. If a medication is not providing meaningful benefit, it is stopped.

**Long-term opioids are NOT recommended for chronic non-cancer pain.**

Your doctor will refer to the Medications in Chronic Pain pathway for specific options (including paracetamol, anti-inflammatories, antidepressants for pain, anticonvulsants, and topical agents).

### 5. Self-Management and Flare-Up Planning

**Self-management principles:**
- Take ownership of your pain management plan
- Start with what you can manage now and build slowly
- Engage your support network

**During a flare-up (non-pharmacological strategies are first-line):**
- Identify and avoid triggers (overactivity or underactivity)
- Use heat pads or cold compresses on the sore area
- Do gentle stretching and movement to prevent stiffness
- Breathe slowly and deeply to reduce tension
- Use distraction (hobbies, conversation about topics other than pain)
- Avoid prolonged rest or inactivity
- Accept the flare-up and know it will pass
- Return to your doctor if you develop new or different symptoms

### 6. Specific Pain Types

Certain conditions are managed with their own specific pathways:
- Chronic pelvic pain, fibromyalgia, headache, postherpetic neuralgia, TMD, vulvodynia
- Facial neuralgia may need ORL (ear, nose, throat) or neurosurgery assessment

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor will consider referring you to a specialised pain service if:

### Complexity Indicators (Biomedical)
- Opioid use for more than 90 days (not for palliative care)
- Refractory neuropathic pain
- Red flags suggesting a serious cause
- Spending more than 60 minutes lying down during waking hours

### Complexity Indicators (Psychological)
- Kessler 10 (K10) score >19 out of 50
- DASS-21: Depression >9, Anxiety >7, or Stress >14
- Unhelpful pain beliefs (e.g. fear-avoidance, catastrophising)
- Expectation that passive treatment or strong analgesia will fix the pain

### Other Reasons for Specialist Referral
- No improvement after 3 months of appropriate management
- Potential benefit from an interventional pain management technique (see below)
- Suspected or confirmed CRPS (urgent)

### Interventional Techniques

These give only short-term relief, work for fewer than 10% of patients, and are used less frequently than in the past. They include:

**Nerve blocks (targeted injections):**
- Diagnostic blocks to identify the pain source
- Therapeutic blocks using local anaesthetic ± steroid for temporary relief
- Specific examples: lumbar epidural injections (for radicular pain), facet joint injections, sacroiliac joint injections, occipital nerve block

**Nerve ablation (radiofrequency rhizotomy):**
- Used when a diagnostic block confirms a facet joint as the pain source
- Produces longer-term relief at that site

**Neurostimulation (spinal cord stimulator):**
- Considered for: failed back surgery pain with radicular symptoms, CRPS of the lower limb, peripheral vascular disease pain, refractory angina
- A positive trial with an external stimulator is required before implanting a permanent device
- Goals should be functional and objective (e.g. reduced analgesic use), not just pain score reduction

---

## Follow-Up

Your doctor will typically see you 1–2 times a week for 4 sessions initially, then monthly, then every 3 months. Follow-up aims to:

- Review and adjust your pain management plan
- Support self-management and avoid dependence on passive treatments
- Consider involving practice nurses or other allied health practitioners
