# Speech and Communication Difficulties in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of speech and communication difficulties in adults once an underlying diagnosis has been established.

---

## Types of Speech and Communication Difficulty

### Motor speech difficulties

These affect how speech is physically produced (phonation, resonance, prosody, articulation):

- **Dysarthria** — A motor speech disorder where the muscles that produce speech are affected by a neurological condition (e.g. stroke, Parkinson's disease, multiple sclerosis). Affects strength, speed, and accuracy of speech movements.
- **Apraxia (dyspraxia)** — A motor speech disorder where the brain's motor programming for speech is impaired, but the speech muscles themselves are normal (e.g. after head injury, stroke, or dementia).

### Language difficulties

- **Aphasia (dysphasia)** — A language disorder from damage to the brain's language centre (left hemisphere). Can affect understanding spoken and written language, and expressing ideas verbally or in writing. It is **not** an intellectual disability. Usually caused by stroke.

### Other types

- **Stuttering/fluency difficulties** — May be long-standing or recently acquired. Newly acquired stuttering needs neurological assessment.
- **Voice problems (dysphonia)** — Hoarseness, voice loss, weak or low voice volume. Can relate to structure, neurology (e.g. Parkinson's disease), muscle tension, transgender voice care, or disease progression.
- **Social communication difficulties** — Including anxiety, Asperger syndrome, cognitive communication difficulties from dementia, traumatic brain injury, or right hemisphere language disorder.
- **Reading and writing difficulties** — Following neurological events such as stroke or brain injury.

## Assessment

Your doctor will allow extra time and check you have your glasses or hearing aids if needed.

### Communication tips your doctor may use

To help you understand:
- Speaking more slowly
- Giving one piece of information at a time
- Writing down key words or using gestures
- Providing written information to take home
- Checking that you have understood

To help you express yourself:
- Asking about any communication device (AAC) you already use
- Asking yes/no questions
- Encouraging writing, drawing, or gesturing
- Using pictures to help

### History

Your doctor will ask about:
- When the difficulty started (sudden or gradual) and how long it has been present
- How you would describe your difficulties
- How the difficulty affects your quality of life
- Any previous specialist or speech language therapy assessment and treatment
- Other symptoms such as neurological changes, swallowing difficulties, or head and neck symptoms

### Examination

Your doctor will assess which type of speech or communication difficulty is present, and may perform a neurological or head and neck examination.

## Management

Management depends on your situation.

### New or sudden-onset difficulty, or progressive neurological condition

Your doctor will:
- Follow the relevant condition pathway (e.g. stroke, Parkinson's disease, motor neurone disease) to establish the diagnosis
- Provide communication strategies while you wait for specialist assessment, or for palliative care
- Refer for speech language therapy assessment when appropriate

### Pre-existing difficulty with no change

Your doctor will consider:
- The impact on your social life, work, education, or independence
- Support needs for carers
- Referring for speech language therapy if you have not had an assessment before
- Practical communication strategies
- Community communication groups

### Pre-existing difficulty with a change in function

Your doctor will consider:
- Whether you need a review from the specialist team who previously managed your condition
- Impact on your social life, work, education, or independence
- Carer stress and support needs
- Referring for speech language therapy assessment
- Reviewing whether assistive communication technology would help (e.g. iPad, GoTalk, Litewriter)

**TalkLink** is a charitable trust providing free assistive technology assessment and services for people with communication difficulties. Specialist speech language therapists help identify the best communication system for your needs.

---

## Referrals

Your doctor may refer you to:

- **Speech language therapy** — for new or acute communication difficulties once the underlying condition is managed; for pre-existing difficulty with significant change or new therapy goals; or for review of an assistive communication device (if not already known to TalkLink)
- **Non-acute neurology assessment** — for new or acute neurological conditions, or recently acquired stuttering
- **TalkLink** — for review of assistive/alternative communication systems if you are already a TalkLink client
- **Private speech language therapy** — via the New Zealand Speech-language Therapists' Association if you do not meet public funding criteria
