# Acute Exacerbation of COPD

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

An acute exacerbation of COPD is a sudden worsening of your usual breathlessness, cough, wheeze, or sputum beyond your normal day-to-day variation. This pathway covers how doctors assess and manage these flare-ups.

---

## Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately if you have:

- Signs of respiratory failure — bluish tinge to the lips or fingernails (cyanosis), oxygen saturation below 90%, or confusion
- Drowsiness or reduced alertness
- Signs of sepsis (serious infection spreading through the body)

---

## About Acute Exacerbations of COPD

- Frequent exacerbations are a sign of more severe COPD. They accelerate decline in lung function, impair quality of life, restrict daily activities, and worsen physical deconditioning.
- Early detection and management can prevent progressive decline and reduce hospital admissions. Learning to recognise the early signs is important for you, your carers, and your family.
- After a hospital admission for a COPD exacerbation, around 1 in 4 people are readmitted within one month. Predictors of readmission are often social rather than medical — such as living alone, distance from a GP, or limited community support.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- **Confirm the diagnosis** — ruling out other causes such as heart failure or pneumonia, which can cause similar symptoms.
- **Review your baseline** — checking your COPD Action Plan (if you have one) and your usual level of function.
- **Ask about your social situation** — such as whether you live alone or have support at home.

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will check:

- Temperature, pulse rate, and breathing rate
- Oxygen saturation (using a finger probe for at least a minute to get a stable reading)
- Chest sounds — quality of air entry, presence of wheeze, signs of pneumonia
- Legs — for signs of fluid retention suggesting heart failure

Your doctor may also calculate a **CURB-65 score**, a tool developed for pneumonia that has also been found to predict short-term risk in COPD.

### Investigations

- **Full blood count (FBC)** — to look for eosinophilia (a type of white blood cell elevation that can guide treatment)
- **C-reactive protein (CRP)** — a marker of inflammation and infection
- **Chest X-ray** — usually only needed if the diagnosis is uncertain or to check for pneumonia, lung cancer risk, or if you are not improving as expected. It is not always necessary.
- **Sputum culture** — not routinely recommended, but may be considered if infection is suspected and not responding to treatment.

### Ceiling of Care

Your doctor will discuss your expectations and goals with you and your family. For people with severe COPD and a short life expectancy, conservative management is usually most appropriate. Invasive intervention (such as ICU admission) is often not helpful and not the right choice for many patients.

---

## Management

Many people with a COPD exacerbation can be managed safely at home, avoiding hospital admission. Your doctor will assess whether this is appropriate for you.

**You will likely need hospital assessment if you have:**

- Respiratory failure (low oxygen, confusion, or cyanosis)
- Drowsiness
- Sepsis
- Failure to improve with initial treatment
- Serious other health conditions

If you are not improving at home despite supports, hospital admission will be arranged.

### Inhaler Technique

Your doctor will check that you are using your inhaler correctly. Poor technique is a common reason for medications not working well.

### Bronchodilators (Reliever Inhalers)

Your doctor will recommend an **increased dose of your reliever inhaler (salbutamol)**:

- **4 to 6 puffs of salbutamol 100 mcg MDI every 3 to 4 hours**, using a large volume spacer for best delivery. Each puff should be followed by 4 to 6 slow, normal breaths.
- If you do not improve with repeated doses of salbutamol, your doctor may add **ipratropium bromide** — unless you are already on a long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA), in which case it is not used.
- **Inhalers with a spacer are as effective as nebulisers** in mild-to-moderate exacerbations, and reduce the risk of spreading infection.

If a nebuliser is used:
- High-flow oxygen is avoided if you are at risk of retaining carbon dioxide (CO2).

**Continue your regular preventer inhalers** — long-acting beta-2 agonist (LABA), long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA), and/or inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) — throughout the exacerbation.

### Antibiotics

Antibiotics are recommended if you have:

- Increased sputum that has become darker or thicker (more purulent), or increased in volume, **and/or**
- A CRP blood test result above 40

**First-line antibiotic:**

- **Amoxicillin** 500 mg three times a day for 5 days

**If amoxicillin does not work:**

- **Amoxicillin + clavulanic acid** 500 mg + 125 mg three times a day

**If you are allergic to penicillin:**

- **Doxycycline** 200 mg on the first day, then 100 mg daily for 5 days

The usual course is 5 days, adjusted based on your response.

If you do not respond to antibiotics, your doctor may consider less common or resistant bacteria (such as *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*) and arrange sputum testing. Seek specialist respiratory advice if non-tuberculous mycobacteria is found on sputum testing.

### Corticosteroids (Oral Steroids)

Your doctor will prescribe a short course of **oral prednisone** if:

- Your exacerbation is moderate to severe, **or**
- You are worsening despite antibiotics and increased bronchodilators

**Prednisone 40 mg daily for 5 days.**

- A lower dose may be used if you have poorly controlled diabetes or other relevant conditions.
- You do not need to taper the dose after a short 5-day course.
- Recovery from an exacerbation typically takes around 30 days — longer courses of oral steroids do not speed this up.
- Do not increase your inhaled steroid dose during an exacerbation.
- Long-term oral steroid use is not recommended.

### Treating Other Conditions

If you also have heart failure or pneumonia, your doctor will treat these alongside the COPD exacerbation.

---

## Follow-Up

Your doctor will arrange a **follow-up appointment approximately 1 week after your exacerbation** to:

- Review your severity and adjust your long-term management plan
- Reassure you that full recovery to your usual baseline may take around 30 days
- Check your medications and inhaler technique
- Help you complete a **COPD Action Plan** if you do not have one — this helps you recognise and respond to future flare-ups early
- Discuss **Advance Care Planning** if appropriate
- Educate you and your family about early warning signs — early treatment slows long-term decline

If anxiety or breathlessness is a significant problem, your doctor can refer you to specialised respiratory nursing support.

If you have been admitted to hospital more than twice in the past year, your doctor will consider referring you to **respiratory specialised nursing**.

---

## When Your Doctor Will Refer You

Your doctor will arrange specialist review or further support if:

- **Emergency hospital assessment** is needed — for respiratory failure, drowsiness, sepsis, failure to respond to treatment, or serious co-existing conditions
- **Respiratory specialised nursing** — if you are admitted to hospital more than twice per year, or need ongoing support with breathlessness and anxiety management
- **Respiratory specialist advice** — if non-tuberculous mycobacteria is found in your sputum, or if there is uncertainty about management
