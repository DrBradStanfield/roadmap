# Allergic Rhinitis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of allergic rhinitis (hay fever) in adults and children.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

See your doctor promptly if you have:

- Unilateral (one-sided) nasal obstruction or a nasal mass
- Persistent, clear nasal discharge from one nostril only — this may indicate a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) leak, especially after head injury, sinus or brain surgery, or forceful nose blowing

---

## Background

Allergic rhinitis is the most common allergic disorder, affecting approximately 20% of the New Zealand population.

It can:
- Significantly impact sleep, concentration, learning, work, and daily function
- Be effectively managed with appropriate treatment

There is a strong link with asthma:
- 50–80% of people with asthma also have allergic rhinitis
- 20–30% of people with allergic rhinitis also have asthma

Treating allergic rhinitis well is important for effective asthma management.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you by taking a history, examining you, and sometimes arranging allergy tests.

### Symptoms your doctor will ask about

- Sneezing, itchy nose, sniffing, rubbing nose
- Bilateral (both sides) clear runny nose
- Nasal blockage or congestion — nasal speech, mouth breathing, snoring
- Itchy throat, frequent need to clear throat
- Post-nasal drip, repeated sore throat

### Timing pattern

Your doctor will ask about timing to identify triggers:

- **Perennial (year-round):** Often triggered by indoor allergens — house dust mite, animal dander, moulds. Nocturnal and early morning symptoms suggest dust mite allergy.
- **Seasonal:** Often triggered by pollens or moulds, sometimes linked to specific environments (e.g., sports fields).
- **Occupational:** Triggered by chemicals, irritants, or allergens at work — symptoms improve when away from the workplace.

### Examination

Your doctor will look for:

- Darkened circles around the eyes, creases under lower eyelids (especially in children), transverse nasal crease from rubbing
- Pale, swollen nasal turbinates; clear watery discharge; nasal polyps
- Red eyes, swollen eyelids, conjunctival changes (allergic conjunctivitis)
- Mouth breathing or cobblestoning of the posterior throat
- Signs of eczema and asthma

### Allergy testing

Your doctor may recommend allergy testing if:

- A specific allergen is suspected and avoidance or desensitisation is practical
- The diagnosis is uncertain from history alone
- Medications are not working

Two types of tests are available:
- **Skin prick test (SPT):** A small amount of allergen extract is placed on the skin. Antihistamines must be stopped 3–4 days beforehand.
- **Allergen-specific IgE blood test (ssIgE):** A blood test for specific allergens — dust mite, pollen, mould, animal dander. Antihistamines do not affect the result.

### Other conditions to consider

Your doctor will also consider other causes of nasal symptoms:

- Chronic rhinosinusitis
- Hormonal changes (pregnancy, menstrual cycle, oral contraceptive pill)
- Drug-induced rhinitis (aspirin, NSAIDs, ACE inhibitors)
- Vasomotor rhinitis
- Granuloma or carcinoma (if red flags present)

Non-allergic and allergic rhinitis can coexist.

## Management

### Allergen avoidance and minimisation

**Avoidance** is most practical for occupational allergens, animals, and irritants (cigarette smoke, perfumes).

**Minimisation strategies** for common allergens:

- **Pollens:** Stay indoors during and after thunderstorms, on high pollen and windy days. Wear sunglasses. Wash and dry clothing inside. Keep windows closed where possible. Shower after outdoor activities.
- **House dust mite:** Wash bedding in hot water regularly. Use hard floors rather than carpet. Prefer wooden or plastic toys over soft toys.
- **Mould:** Remove visible mould with bleach or mould-reduction cleaners. Maximise natural ventilation. Remove indoor plants.

### Nasal sprays — first-line treatment

**Intranasal corticosteroid sprays** (e.g., beclometasone, budesonide, fluticasone) are the most effective treatment for nasal congestion. They need to be used regularly — effects build over days to weeks.

- More effective than antihistamines alone
- There is an additive benefit when used together with antihistamines
- For severe nasal blockage, your doctor may recommend a short course (5 days) of a decongestant nasal spray used 30 minutes before the corticosteroid spray

**How to use nasal sprays correctly:**
1. Prime the spray according to the instructions before first use
2. Shake the bottle before each use
3. Blow your nose first if blocked
4. Tilt your head slightly forward; gently insert the nozzle into the nostril
5. Use your right hand for your left nostril (and vice versa)
6. Aim the nozzle away from the centre of your nose, in line with the roof of your mouth — not upward
7. Avoid sniffing hard during or after spraying

### Antihistamines

Antihistamines work better when used regularly as prevention rather than just for symptom relief when needed.

### Saline nasal irrigation

- Clears allergens and mucus from the nasal passages
- Usually well tolerated and effective
- Can be used alongside other treatments
- Safe and inexpensive
- Larger volume (more than 60 mL) irrigation devices are more effective than simple sprays
- Homemade solution: half a teaspoon of salt and a quarter teaspoon of baking soda dissolved in 250 mL of warm water

### Other medications your doctor may consider

- **Oral corticosteroids:** Short courses (3–7 days) are occasionally used for severe nasal blockage or as short-term rescue therapy. Not suitable for frequent or long-term use.
- **Decongestants (nasal or oral):** Short-term use only (3–5 days) to relieve severe congestion. Long-term intranasal decongestant use can cause rebound nasal congestion.
- **Intranasal ipratropium:** Effective for profuse watery runny nose.
- **Intranasal sodium cromoglycate:** An option if other medications are ineffective.
- **Montelukast (leukotriene antagonist):** Can be tried if other medications are not working.

### Pregnancy and breastfeeding

Up to 20% of pregnant women develop rhinitis — usually in the second trimester, improving 2 weeks after delivery. Reassurance alone may be sufficient.

Your doctor will weigh up the benefit to you against potential risk to your baby before prescribing. Ideally, medications are avoided in the first trimester.

**Generally considered safe:**
- Saline nasal treatments
- Intranasal sodium cromoglycate
- Intranasal ipratropium
- Non-sedating oral antihistamines (second generation)
- Intranasal corticosteroid sprays

**Not recommended in pregnancy or breastfeeding:**
- Oral or intranasal decongestants (cross into breast milk)
- Intranasal azelastine (antihistamine — safety evidence lacking)

Always check with your doctor or pharmacist before using any medication during pregnancy or while breastfeeding.

### Allergen immunotherapy (desensitisation)

Allergen immunotherapy involves regular administration of allergen extracts to build tolerance. It is the only treatment that can change the natural course of the disease.

- Reduces symptom frequency and severity — on average a 50% reduction
- Usually requires 3–5 years of treatment for durable benefit
- Administered by subcutaneous injection or sublingual drops/tablets
- Must be initiated by a specialist with allergy training
- Additional benefits include reduced risk of new sensitisations and reduced risk of progression to asthma in children
- Only available in the private sector in New Zealand — not publicly funded

Your doctor can discuss whether this is appropriate for you and refer you if so.

### Annual allergy review

Your doctor will review your management annually, including medication doses and your treatment plan.

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you to an ear, nose, and throat (ENT/otolaryngology) specialist if:

- **Urgent:** Suspected CSF leak — persistent clear discharge from one nostril, worsened by bending forward, especially after head injury or surgery
- **Non-urgent:** Symptoms remain persistent and troublesome despite trying all available treatments, or you need repeated courses of oral corticosteroids
