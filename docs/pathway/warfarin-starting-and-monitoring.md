# Warfarin - Starting and Monitoring

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers how warfarin is started and monitored for long-term anticoagulation — including which patients are suitable, what the target INR should be, how dosing is adjusted, and how often INR needs to be checked.

> **Important:** All decisions about starting warfarin, adjusting doses, and determining when to stop are made by your doctor. This information is a guide only and should not be used to self-manage warfarin without medical supervision.

---

## What Is Warfarin Used For?

Warfarin is a long-term oral anticoagulant (blood thinner) used to reduce the risk of clot formation in:

- Venous thromboembolism (VTE) — a deep vein thrombosis or pulmonary embolism, or high risk of either
- Arterial embolism
- Atrial fibrillation — to reduce the risk of stroke
- Mechanical prosthetic heart valves

---

## Assessment Before Starting Warfarin

Your doctor will assess whether warfarin is the right treatment for you by working through several factors.

### 1. Suitability for warfarin

Co-morbidities that may affect INR control or bleeding risk include:
- Older age
- Alcohol abuse
- Significant liver or renal disease
- Low platelet count (thrombocytopenia)
- Low body weight or malnutrition
- Chronic diarrhoea
- Recent surgery, gastrointestinal bleed, or intracranial bleed

Warfarin is **not used in pregnancy**.

Your ability to manage dose adjustments will also be assessed — cognitive impairment or alcohol abuse can make warfarin more difficult to manage safely.

### 2. Is warfarin appropriate for the clinical situation?

Your doctor will balance the risk of bleeding against the risk of clot formation or stroke, and reassess this balance regularly.

For atrial fibrillation, two risk scores are used:
- **CHA2DS2-VASc score** — estimates stroke risk
- **HAS-BLED score** — estimates bleeding risk. A score of 3 or more indicates increased bleeding risk, but this alone should not exclude someone from anticoagulation — it means closer monitoring and addressing any modifiable risk factors.

### 3. Is a DOAC a better alternative?

Your doctor will consider whether a direct oral anticoagulant (DOAC) such as dabigatran or rivaroxaban may be more appropriate. Key differences:

| | Warfarin | Dabigatran | Rivaroxaban |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dosing** | Once daily | Twice daily | Once daily (twice daily for 3 weeks loading in acute VTE) |
| **Dietary restrictions** | Consistent vitamin K intake required | None | None |
| **Monitoring** | Regular INR blood tests | Creatinine and LFTs every 3–12 months | Creatinine and LFTs every 6 months |
| **Drug interactions** | Many | Fewer | Fewer |
| **Intracranial bleed risk** | Higher | Lower than warfarin | Lower than warfarin |
| **Antidote** | Vitamin K, fresh frozen plasma, or prothrombin complex concentrate | Idarucizumab | No direct antidote (haematology advice needed for life-threatening bleeding) |
| **Mechanical heart valves** | Suitable | Not recommended | Not recommended |

### 4. Drug interactions with warfarin

Warfarin interacts with many medications. Your doctor and pharmacist will review your medicines carefully. Always check before starting any new medication (including over-the-counter, herbal, or complementary medicines).

**Drugs that increase warfarin effect (raise INR / increase bleeding risk):**
- Antibacterials — especially co-trimoxazole, macrolides, metronidazole, quinolones
- Antifungals — fluconazole, miconazole (including gel and vaginal preparations)
- Antiplatelets — aspirin, clopidogrel, dipyridamole
- Cardiovascular drugs — amiodarone, anti-lipid drugs, propranolol, verapamil, antidepressants (especially SSRIs and venlafaxine)
- Analgesics — tramadol, paracetamol (in high doses), NSAIDs
- Gastrointestinal — omeprazole
- Herbal/alternative products — ginkgo, fenugreek, chamomile, dong quai, cranberry products, fish oils

**Drugs that decrease warfarin effect (lower INR):**
- Antibacterials — rifampicin
- Central nervous system — barbiturates, carbamazepine, phenytoin
- Others — vitamin K supplements, vitamin K-rich foods (avocado, broccoli), St John's wort

### 5. Antiplatelet medications

Combining antiplatelet medications (aspirin, clopidogrel, ticagrelor) with warfarin significantly increases bleeding risk. Your doctor will make a specific plan:

- If you had an acute coronary syndrome within the last 12 months and are on aspirin — continue aspirin unless bleeding risk is high; stop at 12 months
- If on both aspirin and clopidogrel or ticagrelor — specialist advice is needed before starting warfarin
- If you have a cardiac or vascular stent — the specialist who inserted the stent will advise on the antiplatelet plan
- If on antiplatelets for stable cardiovascular disease — these are usually stopped when warfarin is started

### 6. Blood tests before starting

Your doctor will arrange:
- Creatinine
- Full blood count (FBC)
- Liver function tests (LFT)
- Activated partial thromboplastin time (APTT)
- INR

## Target INR

INR is the measure of how "thin" your blood is on warfarin. Your doctor will set a target range based on your condition:

| Indication | Target INR |
|---|---|
| Pre- and perioperative anticoagulation | 1.5–2.0 |
| VTE treatment (DVT or pulmonary embolism) | 2.0–3.0 |
| Recurrent DVT/PE despite therapeutic INR | 3.0–4.0 |
| Atrial fibrillation | 2.0–3.0 |
| Mechanical aortic valve replacement | 2.5 (range 2.0–3.0) — or as advised by your specialist |
| Mechanical aortic valve + AF, previous thromboembolism, mitral stenosis, or low LV ejection fraction | 3.0 (range 2.5–3.5) — or as advised by your specialist |
| Mechanical mitral valve replacement | 3.0 (range 2.5–3.5) — or as advised by your specialist |

---

## Duration of Treatment

| Indication | Recommended duration |
|---|---|
| VTE prevention after knee replacement | 2 weeks |
| VTE prevention after hip replacement | 5 weeks |
| Isolated distal DVT | Variable — 2 weeks to 3 months (discussed with thrombosis team) |
| Provoked DVT or PE | 3 months |
| Unprovoked DVT or PE — low to moderate bleeding risk | Usually lifelong |
| Unprovoked DVT or PE — high bleeding risk | 3 months |
| Recurrent unprovoked DVT or PE | Usually lifelong |
| DVT or PE with active cancer | LMWH or rivaroxaban preferred (higher recurrence on warfarin) |
| Atrial fibrillation | Usually lifelong |
| Mechanical heart valves | Lifelong — do not stop without specialist advice |
| Arterial embolism | Lifelong |

Your doctor will review the risks and benefits of anticoagulation at least annually.

## Starting Warfarin

### Education before starting

Before starting warfarin, your doctor will ensure you understand:

- Why regular INR monitoring is essential, and how to keep a record of your results and doses
- How and where to get blood tests and how you will receive results
- The brand of warfarin prescribed — **Marevan** and **Coumadin** are not interchangeable, so always use the same brand
- Warning signs of bleeding or bruising to watch for
- Never to take any new medication (including over-the-counter medicines, herbal, or complementary products) without checking with your doctor or pharmacist first — especially aspirin and NSAIDs
- Keeping your diet reasonably consistent — particularly vitamin K-rich foods (green vegetables, avocados). Avoid fad diets or large changes in vegetable intake.
- Moderate regular alcohol is unlikely to affect warfarin, but binge drinking can raise your INR unpredictably
- Always telling other healthcare providers (dentists, pharmacists, specialists) that you are on warfarin

### Starting dose

For most patients, warfarin is started at **5 mg on day 1**.

**Bridging with Clexane (enoxaparin):** If the indication is VTE, arterial thrombosis, or restarting warfarin with a mechanical valve, Clexane injections are needed for a minimum of 5 days and until the INR has been therapeutic on 2 consecutive days.

A **higher starting dose (10 mg day 1 and 2)** may be used if the patient is: generally well, younger than 65, has no co-morbidities, and INR is less than 1.3.

A **lower starting dose (e.g. 3 mg)** is used if the patient is: aged 65 or older, has low body weight, has abnormal liver function, or is taking drugs that potentiate warfarin.

### Initiation dosing guide

The table below is a guide your doctor will use to adjust doses in the first 4 days based on daily INR results:

**Days 2–3:**

| INR | Dose for adults <65 years | Dose for adults ≥65 years |
|---|---|---|
| <1.8 (day 2) | 10 mg | 5 mg |
| 1.8–2.0 (day 2) | 1 mg | 1 mg |
| >2.0 (day 2) | 0 mg (omit) | 0 mg (omit) |
| <2.0 (day 3) | 10 mg | 5 mg |
| 2.0–2.5 (day 3) | 4–5 mg | 4 mg |
| 2.6–3.0 (day 3) | 3–3.5 mg | 3 mg |
| 3.1–3.5 (day 3) | 2–2.5 mg | 1–2 mg |
| >3.5 (day 3) | 0–1 mg | 0 mg |

**Day 4:** Your doctor will use a similar table to set the ongoing maintenance dose based on your INR.

Full warfarin effects take 3–4 days to appear. INR is checked daily in the first 5 days and adjusted accordingly. If INR is erratic by day 4, daily monitoring continues until stable.

If you have a poor diet (especially low vegetable intake), your doctor may consider low-dose vitamin K supplementation (e.g. 100 micrograms daily) to stabilise INR.

## Ongoing Monitoring

### How often is INR checked?

Your INR will be checked at increasing intervals as it stabilises:

1. Every 2–3 days until INR is therapeutic on 2 consecutive tests
2. Then every week until INR is therapeutic on 2 consecutive tests
3. Then every 2 weeks until INR is therapeutic on 2 consecutive tests
4. Then monthly, as long as INR remains stable

### Dose adjustment

If your INR drifts out of range, your doctor will adjust the weekly warfarin dose:

| INR (if target is 2.0–3.0) | INR (if target is 2.5–3.5) | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Less than 1.5 | Less than 2.0 | Increase weekly dose by 20%, plus a one-time top-up of 20% of weekly dose |
| 1.6–1.9 | 2.0–2.4 | Increase weekly dose by 10% |
| 2.0–3.0 | 2.5–3.5 | No change |
| 3.1–3.5 | 3.6–4.0 | No change — recheck in 1 week. If persistent, decrease weekly dose by 10–20% |
| 3.6–5.0 | 4.1–5.0 | Omit 1 dose. Decrease weekly dose by 10–20%. Recheck in 2–5 days |
| Greater than 5.0 | Greater than 5.0 | See Warfarin Over-anticoagulation pathway |

Dose changes take several days to affect INR. Changes should not be made more frequently than every 4–5 days in stable patients.

### When INR changes unexpectedly

Your doctor will look for causes including:
- Missed doses or accidental double-dosing
- New medications or changed doses (including supplements or herbal products)
- Major changes in diet or alcohol intake
- Illness such as heart failure, liver disease, thyroid disease, vomiting, or diarrhoea

### Starting or stopping medications

- If a new medication is started, INR should be rechecked 1 week later
- Stopping a medication that interacts with warfarin also affects INR
- Drugs with long half-lives (e.g. amiodarone) require an extended period of more frequent monitoring

### Atrial fibrillation

If you are on warfarin for atrial fibrillation, your doctor will reassess your bleeding risk using the HAS-BLED score at least annually. A score of 3 or more means closer monitoring and attention to modifiable risk factors — but does not automatically mean stopping anticoagulation.

### Mechanical heart valves

If you have a mechanical heart valve and your INR is below the therapeutic range, your doctor will seek cardiology advice about whether bridging with Clexane is needed.

### Point-of-care INR testing

Some community pharmacies offer INR testing using a fingerprick device (CoaguChek). If INR is above 4.0 on this device, a laboratory venous blood sample should confirm the result — accuracy of point-of-care devices decreases at higher INR levels.

## Procedures and Surgery

If you are on warfarin and planning a procedure, tell your doctor in advance:

- **Major surgery, spinal/epidural anaesthesia, or invasive procedures:** requires specific warfarin management planning (see Peri-procedural Management of Warfarin, DOACs, and Antiplatelets pathway)
- **Dental procedures** (scaling, fillings, minor oral surgery): see Warfarin – Dental Extractions pathway
