# Anal Fissure

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

An anal fissure is a longitudinal tear in the skin of the anal canal. Acute fissures often heal with conservative treatment; chronic fissures (lasting more than 6 weeks) usually need topical medication to break the cycle of sphincter spasm and poor healing.

---

## What Is an Anal Fissure?

- A tear in the lining of the anal canal just below the dentate line
- Acute, superficial fissures often heal within a few weeks with simple treatment
- Chronic fissures (lasting >6 weeks or recurring) are usually deeper — sphincter spasm reduces blood flow and prevents healing
- The most common symptom is sharp, intense pain during and after defecation, often lasting up to 1 hour — this distinguishes fissures from haemorrhoids, which are generally not painful unless prolapsed and strangulated

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess:

**Symptoms:**
- Sharp, intense pain during and after defecation
- Bright red blood on toilet paper after defecation

**Risk factors for primary fissures** (benign, related to local trauma):
- Constipation with hard stools
- Prolonged diarrhoea
- Repetitive injury or penetration (e.g., anoreceptive intercourse)
- Pregnancy with vaginal delivery

**Risk factors for secondary fissures** (rare, atypical — suspect if fissure is not in the midline):
- Previous anal surgery
- Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) or Crohn's disease
- Granulomatous diseases
- Squamous cell carcinoma
- Psoriasis or other skin conditions
- Infections: syphilis, tuberculosis, herpes, cytomegalovirus (CMV), chlamydia, HIV

**Physical examination:**
- Your doctor will gently spread the buttocks to inspect the anal canal (usually in the left lateral position)
- Most fissures occur at the posterior or anterior midline of the anus
- Acute fissures appear as fresh, superficial lacerations with well-defined edges
- Chronic fissures have raised edges exposing underlying sphincter muscle, often with a skin tag ("sentinel pile") at the lower end
- A digital rectal examination may be omitted if too painful

If you have recurrent anal ulcers, a herpes PCR swab may be taken to exclude genital herpes.

---

## Management

The goal of treatment is to break the cycle of sphincter spasm, improving blood flow so the fissure can heal. Topical ointments must be applied consistently for 4–6 weeks — they are not "as needed" treatments.

### Step 1: Conservative measures (all patients)

Your doctor may recommend:

- **Dietary fibre and fluid** — softening stools reduces trauma with each bowel motion
- **Warm baths** — frequent sitz baths may relieve pain and help relax the sphincter
- **Stool softeners** — lactulose or fibre laxatives (e.g., Konsyl D) if indicated

Note: coloxyl (docusate) is not first-line for anal fissures due to its stimulant action.

### Step 2: Topical medications

**Glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) 0.2% rectal ointment** — first-line:
- If the fissure has persisted more than 3 weeks, GTN is funded on Special Authority
- Apply daily for 4–6 weeks
- Common side effect: headaches — these are usually tolerable and subside after about 1 week of use. Paracetamol can help

**Diltiazem 2% paste** — alternative (especially if GTN causes headaches):
- As effective as GTN, with fewer headaches
- Suitable for pregnant and breastfeeding women
- Apply daily for 4–6 weeks
- Made to order — your doctor will write a special prescription

**How to apply topical treatments:**
- Apply into the very bottom of the anal canal using just the tip of a cotton bud
- Start with application just before bed, then increase to 3 times a day
- Apply regardless of symptoms for 6–8 weeks (use the full course)
- Lignocaine 2% gel (available over the counter, ~$25) may be applied at the same time for additional pain relief
- Wash hands thoroughly after application

**Other ointments** (Proctosedyl, Ultraproct):
- These provide symptomatic relief only and do not treat the underlying sphincter spasm — they are not a substitute for GTN or diltiazem

*Note (Sept 2025): Ultraproct is currently out of stock until at least early 2026. Proctosedyl is available as a funded alternative.*

### Step 3: If not improving after 6 weeks

If the fissure has not responded to 6 weeks of topical treatment, your doctor will refer you to a general surgeon for further assessment. Surgical options (such as botulinum toxin injection or sphincterotomy) may be considered.

---

## When to See Your Doctor Promptly

- Pain that is severe and preventing normal daily activities
- Fissure that is not in the typical midline position (may indicate a secondary cause)
- Recurrent anal ulcers
- Fissure not improving after completing a full course of topical treatment
