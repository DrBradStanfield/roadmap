# Familial Cancer Syndromes

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: July 2019.*

A familial cancer syndrome is an inherited cause of cancer: a cancer-predisposing gene change (mutation) passed down in a family. An example is a pathogenic mutation in the BRCA 1 or 2 gene.

> **Always discuss your family history and any symptoms with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## If You Have Symptoms Now

If you have any current symptoms, see your doctor about them first. Your doctor will manage current symptoms before fully assessing your family history.

---

## Background

- Most cancer is not due to an inherited mutation. For example, only 5% of all breast and colon cancer is due to an inherited mutation.
- The search for a mutation has to start in a family member who has had cancer.
- Predictive testing is only available for adult relatives once a mutation has been identified in the family.
- People with a potentially high risk of a familial cancer syndrome usually have:
  - Multiple relatives on the same side of the family with cancers of the same or a related type.
  - Cancers occurring at a younger age (not counting childhood cancers).
  - 2 or more primary cancers of the same or different type.

---

## Assessment

**Your doctor may:**
- Ask about any current symptoms.
- Sketch a 3-generation family tree of cancer in your family.
- Check for specific risk factors for uncommon familial cancer syndromes.

### Specific risk factors

- A blood relative who is a known carrier of a cancer-predisposing mutation.
- A rare tumour diagnosed aged younger than 45 years, for example:
  - Adrenal cancer
  - Choroid plexus carcinoma
  - Glioblastoma
  - Paraganglioma
  - Phaeochromocytoma
  - Retinoblastoma
  - Sarcoma
- A rare tumour at any age, with a close relative who had a similar tumour.
- Medullary thyroid cancer at any age.
- Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry.
- You or a first-degree relative with lobular breast cancer diagnosed aged younger than 50 years.
- You or a first-degree relative with gastric (stomach) cancer diagnosed aged younger than 40 years.

---

## Management

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Managing your risk according to the type of cancer in the family, for example familial breast or ovarian cancer syndromes, or familial colorectal or endometrial cancer syndromes.
- If the person with cancer is terminally ill, contacting the genetic service to consider DNA banking (storing a blood sample).

---

## When to Refer

Your doctor may refer you for genetic assessment by a genetic health service or a familial gastrointestinal cancer service if you may be at high risk of familial cancer. For example:
- You have any of the specific risk factors above.
- Multiple relatives on the same side of the family have cancers of the same or a related type.
- Cancers have occurred at a younger age, excluding childhood cancers.
- There have been 2 or more primary cancers of the same or different type.

Public genetic services currently see people who have a relative with a confirmed hereditary cause; your doctor can advise whether a referral is possible.

Private genetic services are available for people who do not meet public service criteria.

If you do not meet the criteria for genetic referral, your doctor will follow the usual population screening guidelines, such as breast screening and bowel screening.
