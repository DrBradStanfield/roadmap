# Recurrent Staphylococcal Infection

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway is about managing recurrent skin and soft tissue infections confirmed to be caused by *Staphylococcus aureus* — including methicillin-resistant *S. aureus* (MRSA) and methicillin-sensitive *S. aureus* (MSSA).

---

## Background

- About 25% of the healthy population carry *S. aureus*, with even higher rates in children. Most carriage is asymptomatic; the most common sites are the nostrils and skin flexures.
- The definition of recurrent staphylococcal infection is subjective. This pathway is most useful if you have had at least 2 infections in a 6-month period.
- Aggressive strains (including MRSA) can cause recurring boils and soft tissue infections in otherwise healthy people. Episodes may continue for up to 2 years, but eventually some immunity develops or the aggressive strain is replaced by a more benign one.
- Generally there is no detectable immune deficiency causing these infections.
- Recurrent infections occur because the normal skin barrier has broken down. Non-intact skin (from eczema, wounds, or insect bites) provides an entry point for infection and increases *S. aureus* density on the skin — making decolonisation much harder.
- *S. aureus* spreads easily within households and close-contact groups (e.g., sports teams, childcare centres). Unaffected household members can carry and transmit the organism without getting infections themselves.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will confirm the diagnosis based on a history of recurrent skin or soft tissue infections (typically boils, abscesses, or impetigo) together with a confirmed *S. aureus* culture from a swab or pus sample.

**History your doctor will ask about:**

- Underlying skin conditions (eczema, dry skin) or other contributing factors
- Any previous MRSA or multi-drug resistant organism (MDRO) infection
- How quickly infections come on and whether there are any warning signs
- Whether you have ever become seriously unwell or been hospitalised
- Whether antibiotics help, and whether full courses have been completed
- Whether other household members have recurrent skin infections or chronic skin problems
- What impact the infections are having — pain, time off work or school, hospital admissions
- Risk factors such as diabetes, immunosuppression, or iron deficiency
- Potential routes of re-colonisation — household members, pets, contact sports

**Physical examination typically includes:**

- Assessment of the skin, looking for eczema, dry or cracked skin, tinea, psoriasis, or scabies
- Noting any pattern in which body areas are affected (areas of abnormal skin, where hair removal is done, where clothing rubs, or areas of repeated skin abrasion)
- If recurrent leg infections, checking for fungal infection, swelling, or poor circulation

**Investigations your doctor may organise:**

- A swab or pus aspirate from any active lesion
- Swabs from both nostrils (to check for nasal carriage)
- If decolonisation is being considered, a throat swab to check for throat carriage
- Routine blood tests are not generally needed

**Alternative diagnoses to consider if *S. aureus* cannot be confirmed:**
Sebaceous cyst, herpes simplex, eczema herpeticum, acne, hidradenitis suppurativa, and other skin conditions.

---

## Management

Your doctor will focus primarily on restoring skin integrity — even minor skin damage can trigger infections in susceptible people.

### Step 1 — Treat any active infection first

Your doctor may consider a short course of oral antibiotics (5 to 7 days) if you have fever, surrounding redness (cellulitis), a skin infection on the face, or a condition such as diabetes. Antibiotic choice is based on swab results:

- **For MSSA:** flucloxacillin is the preferred antibiotic; cefalexin is an alternative for adults who cannot tolerate penicillin
- **For MRSA (or MSSA resistant to above):** trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole, or doxycycline (adults only)
- Most boils can be drained without antibiotics and do not require antibiotic treatment

### Step 2 — Skin care and prevention

Your doctor will advise you on the following measures to reduce future infections:

**Personal hygiene:**
- Bathe or shower daily
- Keep fingernails short and wash hands regularly
- Keep cuts and lesions clean and covered
- Do not share razors, towels, or clothing that may have contacted infected skin
- Wash clothing, towels, and bed linen regularly — hot water is best, or cold wash and dry in the sun

**Protect the skin barrier:**
- Avoid soap and bubble baths — use a soap substitute (e.g., emulsifying ointment) or soap-free wash (e.g., Cetaphil, Dermasoft)
- Avoid shaving with a blade, which damages hair follicles — electric razors or avoiding hair removal may help
- Avoid picking, scratching, or skin abrasion
- Moisturise affected and dry areas at least twice daily — apply in the direction of hair growth (down the limb)
- Use a spoon or spatula to decant creams to avoid contaminating the tub

**Reducing the bacterial load on skin:**

Twice-weekly bleach baths or antiseptic skin cleanser washes can reduce the *S. aureus* burden:

- **Bleach baths** — a diluted solution added to the bath
- **Antiseptic skin cleansers in the shower:**
  - Chlorhexidine gluconate: 4% surgical scrub solution for adults; 1% obstetric lotion for children, infants, and elderly with sensitive skin (unfunded)
  - Octenidine body wash — available over the counter (unfunded)
  - Turn the shower off for 2 minutes to allow contact time before rinsing
  - Always moisturise afterwards to prevent skin drying

If skin irritation occurs, stop the antiseptic wash — dryness and irritation can increase *S. aureus* on the skin.

**Check household members** — skin infections are often present in other family members, and all affected members should be treated.

### Step 3 — If infections persist despite the above, your doctor may consider:

**Suppressive antibiotics** — a 2 to 4 week course to allow the skin to heal while skin care is being established. Options include flucloxacillin, cefalexin, doxycycline, or trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole, chosen based on swab sensitivities.

**Decolonisation** — only considered in motivated patients with intact skin and no active lesions. Decolonisation is unlikely to work if:
- There is throat carriage of *S. aureus*
- Household members cannot also be treated
- There are crowded living conditions where re-colonisation is likely

Decolonisation involves:
- Daily showering with antiseptic skin cleanser (chlorhexidine or octenidine) for 1 week, or bleach baths 2 to 3 times during the week
- **Nasal ointment** applied to the inside of both nostrils 2 to 3 times daily for 5 to 7 days — using fusidic acid ointment, mupirocin, or povidone-iodine (chosen based on sensitivity results)
- **Environmental decontamination** on days 1 and 6: washing all bedding, towels, and clothes (hot wash or cold wash dried in sun/dryer), and a thorough household clean especially in bedrooms and bathrooms

### When to seek specialist input

Your doctor will seek infectious diseases advice (adults) or paediatric advice (children) if recurrent infections persist despite following the treatment plan.

If hidradenitis suppurativa is suspected with severe or recurrent abscesses and scarring, a dermatology assessment may be sought.
