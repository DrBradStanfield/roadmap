# Psychosis in Children and Youth

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the recognition and initial management of psychosis and emerging psychotic disorders in children and young people.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

Seek urgent assessment if any of the following are present:

- Clear cognitive decline
- Disordered thinking
- Severe apathy or profound disengagement from all areas of life
- High distress or agitation

---

## Background

Psychotic and bipolar disorders typically first present between the ages of 14 and 24 years. Presentation earlier than this is extremely rare.

In adolescence, early presentations of bipolar or psychotic disorders tend to be undifferentiated for some time. It may take several years before a definitive diagnosis can be made. Diagnostic flux is expected over the first 2 years — the condition is often called "psychosis not otherwise specified" until a diagnosis can be confirmed.

In adolescence, psychosis may present as:

- Delusions and thought disorder (classic presentation)
- A profound drop-off in cognitive function with deterioration in adaptive function and less clear positive symptoms
- A significant drop-off in function, attenuated symptoms, and brief limited intermittent psychotic symptoms (BLIPS) that the patient can recover from spontaneously without treatment

A psychosis syndrome can be present for weeks, months, or years before being recognised as an episode of psychosis.

Hallucinations (especially derogatory ones) are not necessarily an indication of a psychotic disorder in teenagers — they can occur in trauma and severe anxiety presentations.

A protracted duration of untreated psychosis (DUP) is associated with poorer outcomes, especially when combined with a significant drop in functioning.

The onset of serious and persisting mental illness in adolescence can interfere with development and limit life potential. It can also lead to:

- Poor educational achievement
- Social and vocational derailment
- High suicide risk
- Violence and self-harm
- Substance abuse
- Chronicity of symptoms

## Assessment

Assessment of mental health concerns usually takes place over several appointments and often involves both the GP and practice nurse.

Your doctor will assess whether immediate or emergency management is needed first. If the patient is clearly distressed, agitated, and psychotic, emergency assessment takes priority over detailed history.

### History

Your doctor will obtain a full history, including:

**Symptoms — onset, severity, duration, impact, and triggers/stressors:**

Triggers and stressors to explore include:
- Recent life changes, new trauma, abuse, or bullying
- Major loss or change in family context
- Academic exams or general life stressors
- Substance use or a change in substance use

**Prodromal period** (weeks to months of non-specific symptoms causing increasing disability):
- Low or flat mood, irritability
- Poor concentration or other cognitive changes
- Constant worrying and internal preoccupation
- Sleep and appetite disturbance
- Impairment of general functioning
- Magical or suspicious thinking
- Attenuated symptoms or brief limited intermittent psychotic symptoms (BLIPS)

**Psychotic features:**
- Delusions
- Thought disorder
- Hallucinations (noting that in adolescents these are less predictive of psychosis and may occur in grief, anxiety, or trauma)
- Odd or paranoid thinking
- Marked behaviour change with no apparent logic or benefit to the individual
- Negative symptoms (e.g., avolition, poor self-care, flat affect, loss of interest in usual activities, social isolation)

**Manic features:**
- Mood lability and irritability
- Decreased sleep, racing thoughts, pressure to speak
- Grandiose behaviours
- Hyper-sexuality, out-of-character risk-taking, or aggression
- Self-injurious behaviours, impulsivity, flight of ideas

**General signs of deteriorating mental health** (may be insidious or sudden):
- Worsening school performance
- Decline in cognitive functioning
- Deterioration in adaptive function (hygiene, eating, general organisation)
- Increase in risk-taking, new impairment of sleep pattern
- Escalating drug or alcohol use
- A sense from the patient or their family/whānau that something is not quite right
- Marked change in personality — the patient doesn't seem themselves

**Substance use:** Drug use is extremely common in adolescents presenting with psychosis. Your doctor will ask about:
- Synthetic cannabinoids, cannabis
- Amphetamines, MDMA (ecstasy), hallucinogens
- Alcohol, opioids

**Background and risk factors:**
- Family history — bipolar or psychotic disorder is 4–6 times more likely if a first-degree relative has one of these disorders
- Associated mental health disorders, attachment difficulties, trauma
- Abnormal neurodevelopmental history (e.g., autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disability, history of head injury or other neurological concern)

**Collateral history** is gathered from family or friends where possible, as the patient may be a poor source of information.

**Suicide risk:** There is a high association with suicide in psychosis. Younger males are at higher risk. Risk is raised if hallucinations are command-type or the patient experiences a passivity phenomenon (feeling controlled by an outside force).

### Physical Examination and Medical Causes

Your doctor will examine for medical causes of the symptoms, including:

- **Neurological:** Brain tumour, epilepsy, migraine, head trauma, brain abscess
- **Endocrine:** Phaeochromocytoma, thyroid disorder, pregnancy
- **Infective:** HIV, Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)
- **Systemic:** Hepatic encephalopathy, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)
- **Chemical:** Drug or toxin exposure, drug adverse effect, withdrawal

### Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- Full blood count (FBC), ferritin
- Creatinine, sodium, potassium
- Calcium, phosphate
- Liver function tests (LFT)
- HbA1c
- Lipids
- Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH)
- Urine microscopy
- Urine drug screen

A serious psychiatric disorder is strongly suspected if there is a combination of cognitive and adaptive function drop-off alongside normal examination and no identifiable medical cause.

## Management

Your doctor will take a stepwise approach depending on severity:

**If there is immediate danger:** If the patient is violent or there is a threat of harm to self or others, emergency services (111) will be called.

**If acutely psychotic or manic with clear risks, or profound reduction in cognitive or adaptive function:** Your doctor will arrange urgent child and youth (if aged under 18) or adult (if aged 18 or over) mental health assessment.

**If there is concern about suicide:** Your doctor will involve family and support people and arrange contact with the appropriate crisis team.

**If there is any suggestion of an emerging psychotic disorder:** Your doctor will refer early for early psychosis intervention. Early referral is important because:
- A longer duration of untreated psychosis and younger age at onset are associated with poorer long-term outcomes
- Early intervention can relieve suffering and prevent the development of substance and anxiety disorders
- Behavioural and psychological therapies can address disruptive behaviour and its impact on family

**Regarding medication:** Antidepressants can provoke mania and should only be initiated by a specialist if there is any diagnostic uncertainty. Often a combination of mood stabilisers and antipsychotics is required for bipolar disorder in adolescents. Your doctor will avoid starting psychotropic medications without specialist input.

**Ongoing care:** If the patient is already under specialised mental health care, your doctor will contact their case worker or psychiatrist if there are any concerns about deterioration. If previously discharged, the discharge letter will contain contact details for re-engaging with the service.
