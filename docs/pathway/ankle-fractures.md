# Ankle Fractures

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers management of a diagnosed ankle fracture, including how to assess stability, when urgent orthopaedic review is needed, and how to manage stable fractures in primary care.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Orthopaedic Assessment

Your doctor will arrange urgent orthopaedic assessment if any of the following are present:

- Neurovascular compromise (numbness, loss of circulation to the foot)
- Open fracture (bone through skin)
- Intra-articular (joint) damage
- Bimalleolar or trimalleolar fractures
- Displaced fracture
- Unstable injury (more than one part of the ankle ring structure is injured)
- AO Type C fracture
- Talus fracture
- Proximal fibula fracture (Maisonneuve fracture)

Before transfer, your doctor will give adequate pain relief and apply a splint or backslab for stability during transport.

---

## Assessment

### Is the fracture stable or unstable?

Your doctor will assess:

- A single, undisplaced fracture is usually stable
- The ankle can be thought of as a **ring structure** consisting of the medial malleolus, medial ligament, lateral ligaments, lateral malleolus, and tibiofibular ligaments — if two or more of these are broken (by fracture or ligament rupture), the injury is unstable and requires surgery
- Any neurovascular compromise, or open fracture, is looked for
- Associated injuries are checked, as these change management

### Fracture Classification (AO/Weber)

**Distal fibula fractures** are the most common type, classified by where the fracture sits relative to the ankle joint:

- **Type A** — below the joint line. Horizontal avulsion fractures. Usually stable unless accompanied by another injury (e.g., displaced medial malleolus fracture).
- **Type B** — at the joint line. May be unstable depending on whether there is ligament injury or other fractures on the medial side.
- **Type C** — above the ankle joint. Unstable; involves rupture of the tibiofibular ligament.

**Proximal fibula fracture (Maisonneuve fracture):**
- Proximal fibula fracture alongside a medial malleolar fracture or deltoid ligament disruption
- Caused by an external rotation injury
- Associated with syndesmosis disruption — these are unstable and require orthopaedic assessment

**Talus fractures:**
- Any form (avulsion, talar dome, talar neck, lateral talus/snowboarder's fracture) requires orthopaedic assessment

**Tibial fractures (medial or posterior malleolus):**
- Often associated with ligamentous injuries
- Posterior malleolar fractures rarely occur in isolation
- May lead to non-union or chronic ankle instability

## Management

### When orthopaedic assessment is needed

See the Red Flags section above. Your doctor will also seek orthopaedic advice for AO Type B fractures if there is medial malleolar tenderness on examination, talar shift, or an associated posterior malleolus fracture.

### Stable fractures — management by type

**AO Type A fractures:**
- A cast may not be needed depending on the level of discomfort, as these are stable
- If a very small avulsion fracture: your doctor may consider a moonboot or tubigrip, depending on patient age
- Otherwise: below knee plaster of Paris (POP) for 4 weeks. If significant swelling, a backslab for 1 week then POP. Weight bearing from 1–2 weeks.

**AO Type B fractures (without red flags):**
- Below knee split POP
- X-ray (including mortise view) at 1 week — to confirm plaster completion and check for fracture shift
- X-ray at 2 weeks — to check ankle congruency before initial callus healing
- X-ray and plaster change at 3 weeks
- X-ray and plaster removal at 6 weeks
- Partial weight bearing with cast shoe for the last 7 days only

### Rehabilitation

Once the cast is removed:

- Walking and weight bearing as tolerated
- Regular pain medication to allow increased activity
- Ankle stretching and range-of-motion exercises

Your doctor may consider **physiotherapy** if:
- Persistent pain, swelling, weakness, or stiffness
- You are off work
- You cannot hop 3 times or perform a single leg squat

Your doctor may also consider:
- **Podiatry assessment** for footwear evaluation and possible orthotics if there are poor biomechanics
- **Occupational therapy** if you are having difficulty with everyday activities (self-care, household tasks, driving, work, study, or leisure)
- **Work assessment and rehabilitation** if returning to or sustaining work is a concern
