# Familial Breast or Ovarian Cancer Syndromes

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers how doctors assess and manage patients who may have a hereditary risk of breast or ovarian cancer, including when genetic referral is appropriate.

---

## Background

About 5% of all breast cancer is hereditary, mainly caused by mutations in the BRCA1 and BRCA2 genes, but also other cancer genes (e.g., TP53, PTEN, PALB2). These mutations also cause about 15% of all high-grade serous ovarian cancer.

Searching for a familial mutation must start in an affected family member, because the BRCA genes have more than 3,000 different mutations identified to date.

Each child of a BRCA carrier has a 1 in 2 chance of inheriting the mutation.

**BRCA mutations:**

- Are more likely if there is a personal or family history of early-onset breast or ovarian cancer, bilateral breast cancer, male breast cancer, and/or both breast and ovarian cancer
- Occur in about 1 in every 500 to 800 people (equally in males and females)
- Result in an increased lifetime risk of developing breast and ovarian cancer:
  - In women, breast cancer risk is up to 70% (vs. 10% in the general population)
  - In women, ovarian cancer risk is up to 40% (vs. 1.6% in the general population)
  - In men, breast cancer risk is up to 7% (vs. <1% in the general population); men are also at risk of an aggressive form of prostate cancer

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess for hereditary cancer risk by taking a personal and family history. This is not a pathway for people with current symptoms — if you have breast or ovarian symptoms, your doctor will assess those first.

### Personal History

Your doctor will ask about any history of:

- Breast cancer diagnosed before age 40, especially triple-negative (oestrogen, progesterone, and HER2 receptor negative) histology
- Bilateral breast cancer with first diagnosis before age 50, or alongside family history of breast or ovarian cancer
- Epithelial or high-grade serous ovarian cancer
- Fallopian tube cancer
- Primary peritoneal cancer
- Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry (from Central and Eastern Europe)
- Male breast cancer

### Family History

Your doctor will consider relatives on each side of the family separately. An accurate family history includes:

- All primary cancers in first-degree and second-degree relatives on both sides of the family
- Age at diagnosis for each cancer
- Whether you are a first-degree blood relative of a known gene mutation carrier (e.g., BRCA1 or BRCA2)

| Degree | Relatives |
|--------|-----------|
| First-degree | Mother, Father, Daughter, Son, Sister, Brother |
| Second-degree | Grandparent, Grandchild, Aunt, Uncle, Niece, Nephew, Half-siblings |
| Third-degree | Great-grandparents, Great aunts/uncles, First cousins, Grand-nieces/nephews |

Family history is updated regularly — it may change over time.

## Referral for Genetic Assessment

Your doctor will assess eligibility for referral to a genetics service using eviQ guidelines:

- [Referral Guidelines for Breast Cancer Risk Assessment and Genetic Testing (eviQ)](https://www.eviq.org.au/cancer-genetics/referral-guidelines/1620-referral-guidelines-for-breast-cancer-risk-as)
- [Referral Guidelines for Ovarian Cancer Risk Assessment and Genetic Testing (eviQ)](https://www.eviq.org.au/cancer-genetics/referral-guidelines/1905-referral-guidelines-for-ovarian-cancer-risk-a)

If you meet the criteria, your doctor will request a genetic assessment from the Genetic Health Service New Zealand (GHSNZ). You may be offered advice or an appointment.

---

## Prevention and Screening

Regardless of genetic referral, your doctor may advise:

- **General preventive strategies:** healthy diet, minimal alcohol intake, regular exercise
- **Enrolment in national screening programmes** such as breast screening
