# Urticaria (Hives)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of urticaria (hives) — itchy, raised wheals on the skin that can occur acutely or persist as a chronic condition.

---

## Background

Urticaria (commonly called hives) consists of well-circumscribed, itchy (pruritic) wheals that can appear anywhere on the body. Individual wheals typically resolve within 24 to 48 hours, though new ones may emerge as earlier ones fade.

About 20% of people will experience urticaria at some point in their lives. Most cases resolve spontaneously. Urticaria is classified as:

- **Acute** — symptoms present for less than 6 weeks
- **Chronic** — symptoms persist for more than 6 weeks

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

Discuss with your doctor urgently or seek emergency care if urticaria is accompanied by:

- Signs of anaphylaxis (throat swelling, difficulty breathing, collapse)
- Individual wheals lasting longer than 24 hours, leaving bruising or scarring, or that are tender to touch — these may suggest urticarial vasculitis (a rare but serious condition)
- Fever, blood in urine (haematuria), coughing up blood (haemoptysis), or joint pain (arthralgia) — also suggestive of urticarial vasculitis

---

## Assessment

Your healthcare provider will ask about:

- Onset, duration, and frequency of the rash
- Possible **allergic triggers** — foods (seafood, peanuts, tree nuts, egg, soy, milk), medications (antibiotics, NSAIDs, opiates), or insect stings
- Possible **non-allergic triggers** — infections (viral, bacterial, parasitic), physical stimuli (heat, cold, exercise, sunlight, water, pressure), or stress
- Exacerbating factors such as tight clothing or alcohol
- Associated features, including swelling (angio-oedema)
- Previous episodes of urticaria and any treatments tried
- Relevant medical history (e.g., hypothyroidism, autoimmune conditions)

Your doctor will examine the rash for typical features:

- Transient, itchy pink or white wheals, sometimes with a surrounding red flare
- Well-circumscribed lesions varying in size, sometimes coalescing
- Individual lesions lasting up to 24 hours, with new lesions appearing as earlier ones resolve

Other causes of itchy rash (e.g., scabies, dermographism) will be considered and ruled out.

### Investigations

- Single episodes of urticaria do not usually need investigation.
- Skin prick testing may be considered if there is a clear identifiable trigger and avoidance is possible — it is unlikely to be helpful in chronic urticaria.
- If urticarial vasculitis is suspected, your doctor may arrange blood tests (CRP or ESR) and a skin biopsy (two punch biopsies for light microscopy and immunofluorescence).

---

## Acute Urticaria (Less Than 6 Weeks)

Acute urticaria can be allergic or non-allergic:

- **Allergic** — typically occurs within 30 to 60 minutes of exposure to a trigger
- **Non-allergic** — commonly triggered by infections, physical stimuli, or stress

### Management

1. Identify and avoid any triggers.
2. Your doctor will recommend **non-sedating oral antihistamines** (e.g., cetirizine or loratadine). Note that cetirizine causes sedation in around 10% of people.
3. If antihistamines do not provide adequate relief, your healthcare provider may consider a short course of oral corticosteroids (e.g., prednisone for 3 to 7 days), particularly if angio-oedema is also present.

---

## Chronic Urticaria (More Than 6 Weeks)

- Affects up to 40% of patients alongside angio-oedema
- An external cause is often not identified
- Usually self-limiting — up to 50% of patients are in remission by one year

### Management

1. **Education and reassurance:**
   - Avoid exacerbating factors (heat, hot showers, tight clothing, NSAIDs)
   - Diet restriction is generally not recommended unless a specific food trigger has been clearly identified
   - Life-threatening allergy symptoms are rare in chronic urticaria
   - Emotional distress about symptoms is common

2. **Non-sedating oral antihistamines** are the first-line treatment. Your doctor may recommend higher doses than usual if standard doses are not effective — up to four times the standard dose may be needed in some cases (off-label use).

3. Many patients with chronic urticaria require long-term antihistamines. Your doctor will consider weaning the dose once you have been symptom-free for 3 to 4 weeks.

4. If antihistamines are not sufficient, your healthcare provider may consider **second-line treatments**:
   - H2-receptor antagonists (e.g., famotidine) added to antihistamines
   - Montelukast — used in combination with an antihistamine, not as monotherapy
   - A short course of oral corticosteroids (e.g., 2 to 10 days) in severe cases or high-demand situations — long-term or repeated steroid courses are not recommended due to side effects

5. **Avoid topical treatments** such as corticosteroid or antihistamine creams — their effectiveness is unclear and they may cause skin changes or contact dermatitis.

6. Specialist referral (immunology or paediatrics) may be recommended if you:
   - Do not respond adequately to high-dose antihistamines
   - Have other systemic symptoms suggesting an autoimmune condition

   Before a specialist appointment, your doctor may ask you to track your symptoms using the [Urticaria Activity Score (UAS7)](https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/10226/urticaria-activity-score-uas) or the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) — this helps determine eligibility for advanced treatments.

### Advanced Treatment: Omalizumab

For severe chronic urticaria that does not respond to antihistamines, omalizumab (a monoclonal antibody injection given every 4 weeks) may be an option. It is approved for adults and children aged 12 and older with chronic idiopathic urticaria. Discuss with your specialist whether this is appropriate for you.

---

## Urticarial Vasculitis

Urticarial vasculitis (UV) is a rare but serious condition. It should be suspected if wheals:

- Last longer than 24 hours
- Leave bruising or scarring
- Are tender to touch

Other features may include fever, joint pain, blood in the urine, or coughing up blood. ESR/CRP is typically elevated; a skin biopsy is diagnostic.

Your healthcare provider will arrange specialist assessment (immunology for adults, paediatrics for children) if urticarial vasculitis is suspected.
