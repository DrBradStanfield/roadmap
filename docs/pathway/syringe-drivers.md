# Syringe Drivers

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Syringe drivers (e.g., the NIKI T34) are small portable pumps that deliver medications continuously under the skin (subcutaneously). They are used in palliative care when patients are no longer able to take oral medications reliably. Your palliative care team will assess whether a syringe driver is appropriate and manage its setup and monitoring.

---

## Background

Syringe drivers allow medications to be given continuously to maintain stable symptom control. If symptoms are uncontrolled before or during use, additional breakthrough doses of medication can be given separately.

Not all people with palliative needs or who are dying require this route — many are managed well with oral medications for longer than expected.

---

## Indications

A syringe driver may be considered when:

- There is a **pain crisis**
- The patient has **persistent inability to take oral medications** despite attempts at management, such as:
  - Persistent nausea or vomiting
  - Dysphagia (swallowing difficulty) or aspiration risk
  - Severe oral lesions
- **Poor absorption** of oral medications
- **Frequent breakthrough medication** is needed with escalating symptoms in the terminal phase
- The patient is **unconscious**
- Oral formulations are **not available** for required medications (e.g., ketamine, octreotide)

---

## Advantages and Disadvantages

**Advantages:**
- Reduces the burden of multiple oral medications
- Generally well tolerated
- Patient can remain independent and mobile
- Syringe requires only daily changing
- More comfortable than repeated injections
- Multiple medications can be combined
- Stable drug levels provide better symptom control

**Disadvantages:**
- Can be associated with negative perceptions and fears about dying
- The device can be cumbersome, especially for mobile patients
- Technical issues can arise (batteries, blockages, leakages) requiring trained support
- Some drug combinations are incompatible
- Risk of site reactions and local infections

---

## Management

### If a syringe driver cannot be started immediately

If a driver is not immediately available (e.g., out of hours, death is imminent, trained personnel unavailable), medications can be given as required subcutaneously, or occasionally sublingually.

### Arranging a syringe driver

**At home:**
- Arranged via district nursing or hospice services
- The prescribing doctor is responsible for ensuring appropriate use and correct dosing

**In residential care:**
- Most hospital-level care facilities have their own syringe drivers
- If not available at the facility, contact the local hospice

### Medications

- Use drugs to manage symptoms that are present or anticipated (e.g., an antiemetic if nausea is likely) and titrate to response
- Refer to the Palliative Care Opioid Conversion Guide and Syringe Driver Compatibility Chart to ensure drug combinations are safe
- Use subcutaneous breakthrough doses as needed when the pump first starts — it takes a full 24 hours for the continuous infusion to reach full effect
- Ensure as-required subcutaneous medications are prescribed for each symptom being managed (including any additional anticipated symptoms)

### Pre-filled syringes

Pre-filled syringes are commonly prepared aseptically by specialist pharmacies. Medications must be prescribed as pre-filled syringes rather than ampoules. Your palliative care team or hospice can advise on this.

---

## When to Seek Specialist Input

Contact your local hospice to request a syringe driver. The palliative care team will guide medication choices, compatibility checks, and ongoing management.

---

*Syringe driver use is managed by trained palliative care providers. Always discuss this with your doctor or palliative care team. Decisions are made together with the patient, family/whānau, and the care team.*
