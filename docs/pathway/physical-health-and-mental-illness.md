# Physical Health and Mental Illness

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers proactive physical health screening and management for people living with serious mental illness (SMI) or addiction issues.

---

## Background

People with mental illness and addiction issues experience poorer physical health than the general population, with a shorter life expectancy:

- People with serious mental illness (SMI) — such as schizophrenia, major depressive disorder, bipolar disorder, and schizoaffective disorder — have a premature mortality rate 2 to 3 times higher than the general population.
- Cardiovascular disease (CVD) accounts for 40 to 50% of premature deaths in people with SMI.
- CVD risk is 1.5 to 3 times higher in those with schizophrenia and bipolar disorder, and on average 1.5 times higher in those with major depression.
- Increased CVD risk in SMI is present at an earlier age than in the general population.
- Contributing factors include: side-effects of psychotropic medications, higher smoking rates, obesity and lack of exercise, decreased access to healthcare, stigma, and socioeconomic disadvantage.
- Higher doses, polypharmacy, and treatment of young or older patients are associated with more adverse medication impacts.

## Assessment

Your doctor will proactively screen for physical health problems at every opportunity. This is important — not just screening, but acting on findings early.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Physical health, including history of illness, chronic pain, medications, and allergies
- Oral health
- Lifestyle factors: exercise, diet, smoking, alcohol and non-prescription drug use
- Sexual health and contraception
- Psychosocial factors: family relationships and supports, community involvement, housing and employment, culture or religion

### Examination

Your doctor will:

- Measure weight, height, and waist circumference, and calculate BMI
- Take blood pressure and pulse rate
- Calculate cardiovascular risk (noting that standard risk tools may underestimate risk in people with mental illness or on antipsychotic medication)
- Assess for metabolic syndrome

### Investigations at First Presentation

- Lipids, fasting glucose, HbA1c, liver function tests (LFTs), full blood count (FBC)
- ECG

### Monitoring if Started on Antipsychotic Medication in the Past 12 Months

Your doctor may consider the following schedule:

- **Weight:** Weekly for the first 6 to 8 weeks, then at 3 months, 6 months, and annually. Rapid weight gain (e.g., 5 kg) in the early stages may predict severe longer-term weight gain.
- **Waist circumference, blood pressure, heart rate:** At 3 months, 6 months, then annually.
- **Fasting lipids, fasting glucose, HbA1c, LFTs, FBC:** At 3 months, 6 months, then annually.
- **ECG:** At baseline, 6 months, then annually.
- **Serum prolactin:** Only if specific indications are met (see prolactin section under Management).

## Management

Your doctor will consider the following, whenever you present to primary care:

### 1. Medication Side-Effects

Your doctor may consider:

- Managing metabolic or other side-effects of antipsychotic treatment
- Seeking mental health specialist advice or arranging a community medication review if side-effects such as constipation, sexual dysfunction, or overeating are problematic
- Checking prolactin levels if indicated and managing hyperprolactinaemia (see below)

**Prolactin and hyperprolactinaemia:**

Some antipsychotics — in particular amisulpride, paliperidone, risperidone, and first-generation antipsychotics such as haloperidol and chlorpromazine — can elevate prolactin levels.

Prolactin testing is indicated if you have:
- Galactorrhoea (not within 12 months of weaning)
- Oligomenorrhoea lasting more than 6 months, not explained by stress, exercise, weight change, or breastfeeding
- Male secondary hypogonadism (testosterone less than 5 nmol/L with normal LH)

Long-term effects of raised prolactin include:
- Reduction in bone mineral density
- Possible increased risk of breast cancer
- Delayed sexual maturation and reduced bone growth in children and adolescents

Your doctor may:
- Check thyroid function tests, LFTs, and creatinine to exclude other causes
- Seek neurology advice if prolactin is above 2500 mIU/L (to consider prolactinoma)
- Seek mental health advice if no underlying cause is found, including whether a change in antipsychotic is appropriate
- Advise on reducing osteoporosis risk and arrange regular bone density screening
- Ensure regular breast screening for women with persistent high prolactin
- Review prolactin levels and symptoms every 3 months

### 2. Routine Monitoring

Your doctor may consider:

- Routine monitoring for patients on lithium, clozapine, and antipsychotic medications

**Lithium monitoring:**
- Lithium level, renal function, and electrolytes every 3 to 6 months
- Thyroid function tests, serum calcium, and weight every 6 months
- ECG, lipids, and HbA1c annually if aged over 40 years or obese

**Other routine monitoring:**
- Annual cardiometabolic and metabolic syndrome screening, particularly for those on antipsychotic medications
- Regular breast and bone density screening for women with high prolactin or taking antipsychotics that can cause hyperprolactinaemia
- Cardiovascular risk assessment from the time psychosis is diagnosed (including early-onset psychosis), or from age 25 years — annually for those with CVD risk ≥15%, or every 2 years for those below 15%
- Screening and management of conditions commonly associated with SMI: type 2 diabetes, hypertension, high cholesterol, osteoporosis, and respiratory conditions such as asthma and COPD

### 3. Smoking Cessation

Your doctor may consider:
- Smoking cessation advice and support
- Adjusting antipsychotic doses if smoking habits change — this is particularly important for clozapine, as smoking significantly affects clozapine levels
- Noting that some smoking cessation pharmacotherapies may interact with psychiatric medications or have adverse effects on some psychiatric disorders

### 4. Cancer Screening

Your doctor may ensure you are up to date with national screening programmes and consider cancer assessment based on symptoms, family history, and screening results.

### 5. Self-Management Support

Your doctor may consider referral to:

- Self-management education programmes (group sessions suitable for anyone with an ongoing health condition)
- Health Improvement Practitioners and health coaches for motivation support, behavioural strategies, and lifestyle interventions
- Green Prescription (exercise support)
- Mental health counselling and therapy, or other community support services

**Weight management:**
- If antipsychotic-related weight gain, your doctor may consider metformin
- GLP-1 receptor agonists such as dulaglutide or liraglutide may also be considered; pharmacological agents are used alongside education and lifestyle interventions

### 6. Oral Health

If you have dental pain, your doctor may refer you to emergency dental or relief of pain services, or discuss funding assistance for essential dental treatment.

### 7. Alcohol and Drug Problems

If you have problems associated with alcohol or drug use, your doctor may:

- Provide advice and refer to community alcohol and drug services
- Screen and manage for specific health risks associated with substances:

| Substance | Associated health risks |
| --- | --- |
| Alcohol | Cardiovascular disease, gastrointestinal disease, cancer (breast and gastrointestinal) |
| Cannabis | Cardiovascular disease, respiratory illness |
| Methamphetamine | Cardiovascular disease, cerebrovascular complications, oral health problems |
| Opioids | Cardiovascular disease, gastrointestinal disease |
| All substances | Chronic pain |

- Consider hepatitis C (HCV) screening if there is a history of injecting drug use, tattoos in uncontrolled settings, or other higher-risk exposures

### 8. Chronic Pain

If you have chronic pain, your doctor will follow appropriate chronic pain management pathways.
