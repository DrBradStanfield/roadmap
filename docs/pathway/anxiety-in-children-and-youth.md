# Anxiety in Children and Youth

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of anxiety in patients aged 18 years and under. Anxiety disorders are among the most common mental health conditions in children and adolescents, but are often undetected. Early identification and effective treatment reduces the impact on social and academic functioning, and may reduce persistence into adulthood.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Help

The following require urgent or emergency assessment:

- Significant suicidal thinking and intent — call **111** or go to the emergency department immediately
- Abuse, neglect, or domestic violence — urgent safeguarding response required

Asking about suicidal ideation does not create or worsen suicide risk.

---

## Background

- Anxiety disorders represent one of the most common mental health disorders among children and adolescents
- The level of impairment and degree of distress is more important than the specific diagnosis
- Anxiety commonly coexists with other disorders including behavioural disorders, mood disorders, and autism spectrum disorders

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider the following during assessment.

### Assessment Tools

- **HEeADSSS** — structured assessment tool for adolescents
- **HEARTS** — assessment framework for young children and their families:
  - **H**ome – conduct, general behaviour, manageability
  - **E**ducation – any concerns about behaviour or progress
  - **A**ctivities – attention span, ability to finish tasks, friendships
  - **R**elationships – with peers and parents, any big changes in the family, any bullying
  - **T**emper – mood
  - **S**ize – weight gain, appetite
- **SCARED** — self and parent report questionnaire for childhood (ages 8–18) anxiety disorders

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Severity and duration of symptoms, degree of distress, and avoidance behaviour
- Triggers of anxiety symptoms
- Personal and family history of mental health disorders
- Current or previous therapies and family management strategies trialled
- Psychosocial history (major life stressors, family stressors, school situation, substance use)
- Current support (family, friendship groups)

### Physical Causes to Rule Out

Anxiety symptoms can sometimes have an underlying physical cause. Your doctor may investigate:

- Respiratory illness
- Hearing and vision impairment
- Hyperthyroidism
- Diabetes
- Gastrointestinal or urinary problems
- Cardiac problems
- Sleep disorders
- Seizures

### Symptoms by Age Group

**Early childhood (4–10 years)**
- Children may be unaware of what is bothering them
- May display: crying, tantrums, freezing, clinging, stomach pains, nausea, headaches, or activity avoidance
- Ask: "Is there anything happening around you when your stomach hurts or you feel sick?"

**Middle childhood (8–13 years)**
- May show some self-awareness but symptoms often include social and puberty-related concerns, headache, stomach pains, or nausea
- Ask: "How do you feel when thinking about school or friends? Are there any particular things that make you feel worried, stressed, or frightened?"

**Late childhood onwards (older than 12 years)**
- Older children and adolescents are often unaware of anxiety being an issue
- Common symptoms: concerns about academic or work performance, headaches, stomach pain, anger or refusal to comply with authority
- Ask: "What's worrying you right now?"

### Indicators a Child May Not Be Coping

- Frequently seeking reassurance or approval
- Nausea, stomach aches, or vague pains, especially at specific times or occasions
- Intense anxiety well in advance of planned social events
- Excessive shyness or perfectionism
- Appearing worried or unusually quiet for extended periods
- Overreaction to criticism or routine changes
- Sleep troubles or avoidance behaviour

### Suicidal Ideation

Your doctor will screen for suicidal ideation, intent, and self-harming behaviours. High alert indicators include:

- A suicide or self-harm plan, preparation, or a recent attempt
- Current hopelessness or intense anger
- Isolation, inability to identify supports or reasons for living
- Indirect references to own death
- Recent significant loss
- Substance use or withdrawal

Deliberate self-harm is often a coping mechanism to reduce emotional intensity — a harm reduction approach is used rather than a punitive one.

### Co-morbid Conditions

Your doctor will also screen for conditions that can coexist with or mimic anxiety:

- Depression (screen in all age groups)
- Autism spectrum disorder
- Learning disorders
- ADHD (inattentiveness can look similar to concentration difficulties in anxiety)
- Drug and alcohol issues
- Psychosis (early symptoms can mimic anxiety disorder behaviours)

## Common Anxiety Disorders

Your doctor will consider which type of anxiety disorder best fits:

- **Adjustment disorder with anxiety** — significant anxiety in response to an identifiable stressor (e.g., exam stress, relationship break-up)
- **Social anxiety disorder** — marked fear of social situations involving scrutiny by others (e.g., meeting unfamiliar people, school presentations)
- **Separation anxiety disorder** — developmentally inappropriate fear of separation from a main caregiver (after age 2); may include school refusal and physical complaints on separation
- **Specific phobias** — persistent, intense fear and avoidance of specific things (e.g., dogs, needles)
- **Obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD)** — persistent unwanted thoughts (about dirt, germs, symmetry) followed by repetitive actions (e.g., hand-washing, counting)
- **Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD)** — excessive, uncontrollable worry most of the time about several different events and activities
- **Panic disorder** — fear or worry about having panic attacks; more likely in adolescents than young children. Symptoms include palpitations, sweating, tremor, shortness of breath, chest pain, dizziness, and fear of losing control or dying
- **Trauma-related disorders** — including PTSD and acute stress disorder; features include avoidance, hypervigilance, flashbacks, and nightmares

---

## Severity Grading

- **Mild** — minimal functional impairment
- **Moderate** — some mild impairment and avoidance, with intermittent somatic symptoms
- **Severe** — marked functional impairment (school, friendships, health, or developmental needs), impairment present most days; also includes moderate symptoms that have not responded to multiple previous therapies

---

## Management

Your doctor will use a stepped-care model — starting with the most effective, least intrusive interventions first.

### Urgent Situations

- If there is immediate risk of significant self-harm: emergency mental health assessment or call **111**
- If child abuse or neglect is suspected: referral to child protection services

### Mild to Moderate Anxiety

**Psychoeducation**

Your doctor may:
- Reassure and explain the importance of self and family management
- Encourage increased socialisation, achievable goals, and taking charge of daily activities
- Discourage avoidance and excessive reassurance-seeking (which can perpetuate anxiety)

**Lifestyle advice**

Your doctor may provide information and guidance about:
- Sleep (age-appropriate sleep hygiene)
- Keeping physically active
- Healthy eating
- Screen-time strategies
- Consistent and supportive parenting

**School involvement**

If appropriate and with consent, your doctor may liaise with the school about available supports (e.g., school counsellor, guidance counsellor, visiting psychologist).

### Therapeutic Interventions

Your doctor may refer to or recommend age-appropriate therapy:

**For children:**
- Individual counselling or child psychotherapy
- Parenting programmes (e.g., Fear-Less Triple P for parents of children aged 6–14 with anxiety)
- Barnardos or Family Works (Presbyterian Support) — counselling and family support

**For youth:**
- Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) — the most evidence-based psychological treatment for anxiety in young people
- **SPARX** — free e-therapy tool for ages 12–19 with mild to moderate depression, stress, or anxiety; uses a CBT-based fantasy game format (sparx.org.nz)
- **Just a Thought** — free online CBT courses for ages 16 and over, covering generalised anxiety, social anxiety, OCD, panic, and more (justathought.co.nz)
- School counsellors

### Medication

Your doctor may consider medication for severe anxiety, especially when combined with CBT. Medication is more likely to be effective when used alongside psychological therapy.

**SSRI antidepressants** may be considered if:
- Psychological therapy has been trialled and is insufficient or unsuccessful
- Therapy is proving difficult without medication support
- The condition is severe or life-threatening

**Fluoxetine** is the SSRI with the greatest evidence of benefit in this age group:
- Long half-life, lowest association with suicidality, relatively safe in overdose
- Suggested starting dose: 10 mg once daily (may start at 5 mg once daily if anxiety is prominent)
- Can increase to 20 mg once daily if necessary and tolerated

**Sertraline** also has evidence of benefit for anxiety and depression in young people.

Other medications (paroxetine, venlafaxine, tricyclic antidepressants) are not recommended in primary care due to the potential for serious adverse effects in this age group.

Important notes on SSRIs in young people:
- Use in patients aged 16 and over; use with caution
- Monitor closely for emergence of suicidal ideation
- Use with particular caution if there are signs of manic symptoms or a family history of bipolar disorder
- Not usually indicated for PTSD or simple phobias
- Use in young people is off-label — your doctor will inform you of this

### Follow-Up

Your doctor will typically arrange follow-up in 2–4 weeks, or earlier if there are concerns. If improving, review may be extended to 1–2 months.

### When Referral May Be Needed

Your doctor may refer to specialist child and youth mental health services if:
- Severe anxiety that limits normal age-appropriate behaviour or school attendance on a regular basis
- Moderate anxiety that has not responded to community intervention
- Significant functional impairment across more than one setting (home, school, social) with suspected intellectual disability or autism spectrum disorder
