# Needle Phobia and Vaccination

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of needle phobia and vaccination anxiety in adults and children.

---

## Background

A dislike of needles is common, but most people will accept vaccination because they understand the benefits. Around 10% of the population has needle phobia — an out-of-proportion fear of needles that may cause people to avoid vaccination.

Some people faint at the sight of blood or needles, or with the sensation of a needle piercing the skin. This is a well-recognised physiological response, not a character flaw.

Needle phobia:
- Affects up to 1 in 10 people
- May have an evolutionary basis — avoiding puncture wounds historically reduced the risk of infection
- Can trigger a vasovagal reflex that drops blood pressure, causing light-headedness and fainting
- Is often related to previous medical experiences, especially distressing ones in childhood or overseas
- Has a strong familial pattern — around 80% of adults with needle phobia have a first-degree relative who also has it

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask:

- What specific aspect causes anxiety (the idea of the injection, the sight of the needle, the sensation of skin piercing)
- Whether you have had symptoms such as light-headedness, fainting, or panic
- Whether you have other related phobias (blood phobia, venesection phobia)
- What strategies have helped in the past

Your doctor will validate and normalise your experience. Having needle phobia does not mean you are weak or irrational — it is a recognised condition with a biological basis.

It is helpful to document needle fear or phobia in your clinical record so vaccinators are aware at future appointments.

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following approaches depending on the type of reaction:

### If you faint or feel faint (vasovagal response)

**Applied muscle tension exercises** can prevent fainting. Practice this sequence 3 times a day for about a week before vaccination:

1. Sitting in a chair, tense or squeeze the muscles in your legs and stomach for 10–15 seconds until your face feels flushed or warm
2. Release the tension for 20–30 seconds
3. Repeat until the injection is over, or the feeling of faintness passes
4. You can hold a squeezy ball or a support person's hand during this

Alternatively, lying down during vaccination is a simple and effective option.

### If anxiety or needle phobia is the main issue

**Prepare for the vaccination event:**
- Focus on the benefits of being vaccinated
- Book an appointment time when you are not rushed or tired — earlier in the day helps reduce time spent worrying
- Plan a positive activity to look forward to afterwards
- Bring a calm support person
- Wear a short-sleeved shirt
- Avoid looking at the needle or equipment before or during the procedure
- Let the vaccinator know your concerns in advance — they can talk you through the procedure or, if you prefer, simply proceed without commentary ("just do it")

**Relaxation, calm breathing, and distraction techniques:**
- Progressive muscle relaxation — tense and release muscle groups from feet upward
- Calm breathing — slow exhale, focusing on the out-breath
- Distraction — bring headphones and listen to music, read a book, use your phone, or chat with a friend

### Reducing injection site pain

Your doctor may consider options to reduce pain at the injection site (mid upper arm, in line with the axilla):

- **Tactile stimulation** — a support person rubs or strokes the skin near the injection site with moderate pressure before vaccination to reduce pain at the time of injection
- **Buzzy Bee** — a handheld vibrating device with ice wings applied near the injection site; combines cold and vibration to block sharp sensations. Costs approximately $90 (home use)
- **EMLA cream or patches** (lidocaine and prilocaine) — applied as a thick layer under an occlusive dressing to the vaccination site for 60–90 minutes before vaccination. Wipe off cream before the injection. Available from a pharmacy without prescription (approximately $20 for a 5 g tube)
- **Ametop gel** (tetracaine) — applied under an occlusive dressing for 30–45 minutes before vaccination. May cause redness, which is normal and not an allergy. Wipe off before injection. Available from a pharmacy without prescription (approximately $15 per 1.5 g tube)
- **Ethyl chloride spray** — rapidly freezes superficial tissue to cause local anaesthesia. Applied perpendicular to the skin from 5 cm away, 2 sprays of 5 seconds each. Available from a pharmacy without prescription (approximately $23 per 100 mL can)

### For significant needle phobia

Where needle phobia is a serious barrier to vaccination, your doctor may consider:

- A **personalised approach** — vaccination at a familiar location, by a clinician who knows you
- A single dose of a **benzodiazepine** (e.g., lorazepam 0.5–1 mg orally, taken 1–2 hours before vaccination) for adults with no contraindications. Your doctor will:
  - Obtain written, informed consent before you take the medication — be aware that sedation affects your capacity to give consent, so this must happen beforehand
  - Advise that you will need someone to drive you home and stay with you for 4 hours after receiving sedation
  - Arrange a longer post-vaccination monitoring period before you are discharged
  - Use lower doses in elderly, low-weight, or debilitated patients, or those with hepatic or severe renal impairment
  - Avoid in pregnancy unless there is no safer alternative

- **Referral to a psychologist** with experience in needle phobia if anxiety is moderate to severe, or if initial measures have not helped. Note that publicly funded psychology is unlikely to cover vaccination-related needle phobia; private referral is usually required. Avoidance is a core feature of phobias — if attending appointments feels difficult, let your doctor know so they can help you engage with care.
