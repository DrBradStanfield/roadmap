# IBD Medications

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This page explains the four groups of medications used to treat inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD). These medications are prescribed and monitored by your doctor — always discuss any changes or concerns with them.

---

## Important: Immunosuppression Risk

Steroids, thiopurines, methotrexate, and biologics all suppress the immune system. If you are on any of these medications, your doctor should be aware and you should receive an annual flu vaccination.

---

## 1. Aminosalicylates (ASA)

Common examples: mesalazine (Pentasa, Asacol), olsalazine, sulphasalazine.

### What they are used for

- First-line treatment for ulcerative colitis (UC) — used for both inducing and maintaining remission.
- Mesalazine is the most commonly used and is generally well-tolerated.
- Limited benefit in active Crohn's disease.
- Sulphasalazine and olsalazine are used less often due to side effects but may suit certain patients.

### How they are taken

- **Mesalazine (Asacol):** Oral tablets releasing in the colon only, or suppositories. Standard dose up to 3.2 g per day in divided doses.
- **Mesalazine (Pentasa):** Oral tablets, sachets, suppositories, or enemas — releases throughout the gut from duodenum to rectum. Standard oral dose up to 4 g per day, can be taken as single or divided doses.
- **Sulphasalazine:** In acute disease 1–2 g four times daily; maintenance 500 mg four times daily. May cause yellow-orange discolouration of skin, urine, and body fluids, and can stain soft contact lenses.
- Rectal therapies (suppositories, enemas) are effective for disease affecting the lower bowel, used alone or combined with oral ASA.

### Possible side effects

ASAs can rarely cause blood disorders. Report any unexplained bleeding, bruising, purpura, sore throat, fever, or malaise to your doctor promptly.

### Monitoring

Your doctor will check kidney function (creatinine) and urine every 6 months.

## 2. Steroids

Common example: prednisone.

### What they are used for

- Used to achieve remission during flare-ups in both UC and Crohn's disease.
- Steroids have **no role as long-term maintenance therapy** — they are for short-term use only.
- If you need more than two courses of steroids per year, or prolonged courses, your doctor will consider stepping up to an immunomodulator or biologic.

### How they are taken

- Prednisone is typically started at 40 mg per day and gradually reduced (tapered) over approximately 8 weeks, decreasing by 5 mg per week.
- A course usually lasts 4–8 weeks. Do not stop abruptly without medical guidance.

### Risks and monitoring

- Steroids increase the risk of infection and sepsis — seek medical attention promptly if you develop signs of infection.
- Long-term or repeated steroid use increases the risk of osteoporosis. Your doctor may recommend vitamin D, calcium, and bone density monitoring (DXA scan).

---

## 3. Immunomodulators

Two types: **thiopurines** (azathioprine, mercaptopurine) and **methotrexate**.

### Thiopurines (azathioprine and mercaptopurine/6MP)

#### What they are used for

- Maintenance therapy in UC when aminosalicylates are not effective, or when repeated steroid courses are needed.
- Induction of remission in Crohn's disease with severe or complex disease.
- Only started by a gastroenterologist after a full discussion of risks and benefits.

#### How they work and timing

- Slow-acting: may take 3–6 months to achieve remission.
- Before starting, a blood test for TPMT (thiopurine methyltransferase) is done — this enzyme processes thiopurines. About 1 in 250 people have very low TPMT levels and are at risk of serious side effects; in these cases the drug is avoided or used at a very low dose.
- Thiopurine metabolite levels (6-TGN, 6-MMP) can be measured to guide dose adjustment and check adherence.

#### Important precautions

- **Do not take with allopurinol** (used for gout) — the combination significantly increases the risk of bone marrow suppression. Only used together under close specialist supervision in resistant disease.
- **Live vaccines are contraindicated** while on thiopurines. Avoid: herpes zoster, varicella, MMR, oral polio, intranasal influenza, BCG, oral typhoid, and yellow fever vaccines.
- Pregnancy: azathioprine is generally considered safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding. Mercaptopurine is widely used in pregnancy by specialist advice, but classified as category D by the NZF — always discuss with your doctor.

#### Side effects

- Common: nausea, vomiting, flu-like symptoms (headache, muscle aches).
- Serious: myelosuppression (bone marrow suppression), liver toxicity (hepatotoxicity).
- Long-term: small increased risk of skin cancer and lymphoma due to immunosuppression.

#### Monitoring

- FBC (full blood count) and LFTs (liver function tests) at least weekly for the first 4 weeks, then every 3 months ongoing.

---

### Methotrexate (second-line immunomodulator)

#### What it is used for

- Second-line option when thiopurines are not tolerated or disease is not responding.
- Used weekly, either orally or by subcutaneous injection.

#### Important precautions

- **Contraindicated in pregnancy** — causes birth defects. Both men and women must use effective contraception and delay pregnancy for at least 3–6 months after stopping.
- Always co-prescribed with **folic acid 5 mg once weekly** (taken on a different day to methotrexate) to reduce side effects.

#### Monitoring

- FBC and LFTs every 3 months.

---

## 4. Biologics (TNF Blockers)

Common examples: infliximab (Remicade), adalimumab (Amgevita, Humira).

### What they are used for

- Potent anti-inflammatory medications that block tumour necrosis factor (TNF).
- Used for moderate-to-severe IBD not responding to other treatments.
- Initiated and managed by a gastroenterologist.
- In New Zealand, infliximab and adalimumab are the two funded biologics (outside of clinical trials). Adalimumab is funded via Special Authority — initial application is made by a gastroenterologist; GPs may apply for renewals.

### Before starting

Full screening is required before biologics are started:
- Varicella zoster virus (VZV) immunity
- Hepatitis B and C
- Tuberculosis (TB) — biologic therapy can reactivate latent TB

### Important precautions while on biologics

- **Withhold the dose** if you have signs of active infection — contact your doctor or gastroenterologist.
- **Before surgery:** Withhold biologics and discuss timing with your surgeon and gastroenterologist.
- **Live vaccines are contraindicated.** Avoid: herpes zoster, varicella, MMR, oral polio, intranasal influenza, BCG, oral typhoid, and yellow fever vaccines.
- Flu and pneumococcal vaccines are recommended but may be less effective while on biologics — still worth having.
- Adalimumab is given by subcutaneous self-injection; training is provided by gastroenterology staff.

### Monitoring

There are no routine regular blood tests required for biologics, but your gastroenterologist will review you at least every 6 months once stable.

## Summary: When Each Medication Class Is Used

| Medication | UC | Crohn's | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Aminosalicylates | First-line | Limited benefit | Remission + maintenance |
| Steroids | Yes | Yes | Short-term flares only |
| Thiopurines | Yes (if ASA fails) | Yes (severe/complex) | Maintenance |
| Methotrexate | Less common | Yes | Second-line maintenance |
| Biologics | Yes (moderate-severe) | Yes (moderate-severe) | When other treatments fail |

---

*All IBD medications carry risks and require medical supervision. This information is for general awareness only — your doctor will tailor treatment to your individual situation. Always discuss any concerns or side effects with your doctor before making changes.*
