# Giant-cell Arteritis (Temporal Arteritis)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of giant-cell arteritis (GCA), also called temporal arteritis, in adults.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

Contact your doctor urgently or go to the emergency department if you have:

- **Any visual symptoms** (double vision, loss of vision in one eye, visual disturbance)
- New onset or new type of headache if aged over 50 years

GCA must be treated urgently with corticosteroids to reduce the significant risk of permanent vision loss (up to 20%).

---

## Background

Giant-cell arteritis (GCA) is an immune-mediated condition caused by inflammation in the wall of medium to large arteries. The temporal artery is usually the only artery where physical changes are clinically apparent.

Key facts:
- GCA usually affects people aged over 50 years, is most prevalent in Caucasians, and is 2 to 3 times more common in females than males.
- 40 to 60% of patients with GCA have concurrent polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR).
- About 15% of patients with PMR will develop GCA.
- Sudden blindness may present without early warning symptoms, and CRP and ESR may be normal even in confirmed GCA.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about and examine for:

**Headache** (most common symptom, seen in over two-thirds of patients):
- Often severe
- Temporal or occipital location
- Can be worse at night
- May be a new type of headache

**Visual disturbances:**
- New-onset double vision
- Unilateral visual symptoms
- Loss of vision
- Any visual disturbance is of critical importance

**Other symptoms:**
- Scalp tenderness, especially when combing hair or resting on a pillow (25% of patients)
- Jaw claudication — pain when chewing or talking (25% of patients)
- Systemic features (about half of patients): low-grade fever, anorexia, fatigue
- Signs or symptoms of polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR)

**Physical examination includes:**
- Eye examination: visual acuity, visual fields, pupillary reactions, eye movements, fundoscopy
- Temporal artery examination — arteries may be thickened, hardened, nodular, or have reduced/absent pulses (a normal temporal artery does not exclude GCA)

**Investigations:**
- CRP and ESR — both should be requested. Either or both may be raised. In 5 to 10% of patients with GCA, both may be normal. An isolated elevation strongly suggests GCA in the right clinical context.
- Other blood tests (FBC, LFTs) to exclude alternative diagnoses — WBC, platelets, and ALP may be elevated in GCA.

**Other diagnoses to consider:**
Migraine, intracranial haemorrhage, herpes zoster, TIA, temporomandibular disorder, trigeminal neuralgia, other causes of sudden vision loss.

---

## Management

GCA is an acute rheumatology condition. All patients with suspected GCA should be discussed urgently with a rheumatologist at the time of diagnosis or suspected diagnosis.

**Your doctor will:**

1. **Seek acute rheumatology advice immediately** — the rheumatologist will advise on investigations and management for each patient.

2. **Start corticosteroids urgently** — do not wait for investigation results before starting treatment.
   - Usual starting dose: **prednisone 40 mg daily**
   - If visual symptoms are present: **prednisone 60 mg daily** (dose confirmed with ophthalmologist)
   - Symptoms typically improve within one week

3. **Arrange confirmation of diagnosis** via temporal artery biopsy or bilateral temporal and axillary artery Doppler ultrasound. Diagnosis via biopsy or ultrasound can be delayed up to 7 days without substantially hindering interpretation. Temporal artery biopsy can be negative due to skip lesions — if clinical suspicion remains strong, steroid therapy should continue.

4. **Monitor and reduce prednisone** (or as advised by rheumatology):
   - Reduce by 10 mg every 2 weeks, down to 20 mg
   - Then reduce by 2.5 mg every 2 to 4 weeks, to 10 mg
   - Then reduce by 1 mg every 4 to 8 weeks if no relapses
   - Monitor by clinical response (resolution of headache) rather than CRP/ESR alone
   - Do not increase the dose based on isolated CRP/ESR elevation without clinical symptoms
   - 50% of patients relapse during the first 12 to 18 months
   - Most patients require prednisone for 2 to 3 years

5. **Arrange osteoporosis prophylaxis:**
   - Bone density scan
   - Consider bisphosphonates, especially in older or frail patients
   - Adequate dietary calcium and consider vitamin D replacement

6. **Arrange follow-up:**
   - Within a few days of initial consultation
   - At 1, 3, and 6 weeks
   - Then every 3 months for the duration of steroid treatment

7. **Request rheumatology assessment** if:
   - Failure to respond to therapy
   - Failure to wean off prednisone due to relapse
   - Consideration of steroid-sparing therapy (e.g., low-dose oral methotrexate) in those at high risk of steroid complications

8. **Manage relapses** by increasing prednisone in conjunction with rheumatology advice:
   - Headache suggesting GCA: return to previous effective dose
   - Jaw claudication: return to 40–60 mg prednisone and seek rheumatology advice
   - Visual symptoms: treat with 60 mg prednisone and arrange urgent ophthalmology review

**Note on antiplatelet therapy:** Routine use of aspirin or anticoagulation is no longer recommended for GCA.

---

## Steroid Side-Effects to Be Aware Of

Long-term prednisone use can cause:
- Insomnia, weight gain, skin thinning, easy bruising, delayed wound healing
- Gastrointestinal ulceration, hypertension, muscle weakness, immunosuppression
- New-onset or worsened diabetes
- Psychiatric disturbances (more common at doses > 40 mg or with pre-existing mood disorders)
- Reactivation of latent infections

Your doctor will discuss these risks before starting treatment.
