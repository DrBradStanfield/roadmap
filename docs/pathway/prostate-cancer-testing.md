# Prostate Cancer Testing

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers primary care prostate cancer testing in **asymptomatic** men — assessing risk, making an informed decision about PSA testing, and acting on results.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Medical Assessment

See your doctor promptly if you have any of the following:

- Macroscopic haematuria (visible blood in urine)
- Renal failure symptoms (nausea, fatigue, peripheral swelling)
- New onset, severe, or progressive bone pain (especially localised pain, pathological fractures, or non-traumatic pain that may indicate metastases)
- Neurological signs suggesting spinal cord compression

---

## Background

Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) is a protein made mainly in the prostate gland. Since PSA testing was introduced in the 1990s, there has been a 50% reduction in presentations of prostate cancer with metastases and a 38% reduction in mortality. After 10 years of screening, for every 1000 men screened, one death is prevented.

However, PSA testing is not a simple yes/no decision. There are genuine trade-offs, and testing is not routinely recommended without an informed discussion.

Your doctor will help you weigh up whether testing is right for you.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider:

### Factors that increase prostate cancer risk

- **Family history:**
  - First-degree relative (father or brother) with prostate cancer — doubles the baseline risk
  - Second- or third-degree relative — smaller but meaningful increase
- **BRCA gene** — associated with higher prostate cancer risk
- **Elevated PSA** on prior testing
- **Abnormal digital rectal examination (DRE)** — even with a normal PSA, prostate cancer may be palpable as asymmetrical, hard areas or nodules

### Factors that affect PSA results

PSA can be elevated for non-cancer reasons:
- Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH)
- Urinary tract infection (UTI) or prostatitis
- Prostate biopsy or cystoscopy
- Ejaculation within 48 hours before the PSA test
- Intense exercise or long-distance cycling within 48 hours before the PSA test

PSA can be falsely lowered by some BPH medications (e.g., alpha reductase inhibitors such as finasteride or dutasteride), which roughly halve PSA levels.

### Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS)

If you have any of the following, your doctor will follow a different pathway:

**Obstructive symptoms:**
- Poor urine flow
- Hesitancy
- Terminal or post-micturition dribbling
- Overflow incontinence

**Irritative symptoms:**
- Urinary frequency
- Dysuria (pain when urinating)
- Nocturia (needing to urinate at night)
- Urgency

Most men with early prostate cancer do not have symptoms. Unless there is a red flag, lower urinary tract symptoms are most likely due to benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), not cancer.

### Digital rectal examination (DRE)

Your doctor will offer a DRE as part of the assessment. Prostate cancer may be palpable as asymmetrical, hard areas or nodules — even when PSA is normal, not all prostate cancers cause PSA elevation.

If you decline a DRE, PSA testing can still be performed, and referral can still occur based on two clearly abnormal PSA results, or a single result above 50 ng/mL.

---

## Making an Informed Decision About PSA Testing

Your doctor will discuss both sides before proceeding. Testing is not recommended for asymptomatic men whose life expectancy is less than 10 years due to other health conditions.

### Benefits of PSA testing

- May reduce prostate cancer mortality, though all-cause mortality has not been shown to be reduced
- Reduces the chance of presenting with advanced, metastatic disease

### Potential harms of PSA testing

- **High false positive rate** — 3 in 4 men with an elevated PSA do not have cancer
- **Biopsy risks** — if elevated PSA leads to biopsy:
  - Biopsy may be done transrectally or transperineally
  - Both methods can cause urinary retention or bleeding (urethral, rectal, or haematospermia)
  - Sepsis risk: approximately 2% with transrectal biopsy; less than 0.01% with transperineal biopsy
- **False negatives** — 8 to 10% of prostate cancers have a normal PSA level; some of these are detectable on DRE
- **Overdiagnosis** — approximately 28 in 1000 men (2.8%) receive a diagnosis of cancer that may remain asymptomatic for life
- **Overtreatment** — approximately 25 in 1000 men (2.5%) choose treatment they may not have needed

### Diagnosis does not mean treatment

Not all diagnosed prostate cancers require treatment:
- 42% of diagnosed cancers go on to active surveillance, not treatment
- 58% will be treated

Of those treated with surgery:
- 50% risk of lasting impotence
- 5 to 10% risk of lasting urinary incontinence

## Management

If you choose to go ahead with PSA testing after a full discussion with your doctor, next steps depend on the results.

### If PSA is less than 4 ng/mL, with no red flags and no suspicious DRE

Your doctor may:
- Reassure you that prostate cancer is highly unlikely
- Manage any lower urinary tract symptoms
- Recommend follow-up based on your family history and preference:
  - **Strong family history** (first-degree relative diagnosed before age 65): PSA and DRE every 12 months, for men aged 40 to 70
  - **No significant family history:** Testing every 1 to 4 years if you choose to continue; testing is not likely to benefit men over 70

### If PSA is 4 ng/mL or higher, with no red flags and no suspicious DRE

Your doctor may:
- Refer directly to urology if PSA is 50 ng/mL or more (no repeat test needed before referral)
- Seek specialist urology advice if there is high concern after a single test
- Otherwise, repeat the PSA test in 6 to 12 weeks, after addressing any reversible causes:
  - Treat any active UTI or prostatitis first; repeat in 12 weeks
  - If no reversible cause identified, repeat in 6 weeks

**Referral thresholds based on age** (Ministry of Health guidelines):

| Age group | Refer if PSA is |
|-----------|----------------|
| 70 years or younger | ≥ 4 ng/mL |
| 71 to 75 years | ≥ 10 ng/mL |
| 76 years or older | ≥ 20 ng/mL |

These thresholds apply to men with a normal DRE. Men with a palpable prostate abnormality on DRE are referred regardless of PSA level.

If a second PSA test is abnormal for your age, your doctor will refer you for urology assessment.

### If PSA is persistently or significantly rising

Your doctor may refer for urology assessment if:
- PSA is rising more than 1.5 ng/mL per year with previously low values
- DRE is suspicious (asymmetrical, hard areas or nodules), even with a normal PSA
- There is other clinical concern about possible prostate cancer

Your doctor will take your other health conditions and personal preferences into account when making referral decisions.
