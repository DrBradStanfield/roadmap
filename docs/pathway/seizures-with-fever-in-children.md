# Seizures with Fever in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers children aged 6 months to 6 years who have a seizure during a febrile illness. After careful assessment to exclude serious underlying causes, most seizures can be managed as simple febrile seizures.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance (111) immediately if:

- New-onset neurological abnormality
- Seizure lasting longer than 5 minutes
- Suspected meningitis or encephalitis
- Signs of serious sepsis (severe tachycardia or tachypnoea, signs of poor perfusion)

---

## Background

Epileptic seizures can occur with fever in three ways:

- A **febrile seizure** — the most common and usually benign cause
- A seizure caused by an underlying **central nervous system infection** (e.g. meningitis or encephalitis)
- A fever **triggering a seizure** in a child with known epilepsy

Seizures with fever are classified as:

- **Simple** — lasts less than 15 minutes, is generalised (not focal), and occurs only once in 24 hours
- **Complex** — lasts over 15 minutes, or 2 or more seizures in 24 hours, or is focal

### About Febrile Seizures

- Febrile seizures occur in children aged 6 months to 6 years who do not have a central nervous system infection and have no history of afebrile seizures
- They affect about 1 in 20 children; there is often a family history
- Repeated seizures in the same illness occur in 10 to 15% of children
- Seizures recur in subsequent febrile illnesses in about one third of children
- Most seizures are brief (1 to 2 minutes) and do not require specific treatment
- Paracetamol and ibuprofen do not prevent febrile seizures, but either can help with the discomfort of the illness

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess the child to exclude serious underlying causes. Key steps include:

### If the Child is Actively Seizing

- Move the child away from objects that could cause harm
- Protect the airway with a jaw thrust if needed — do not attempt to open the mouth
- Time the seizure
- Check temperature, pulse, and blood glucose

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- What the child was doing just before the seizure
- Description of the event — onset, duration, what happened, behaviour afterwards
- Any previous seizures with or without fever
- Family history — particularly sudden deaths at a young age, drownings, events triggered by exercise or fright (may suggest a cardiac cause), arrhythmic disorders, or epilepsy
- Other possible causes: head injury, low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia), hypoxia, trauma, accidental ingestion
- Recent immunisations in the last 12 days — vaccine-related febrile seizures are rare and almost always benign

### Examination

Your doctor will examine for:

- The cause of the fever — and specifically assess for sepsis, meningitis, and encephalitis
- General appearance, alertness, hydration, heart rate, and breathing rate
- Focal neurological signs; in infants, whether the fontanelle is bulging or tense
- Consciousness level using a standardised paediatric scale

### Investigations

Investigations are not usually needed unless required to determine the cause of the fever. If there is a significant family history of cardiac events, your doctor may consider an ECG to rule out a cardiac cause (e.g. long QT syndrome).

---

## Management

### Acute Seizure Management

Your doctor will:

1. Keep the child safe while actively seizing (away from hazards, airway protected, seizure timed, blood glucose checked)
2. Correct any low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia) if present
3. If the seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes, administer rescue medication and call 111 for an ambulance

**Rescue medication** used to stop seizures in children:

- **Midazolam** (buccal or intranasal) is the preferred drug. Doses by age:
  - Under 3 months: 0.5 mg/kg (maximum 2.5 mg)
  - 3 to 11 months: 2.5 mg
  - 1 to 4 years: 5 mg
  - 5 to 9 years: 7.5 mg
  - 10 to 18 years: 10 mg (maximum dose)
- **Diazepam** (rectal) is an alternative:
  - 1 month to 2 years: 5 mg
  - 2 to 12 years: 5 to 10 mg
  - 12 to 18 years: 10 to 20 mg
  - Note: delayed respiratory depression may occur after rectal administration

A second dose may be given 5 minutes after the first if the seizure continues.

---

### Post-Seizure Management

Once the seizure has stopped, your doctor will:

- Place the child in the left lateral (recovery) position
- Measure blood pressure and pulse
- Assess consciousness level and mental state
- Manage fever: undress to a single layer, encourage fluids, offer paracetamol 15 mg/kg if uncomfortable (do not sponge or allow shivering)
- Monitor neurological observations every 15 minutes until recovery from the postictal period

### When Urgent Paediatric Assessment Is Needed

Your doctor will arrange urgent assessment if any of the following apply:

- Child is younger than 6 months
- Seizure lasted 5 minutes or longer
- Seizures with focal neurological signs or a non-tonic-clonic pattern
- Repeated seizures in the same illness
- Marked drowsiness after the seizure (reduced consciousness level)
- Normal conscious level not regained after 2 hours
- New neurological signs persist after the seizure resolves
- Child is very unwell from the underlying illness
- Suspected meningitis or encephalitis
- Cause of the fever is unclear after careful assessment
- Hypoglycaemia requiring acute management

### Non-Urgent Paediatric Referral

Your doctor may consider a non-urgent paediatric referral if:

- The child has had seizures during 3 or more separate febrile illnesses
- The child continues to have fever-related seizures beyond the age of 6 years

---

### Simple Febrile Seizure — Reassurance and Home Care

If assessment finds no concerning features and the child has recovered well, your doctor will:

- Manage the underlying febrile illness
- Provide fever management advice
- Reassure parents and carers that brief, generalised febrile seizures that occur only once in a 24-hour period do not cause neurological damage and are not typically associated with a future diagnosis of epilepsy
- Arrange a follow-up review (in-person or phone) in 24 to 48 hours to confirm no further seizures and that the illness is improving
