# Warts and Verrucas

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of warts and verrucas (plantar warts) in primary care.

---

## Background

Warts are caused by the human papilloma virus (HPV). They are very common in children and teenagers. They usually resolve spontaneously over months to years, without any treatment. Spread is by direct skin contact or autoinoculation (spreading from one part of the body to another by scratching or touching).

People who are immunosuppressed — for example, after an organ transplant — are at higher risk and may have warts that are more resistant to treatment.

---

## How Warts Are Diagnosed

Your doctor will look for features that distinguish warts from corns and calluses:

| Feature | Corns and calluses | Warts |
|---|---|---|
| Location | Usually over weight-bearing areas or bony prominences | Anywhere |
| Bleeding when pared down | No pinprick bleeding | Multiple pinprick bleeding points |
| Skin lines | Pass through the lesion | Are interrupted by the wart |
| Pressure pain | Pain with direct pressure | Visual assessment is more accurate than the squeeze test |
| Other | May have a central core | May have overlying callus in weight-bearing areas |

Your doctor will also assess the vascular and neurological condition of your feet — particularly important if you have diabetes or peripheral vascular disease.

### When to suspect something more serious

Your doctor will consider biopsy or urgent review if there is:
- Rapid growth
- Atypical appearance or unexplained bleeding

---

## Do All Warts Need Treatment?

No. Your doctor will discuss with you whether treatment is actually needed:

- Warts are benign (non-cancerous) lesions. In children, 50% disappear on their own within 1 year, and about 67% within 2 years.
- All treatments can be painful, require regular application, and may need to continue for months or years. Patience and perseverance are required.
- Treatment is generally recommended if the wart is painful, on the sole of your foot, or causing significant embarrassment.
- If you have diabetes, vascular disease, or nerve problems affecting your feet, your doctor will avoid aggressive treatments.

---

## Treatment Options

Your doctor will choose the least painful approach first, especially for young children. There is no strong evidence that cryotherapy is better than salicylic acid, or vice versa.

### 1. Occlusive dressing (duct tape method)

This is the least invasive option and is often tried first in children.

- Cover the wart continuously with duct tape or strapping tape for 6 days.
- On the 6th night, remove the tape, soak the area, and gently debride (rub away dead tissue).
- Recover the wart the next morning.
- Repeat until the wart resolves, but stop after 2 months if there is no improvement.

Evidence for this method is conflicting, but it is safe and painless.

### 2. Salicylic acid

- Cure rate is approximately 15% for hand warts and 33% for plantar warts.
- Treatment should continue for at least 3 months to give it the best chance of working.
- Available in various strengths:
  - **Home use:** Salicylic acid 16.7% with lactic acid 15% (e.g. Duofilm, unfunded) or similar. Apply daily after soaking the wart in warm water and removing dead surface tissue with a pumice stone or emery board. Allow to dry, then cover with a plain plaster.
  - **Funded option:** Salicylic acid 15–27% in white soft paraffin (requires a prescription).
  - **In-clinic (GP):** Stronger preparations (e.g. salicylic acid 60% in glycerine) may be used for plantar warts, under careful conditions only. Not generally used in children.
- Do not apply salicylic acid to the face or skin folds — risk of scarring and irritation.

### 3. Cryotherapy (liquid nitrogen)

Applied in clinic at 2 to 3 weekly intervals.

- Two freeze-thaw cycles per session are recommended.
- Your doctor will freeze until a halo of ice appears around the wart, then hold for 5–30 seconds.
- Debridement between sessions can improve the cure rate.
- Generally avoided in young children due to pain and the need for repeated visits.
- Over-the-counter cryogenic sprays are unlikely to be effective for plantar warts.
- Possible side-effects: pain, blistering, scarring, hypopigmentation (loss of skin colour), or spread of the wart.
- Care is needed near tendons and on the sides of fingers, where freezing can damage underlying structures.

### 4. Refractory warts (if first-line treatments fail)

If warts are not responding, your doctor may consider:

- **5-Fluorouracil cream (off-label):** Applied twice daily under an occlusive dressing for up to 12 weeks. In studies, eradication of plantar warts may be as high as 95% with good adherence. Not suitable for children or pregnancy. Can cause pain, blistering, irritation, or nail detachment if applied near a nail.
- **Surgical debridement** (shave excision or curettage and cautery): May be considered in non-cosmetically sensitive areas, but it is not curative. Surgery on the sole of the foot is generally avoided due to risk of permanently painful scarring.
- Podophyllotoxin and imiquimod (used for genital warts) are not effective for skin warts and should not be used in pregnancy.

### Knowing when the wart has cleared

Restored skin lines will be visible across the area where the wart used to be.

---

## When to Review the Treatment Plan

Your doctor will reassess after every 2–3 treatment sessions:

- If there is no regression, a different treatment will be considered.
- If the wart is no longer painful or embarrassing, watchful waiting (no treatment) may become the preferred option.

---

## HPV Vaccination

If you are eligible, your doctor may encourage HPV vaccination. The HPV vaccine currently funded in New Zealand is not specifically targeted at the HPV strains that cause plantar warts, but there are case reports suggesting the vaccine may induce a broader immune response.

---

## Hygiene and Prevention

To reduce the spread of warts:

- HPV spreads by direct skin contact — avoid touching warts and then other skin areas.
- Wear jandals or sandals in communal showers, swimming pools, and gymnasiums.
- Avoid scratching lesions, biting nails, or putting fingers with lesions in your mouth.
- Do not share files, pumice stones, or other equipment used to pare down warts.
