# Autism in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: December 2024.*

This pathway is about the care of autistic adults after diagnosis. It also covers adults who have traits of autism but have never been formally diagnosed.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Background

Autism is a neurodevelopmental difference. Its core characteristics are:
- Differences in social interaction and communication.
- Repetitive behaviours or thinking.
- Intense interests, and strong focus on these.
- Strong adherence to routines, and difficulty tolerating change.
- Sensory or interoceptive (internal body sensation) over- or under-sensitivity.

The word "spectrum" reflects the breadth of differences autistic people may experience, and how much they may be affected. The impact varies with stage of life, response to interventions, other health conditions, and how supportive the person's environment and the people around them are.

- Autism may occur alongside other conditions, such as anxiety, depression, epilepsy, and intellectual disability. It is also often associated with sensory processing differences.
- It is estimated that 1 in 36 people are on the autism spectrum.
- Autism is more commonly diagnosed in males (3:1). This likely reflects under-diagnosis of females, and the true ratio is likely closer to 2:1.

**Language:** The general community consensus is to use identity-first language, such as "autistic" or "autistic person". Within the neurodiversity movement, autism is seen as a natural variation of human neurology rather than a disorder. Many autistic people object to the terms "disorder", "autism spectrum disorder", and "ASD". Individual preferences should guide the language used.

## Assessment

### How your doctor may adapt your appointment

A neurodiversity-affirming approach is essential for a good assessment. Your doctor may:
- Take time to understand how you usually interact, how you prefer to communicate, how you cope with change, which situations raise your anxiety, your living environment, your support people, and any recent or significant life changes.
- Be clear and direct, ask specific questions, and avoid leading or complex questions, medical jargon, idioms, and figures of speech.
- Check that you have understood, allow extra time to respond (especially if you use non-speech communication such as gestures, sign language, or electronic devices), and encourage your questions.
- Consider communication aids, such as pictures.
- Respect personal space and varying eye contact, for example by changing the seating layout.
- Keep in mind that you may experience body sensations, such as pain, differently.
- Ask whether you want to speak to them alone. A support person, if present, should be someone who knows you well and whom you trust.

Practices may also help by:
- Booking the first appointment of the session, so you are less likely to be kept waiting.
- Allowing extra time. More than one consultation may be needed.
- Offering a quiet place to wait instead of the waiting room.
- Reducing background noise, bright light, visual stimuli, and smells, and allowing movement or sensory tools such as fidget toys and weighted blankets.

### Screening and diagnosis

Autism is usually diagnosed in childhood. However, many adults may never have been diagnosed, or may have been misdiagnosed, and may have learnt to mask autistic behaviours in social situations. Some may lose contact with health services when moving into adulthood. This can lead to social and economic isolation, a high risk of mental health conditions, contact with the justice system (as victims or offenders), and a higher risk of physical health problems and death.

An accurate diagnosis:
- Is important for an appropriate management plan.
- Gives clarity and reassurance.
- May allow access to more support in the community.
- Can be difficult, because many related conditions overlap with autism.

If you have traits of autism but no formal diagnosis, or there is doubt about a previous diagnosis, your doctor may discuss screening with you. An adult autism diagnosis is a significant event, and it is your choice whether to pursue it. Screening tools that can help include the **Ritvo Autism and Asperger Diagnostic Scale (RAADS-14)** and the **Autism Quotient (AQ)**. Results are attached to any referral for diagnostic assessment.

### Traits of autism

- Persistent differences in social interaction and communication. This can include confusion in social situations (especially groups), and differences in eye contact, forming close friendships, working out what others think or feel, noticing non-verbal behaviour, and understanding nuance rather than taking things literally.
- Repetitive movements, such as fidgeting, pacing, or self-stimulatory behaviour (stimming), for self-regulation and calming.
- Insistence on order, structure, and things being "right".
- Intensely focused interests that take up a lot of time (or did when younger).
- A strong preference for consistent routines, with anxiety or annoyance at changes, especially unexpected or unexplained ones.
- Sensory differences: over- or under-responsiveness to sensory input. This can cause fear, overwhelm, overstimulation, and meltdowns. Some people seek sensory input (for example sniffing objects), others avoid everyday sounds and textures (for example clothing tags or sand). Unmet sensory needs can increase stress, anxiety, fatigue, irritability, and dysregulation.

Keep in mind:
- Many autistic adults have learned to modify their behaviour to fit society's expectations (masking or camouflaging). Masking can be exhausting.
- People assigned female at birth (women, transgender men, and gender diverse people) are often under-diagnosed. They may mask traits, manage socially while relationships vary in depth, have more subtle stimming, appear fine with change but become overwhelmed later, may notice others' emotions very readily, and may have interests that seem socially based or typical but become less age appropriate over time.

### Co-occurring conditions

Your doctor will try to avoid **diagnostic overshadowing**: assuming symptoms are due to autism without fully exploring other causes, including undiagnosed illness.

**Mental health:**
- Mental illness is more common in autistic people (4 to 10 times).
- It may relate to traumatic events or adversity, which autistic people are exposed to more often.
- It may be hard to distinguish from autistic characteristics, and is linked with poor long-term outcomes.
- It may include anxiety, depression, obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD), eating disorders, post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), bipolar disorder, or psychosis.
- It may occur with other neurodevelopmental conditions, such as learning difficulties or ADHD, which has a 50 to 70% concurrence.

**Challenging behaviours** (such as aggression, meltdowns, tantrums, self-injury, or pica): your doctor may look for contributing factors and their impact on you, your family, or carers, such as new or worsening mental health problems, altered sensory processing, pain, physical illness, environment, communication problems, or changes to routine or circumstances.

**Intellectual disability:** a full annual health assessment may be considered.

**Sexual orientation and gender identity:** diversity of both appears greater in autistic people. Gender incongruence is approximately 10 times more common in autistic people.

**Physical health problems** are common and may contribute to mental health problems, challenging behaviours, and early death. Being over- or under-sensitive to internal body signals can make changes harder to notice. Common conditions include:
- Epilepsy (30%).
- Bowel problems: constipation, diarrhoea, gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD).
- Metabolic syndrome and diabetes.
- Ehlers-Danlos syndrome (EDS).
- Postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome (POTS).
- Post-viral illnesses: ME/CFS and long COVID.
- Sleep disorders.
- Allergies and food intolerances.
- Dental problems.

### Examination

A physical examination can be distressing. Your doctor should ask for consent and explain each step. They may use a gradual approach, for example examining limbs before the heart and lungs, spread over time. The examination may include:
- Physical function: feet, mobility, balance, and gait.
- Teeth and gums, hearing, vision, and nutrition.
- Height, weight, BMI, waist circumference, and blood pressure.
- Sexual health and gender-specific examinations, if indicated.
- Signs of physical, psychological, or sexual abuse, if your history suggests a need.

### Investigations

There are no specific tests for autism. Because some conditions are more common, your doctor may have a lower threshold for screening. **Your doctor may consider:**
- Hearing and vision assessment, because these can strongly affect communication.
- Metabolic tests: lipids and HbA1c. Cardiovascular risk screening may start from age 25 if you also have a serious mental illness.
- The usual recommended screening tests for your age.
- Genetic screening, in some cases.

## Management

Autistic characteristics are not changed by medicines, physical, or dietary interventions. Care focuses on preventing and managing co-occurring health conditions, and supporting wellbeing and abilities.

### Diagnosis and specialist input

- For formal diagnosis, your doctor may suggest a private assessment by a psychologist or psychiatrist. Adult autism assessments are not publicly funded.
- If you have moderate to severe mental health difficulties and they need to be told apart from autism to be treated, your doctor may seek mental health advice or refer you for mental health assessment.

### Support and life skills

Your doctor may suggest:
- Life skills training, such as social learning programmes, structured leisure activities based on your interests, anger management, help with decision-making and problem-solving, and supported employment programmes.
- Speech language therapy, occupational therapy, or psychology assessment.
- A needs assessment if you have additional support needs. This may give access to ongoing support or therapy, personal care, household help, respite and carer support, life skills training, and supported employment.

### Mental health

- Mental illness is managed as it is for everyone else, but medicine doses may need adjusting because autistic people may be more sensitive to both benefits and side effects.
- Your doctor may take special care to monitor for heart and metabolic side effects, review medicines regularly, and consider stopping them where appropriate.
- Your doctor may suggest private psychological therapy, using neurodiversity-affirming approaches with appropriate adaptations. These may include Acceptance and Commitment Therapy, cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), dialectical behaviour therapy, or mindfulness-based therapies.
- If mental health difficulties are moderate to severe, your doctor may seek mental health advice or refer you for mental health assessment. Treatment may be more complex with confirmed or suspected autism.

### Medication review

Your doctor may:
- Review medicines regularly, and consider stopping any started for a diagnosis that your history does not support.
- Aim for consistency, for example avoiding brand substitutions and packaging changes. Using one pharmacy routinely can help.
- Suggest aids to help you take medicines correctly.
- Arrange appropriate monitoring if you take an antipsychotic, and seek mental health advice before starting one.
- Start new medicines at a lower dose, because of possible sensitivity.

### Complementary and alternative medicines

- These are widely used by autistic people, but their benefit is uncertain.
- Existing evidence suggests **melatonin** can be recommended for sleep problems.
- Relaxation techniques such as yoga, massage, and music therapy are safe and have some evidence of benefit.
- There is insufficient evidence for modified diets (such as gluten- and casein-free diets), hyperbaric oxygen therapy, immune therapy, and vitamin and fatty acid supplements.
- Secretin and chelation therapy are not effective treatments.

### Long-term plan and follow-up

- Your doctor may develop a long-term plan that manages physical, mental, and social health risks early, and give you written information about what was discussed and the next steps.
- Follow-up can be hard for autistic people. Practices may help by noting on your file that you are autistic, offering the first appointment of the clinic with extra time, confirming the time, place, and person in writing, and offering email or a patient portal instead of the phone.
