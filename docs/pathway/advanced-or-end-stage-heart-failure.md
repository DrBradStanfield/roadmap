# Advanced or End-Stage Heart Failure

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers severe heart failure in patients who remain symptomatic on maximal treatment, or who have multiple co-morbidities and a short life expectancy.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Help

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if there is:

- Sudden severe breathlessness at rest
- Acute fluid overload that cannot be managed at home
- Rapid deterioration in consciousness or collapse

---

## Overview

Advanced or end-stage heart failure is a serious condition. Your doctor will work with you to understand your wishes, manage your symptoms, and coordinate care — including palliative support when appropriate. Several conversations may be needed, and your family or support people are encouraged to be involved.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will look for indicators that heart failure has reached an advanced or end-stage phase.

**Indicators of deteriorating health:**

- Poor or deteriorating ability to carry out daily activities, with limited chance of improvement — for example, needing help with personal care, or spending 50% or more of the day in bed or in a chair
- Two or more unplanned hospital admissions for heart failure in the past 6 months, despite taking medications as prescribed, in a previously stable patient
- Unintentional weight loss (5–10%) over the past 3–6 months, or a BMI below 20
- Requiring residential care, or increasing support to remain at home
- Patient requesting supportive and palliative care, or wishing to withdraw from active treatment

**Indicators of end-stage heart failure:**

- Increasing shortness of breath at rest or with minimal activity, despite being on optimal therapy
- Loss of appetite, significant weight loss (cachexia), and extreme fatigue
- Hospital admission for heart failure with no identifiable trigger, while already on optimal therapy and with objective evidence of deterioration (e.g., repeat echocardiography)
- Severe heart failure alongside other advanced medical conditions
- Development of organ dysfunction — especially progressive kidney impairment — as a result of heart failure or its treatment
- Increasing resistance to diuretic medications to maintain fluid balance

---

## Management

### Advance Care Planning

Your doctor will have early, honest conversations with you and your family about:

- Your current situation and likely changes ahead
- Your expectations and goals of care
- Referral to specialist palliative care if appropriate
- Appointing an enduring power of attorney (EPA) for healthcare decisions if you have not already done so
- Creating or reviewing an advance care plan
- Agreed levels of intervention, including resuscitation preferences — in advanced heart failure, resuscitation is often unlikely to be effective, and your doctor will discuss this openly with you

### ICD Deactivation

If you have an implantable cardioverter defibrillator (ICD), your doctor will discuss whether deactivating the device is appropriate at this stage. This is a significant conversation that involves your cardiology team and palliative care support.

### Continuing Heart Failure Treatment

Your doctor will continue standard heart failure management until treatment is no longer effective at controlling symptoms.

### De-prescribing Cardiac Medications

At end-stage, your doctor may consider gradually stopping some cardiac medications that are no longer providing benefit and may be causing harm. Diuretics are usually continued until the very final stages as they help manage fluid overload.

Your doctor will consider stopping medications in a specific order based on your clinical situation. Key considerations include:

- **If kidney function is worsening:** ACE inhibitors or ARBs are usually stopped first. If eGFR falls below 30 mL/min/1.73 m², your doctor may stop ACE inhibitors, ARBs, aldosterone receptor antagonists, sacubitril/valsartan (Entresto), SGLT2 inhibitors (e.g. empagliflozin), and direct oral anticoagulants (e.g. dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban)
- **If there is increased risk of low blood sugar or recurrent urinary/genital infections:** SGLT2 inhibitors (e.g. empagliflozin) may be stopped
- **If there is increased risk of bleeding or falls:** Direct oral anticoagulants (e.g. dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban) may be stopped
- **If atrial fibrillation is present:** Antianginals, digoxin, and beta blockers are usually continued as long as possible for symptom control

Otherwise, your doctor may consider stopping medications in this general order:
1. Statins and ezetimibe
2. ACE inhibitors and ARBs
3. Aldosterone receptor antagonists
4. Antiplatelet agents and anticoagulants (with specialist input if you have a mechanical heart valve or recent coronary stent)
5. SGLT2 inhibitors (e.g. empagliflozin)
6. Sacubitril/valsartan (Entresto)
7. Beta blockers
8. Diuretics — usually continued until the very end to manage fluid

### Symptom Management

Your doctor will manage symptoms as they arise. Common symptoms include:

**Fluid overload (swelling, pulmonary oedema)**

Your doctor may:
- Adjust or add diuretic medications, including metolazone alongside loop diuretics, or switch from furosemide to bumetanide (which has better absorption — 1 mg bumetanide is equivalent to 40 mg furosemide)
- Consider subcutaneous furosemide (up to 40 mg at two separate sites) or intramuscular furosemide (up to 80 mg at two separate sites) if oral absorption is poor
- Consider nitrates such as glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) for acute pulmonary oedema; if GTN is needed regularly, a longer-acting nitrate (isosorbide mononitrate modified release or GTN patch) may be introduced
- Avoid nitrates if you have severe aortic stenosis or low blood pressure

**Fatigue**

Fatigue is universal in advanced heart disease. Your doctor will look for reversible contributing causes and may suggest gentle exercise where appropriate.

**Congestive symptoms and bloating**

These can be difficult to manage. Skin care for swollen limbs is important. If significant fluid accumulation in the abdomen (ascites) develops, specialist input may be needed.

**Breathlessness (dyspnoea)**

Your doctor will follow palliative care principles to manage breathlessness.

**Depression**

Depression is common in heart failure. Your doctor may consider non-pharmacological approaches first. If medication is needed, SSRIs, SNRIs, or mirtazapine may be appropriate — tricyclic antidepressants should be avoided.

**Refractory angina**

Short-acting opioids may be considered if nitrates do not adequately control angina.

**Pain**

Your doctor will assess the specific cause of pain, which may be positional, related to fluid retention, or due to other conditions such as angina, liver capsule distension, arthritis, or gout. NSAIDs should be avoided as they worsen heart failure.

**Kidney impairment**

Oscillating kidney function is common in advanced heart failure and reduces the effectiveness of diuretics. It can also affect how some opioid medications are cleared from the body — particularly when eGFR is below 30 mL/min. Your doctor will monitor this carefully.

**Weight loss and poor appetite**

Palliative care principles apply to managing cachexia and anorexia.

**Nausea and constipation**

These are managed according to palliative care principles.

### Support Services

Your doctor may organise or discuss:

- Occupational therapy assessment to look at safety and functioning at home, including equipment and home modifications
- Needs assessment for personal care assistance
- Transportation support (mobility parking permits, disability parking identification, and subsidised travel schemes may be available depending on your situation)
- Carer support resources for family members and caregivers
- Palliative care support for complex psychological or emotional concerns, including grief, loss of meaning, and difficult family dynamics
