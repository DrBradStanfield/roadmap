# Cough in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers management of cough in adults receiving palliative care for a life-limiting illness.

---

## Background

Cough is a common symptom in palliative care, particularly in respiratory conditions such as primary or metastatic lung cancer and lymphangitis. It often has more than one cause — many patients also have underlying COPD or interstitial lung disease.

Cough can seriously impair quality of life by disturbing sleep or affecting communication, and may cause incontinence, headache, hernias, or vomiting.

---

## Identifying the Cause

Your doctor will explore the possible cause of cough. Causes fall into three categories:

**Direct result of cancer:**
- Major airway or endobronchial lesions
- Pleural disease
- Infiltration of lung tissue
- Lymphangitis carcinomatosa (cancer cells infiltrating lung lymphatics)

**Complications of cancer or treatment:**
- Pleural or pericardial effusion
- Radiation-induced pneumonitis or fibrosis
- Chemotherapy-induced fibrosis
- Pneumonia
- Tracheo-oesophageal fistula
- Superior vena cava syndrome
- Metastatic spread

**Non-malignant causes:**
- Aspiration (e.g. from muscle weakness or swallowing difficulties)
- Post-nasal drip
- Asthma, COPD, or interstitial lung disease
- Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD)
- Infection (e.g. bronchitis, bronchiectasis)
- Medications (e.g. ACE inhibitors, beta-blockers, nitrofurantoin)
- Sarcoidosis
- Heart failure
- Pulmonary embolism

Your doctor will assess triggers, whether the cough is worse at night, the volume and colour of any sputum, and associated symptoms such as breathlessness or wheeze. Investigations like chest X-ray are only done if they would change management.

---

## Management

### Key Decision: Suppress or Treat the Cause?

Your doctor will decide whether it is more appropriate to suppress the cough or treat the underlying cause. If a patient is dying and can no longer clear sputum, suppressing the cough may be appropriate. Some treatments (e.g. proton-pump inhibitors, inhaled corticosteroids) take several weeks to work.

If lung malignancy is the suspected primary cause, your doctor may seek oncology advice about radiotherapy or chemotherapy.

### Dry Cough

In palliative care, some medications are used at doses or by routes that are outside their licensed indications — your doctor will explain this.

Simple measures to try first:
- Humidified room air
- Repositioning

Medications your doctor may consider:
- **Nebulised saline** (sodium chloride 0.9%)
- **Pholcodine linctus** — an antitussive (cough suppressant), 10 to 15 mg up to four times daily
- **Low-dose morphine** — usually starting at 2.5 to 5 mg every 4 to 6 hours, or as needed
- **Corticosteroids** — for specific causes; specialist advice is sought before starting

### Moist (Productive) Cough

Medications your doctor may consider:
- Nebulised saline
- Oral prednisone if bronchoconstriction is suspected
- Antibiotics if infection is suspected

**Important:** Anticholinergic medications are avoided for thick, tenacious secretions as they make it harder to cough up mucus. Anticholinergics include antihistamines, antimuscarinics, antiparkinsonians, antispasmodics, tricyclic antidepressants, and neuroleptics.

Breathing techniques such as the **Active Cycle of Breathing** may help. Chest physiotherapy is sometimes available through hospices.

---

## When to Involve a Specialist

Your doctor will seek adult palliative care support and advice if:
- Symptoms persist despite treatment
- A trial of corticosteroids is being considered
- Oncology input is needed for cancer-related cough
- Chest physiotherapy via a hospice is needed for moist cough
