# Fatigue

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to fatigue in adults — identifying the cause, ruling out serious conditions, and managing symptoms.

---

## Red Flags — See Your Doctor Promptly

Your doctor needs to know urgently if you have:

- Recent-onset fatigue in a previously well older person
- Unintentional weight loss or gain
- Unexplained swollen glands (lymphadenopathy)

These features can indicate cancer, a blood disorder, or a new serious condition and require prompt assessment.

---

## What Is Fatigue?

Fatigue is a state of physical or mental exhaustion with difficulty initiating or maintaining activity. It can affect alertness, physical skills, judgement, and decision-making — and for some people it is extremely debilitating.

Fatigue is common and non-specific. Common causes include overexertion, deconditioning, viral illness, anaemia, lung disease, medications, cancer, and depression. In many cases, no specific cause is found.

Fatigue is different from:
- **Excessive daytime sleepiness** — the tendency to fall asleep; sleepy people feel better after a nap, whereas fatigue is worsened by activity and not relieved by sleep
- **Muscle weakness** — difficulty performing specific tasks (climbing stairs, combing hair) due to weakness rather than exhaustion

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history asking about:

- The fatigue itself: onset, duration, pattern (constant or variable, worse with exertion), and how much it affects daily life
- Associated symptoms: pain, breathing problems, menstrual changes, gut symptoms
- Lifestyle: alcohol and drug use, diet, caffeine, physical activity, sleep quality, shift work, social pressures, financial or relationship stress
- Past medical history, family history, and all medications (including over-the-counter and supplements)
- Your own thoughts about what might be causing it

### Post-Exertional Malaise (PEM)

If your fatigue worsens significantly after physical, mental, or emotional effort — typically 12–48 hours later, lasting days to weeks — this is called post-exertional malaise (PEM). Your doctor will consider chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS) or Long COVID if PEM is present. Keeping a symptom diary can help clarify this pattern.

### Screening for Depression and Anxiety

Your doctor will ask a couple of screening questions about mood and worry, as depression and anxiety are common contributors to fatigue.

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will examine you including:
- Vital signs, weight, and BMI
- Looking for pallor, jaundice, or skin changes
- Thyroid examination
- Lymph nodes and organ enlargement
- Heart and lung examination
- Neurological and cognitive assessment

## Possible Causes

### Common Causes

- Anaemia
- Thyroid disorders (underactive thyroid is a frequent culprit)
- Diabetes
- Sleep disorders (including obstructive sleep apnoea)
- Viral or post-viral illness
- Menopause
- Medications (antidepressants, opioids, beta blockers, antihistamines, antihypertensives, muscle relaxants)
- Alcohol or drug use
- Obesity
- COPD (lung disease)
- Pregnancy
- Depression or anxiety

### Less Commonly Considered

- Chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS)
- Coeliac disease
- Haemochromatosis (iron overload)
- Hepatitis C
- HIV
- Chronic kidney or liver disease
- Addison's disease (adrenal insufficiency)
- Heart failure or coronary artery disease
- Electrolyte disorders (low sodium, high calcium)
- Rheumatological conditions: fibromyalgia, polymyalgia rheumatica, lupus, rheumatoid arthritis
- Malignancy
- Multiple sclerosis
- Tuberculosis
- Subacute bacterial endocarditis

---

## Investigations

Your doctor will arrange targeted tests based on your history and examination — not a blanket panel. If you have no red flags, your doctor may wait a month before testing, as fatigue often resolves on its own.

Tests that may be arranged include:

- Full blood count (FBC)
- CRP (inflammation marker)
- Electrolytes, kidney function, and liver function
- Blood glucose and/or HbA1c (diabetes screen)
- Calcium
- Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH)
- Serum ferritin (iron stores)
- B12 (if there are specific indications)
- Urinalysis
- Coeliac antibodies
- Pregnancy test (beta-hCG) if relevant

Additional tests (HIV, hepatitis, reproductive hormones, sleep study, vitamin D) may be ordered if your history suggests them.

**Note:** Tests for "adrenal fatigue" (e.g. cortisol) are not recommended for fatigue alone — "adrenal fatigue" is not a recognised clinical diagnosis.

If no cause is found initially, your doctor will reassess in 1–3 months and may repeat baseline tests.

## Management

Management depends entirely on the cause found. If a specific diagnosis is made, your doctor will treat that condition. If ME/CFS is suspected, management follows a different pathway (see ME/CFS).

### Education and Support

Your doctor will:
- Acknowledge the real impact fatigue has on your life
- Name the condition where possible
- Explain the role of investigations and review results with you
- Consider recommending a fatigue diary to track activity patterns and triggers

### Lifestyle Changes

**Stress management:**
- Identify and modify sources of stress where possible (work conditions, shift work, financial stress)
- Relaxation techniques: breathing exercises, progressive muscle relaxation, mindfulness

**Sleep hygiene:**
- Consistent bed and wake times every day
- Use the bed only for sleep and sex
- Reduce daytime napping
- Limit screens before bed
- Reduce caffeine, alcohol, and smoking

**Diet:**
- Regular meals and nutritious snacks; at least 8 cups of fluid per day
- Avoid relying on caffeine or sugary drinks for energy
- If following a restrictive diet, you may need supplements
- Referral to a dietitian if needed

**Physical activity:**
- Start gently and build gradually
- Choose enjoyable activities (walking, swimming, cycling, gardening)
- Avoid overdoing it — this can worsen fatigue and lead to setbacks
- A daily 30-minute walk may help reduce fatigue over time
- Physiotherapy referral or Green Prescription may be available

**Alcohol and drugs:** Address if contributing.

**Mental health:** If depression, anxiety, grief, or other mental health issues are contributing, counselling or therapy will be considered.

**Medication review:** Your doctor will review whether any of your current medicines may be contributing to fatigue.

**Work and daily activities:** If fatigue is affecting your ability to work or carry out daily tasks, your doctor may refer to occupational therapy or community employment services. Pacing strategies — spreading activities evenly across the week at a measured pace — can help.

### Follow-Up

Your doctor will arrange review in 1–3 months. If there is no improvement after **6 months** of appropriate management, or if the diagnosis remains uncertain, a referral to a general medicine specialist will be considered.

---

## Referrals Your Doctor May Consider

- **General medicine specialist:** if red flags are present, no improvement after 6 months, or diagnosis is uncertain
- **Physiotherapist:** to support graded activity
- **Green Prescription:** community exercise support programme
- **Occupational therapist:** if fatigue affects work or daily function
- **Dietitian:** if diet is a contributing factor
- **Mental health services / counselling:** if psychological factors are prominent
- **Health coach or health improvement practitioner:** if available within the practice
