# Bacterial Vaginosis

> **Note:** This information is a clinical reference summary. Diagnosis and treatment decisions should be made by your doctor or a qualified health professional, who can assess your individual circumstances.

## What is bacterial vaginosis?

Bacterial vaginosis (BV) is a clinical syndrome caused by a loss of normal vaginal lactobacilli and overgrowth of diverse anaerobic bacteria (including *Gardnerella vaginalis*, *Atopobium vaginae*, *Mobiluncus* spp., and *Prevotella* spp.). This causes a rise in vaginal pH and increased amines, which can produce an odour.

The absence of inflammation distinguishes vaginosis from vaginitis.

Key facts:
- BV is the most common cause of abnormal vaginal discharge in people of childbearing age; recurrence is common.
- Up to 50% of people with BV are asymptomatic.
- BV is not currently considered a sexually transmitted infection (STI).
- BV is associated with STIs including chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and trichomoniasis, and with increased risk of HIV transmission and acquisition.

## Symptoms

- Offensive fishy odour (often worse after sex)
- Thin, white, homogenous vaginal discharge
- Many people have no symptoms at all

## Risk factors

- Change in sexual partner, or sex with the same partner (for recurrent BV)
- Vaginal douching
- Intrauterine device (IUD)
- Smoking

## Protective factors

- Hormonal contraception (regardless of type)
- Condom use
- Having a male partner who is circumcised

## Complications

BV may increase the risk of:
- Spontaneous abortion
- Premature labour
- Chorioamnionitis
- Postpartum endometritis and pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), especially following termination of pregnancy (TOP), IUD insertion, or other uterine instrumentation

Routine screening and treatment has not been conclusively shown to reduce these risks.

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis requires a clinical assessment including speculum examination. Your doctor may take:

- A high vaginal Amies culture swab for BV and *Candida* (clinical details must be included on the lab form)
- A vaginal PCR/NAAT swab for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and *Trichomonas vaginalis*
- Possibly additional tests as part of a full sexual health check

If examination is declined, self-collected swabs are an acceptable alternative.

**Important:** Routine screening is not recommended for people without symptoms.

## Treatment

Treatment decisions should be made with your doctor, who can tailor the approach to your specific situation. The following is a summary of guideline-based options.

### Symptomatic patients (first-line)

**Metronidazole 400 mg orally with food, twice daily for 7 days** (preferred)

Alternatives:
- Metronidazole 2 g orally as a single dose (less effective)
- Ornidazole 500 mg orally twice daily for 5 days (avoid in pregnancy)
- Clindamycin 300 mg orally twice daily for 7 days

### Pregnancy

- If symptomatic: metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 7 days is safe.
- If there is a history of preterm delivery, obstetric advice should be sought — treatment may reduce the risk of recurrence.
- Treatment of asymptomatic BV in pregnancy has not been shown to improve pregnancy outcomes.

### Breastfeeding

Metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 7 days. High doses should be avoided as metronidazole may affect the taste of breast milk.

### Postmenopausal patients

Menopause alters the vaginal microbiome and affects the validity of standard diagnostic criteria. Symptoms may be related to atrophic vaginitis rather than BV. A doctor should assess whether topical oestrogen or menopause hormone therapy (MHT) is appropriate.

### Before uterine procedures (e.g., TOP, IUD insertion)

- If asymptomatic: benefit of treatment is inconclusive.
- If symptomatic: treat with metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 7 days.

### Recurrent BV (3+ episodes in 12 months)

Recurrence is common. Using condoms may reduce relapse by preventing recolonisation. Second-line options exist (including longer metronidazole courses, boric acid pessaries, and vaginal clindamycin preparations). These require assessment and prescription from a doctor — seek review if BV keeps recurring.

**Note on boric acid:** Boric acid pessaries are an unfunded treatment option for recurrent BV. Boric acid is contraindicated in pregnancy and must never be taken orally.

### Allergy or intolerance to metronidazole (nitroimidazoles)

Clindamycin 300 mg orally twice daily for 7 days is an alternative. Seek specialist advice if allergic to standard options.

## Genital skin care (for patients with itch)

- Avoid soaps, detergents, and perfumed products on the vulval area — use water only or soap substitutes
- Avoid scratching or rubbing (a single scratch can perpetuate itching for a week)
- Avoid staying in wet swimwear or clothing
- Prefer tampons or menstrual cups over pads or panty liners (especially scented ones)
- Keep cool, especially at night
- Wear loose-fitting, natural-fibre underwear
- Use cold compresses to manage itch
- Apply emollients (e.g., sorbolene) to prevent skin fissuring — kept refrigerated is often more soothing
- Barrier creams (e.g., zinc and castor oil, Vaseline) can be protective

## Sexual contacts

- Treatment may be required for sexual contacts with a vagina.
- Treatment of sexual contacts with a penis is not currently recommended — evidence does not show it reduces recurrence risk.

## When to see your doctor

You should see your doctor if you have symptoms suggesting BV. Seek prompt medical review if:

- Symptoms persist or do not improve with treatment
- You have had more than 3 episodes of BV in 12 months
- You are pregnant and have a history of preterm delivery
- You have known allergy to standard treatments
- You are concerned about possible co-existing STIs
