# Post-Natural Disaster Health

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers health problems arising from natural disasters or adverse weather events — including floods, fires, storms, heatwaves, and droughts.

---

## Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if you have:

- Respiratory symptoms (shortness of breath, wheeze, chest pain) after submersion in water or smoke exposure
- Signs of severe infection: high fever, spreading redness, confusion, or rapid deterioration
- Chest pain, palpitations, or collapse — especially if you have a pre-existing heart or lung condition
- Suicidal thoughts or intent
- Delusional or psychotic symptoms
- A child with bloody diarrhoea and fever (risk of haemolytic uraemic syndrome — do not delay)

---

## Background

Following a natural disaster, most people recover with minimal assistance, but these events can cause significant physical and mental illness.

**Common physical issues include:**
- Exacerbation of chronic health conditions
- Injuries and infections (less common but important)

**Mental health:** Distress immediately after a disaster is normal and expected. Most people do not develop long-term mental health problems. However, a minority do, and may continue to present with ongoing issues for months to years. Possible mental health concerns include:
- Normal grief and stress reactions
- Acute stress disorders and adjustment disorders
- Exacerbation of an existing mental health condition
- Substance use disorders
- Anxiety, panic, and depressive disorders
- Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD)

Psychological recovery may take weeks to years.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history, asking about:

- Your current safety — access to shelter, safe drinking water, and food
- Main priorities and concerns (e.g., bereavement, financial stress, loss of property or pets)
- Injuries requiring immediate attention (e.g., lacerations, burns)
- New physical symptoms (e.g., shortness of breath, diarrhoea, vomiting, fever)
- Any exacerbation of chronic health conditions
- Regular medications (including whether you can access them)
- Vaccination status (especially tetanus, influenza, and COVID-19)

> Patients may be too distressed to discuss their experience. A doctor will not pressure you to talk about events — this can cause psychological harm. If you wish to share, they will listen and empathise.

### Disaster-Specific Physical Issues

**Floods:**
- Skin wounds and infections from contaminated floodwater, silt, or chemicals
- Ingestion or inhalation of flood water
- Hypothermia or sunburn from exposure
- Bites or scratches from displaced animals
- Asthma and allergy exacerbation from dried flood debris and dust
- Infections to consider: cellulitis, gastroenteritis (e.g., *Campylobacter*), *Legionella*, and — rarely in New Zealand — leptospirosis or hepatitis A

**Fires:**
- Smoke inhalation
- Exacerbation of pre-existing cardiovascular, respiratory, or renal conditions
- Dehydration and heat exhaustion (especially in older people)
- Burns

**Storms:**
- History of allergic rhinitis, rye grass allergy, or asthma can predispose to thunderstorm asthma
- Increased shortness of breath, wheeze, or chest pain may indicate an episode of thunderstorm asthma

**Heatwave and drought:**
- Dehydration and heat exhaustion (especially in older people)
- Sunburn
- Damage to medications stored in heat

### Mental Health Assessment

Your doctor will assess whether symptoms are mild to moderate or severe:

**Mild to moderate symptoms of distress:**
- Tiredness, loss of energy
- Difficulty sleeping, unpleasant dreams
- Irritability, nervousness, anger, or guilt
- Tearfulness
- Family distress
- Anxiety or depression
- Harmful use of alcohol or illicit substances
- Intrusive thoughts or flashbacks
- Worsening of a current or previous mental health condition

**Severe symptoms (urgent):**
- Impaired functionality
- Suicidal ideation or intent
- Delusional or psychotic symptoms
- Marked hyperarousal or avoidance
- Deterioration in functioning
- Treatment-resistant depression

People react differently to traumatic events. Distress in the weeks to months following a disaster is a normal response, and for most people these symptoms lessen with time.

**Higher risk groups for mental health problems following disasters:**
- Pre-event: socio-economic vulnerability; previous trauma, mental health issues, or substance abuse
- During event: high exposure or perceived risk; bereavement; physical injuries; those in emergency or front-line roles; significant financial losses
- Post-event: lack of social support; prolonged isolation or living alone; recent migrants or refugees; ongoing domestic violence; housing or insurance problems; financial losses or unemployment

Children of all ages, including infants, can be affected. Behavioural changes are common.

### Social Circumstances

Your doctor will also consider social factors:
- Strong family and community connections (including with pets) promote well-being in times of disaster
- Financial stress from loss of income, grant applications, or insurance processes is common
- Relocation from uninhabitable housing is particularly hard for older people — evidence shows increased morbidity and functional deterioration in those who are relocated
- Previous disaster experience — some communities have been exposed to multiple events

## Management

### Acute Injuries and Infections

**Wounds, lacerations, and cellulitis:**
- Do not enter any body of water with an open wound or abrasion
- Meticulous wound cleaning and debridement is essential
- Cover all wounds with a waterproof dressing
- Tetanus immunisation must be up to date (within five years for contaminated wounds)
- Prophylactic antibiotics are not necessary for all wounds
- Your doctor may consider early empiric antibiotics if you have risk factors (diabetes, deep puncture wounds, contaminated wound closure, liver disease, malignancy, or immunosuppression)

For infected wounds from flood water, your doctor may prescribe:

*Adults:*
- Amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 625 mg three times daily for 7 days, **and** doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days
- If penicillin allergy: co-trimoxazole 960 mg twice daily for 7 days, **and** doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days
- If systemically unwell or significant co-morbidities, ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 7 days may be added
- If soil- or sewage-contaminated water (and not already on amoxicillin + clavulanic acid): add metronidazole 600 mg twice daily orally

*Children:*
- Amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 15–30 mg/kg (maximum 625 mg) three times daily for 7 days, **or**
- If penicillin allergy: co-trimoxazole 24 mg/kg (maximum 960 mg) twice daily for 7 days
- If soil- or sewage-contaminated water (and not already on amoxicillin + clavulanic acid): add metronidazole 7.5 mg/kg (up to 400 mg) three times daily orally

**COVID-19 and influenza:**

Outbreaks may occur in evacuation centres or overcrowded areas. If symptomatic:
- Rapid antigen test for COVID-19
- Isolate where possible
- Hand hygiene; cough/sneeze into elbow; wear a mask
- Your doctor may consider antiviral treatment if you meet criteria

**Gastroenteritis:**

Diarrhoea and vomiting are more common after natural disasters due to disruption of sanitation, contaminated water or food, and exposure to pathogens during clean-up.

Food safety advice:
- Dispose of any refrigerated food left above 5°C for more than 4 hours
- Dispose of any frozen food if the freezer was disconnected for more than 24 hours
- Discard food with damaged canning or packaging, or any food in contact with flood water or mud
- Practise hand hygiene wherever possible

Supportive measures are usually sufficient. Empiric antibiotics are not indicated for most community-acquired infectious diarrhoea.

If severe acute infectious diarrhoea, your doctor may:
- Send stool for culture and PCR
- Start empiric antibiotics:
  - *Adults:* azithromycin 500 mg once daily orally for 3 days, or ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 3 days
  - *Children with severe diarrhoea (but not bloody diarrhoea without fever):* azithromycin 10 mg/kg (up to 500 mg) daily for 3 days

> Children with bloody diarrhoea without fever or sepsis: do not use empiric antibiotics (risk of haemolytic uraemic syndrome). Seek urgent paediatric assessment.

**Heat stroke and heat exhaustion:**

See [Healthify: Heat Stroke and Heat Exhaustion](https://healthify.nz/health-a-z/h/heat-stroke-and-heat-exhaustion).

**Less common but important infections:**

*Legionella:*
- Found in contaminated water and soil; more likely after natural disasters
- Incubation period typically 2–10 days (up to 26 days)
- Initially non-specific: fever, muscle aches, headache. May progress to pneumonia (legionnaires' disease) or a milder illness called Pontiac fever
- Patients with legionella pneumonia often do not improve on standard antibiotics like amoxicillin
- If systemically well, your doctor may prescribe azithromycin 500 mg once daily for 5 days, or ciprofloxacin
- If systemically unwell, hospital assessment is required

*Leptospirosis (rare in New Zealand):*
- Bacteria excreted in animal urine; can survive in water or soil for months
- Incubation period typically 10 days (range 2–30 days)
- Symptoms: fever, muscle aches, headache, nausea, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, red eyes without discharge. Severe form (Weil's disease) causes jaundice
- Your doctor will arrange blood and urine tests for leptospirosis
- If systemically well, your doctor may prescribe:
  - *Adults:* doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days (if doxycycline allergy or pregnancy: amoxicillin 1 g three times daily for 7 days)
  - *Children:* azithromycin (10 mg/kg day 1, then 5 mg/kg for 3 days total), or amoxicillin 50 mg/kg/day in three divided doses for 7 days
- If systemically unwell, hospital assessment is required
- The Public Health Unit must be notified if leptospirosis is suspected — no need to wait for laboratory confirmation

*Submersion and near-drowning:*
- Most people who present to their GP after submersion will not have aspirated water
- If submerged for longer than a breath hold, or if there is any concern about aspiration, your doctor may arrange emergency assessment — symptoms can be delayed, and 6–8 hours of observation in hospital is recommended
- Chest X-ray is not required if there are no respiratory symptoms
- Prophylactic antibiotics are generally not needed unless the water was grossly contaminated

**Tetanus:** Ensure tetanus immunisation is up to date (within the last five years) if involved in clean-up after a natural disaster.

### Psychological Care — Staged Approach

**Level 1 — Immediate aftermath (psychological first aid):**
- Listen and empathise
- Promote a sense of safety, calm, social connectedness, self-efficacy, and hope
- Reassure that psychological distress after a traumatic event is normal and usually settles with time — but if distress persists or worsens, seek further medical assessment
- For severe, disabling distress, your doctor may consider a single dose of a short-acting benzodiazepine (e.g., lorazepam 0.5–1 mg)
- Re-engaging with normal daily routines as soon as possible has a calming psychological effect
- Building and maintaining social connections reduces the risk of mental illness considerably
- Encourage regular eating, relaxation, and avoidance of alcohol and substances

**Level 2 — Persistent mild to moderate symptoms:**

For symptoms continuing after the initial period, your doctor or a health professional may use brief, practical psychological strategies:
- Skills for managing emotional reactions
- Problem-solving
- Promoting helpful thinking
- Activity scheduling
- Referral to a Health Improvement Practitioner, Kaiāwhina, or Health Coach if available

**Level 3 — Formal mental health interventions:**

For patients with severe distress or altered function persisting for months after the disaster, including PTSD, prolonged grief, or diagnosable mental health conditions:
- Your doctor may consider pharmacological treatment
- Referral to a psychologist or counsellor with skills in psychological interventions
- If persistent severe mental illness, your doctor may refer for mental health assessment

*Prolonged or complicated grief:* More frequent in those bereaved by disaster or violent death, or parents who lose a child. Diagnosis is based on symptoms and level of function — function matters more than time since bereavement.

### Children

Children of all ages, including infants, can be affected by traumatic events. Your doctor will provide parents and carers with resources and advice:

- Maintain, as much as possible, usual routines — this provides a sense of safety
- Talk about events with age-appropriate language; include positive discussion about recovery steps
- Allow positive distractions (games, books, time outside if safe)
- Monitor children's media exposure; sit with the child to discuss concerns and help them make meaning of the event
- It is normal and healthy for children to experience distress — for most, issues will settle over time

For support: [Emerging Minds Community Trauma Toolkit](https://emergingminds.com.au/resources/toolkits/community-trauma-toolkit/)

### Flood-Specific Advice

**Water safety:**
- Follow Civil Defence and local council advice on drinking water
- Consider boiling water for drinking; avoid tank water from flood-affected properties unless confirmed safe
- Tap water: your water provider can advise if it is safe

**Food safety:**
- Dispose of refrigerated food left above 5°C for more than 4 hours
- Dispose of frozen food if freezer was disconnected for more than 24 hours
- Discard food with damaged packaging or any food in contact with flood water or mud
- Do not eat home-grown fruit or vegetables in contact with flood water — contaminants in soil may take a month or more to disperse

**During clean-up:**
- Avoid skin contact with flood water or mud (contaminated with sewage; pathogens thrive in warm water and mud)
- Wear protective clothing: gloves, plastic apron, long boots, mask, and goggles
- Do not use petrol or diesel generators indoors — exhaust contains harmful carbon monoxide
- Use heaters or dehumidifiers to dry homes
- Clean small areas of mould with domestic products; extensive mould may require a specialist
- Flush plumbing to remove contaminated debris and biofilm (Legionella risk)
- Scrub hard surfaces with hot water and detergent; dry belongings outside; wash fabrics in hot water

### Fire-Specific Advice

- Avoid breathing smoke: stay inside with windows closed, or evacuate early
- Reduce physical activity
- If using air conditioning, switch to "re-circulate" to maintain air quality
- If you have a pre-existing respiratory or cardiac condition, watch for increasing shortness of breath, wheezing, or chest pain — seek medical attention if these develop
- If reliant on tank water, ensure water safety before drinking after a fire

### Storm-Specific Advice

- Older people, young children, and those with respiratory or cardiac conditions are at highest risk
- Stay indoors with windows and doors closed
- If using air conditioning, switch to "re-circulate"
- If you have a known respiratory condition, activate your medical action plan and seek attention at the first sign of exacerbation

### Heatwave and Drought Advice

- Older people, young children, pregnant women, those with low fitness, and those with cardiac conditions are at highest risk for heat-related illness
- Reduce excess clothing, increase fluid intake, and use home cooling devices
- Store medication in a cool location (below 25°C)

### Family Violence and Child Safety

If there is concern about domestic or family violence:
- Phone **0800-733-843** (Women's Refuge) for advice, support, and safe accommodation
- Phone **0800-044-334** (Safe to Talk) if worried about your own behaviour

If there is any concern about the safety of a child, your doctor will follow the Child Abuse and Neglect pathway.

## When Your Doctor May Refer

Your doctor may refer or seek specialist advice when:

- You are systemically unwell with a post-disaster infection — hospital medical assessment may be needed
- Severe acute infectious diarrhoea — hospital medical assessment may be needed
- A child has severe or bloody diarrhoea — urgent paediatric assessment
- Respiratory symptoms follow submersion, or the water was grossly contaminated — infectious diseases advice may be needed
- Prolonged or severe mental illness — referral for mental health assessment or to a psychologist or counsellor
