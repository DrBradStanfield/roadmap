# Knee Injuries

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and initial management of acute knee injuries in adults and adolescents.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department immediately if:

- Suspected knee dislocation (significant deformity, swelling, and instability after major trauma)
- Absent or reduced pulses below the knee, or loss of movement or sensation distal to the injury (neurovascular compromise)
- Any knee fracture with inability to bear weight
- Injury to the extensor mechanism (inability to perform a straight leg raise)
- Acutely locked knee that will not straighten

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history looking for clues to the likely injury:

### Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) Injury

- Commonly caused by a valgus twisting mechanism — forcefully landing on the leg while rapidly changing direction
- A popping sound is often heard at the time of injury
- Patient typically falls, is unable to finish sport, and needs help leaving the field
- Knee usually swells quickly and weight-bearing is difficult
- Acute swelling in the absence of fracture strongly suggests ACL injury

### Posterior Cruciate Ligament (PCL) Injury

- Generally caused by a direct blow to a flexed knee
- May present with posterior knee pain, a subtle limp, modest swelling, and reduced end-of-range knee flexion
- More severe symptoms suggest injury to other structures as well

### Collateral Ligament Injury (MCL / LCL)

- Includes both medial (MCL) and lateral (LCL) collateral ligaments — MCL is most common
- Caused by significant varus or valgus strain, usually a contact injury
- Does not typically cause significant swelling, as the ligament sits outside the joint

### Meniscal Injury

- Generally occurs when a loaded knee is twisted
- Locking, blocking, or catching sensation suggests meniscal injury
- A mild effusion may develop

### Knee Fracture

- Femoral condylar fractures — often unable to bear weight, pain over the distal femur, may have blood in the joint (haemarthrosis)
- Tibial eminence fractures — mostly in children aged 8–14 years, from ACL-type or hyperextension injuries
- Tibial tuberosity fractures — mostly adolescents involved in jumping sports (basketball, gymnastics, football)
- Patellar fracture — caused by a direct blow, or forceful quadriceps contraction against a semi-flexed knee; significant swelling may be present
- Tibial plateau fractures — from axial loading with varus/valgus stress; tenderness over the proximal tibia; often prevent weight-bearing; may be associated with ligament injuries

### Patellar Dislocation

- Caused by a twisting mechanism or direct blow to a partially flexed knee
- More common in adolescents
- Presents with a very painful knee in a flexed position with the kneecap displaced outward; large swelling may be present

### Knee Dislocation

- Rare but potentially limb-threatening
- Generally occurs after high-energy trauma
- Neurovascular compromise must be ruled out

## Physical Examination

Your doctor will examine the knee — note that acute knee injuries can be difficult to assess fully at first visit; examination is often more informative once swelling settles.

Examination typically includes:

- Ability to bear weight
- Tenderness and swelling with palpation
- Range of movement and muscle strength
- Tests for specific injuries:
  - ACL — Lachman and pivot shift tests
  - PCL — posterior drawer test and posterior sag
  - Collateral ligament — varus and valgus stress tests
  - Meniscus — McMurray's and Thessaly tests
  - Patella instability — patellar apprehension test
- Straight leg raise to check the extensor mechanism
- Pulses, movement, and sensation below the knee to rule out neurovascular compromise

A large effusion raises suspicion for fracture, ligament tear, or blood in the joint (haemarthrosis).

---

## Investigations

### X-ray

Your doctor may use the **Ottawa Knee Rules** to decide if an X-ray is needed. X-ray is indicated if any of the following apply after acute knee injury:

- Age 55 years or older
- Tenderness at the head of the fibula
- Isolated tenderness of the patella
- Inability to flex the knee to 90 degrees
- Inability to bear weight (unable to take four steps) immediately after the injury and at assessment

If a first patellar dislocation, X-ray is arranged to look for a fracture to the medial patella.

### MRI

Your doctor may consider an MRI if criteria are met — this is not routinely arranged for all acute knee injuries.

### Ultrasound

Ultrasound is not routinely arranged for acute knee injuries.

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following, depending on the injury:

### Emergency Referral

Acute orthopaedic assessment is needed for:

- Any knee fracture
- Knee dislocation
- Extensor mechanism injury
- Concerns about neurovascular status
- Acutely locked knee
- First patellar dislocation with fracture on X-ray
- PCL or collateral ligament injury with major lateral-sided tear, associated injuries, or significant laxity

### Swollen Knee Without Fracture

If the knee is swollen but no fracture is found, early physiotherapy is arranged. If the knee does not settle quickly, orthopaedic assessment is requested.

### ACL Injury

- Initial treatment: Tubigrip compression bandage, RICE (rest, ice, compression, elevation), and crutches if needed; weight-bear as able
- If large effusion, aspiration may be considered for pain relief
- Early physiotherapy to strengthen quadriceps and hamstrings and restore range of motion
- Follow-up at 2 weeks; if ACL injury is confirmed, orthopaedic assessment is arranged
- Surgery (when indicated) is performed once full range of motion and good strength are restored — unlikely before 3 weeks post-injury

### PCL and Collateral Ligament Injuries

- Minor (incomplete tears): analgesia, RICE, Tubigrip, and crutches for a few days, then mobilise as able
- More significant (complete tears): a hinged brace may be considered
- Review after 1–2 weeks; if significant laxity persists, orthopaedic assessment is requested

### Meniscal Tear

- RICE, early physiotherapy, and gradual mobilisation
- Many meniscal tears settle over 6–8 weeks
- If the knee does not settle, orthopaedic assessment is arranged
- If the knee is locked: most unlock with analgesia, rest, and support; if not, acute orthopaedic assessment is needed

### Stable Injury Without Large Effusion

- Ice and analgesia — paracetamol or a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) such as ibuprofen or naproxen — for pain
- If effusion is present: RICE, Tubigrip, crutches, and physiotherapy referral; review in 2 weeks
- If no swelling at any stage, significant injury is unlikely and no further management is usually needed

### Return to Work and Sport

Your doctor will advise based on the injury type:

- Minor injuries: usually 2–3 weeks
- Complete ligament ruptures: may be more than 6 weeks before returning to active sport
- ACL injuries and lateral-sided ligament injuries may require surgery and a longer recovery
