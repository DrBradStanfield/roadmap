# Knee Injuries

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: September 2021.*

This pathway covers the assessment and initial management of acute knee injuries.

> **Always discuss a knee injury with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

These need acute orthopaedic assessment in hospital. Call 111 or go to the emergency department if you have:

- A suspected knee dislocation, especially after a major knee injury with significant deformity, swelling, and instability
- Concerns about the pulses, movement, or feeling below the injury (neurovascular status)
- A suspected knee fracture: for example, you cannot take four steps (two on each leg) after the injury
- An injury to the extensor mechanism of the knee (checked with a straight leg raise)
- A knee that has locked and stays locked (most locked knees unlock on their own)

If the knee swells quickly after an injury but you can take four steps, see your doctor the same day. They may arrange an X-ray.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history looking for clues to the likely injury:

### Anterior Cruciate Ligament (ACL) Injury

- Commonly caused by a valgus twisting mechanism, such as forcefully landing on the leg while trying to change direction quickly
- A popping sound is often heard at the time of injury
- People fall to the ground, cannot finish the game, and need help off the field
- The knee usually swells quickly, and the person cannot bear weight on it
- If there is no fracture, acute swelling suggests an ACL injury

### Posterior Cruciate Ligament (PCL) Injury

- Generally caused by a direct blow to a flexed knee
- May present with posterior knee pain, a subtle limp, modest swelling, and reduced end-of-range knee flexion
- More severe symptoms suggest injury to other structures as well

### Collateral Ligament Injury (MCL / LCL)

- Includes both medial (MCL) and lateral (LCL) collateral ligament injuries. MCL injury is the most common.
- Can be caused by significant varus or valgus strain
- Normally needs a contact injury to damage a collateral ligament
- Does not cause significant swelling, as the ligament sits outside the joint

### Meniscal Injury

- Generally occurs when a loaded knee is twisted
- Locking, blocking, or catching suggests meniscal injury
- A mild effusion can develop

### Knee Fracture

- Femoral condylar fractures: people are often unable to bear weight, have pain over the distal femur, and often have blood in the joint (haemarthrosis)
- Tibial eminence fractures: seen mostly in children aged 8 to 14 years, from ACL-type or hyperextension injuries
- Tibial tuberosity fractures: mostly seen in adolescents who do jumping sports (basketball, gymnastics, football)
- Patellar fracture: caused by a direct blow to the knee, or forceful quadriceps contraction against a semi-flexed knee; significant swelling may be present
- Tibial plateau fractures: often from axial loading with varus or valgus stress. They may be caused by falling onto a straight leg, such as a fall from a height, or a low-energy fall in older adults with osteoporosis. They cause tenderness over the edge of the proximal tibia, often prevent weight-bearing, and may be associated with ligament injuries.

### Patellar Dislocation

- Can be caused by a twisting mechanism or a direct blow to a partially flexed knee
- More common in adolescents
- Generally presents with a very painful knee held bent, with the kneecap displaced outward; large swelling may be present

### Knee Dislocation

- Rare but potentially limb-threatening
- Generally occurs after high-energy trauma
- Neurovascular compromise needs to be ruled out

## Physical Examination

Acute knee injuries are often hard to examine at the first visit. Examination may be more helpful once the knee is settling.

Examination typically includes:

- Ability to bear weight
- Tenderness and swelling with palpation
- Range of movement and muscle strength
- Tests for specific injuries:
  - ACL: Lachman and pivot shift tests
  - PCL: posterior drawer test and posterior sag
  - Collateral ligament: varus and valgus stress tests
  - Meniscus: McMurray's and Thessaly tests
  - Patella instability: patellar apprehension test
- Straight leg raise to check the extensor mechanism, as these injuries can be missed
- Pulses, movement, and sensation below the injury to rule out neurovascular compromise

A large effusion may point to a knee fracture, or to blood in the joint (haemarthrosis) from a ligament or meniscal tear.

## Investigations

### X-ray

Your doctor may use the **Ottawa Knee Rules** to decide if an X-ray is needed to rule out a fracture. X-ray is indicated if any of the following apply after acute knee injury:

- Aged 55 years or over
- Tenderness at the head of the fibula
- Isolated tenderness of the patella
- Inability to flex the knee to 90 degrees
- Inability to bear weight (unable to take four steps, two on each leg, regardless of limping) immediately after the injury and at assessment

X-ray changes may be subtle. If a tibial plateau fracture is suspected, extra X-ray views may be arranged.

After a first patellar dislocation, X-rays look for a fracture of the medial patella: with a fracture, acute orthopaedic assessment follows; without one, physiotherapy. After a second or later dislocation, your doctor may suggest a non-acute orthopaedic assessment to discuss possible surgery, if you wish.

### MRI

GPs who have done ACC MRI training can arrange a knee MRI for certain injuries when the criteria are met. If the knee is slow to settle or the diagnosis is uncertain, your doctor may suggest a private sports medicine doctor or orthopaedic surgeon, who may consider further imaging.

### Ultrasound

Ultrasound is not routinely arranged for acute knee injuries.

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following, depending on the injury:

### Emergency Referral

Acute orthopaedic assessment in hospital is needed for:

- Any knee fracture
- Knee dislocation
- Extensor mechanism injury
- Concerns about neurovascular status
- Acutely locked knee
- First patellar dislocation with a fracture on X-ray
- PCL or collateral ligament injury with a major lateral-sided tear, other injuries (such as a meniscal tear or cruciate rupture), significant laxity at review, or when a hinged brace is being considered

### Swollen Knee Without Fracture

If the knee is swollen but there is no fracture, early physiotherapy is arranged. If the knee does not settle quickly, your doctor may request an orthopaedic outpatient assessment.

### ACL Injury

- Initial treatment: a tubular compression bandage, RICE (rest, ice, compression, elevation), and possibly crutches; weight-bear as able
- If there is a large effusion, draining the fluid (aspiration) may be considered for pain relief
- Early physiotherapy to strengthen the quadriceps and hamstrings, with range of motion exercises. These help reduce the swelling and improve movement and strength.
- Follow-up at 2 weeks; if the examination shows an ACL injury, orthopaedic assessment is arranged
- Whether to have surgery depends on your previous activity level. Surgery helps prevent further knee instability, and rehabilitation before surgery helps prevent problems after it.
- Surgery is performed once the knee has full range of movement and good strength. This is unlikely less than 3 weeks after the injury.

### PCL and Collateral Ligament Injuries

- Minor (incomplete tears): analgesia, RICE, a tubular compression bandage, and crutches, usually only for a few days, then mobilise as able
- More significant (complete tears): a hinged brace may be considered
- Review after 1 to 2 weeks; if there is significant laxity, acute orthopaedic assessment in hospital is requested

### Meniscal Tear

- RICE, early physiotherapy, and gradual mobilisation
- Many meniscal tears settle over 6 to 8 weeks
- If the knee does not settle after at least 6 weeks with physiotherapy, a non-acute orthopaedic assessment may be requested
- If the knee is locked, most will unlock on their own with analgesia, rest, and support. A locked knee needs acute orthopaedic assessment in hospital.

### Stable Injury Without Large Effusion

- Ice and analgesia (paracetamol or a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, NSAID) for pain
- If effusion is present: RICE, a tubular compression bandage, crutches, and physiotherapy; review in 2 weeks
- At review, significant laxity, being unable to bear weight, or ongoing significant swelling may lead to an orthopaedic outpatient assessment
- If there is no swelling at any stage, a significant injury is unlikely and no other management is needed

### Return to Work and Sport

Your doctor will advise based on the injury type:

- Minor injuries: usually 2 to 3 weeks
- Complete ruptures: it may be more than 6 weeks before returning to active sport
- ACL injuries and lateral-sided ligament injuries may require surgery, and more time off work and sport

If returning to work is hard, your doctor may consider an ACC work assessment and rehabilitation.
