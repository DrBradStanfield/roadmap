# Iron Deficiency in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: August 2023.*

Iron deficiency can be present with or without anaemia (haemoglobin below the local reference range). Iron deficiency and anaemia may occur independently or together as iron deficiency anaemia (IDA). The cause should always be found.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department if:**
- You feel unwell from blood loss, for example severe anaemia (Hb less than 60), an unstable pulse or blood pressure, or heart or breathing compromise. Hospital assessment is needed.
- You have definite bleeding from the gut right now, including variceal bleeding.

**Tell your doctor if you have any of these red flags:**
- Visible bleeding from the gut.
- Symptoms of anaemia (see below).
- Weight loss you cannot explain.
- A lump in the abdomen or rectum. Your doctor may request an urgent specialist gut (gastroenterology) assessment.

---

## Background

- Iron deficiency and iron deficiency anaemia are more common in Māori, Pacific and Indian women.
- **Anaemia** is diagnosed when haemoglobin (Hb) is:
  - Men: lower than 130 g/L.
  - Women: lower than 115 g/L (including in pregnancy).
- **Iron deficiency** is diagnosed when ferritin is:
  - Less than 20 micrograms/L in adults, including pregnant women.
  - Less than 100 micrograms/L in people with chronic inflammatory conditions, including congestive heart failure.

### Possible causes

- **Excessive blood loss**, for example:
  - From the gut: anti-inflammatory painkillers (NSAIDs), cancer, ulcers.
  - Heavy periods (menorrhagia).
  - Repeated nosebleeds.
  - Cancer of the kidney or urinary tract.
  - Regular blood donation.
  - After major surgery without enough replacement.
  - Breakdown of red blood cells (haemolysis).
- **Not enough iron in the diet**, especially in older people and vegetarians.
- **Poor iron absorption**, for example from medicines (antacids, proton-pump inhibitors (PPIs), calcium supplements), coeliac disease, or after stomach surgery.
- **Higher needs**, for example pregnancy.

## Assessment

### History

Your doctor may ask about:
- **Symptoms of anaemia:** fatigue, palpitations, shortness of breath on exertion, worsening angina, poor concentration, headache and dizziness.
- **Blood loss:**
  - Gut: change in bowel habit, rectal bleeding, weight loss, indigestion (dyspepsia).
  - Periods: heavy or abnormal bleeding.
  - Repeated nosebleeds.
  - Blood in the urine (haematuria).
  - Blood donation.
  - Recent surgery.
- How much iron is in your diet.
- Poor absorption, for example coeliac disease or inflammatory bowel disease.
- Higher needs: pregnancy, breastfeeding, athletes.
- **Medicines:**
  - NSAIDs.
  - Antiplatelets, for example aspirin, clopidogrel, dipyridamole, ticagrelor.
  - Anticoagulants, for example warfarin, dabigatran, rivaroxaban, apixaban, heparin, enoxaparin.
- Chronic disease or an inherited haemoglobin disorder (haemoglobinopathy).
- **Previous gastrectomy** (stomach removal surgery). These patients:
  - commonly have iron deficiency anaemia because of poor absorption.
  - have a 2- to 3-fold increase in stomach cancer risk after 20 years.
  - Gastroscopy is recommended for patients with a previous gastrectomy who are older than 50 years and have IDA.

### Examination

- Signs of anaemia: pallor, and pulse and blood pressure to look for signs of unstable circulation.
- Signs of a cause of blood loss: weight loss, a lump in the abdomen, possibly a rectal examination, and in women a pelvic examination if a pelvic mass is suspected.

### Investigations

**Your doctor may consider:**
- **Full blood count (FBC)** for haemoglobin and small (MCV less than 80) or pale (hypochromic) red cells.
- **Iron studies.**
- **Soluble transferrin receptor** if needed to confirm iron deficiency when an inflammatory condition is also present. It is less affected by inflammation than ferritin.
- **Urine test** for blood.
- **Coeliac disease screening.** For the tests to be reliable, you must be eating gluten for at least 4 weeks before testing (adults: 4 slices of wheat bread per day).

Faecal occult blood tests (FOBT) and faecal immunochemical tests (FIT) are of no benefit in investigating iron deficiency anaemia and should not be used for this.

### When a Gastroscopy or Colonoscopy May Be Needed

**Gastroscopy and colonoscopy (or CT colonography)** if:
- IDA is unexplained in a man, or in a woman who is not menstruating or is postmenopausal, even without gut symptoms.
- IDA is still unexplained after the first tests.
- IDA persists after a 3 month trial of iron treatment.

**Gastroscopy** if you:
- have IDA with upper gut symptoms, even if you are menstruating.
- are older than 50 years, have IDA, and have had a previous gastrectomy.

**Colonoscopy or CT colonography** if you have IDA with lower gut symptoms, even if you are menstruating.

## Management

### Treating the Cause

Your doctor will manage any cause found:
- **Indigestion with iron deficiency:** referral for gastroscopy.
- **Taking NSAIDs** (with or without acid-lowering medicines) and no indigestion or heartburn: your doctor may seek specialist gastroenterology advice.
- **Bowel symptoms or heavy periods:** managed through the relevant pathway.
- **Other visible blood loss**, for example blood in the urine: managed as clinically appropriate.
- **Positive coeliac blood test:** managed as coeliac disease.
- **Low dietary iron:** diet changes, and your doctor may consider a dietitian.

### Oral Iron

If you have iron deficiency, with or without chronic inflammation, your doctor may consider:
- A trial of **oral iron**, with FBC and ferritin rechecked every 3 months for one year, then yearly or as clinically appropriate.
- With chronic inflammation, haemoglobin shows response to treatment better than ferritin does.

If there is no response at 3 months, or iron deficiency comes back, your doctor may:
- Check that the iron is being taken as prescribed.
- Check for medicines and drinks that interact with iron:
  - Tetracyclines (avoid taking iron and doxycycline within 2 hours of each other).
  - Quinolone antibiotics.
  - Proton-pump inhibitors (PPIs).
  - Calcium, zinc.
  - Antacids.
  - Bisphosphonates.
  - Caffeine, for example coffee and tea.
- Arrange gastroscopy and colonoscopy or CT colonography.

### IV Iron Infusion

The infusion used is ferric carboxymaltose.

**Safety concern:** IV ferric carboxymaltose has been shown to impair bone formation, cause osteomalacia (soft bones), and increase fracture risk, even after a single dose. Oral iron should be optimised, and checked that it is being taken correctly, before IV iron is offered.

**Your doctor may consider IV iron for:**
- Intolerance to, poor response to, or poor absorption of oral iron.
- Ongoing blood loss, for example from the gut or heavy periods.
- Anaemia from iron deficiency together with a chronic inflammatory disease, for example end-stage kidney failure or cancer.
- Surgery that cannot be delayed.
- Poor absorption from the gut, for example inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease, or bariatric surgery.
- Symptomatic anaemia where iron stores need to be replaced quickly, to avoid worsening and reduce the need for further transfusion.

**IV iron is not given if you have:**
- Known anaphylaxis to iron polymaltose, iron carboxymaltose, or iron sucrose.
- Known hypersensitivity to these that does not respond to antihistamine and corticosteroid.
- Phosphate lower than 0.8 mmol/L (IV iron is deferred until phosphate is normal).
- First trimester pregnancy.
- Anaemia not caused by iron deficiency, for example haemolytic anaemia, marrow failure, or B12 or folate deficiency.
- Iron overload, for example haemochromatosis or iron overload from transfusions.
- Polycythaemia vera.
- Acute hepatitis or decompensated liver cirrhosis.
- Acute kidney inflammation, for example glomerulonephritis.

**Other points:**
- Intramuscular (IM) iron injections are not recommended because of side effects and variable absorption.
- IV iron increases red blood cell production within 2 weeks.
- IV iron may reduce the need for a blood transfusion.

### Before Surgery

- Iron deficiency anaemia should be treated with iron before surgery. IV iron may be considered.
- Your doctor may consider iron before surgery if your iron stores are low (ferritin less than 100 microgram/L) and the surgery is associated with significant blood loss, for example joint replacement. This helps build iron stores and recovery from anaemia after surgery.

### Blood Transfusion

Your doctor may consider whether a transfusion is needed. As a guide:
- **Hb less than 70 g/L:** transfusion of red cells is usually indicated. A lower threshold may be acceptable without symptoms, or where specific treatment is available (for example vitamin B12 for pernicious anaemia).
- **Hb 70 to 100 g/L:** transfusion is likely to be appropriate if there are signs or symptoms of poor oxygen delivery, or during surgery with major blood loss.
- **Hb 80 to 100 g/L with chronic anaemia:** transfusion may be appropriate to control anaemia symptoms.
- **Hb more than 100 g/L:** transfusion is not likely to be appropriate unless there are specific reasons.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you or seek advice if:
- **Hospital (acute) assessment:** you are unwell from blood loss, or have a definite acute gut bleed (see the emergency section above).
- **Urgent gastroenterology assessment:** a lump is felt in the abdomen or rectum.
- **Gastroenterology advice:** you take NSAIDs and have no indigestion or heartburn.
- **General medicine advice or the relevant specialty:** there are concerns about tests or treatment options.
- **Dietitian:** the cause is dietary.
