# Abnormal Liver Function Tests

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: November 2022.*

This pathway covers abnormal liver function tests (LFTs): how the pattern of results is read, how the cause is looked for, and when further assessment or referral is needed.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department** if you have signs of severe decompensated liver disease (the liver is failing to cope):
- New-onset fluid in the abdomen (ascites)
- Abdominal pain
- Confusion
- Jaundice (yellow skin or eyes) in someone with known or suspected cirrhosis

**See your doctor the same day** if a blood test shows reduced albumin, reduced platelets, and/or increased INR alongside abnormal LFTs.

---

## What Are Liver Function Tests?

LFTs include:
- **ALT** (alanine transaminase) and **AST** (aspartate transaminase)
- **ALP** (alkaline phosphatase) and **GGT** (gamma-glutamyltransferase)
- **Bilirubin**

**Albumin**, **INR** (international normalised ratio) and prothrombin time can be used to check how well the liver makes proteins (synthetic liver function).

Key points your doctor considers:
- The degree of rise in ALT and AST does not always match severity. Mild rises may not matter, and cirrhosis may be present with normal LFTs.
- Any other illness often causes short-lived rises in LFTs with no lasting effect, so comparison with previous results matters.
- Two main patterns: **hepatocellular** (ALT and AST raised, a problem with liver cells) and **cholestatic** (ALP and GGT raised, which may mean disease of the portal tracts or infiltration of the liver).
- A low platelet count suggests portal hypertension (raised pressure in the liver's blood supply).

## Assessment

### When LFTs Are Ordered in People Without Symptoms

Your doctor will only check LFTs in people without symptoms if there are risk factors such as:
- Diabetes or metabolic syndrome
- Being at risk of fatty liver disease (MASLD, previously called NAFLD)
- Excess alcohol intake
- Chronic hepatitis B or C
- Monitoring of medicines
- Other blood test abnormalities, such as high ferritin, large red blood cells (macrocytosis), low neutrophils, low platelets, prolonged INR, or low albumin

### History Your Doctor Will Take

Possible causes of liver injury your doctor will ask about:

**Metabolic risks for fatty liver:**
- Diabetes or metabolic syndrome
- Obesity
- Abnormal blood fats (dyslipidaemia)

**Infections:**
- Viral hepatitis, including hepatitis B and C (risk factors include recent overseas travel, transfusions, tattoos, and unprotected sex)
- Other viral infections, such as cytomegalovirus (CMV) or Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)
- Sepsis

**Systemic conditions:**
- Haemochromatosis (iron overload)
- Autoimmune disorders, coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease
- Cancer, Wilson's disease, alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency

**Other causes:**
- Pregnancy
- Excess alcohol intake, which also makes medicines more toxic to the liver
- Illicit drug use

**Medications** (prescription and over-the-counter) that can be hepatotoxic:
- Antibiotics (especially flucloxacillin, erythromycin, or amoxicillin + clavulanic acid)
- NSAIDs (anti-inflammatory medicines), prescribed or over the counter
- Statins
- Anticonvulsants
- Paracetamol

**Complementary and herbal treatments** that can cause liver injury:
- Black cohosh, Chinese herbal medicines, germander, kava, noni juice, pennyroyal, usnic acid, green tea extract, and weight loss or bodybuilding supplements

### Examination

Your doctor will look for signs of liver disease, which may not be present in early impairment:

**Signs:**
- Jaundice (yellowing of skin/eyes)
- Tender liver, an enlarged liver or spleen, or an irregular liver or masses
- Ascites (fluid in the abdomen)
- Peripheral oedema (swollen ankles)
- Spider naevi, palmar erythema, gynaecomastia
- Flapping tremor
- Caput medusae (dilated abdominal veins)
- Muscle wasting (sarcopenia)

**Symptoms to report:**
- Fever, abdominal pain
- Dark urine, pale stools
- Itching (pruritus)
- Lethargy, malaise, drowsiness
- Weakness and loss of muscle strength

### LFT Pattern Interpretation

| Test | Likely to be abnormal when | Specificity for liver disease | Other causes |
|------|---------|-------------|--------------|
| ALT | Hepatocellular injury: hepatitis, especially viral, drug-induced, MASLD, iron overload | Sensitive and specific | Acute obstructive jaundice within the first 24 hours |
| AST | Hepatocellular injury: hepatitis, especially alcoholic, liver fibrosis, cirrhosis | Less sensitive and specific than ALT | Heart or skeletal muscle injury |
| ALP | Cholestasis | Less specific than GGT, but more specific when ALP and GGT are raised together | Bone disease, pregnancy |
| GGT | Cholestasis, alcohol | More sensitive than ALP; may not indicate significant liver disease | Medications, liver congestion in heart failure |

## Management by Pattern

Your doctor will investigate and manage based on the pattern of results and your clinical situation. The following gives you an idea of what this involves.

### ALT and/or AST Elevation (Hepatocellular Pattern)

A rise in ALT and/or AST suggests liver cell injury. ALT is most specific for liver injury. If only AST is elevated, your doctor will consider non-liver causes (e.g., muscle or heart). The level of ALT and AST does not match how severe the liver disease is.

Causes to consider include MASLD, viral hepatitis, alcohol, drugs and herbal remedies, haemochromatosis, and autoimmune disease.

**Checking the AST:ALT ratio:**
- Ratio 1 or less (AST equal to or less than ALT): likely acute liver cell injury. ALT is usually higher than AST in viral hepatitis (HAV, HBV, HCV, CMV, EBV), though in early acute hepatitis AST can sometimes be higher.
- Ratio more than 1: more likely alcoholic liver disease, drug-induced, malignancy, cirrhosis, or non-liver disease.

**What happens based on how elevated (if you have no symptoms):**
- Less than 3 times upper limit of normal (ULN): repeat in 1 to 3 months. Investigate if two results 3 months apart remain elevated and are not improving.
- More than 3 times ULN: investigate further to find a cause.
- More than 5 times ULN: the cause is unlikely to be MASLD or alcohol alone.

**First-line investigations your doctor may arrange:**
- LFT, INR, albumin, FBC, creatinine, electrolytes, HbA1c, fasting lipids, ferritin, iron, iron-binding capacity, hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), anti-HCV antibodies
- Alpha-fetoprotein if HBV, suspected cirrhosis, or liver mass
- Liver ultrasound if biliary obstruction is suspected, or if platelets are low (possible cirrhosis and portal hypertension)

**Second-line (if no cause found):**
- Antinuclear antibodies (ANA), liver autoantibodies (anti-smooth muscle antibody and anti-mitochondrial antibody), serum immunoglobulins, serum caeruloplasmin
- HFE gene (haemochromatosis) if ferritin is raised and iron saturation is more than 50%

**For acute hepatitis** (ALT and/or AST more than 10 times ULN with bilirubin above ULN):
- HCV RNA, anti-HBc IgM, HAV IgM, EBV IgM/IgG, CMV IgM/IgG, HEV RNA

If ALT stays more than 1.5 times ULN for longer than 3 months, your doctor will arrange a liver ultrasound.

### Isolated GGT Elevation

An isolated raised GGT does not mean the patient drinks alcohol. With no other reason to suspect excess alcohol, it has only a 30 to 40% positive predictive value for excess alcohol intake. It can be raised in many non-liver conditions and may not indicate significant liver disease. A rise usually matters only if other LFTs are also raised, and specialist referral is not usually needed.

Most common causes: excess alcohol, fatty liver, medications (especially anticonvulsants), and transient viral infections.

**Management of an unexplained rise:**
- 5 times or less ULN: retest every 2 to 3 months; your doctor may investigate further if the rise persists or levels climb.
- More than 5 times ULN: your doctor may repeat LFTs in another 1 to 2 months. If the cause is unclear and levels are not improving despite a break from alcohol and enzyme-inducing drugs, your doctor may consider an abdominal ultrasound to look for a mass or biliary obstruction.

### Raised ALP

ALP is normally elevated in children whose bones are growing, and rises modestly around menopause.

Most likely pathological causes:
- Biliary obstruction, usually gallstones or malignancy. A liver ultrasound is arranged if suspected.
- Medications (flucloxacillin, amoxicillin/clavulanate)
- Sepsis, especially atypical pneumonia
- Autoimmune liver disease (primary biliary cholangitis); liver autoantibodies may be checked
- Paraneoplastic syndromes (especially lymphoma)

If ALP alone is raised, non-liver causes are considered: bone disease, heart failure, hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, pregnancy, renal impairment, vitamin D deficiency. A high ALP may also prompt a check for malignancy.
- Serum P1NP (bone turnover marker) can help: a high level suggests a bone source rather than the liver. If P1NP is normal, a vitamin D test adds little.

If both ALP and GGT are raised, a liver cause is more likely.

**Follow-up if there is no clear cause and systemic or liver disease is unlikely:**
- Less than 1.5 times ULN: recheck a fasting level in 3 months
- 1.5 to 3 times ULN: recheck in 3 months; liver ultrasound if it stays raised
- More than 3 times ULN: liver ultrasound arranged

### Raised ALP and GGT Together

If ALP, GGT, and bilirubin are all above the upper limit of normal, this suggests cholestasis or biliary obstruction. Your doctor will arrange an urgent ultrasound.

If ALP and GGT continue to rise without apparent cause, your doctor will seek specialist liver advice.

### Raised Bilirubin

In liver disease, raised bilirubin is almost always accompanied by other LFT abnormalities.

**Common causes:**
- **Gilbert's syndrome** (occurs in 2 to 10% of the population): slight rise in unconjugated bilirubin, otherwise normal LFTs, no signs of liver disease or haemolysis. Checked with total and conjugated bilirubin when well; haemolysis tests may be added even if the FBC is normal. No further bilirubin testing is required.
- **Haemolysis** (unconjugated): your doctor may check reticulocyte count, blood film, haptoglobin, and LDH
- Inherited haemolytic anaemia (spherocytosis, thalassaemia)
- Immune reactions, autoimmune disorders, systemic disease, drugs, infections, mechanical causes, an overactive spleen, burns

**Management if there is no clear cause:**
- Up to 2 times ULN: retest when well in 3 months
- More than 2 times ULN: test for unconjugated bilirubin and arrange a liver ultrasound. If you are well with otherwise normal LFTs, TSH, and FBC, Gilbert's syndrome is likely, and repeat testing is not needed.

### Reduced Albumin / Raised INR

Serum albumin and INR reflect liver function. They can indicate severe, decompensated liver disease.

- Severe decompensated liver disease: call 111 or go to the emergency department
- Liver cause suspected: your doctor will urgently arrange further tests and request a non-acute gastroenterology assessment

## When Ultrasound Is Arranged

Your doctor may consider an abdominal ultrasound if any of these criteria are met:

- First presentation with ALP more than 2 times ULN and GGT higher than ULN (marked urgent, within 2 weeks)
- Follow-up imaging when ALP was more than 2 times ULN at first presentation and, on repeat testing more than 3 months later, ALP and GGT are both still higher than ULN
- ALT higher than 120 units/L in someone with high risk factors (diabetes, obesity, or liver-affecting medicines such as statins), when weight loss, lifestyle or medicine changes have not lowered it and there are no worrying features
- Isolated GGT more than 5 times ULN that persists on repeat testing after 3 months

The pattern sections above give other reasons for an ultrasound, such as suspected biliary obstruction or low platelets.

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Arranged

**Hospital (acute liver assessment):** severe decompensated liver disease, meaning new-onset ascites, abdominal pain, confusion, or jaundice in someone with known or suspected cirrhosis. Call 111 or go to the emergency department.

**Non-acute gastroenterology assessment** if:
- Decompensated liver disease not requiring acute assessment (marked urgent)
- High suspicion of upper gastrointestinal cancer, such as painless obstructive jaundice or an upper abdominal mass (marked "high suspicion of cancer")
- Ultrasound suggests malignancy (urgent, "high suspicion of cancer")
- ALT and/or AST more than 10 times ULN
- INR is abnormal
- Reduced albumin with raised INR and liver cause suspected
- HFE gene (genetic haemochromatosis) with abnormal LFT, hepatomegaly, or ferritin more than 1000 micrograms/L
- Persistent unexplained abnormal LFT

**Liver advice sought if:**
- Liver elastography (fibroscan) shows stiffness of 10.5 to 12.5 kPa
- Liver stiffness more than 12.5 kPa: non-acute gastroenterology assessment instead
- ALP and GGT continuing to rise without apparent cause

## General Management Principles

### Drug, Alcohol, or Obesity-Related

- The medicine and/or alcohol is stopped and the specific liver test repeated within 6 to 8 weeks.
- Note: **statins rarely cause hepatotoxicity.** The risk is very small compared with the overall benefits, even in established liver disease. Statins are usually not the cause of deranged LFTs, especially with long-term use.
- Weight management is advised for obesity. The risk of fatty liver (steatosis) in severe obesity is nearly 100%, and the treatment for steatosis is the same as for obesity.

### Mild Elevation (<1.5× ULN), Asymptomatic

For a minor rise (usually less than 1.5 times ULN) without symptoms:
- History and risk factor review
- Address potential causes (weight loss if obese, alcohol reduction)
- Consider more than one cause
- Retest in 3 months

If chronic hepatitis B, fatty liver (MASLD), or chronic hepatitis C is found, your doctor will manage via the relevant specific pathway.
