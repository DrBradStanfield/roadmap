# Abnormal Liver Function Tests

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to abnormal liver function tests (LFTs) — interpreting the pattern of results, investigating the cause, and deciding when further assessment or referral is needed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

Your doctor will arrange urgent assessment if:

- Reduced albumin, reduced platelets, and/or increased INR alongside abnormal LFT — these suggest serious liver impairment

---

## What Are Liver Function Tests?

LFTs include:
- **ALT** (alanine aminotransferase) and **AST** (aspartate transaminase) — reflect hepatocyte (liver cell) injury
- **ALP** (alkaline phosphatase) and **GGT** (gamma-glutamyltransferase) — reflect cholestasis (bile flow blockage)
- **Bilirubin** — a breakdown product of red blood cells, cleared by the liver

**Albumin** and **INR** (international normalised ratio) reflect how well the liver is actually making proteins — these are markers of synthetic liver function.

Key points your doctor considers:
- The degree of elevation does not always match severity — mild rises may be insignificant; cirrhosis can exist with normal LFTs
- A single abnormal result may be a transient effect of any intercurrent illness; comparison with previous results is important
- Two main patterns: **hepatocellular** (ALT/AST raised — liver cell injury) and **cholestatic** (ALP/GGT raised — bile duct/portal tract problem)

## Assessment

### When LFTs Are Ordered in People Without Symptoms

Your doctor will generally only check LFTs without symptoms if there are risk factors such as:
- Diabetes or metabolic syndrome
- At-risk for fatty liver disease (MASLD/NAFLD)
- Excess alcohol intake
- Chronic hepatitis B or C
- Monitoring medications that may affect the liver
- Other blood test abnormalities (high ferritin, low platelets, prolonged INR, low albumin)

### History Your Doctor Will Take

Possible causes of liver injury your doctor will ask about:

**Metabolic / lifestyle:**
- Obesity, diabetes, metabolic syndrome, high cholesterol (fatty liver)
- Excess alcohol intake

**Infections:**
- Viral hepatitis B and C (risk factors: overseas travel, transfusions, tattoos, unprotected sex)
- CMV or Epstein-Barr virus
- Sepsis

**Systemic conditions:**
- Haemochromatosis (iron overload)
- Autoimmune disorders, coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease
- Cancer, Wilson's disease, alpha-1-antitrypsin deficiency

**Medications** (prescription and over-the-counter) that can be hepatotoxic:
- Antibiotics (especially flucloxacillin, erythromycin, amoxicillin + clavulanic acid)
- NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen)
- Statins
- Anticonvulsants
- Paracetamol (in excess)

**Complementary and herbal treatments** that can cause liver injury:
- Kava, black cohosh, green tea extract, Chinese herbal medicines, weight loss or bodybuilding supplements

### Examination

Your doctor will look for signs of liver disease, which may not be present in early impairment:

**Signs:**
- Jaundice (yellowing of skin/eyes)
- Enlarged or tender liver or spleen
- Ascites (fluid in the abdomen)
- Peripheral oedema (swollen ankles)
- Spider naevi, palmar erythema, gynaecomastia
- Flapping tremor (asterixis) — a sign of hepatic encephalopathy
- Caput medusae (dilated abdominal veins)
- Muscle wasting (sarcopenia)

**Symptoms to report:**
- Fever, abdominal pain
- Dark urine, pale stools
- Itching (pruritus)
- Lethargy, malaise, drowsiness
- Weakness

### LFT Pattern Interpretation

| Test | Pattern | Specificity | Other causes |
|------|---------|-------------|--------------|
| ALT | Hepatocellular injury (hepatitis, MASLD, drug-induced, iron overload) | Sensitive and specific | Acute obstructive jaundice within first 24 hours |
| AST | Hepatocellular injury (alcoholic hepatitis, fibrosis, cirrhosis) | Less specific than ALT | Cardiac or skeletal muscle injury |
| ALP | Cholestasis | Less specific than GGT; more specific when both ALP and GGT raised together | Bone disease, pregnancy |
| GGT | Cholestasis, alcohol | More sensitive than ALP; may not indicate significant liver disease | Medications, heart failure |

## Management by Pattern

Your doctor will investigate and manage based on the pattern of results. The following gives you an idea of what this involves.

### ALT and/or AST Elevation (Hepatocellular Pattern)

ALT is most specific for liver injury. If only AST is elevated, your doctor will consider non-liver causes (e.g., muscle or heart).

**Checking the AST:ALT ratio:**
- Ratio ≤1 (ALT ≥ AST): suggests acute hepatocellular injury; typical of viral hepatitis (HAV, HBV, HCV, CMV, EBV)
- Ratio >1 (AST > ALT): more typical of alcoholic liver disease, drug-induced, malignancy, cirrhosis, or NASH

**What happens based on how elevated:**
- Less than 3× upper limit of normal (ULN): repeat in 1–3 months; investigate if two results 3 months apart remain elevated
- 3–5× ULN: investigate further
- More than 5× ULN: MASLD or alcohol alone unlikely to be the cause

**First-line investigations your doctor may arrange:**
- LFT, INR, albumin, FBC, creatinine, electrolytes, HbA1c, fasting lipids, ferritin, iron studies, hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), anti-HCV antibodies
- Alpha-fetoprotein if HBV, suspected cirrhosis, or liver mass
- Liver ultrasound if biliary obstruction suspected or low platelets

**Second-line (if no cause found):**
- Antinuclear antibodies (ANA), tissue autoantibodies, serum immunoglobulins, serum caeruloplasmin
- HFE gene (haemochromatosis) if ferritin raised and iron saturation >50%

**For acute hepatitis** (ALT/AST >10× ULN with raised bilirubin):
- HCV RNA, anti-HBc IgM, HAV IgM, EBV IgM/IgG, CMV IgM/IgG, HEV RNA

### Isolated GGT Elevation

An isolated raised GGT does not necessarily mean the patient drinks alcohol — it has only a 30–40% positive predictive value for excess alcohol when there is no other clinical suspicion. It can be raised in many non-liver conditions and may not indicate significant liver disease.

Most common causes: excess alcohol, fatty liver, medications (especially anticonvulsants), and transient viral infections.

**Management:**
- ≤5× ULN: retest every 2–3 months; investigate further if elevation persists or rises
- >5× ULN: repeat LFT in 1–2 months; if cause unclear and not improving after abstaining from alcohol and inducing drugs, consider ultrasound abdomen

### Raised ALP

ALP is normally elevated in growing children and perimenopausal women.

Most likely pathological causes:
- Biliary obstruction (gallstones, malignancy) — ultrasound arranged if suspected
- Medications (flucloxacillin, amoxicillin/clavulanate)
- Sepsis, especially atypical pneumonia
- Autoimmune liver disease (primary biliary cholangitis)
- Paraneoplastic syndromes (especially lymphoma)

If isolated raised ALP, non-liver causes are considered: bone disease, heart failure, hyperthyroidism, hyperparathyroidism, pregnancy, renal impairment, vitamin D deficiency.
- Serum P1NP (bone turnover marker) can help: high level suggests bone source; if normal, vitamin D testing is not particularly useful.

**Follow-up if no obvious cause:**
- <1.5× ULN: recheck fasting in 3 months
- 1.5–3× ULN: recheck in 3 months; ultrasound if persistent
- >3× ULN: liver ultrasound arranged

### Raised ALP and GGT Together

If ALP, GGT, and bilirubin are all above normal, this suggests cholestasis or biliary obstruction — urgent ultrasound is arranged.

If ALP and GGT continue to rise without apparent cause, your doctor will seek specialist liver advice.

### Raised Bilirubin

In liver disease, raised bilirubin is almost always accompanied by other LFT abnormalities.

**Common causes:**
- **Gilbert's syndrome** (occurs in 2–10% of population): slight rise in unconjugated bilirubin, otherwise normal LFTs, no haemolysis. Confirmed with total and conjugated bilirubin when well. No further testing required once diagnosed.
- **Haemolysis** (unconjugated): reticulocyte count, blood film, haptoglobin, LDH tested
- Inherited haemolytic anaemia (spherocytosis, thalassaemia)
- Immune reactions, autoimmune disorders, systemic disease, drugs, infections, mechanical causes

**Management:**
- ≤2× ULN: retest when well in 3 months
- >2× ULN: test for unconjugated bilirubin, arrange liver ultrasound

### Reduced Albumin / Raised INR

These reflect how well the liver is making proteins — abnormalities indicate synthetic liver failure.

- Severe decompensated liver disease: urgent acute liver assessment
- Liver cause suspected: urgent investigations and non-acute gastroenterology assessment

## When Ultrasound Is Arranged

Your doctor may arrange an abdominal ultrasound if any of the following criteria are met:

- Platelets <150 × 10⁹/L with any persistent LFT abnormality — possible cirrhosis/portal hypertension (urgent)
- Bilirubin >2× ULN for any duration (urgent)
- ALP, GGT, and bilirubin all above normal (urgent)
- Clinical suspicion of biliary obstruction
- ALP and GGT both >1.5× ULN for >3 months
- Isolated GGT >5× ULN persisting >4 weeks without obvious cause despite abstinence
- ALP >3× ULN
- ALP 1.5–3× ULN persisting >3 months
- ALT >1.5× ULN for >3 months
- Ferritin raised alongside other abnormal LFT (if other criteria met)
- Acute symptoms suggesting severe hepatitis or hepatic infiltration (jaundice ± upper abdominal pain)

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Arranged

**Urgent acute liver assessment** if:
- Severe decompensated liver disease: new-onset ascites, abdominal pain, confusion, or jaundice in a patient with known or suspected cirrhosis

**Non-acute gastroenterology assessment** if:
- Decompensated liver disease not requiring acute assessment (marked urgent)
- High suspicion of upper GI cancer (marked "high suspicion of cancer")
- Ultrasound suggests malignancy (urgent, "high suspicion of cancer")
- ALT and/or AST >10× ULN
- INR is abnormal
- Reduced albumin with raised INR and liver cause suspected
- HFE gene (genetic haemochromatosis) with abnormal LFT, hepatomegaly, or ferritin >1000 micrograms/L
- Persistent unexplained abnormal LFT

**Liver advice sought if:**
- Liver elastography (fibroscan) shows stiffness 10.5–12.5 kPa
- LSM >12.5 kPa → non-acute gastroenterology assessment
- ALP and GGT continuing to rise without apparent cause

## General Management Principles

### Drug, Alcohol, or Obesity-Related

- The offending medication is stopped (where appropriate) and liver tests repeated in 6–8 weeks
- Note: **statins rarely cause hepatotoxicity** — the risk is very small compared to cardiovascular benefits. Statins are usually not the cause of deranged LFTs, especially with long-term use. Your doctor will not reflexively stop them.
- Weight management is advised for obesity — steatosis risk is nearly 100% in severe obesity; treatment is the same as for obesity itself

### Mild Elevation (<1.5× ULN), Asymptomatic

- History and risk factor review
- Address potential causes (weight, alcohol reduction)
- Consider multiple concurrent causes
- Retest in 3 months

If chronic hepatitis B, fatty liver (MASLD), or chronic hepatitis C is found, your doctor will manage via the relevant specific pathway.
