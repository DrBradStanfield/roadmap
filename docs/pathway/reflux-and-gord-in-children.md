# Reflux and GORD in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers reflux and gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) in infants, children, and adolescents — including when spilling is normal, when GORD is the diagnosis, and what treatments your doctor may consider.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Take your child to the emergency department or call an ambulance if any of the following are present:

- Suspected pyloric stenosis (see below)
- Bile-stained (green) vomiting
- Poor weight gain
- Haematemesis (blood in vomit)

---

## Background

Reflux is the passage of gastric contents into the oesophagus, with or without vomiting. It is a **normal physiological process in infancy**.

- Spilling (regurgitation) peaks at 4 months of age and affects about 67% of babies at that age
- Half of infants under 3 months have at least one episode per day; only 5% still have episodes at 10–12 months
- Most spilling happens after feeds and resolves on its own without treatment

**Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD)** is diagnosed when reflux causes troublesome symptoms (e.g., frequent vomiting with significant distress) or complications such as poor weight gain or erosive oesophagitis.

In most infants, history and examination alone can distinguish normal spilling from GORD.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your child by:

1. **Checking for red flags** — particularly pyloric stenosis, bile-stained vomiting, poor weight gain, and haematemesis

   **Pyloric stenosis** typically presents between 2 and 6 weeks of age:
   - Vomiting that is progressively more forceful and may become projectile
   - Non-bilious (not green), but may be blood-stained in up to 10% of cases
   - Baby is often hungry again immediately after vomiting
   - May have weight loss or inadequate weight gain

2. **Taking a full history** including severity of symptoms, feeding history, weight gain, parental distress, and risk factors

   GORD is more common in children with:
   - Neurological impairment (e.g., cerebral palsy)
   - Prematurity
   - Cystic fibrosis
   - Oesophageal abnormalities

   Symptoms that make GORD more likely:
   - Poor weight gain
   - Haematemesis
   - Swallowing difficulties
   - Recurrent respiratory symptoms
   - Significant distress following feeds

3. **Performing a full physical examination** to look for other causes of vomiting (e.g., infection, gastroenteritis, urinary tract infection, acute abdomen, raised intracranial pressure)

4. **Considering other causes** of upper gastrointestinal symptoms:
   - **Cow's milk protein intolerance** — associated with GORD in fewer than 10% of cases; more likely if there is poor growth, loose stools, or early severe eczema
   - **Eosinophilic oesophagitis** — an inflammatory condition where the oesophagus becomes filled with eosinophils; diagnosed by gastroscopy. In infants it presents similarly to GORD (poor feeding, poor growth); in older children it causes dysphagia and food getting stuck. Often associated with atopy. All cases need specialist referral.

5. **Investigations** are not routinely needed. Some children with severe or complicated disease may require upper gastrointestinal endoscopy or oesophageal pH studies — your doctor will advise if this is warranted.

6. In **older children and adolescents**, symptoms are similar to adults. Oesophagitis is less common than in adults.

## Management

### Reflux and Spilling in Infants

For most healthy infants with spilling, reassurance and simple measures are all that is needed.

**Education**

Your doctor may explain:
- More than 50% of babies spill regularly in the first months of life — this is normal
- The term "spilling" is preferred over "reflux" to reflect its benign nature
- Spilling peaks at 4 months and usually stops by 12 months
- It generally improves as feeds become more solid, the baby spends more time upright, and gut function matures

**Lifestyle changes**

Your doctor may recommend:
- Keeping baby upright after feeds
- Avoiding second-hand smoke
- **Do not** elevate one end of the cot or bassinet — this is ineffective and contradicts safe sleep guidance
- Babies should always sleep on their backs; they have not been shown to aspirate if they spill while lying on their back

**Thickened formula**

Your doctor may consider thickened formula. Note that it may reduce the volume of regurgitation but does not significantly reduce the frequency or symptoms of spilling.

**Medications and investigations**

These are not routinely indicated for normal spilling in infants. If symptoms have not resolved by 18 months, or if there are concerns about aspiration, your doctor will consider referral for investigation.

---

### GORD in Infants

Your doctor will usually begin with the same reassurance, education, and lifestyle measures described above. In most cases this is sufficient, as symptoms improve over time.

**Medications your doctor may consider:**

- **Omeprazole** is generally not recommended for infants in primary care — there is limited evidence of effectiveness and some concerns about safety
- **Alginates (e.g., Gaviscon Infant)** — evidence is inconsistent but they may have a role in GORD in infants, for short-term or on-demand use rather than regular or long-term use. Your doctor will discuss risks and benefits before trialling these.
- **Cow's milk protein intolerance** — your doctor may consider this as a contributing factor, particularly if there is poor growth, loose stools, or early severe eczema

If your infant has GORD with poor weight gain (with or without oral aversion), your doctor will arrange a paediatric assessment.

---

### GORD in Older Children and Adolescents

**Lifestyle changes your doctor may recommend:**

- Small, frequent feeds or meals
- Avoid meals close to bedtime
- Avoid acidic or spicy foods
- Avoid caffeine and alcohol (these relax the lower oesophageal sphincter)
- Avoid second-hand smoke

**Medications your doctor may consider:**

- **Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs)** such as omeprazole — a time-limited trial can help determine whether GORD is causing the symptoms. Short-term use is generally well tolerated; headaches are the most common side effect.
- PPIs are equally effective as alginates in older children
- PPI medications should be taken before meals (or 90 minutes after eating) for best effect

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer your child for paediatric assessment if:

- Red flags are present (acute assessment)
- Acutely unwell (acute assessment)
- Faltering growth with or without oral aversion (non-acute assessment)
- Concern about aspiration (non-acute assessment)
- Child continues to regurgitate beyond 18 months of age (non-acute assessment)
