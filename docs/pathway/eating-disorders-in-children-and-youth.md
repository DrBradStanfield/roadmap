# Eating Disorders in Children and Youth

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of eating disorders in patients aged 17 years and younger.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Seek immediate medical attention if any of the following are present:

- Rapid weight loss
- Acute medical instability
- Ceased fluid intake
- Suicidal ideation

---

## Background

Eating disorders in children and adolescents have several important features your doctor will be aware of:

- Early diagnosis and treatment improves health outcomes
- A normal BMI does not exclude a serious eating disorder — serious nutritional and physiological deficiencies can occur after rapid weight loss in a previously overweight child even when current BMI appears normal
- Younger adolescents may present with weight loss or failure to achieve expected growth for age
- The highest incidence is in the 15 to 19 years age group, but eating disorders can present at any age — males comprise at least 25% of this group
- Deterioration can be rapid and the risk of complications is greater in younger patients
- Anorexia nervosa has the highest mortality rate of any psychiatric disorder
- There is a high prevalence of suicide and self-harm in patients with an eating disorder

### Avoidant Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID)

ARFID presents with similar features to an eating disorder but without disordered body image. Children with ARFID may have:

- Nutritional deficiencies secondary to eating behaviours despite having a healthy height and weight
- A lack of interest in food, textural sensitivities to certain foods, or a fear of eating after an aversive experience such as choking
- Co-morbid anxiety — up to 60% have a generalised anxiety disorder

## Types of Eating Disorders

Your doctor will consider which eating disorder best fits the clinical picture:

### Anorexia Nervosa

- Usually earlier age of onset than other eating disorders (average age 14 to 16 years)
- Significant weight loss, intense fear of weight gain, and extreme food restriction
- Patients often have body image distortion or over-evaluation of weight and shape
- Two subtypes:
  - **Restrictive type** — calorie restriction only
  - **Purging type** — calorie restriction plus regular purging or binge eating in the previous 3 months

### Bulimia Nervosa

- Weight may fluctuate slightly above and below a healthy BMI
- Recurrent episodes of binge eating (eating much more than intended in a discrete time period, with a sense of loss of control) followed by compensatory behaviours to prevent weight gain, such as self-induced vomiting, misuse of laxatives or diuretics, fasting, or excessive exercise
- Episodes occur on average at least once a week for 3 months
- Self-evaluation is unduly influenced by body shape and weight

### Binge Eating Disorder

- Very large volumes of food rapidly ingested in discrete time frames, with associated guilt, remorse, and self-disgust afterwards
- The patient is unable to control the length and frequency of binges
- Binges are used as a way to manage stress or psychological distress
- Unlike bulimia, there are no compensatory behaviours (purging, restricting, or medication misuse)
- The patient is often highly distressed and secretive about their eating behaviours

### Avoidant / Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID)

- Patients avoid or restrict food intake to the point of significant weight loss, nutritional deficiencies, dependence on oral supplements, or marked disruption to psychosocial functioning
- Patients are not preoccupied with weight — instead they may lack interest in eating, dislike sensory characteristics of foods, or fear choking, gagging, or vomiting

### Other Specified Feeding or Eating Disorder (OSFED)

- A pattern of disordered eating that does not fully meet criteria for anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa
- Examples include atypical anorexia nervosa (criteria met but with weight within or above normal range), low-frequency bulimia nervosa, low-frequency binge eating disorder, and purging disorder without binge eating

## Assessment

Your doctor will conduct a thorough assessment. Early recognition and intervention is associated with better outcomes. Patients with an eating disorder may not always disclose the full extent of their behaviours, or may deny or downplay the severity of symptoms.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

**Weight and body image**
- Pre-morbid weight and rate of weight loss
- BMI centile and velocity plotted on growth charts
- Whether growth progression has tapered off
- Body image distortion and fear of weight gain

**Eating habits**
- Typical daily food intake
- Attitudes to food and meal patterns — dietary restrictions, skipping meals, ritualistic eating, absence from family meals, picky or avoidant eating
- Binge eating — frequency and extent

**Compensatory behaviours**
- Self-induced vomiting (prolonged visits to the toilet after meals)
- Misuse of medications such as laxatives, diuretics, insulin, thyroxine, or weight loss preparations
- Increased gum chewing or smoking
- Excessive exercise — frequency, duration, and type
- Use of natural therapies such as herbal teas and supplements

**Cognitive and social changes**
- Impaired concentration and alertness
- Deterioration in school work
- Easily distracted and lethargic
- Irritability
- Intrusive thoughts about food
- Withdrawal from social activities, especially those involving eating and drinking

**Physical features**
- Disrupted sleep
- Gastrointestinal symptoms — abdominal pain, constipation, diarrhoea, heartburn
- Cold intolerance
- Fatigue, light-headedness, low energy, cognitive slowing
- Recurrent overuse injuries (secondary to excessive exercise)
- Unexplained weight loss or gain
- Menstrual history in females — primary or secondary amenorrhoea, age of menarche, weight at which periods ceased

**Other areas**
- Extent of disruption to day-to-day living
- Co-morbid conditions including depression, anxiety, obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD), self-harm, and suicide risk
- Past history of eating disorder
- Family history of eating disorders, anxiety, or depression
- School year, academic progress, and recent changes in grades or friendships

Your doctor will also seek collateral history from family members with the patient's consent, as family members often play a central role in identifying an eating disorder.

### Examination

Your doctor will examine the patient at each contact, with the level of detail depending on the level of concern. This includes:

- Temperature
- Blood pressure and postural drop
- Pulse rate, rhythm, and any postural change
- Height and weight, plotted on growth charts
- Hydration status including capillary refill and peripheral perfusion
- Cardiac examination — listening for murmurs and assessing for cardiac failure
- Abdomen — bowel sounds, constipation
- Squat test and gait
- Mental status assessment
- Skin and hair changes
- Oral examination — erosion of dental enamel or inflamed throat from vomiting

### Signs of Acute Medical Instability

**In patients aged 17 years or older**, your doctor will check for:

- Severe or sudden weight loss — more than 1 kg per week for more than 2 weeks, or 15 to 20% body weight in 3 months
- Cardiac instability, including syncope:
  - Resting systolic blood pressure less than 80 mmHg, or postural drop of more than 20 mmHg after standing for 2 minutes
  - Bradycardia — resting heart rate less than 40 beats per minute
  - Postural change in heart rate of more than 20 beats per minute from lying to standing
  - ECG changes — QTc greater than 500 ms or ventricular arrhythmia
- Neurological symptoms — seizures, confusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, tetany
- Severe dehydration
- Muscle weakness
- Rapidly diminishing exercise tolerance
- Hypothermia (body temperature below 35°C)
- Biochemical abnormalities — blood glucose less than 2.5 mmol/L, potassium less than 2.5 mmol/L, phosphate less than 0.7 mmol/L, hyponatraemia, severe neutropenia, or eGFR less than 60

**In patients aged 16 years or younger**, your doctor will check for:

- Temperature less than 35.5°C
- 15 to 20% weight loss over the last 3 months
- Total body weight less than 75% expected for height and age
- Blood pressure less than 80/40, or postural drop of 20 mmHg or greater
- Bradycardia less than 50 beats per minute, or increase of more than 40 beats per minute from lying to standing
- Acute medical complications of malnutrition including syncope, seizures, pancreatitis, cardiac failure, or gastric dilatation
- Fasting hypoglycaemia (indicates severe illness and risk of sudden death)
- Urinary or blood ketones
- Electrolyte disturbances — potassium less than 3 mmol/L, low phosphate
- ECG abnormalities — cardiac arrhythmia, QTc greater than 450 milliseconds, or diminished amplitude of QRS complex and T waves (rapid changes are always of concern even if values remain within normal range)

### Investigations

Depending on the level of concern, your doctor may arrange:

**Blood tests**
- Full blood count (FBC), ferritin, B12, folate
- Electrolytes including calcium, phosphate, magnesium
- Renal function and creatinine
- Glucose
- Thyroid function tests (T3 is reduced with malnutrition)
- Creatinine kinase (elevated with excessive exercise)
- Bicarbonate (elevated with purging, reduced with laxative use)
- Liver function tests including albumin
- Amylase
- Hormones — luteinising hormone (LH), follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH), estradiol (or testosterone in males)

**Other tests**
- Urine for ketones
- Baseline ECG — particularly to check QT and PR intervals for risk of sudden death

## Management

Your doctor will determine the appropriate management pathway based on age and clinical stability.

### When Emergency or Urgent Assessment is Needed

**For patients aged 17 years or older**, your doctor will arrange acute general medical assessment if any of the following are present:

| Criterion | Detail |
|-----------|--------|
| Life-threatening weight loss | Total body weight less than 70% of ideal (BMI less than 13), or rapid weight loss of 20% or more over 3 months |
| Acute medical complications of malnutrition | Syncope, seizures, cardiac failure |
| Severe dehydration | May require brief admission for rehydration |
| Hypoglycaemia | Blood glucose less than 2.5 mmol/L with symptoms (sweating, fatigue, palpitations, tremors, hunger, dizziness, confusion, or seizure) |
| Electrolyte or blood imbalance | Potassium less than 2.5 mmol/L; phosphate less than 0.7 mmol/L; eGFR less than 60; AST or ALT greater than 500 units/L; neutrophils less than 1.0 |
| Physiological instability | Bradycardia (heart rate less than 40 bpm); hypotension (systolic BP less than 80 mmHg); postural tachycardia (heart rate increase more than 20 bpm on standing); hypothermia (below 35°C) |
| Abnormal ECG | Arrhythmia; prolonged QTc greater than 500 milliseconds |

**For patients aged 16 years or younger**, your doctor will arrange acute paediatric assessment if the patient:
- Has any signs of acute medical instability (listed in the assessment section above)
- Is continuing to deteriorate despite maximal outpatient therapy
- Has co-morbid medical conditions such as diabetes
- Has rapid weight loss with physical signs

Your doctor will seek paediatric advice to discuss admission if:
- Complete food refusal for more than 48 hours
- Significant dehydration, or complete fluid refusal for more than 24 hours
- Significant co-morbid mental health conditions are compromising nutrition

### Psychiatric Risk

Even if medically stable, your doctor will arrange urgent mental health assessment if there are signs of acute psychiatric risk, including:

- Acute food or fluid refusal
- Failure of outpatient treatment
- Suicidal ideation
- Uncontrollable bingeing and purging
- Co-morbid psychiatric diagnosis interfering with treatment
- Family exhaustion

### Outpatient Management

If the patient is medically stable with no acute concerns, your doctor will:

- Refer early to child and youth mental health services — your doctor will maintain a low threshold for referral and refer even if the patient does not fulfil all diagnostic criteria
- Arrange regular follow-up while awaiting assessment — weekly appointments are typical, often as double consultations
- Consider referral for cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) or mental health support through your practice or primary health organisation
- Encourage involvement of a family member or support person in the patient's management long term
