# Eczema in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of eczema (atopic dermatitis) in children, from everyday skin care through to treating flares and infections.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if:

- Child is systemically unwell (lethargic, unable to drink, fever above 38°C, in pain, or immunocompromised)
- Signs of cellulitis, sepsis, or periorbital cellulitis
- Infected eczema in a child younger than 3 months
- Suspected eczema herpeticum — widespread or severe sudden-onset herpes infection, or any herpes infection near the eye
- Failure to thrive
- Child is younger than 6 weeks

---

## Background

Eczema (atopic dermatitis) is a chronic, relapsing, intensely itchy skin condition. It is more common in children with a family history of eczema, asthma, or hay fever, and is caused in part by dysfunction of the skin barrier.

- Onset is often in the first year of life, with a relapsing and remitting course
- Eczema can cause severe itch, soreness, skin infection, and sleep disturbance
- There is no cure, but many children grow out of it before age 5
- With good management, the impact on daily life can be greatly reduced
- Higher rates occur among young Māori and Pacific New Zealanders; targeted education and sustained management are important

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. **Take a history**, including:
   - Age of onset (onset before 6 weeks may suggest an alternative diagnosis)
   - Symptoms: itch, scratching, inflamed or dry scaling skin, relapsing course
   - Chronic features: skin thickening (lichenification), pigmentary changes
   - Bathing frequency and topical treatment use — underuse of treatments is a common cause of treatment failure
   - Psychosocial effects: sleep, school, family impact
   - Triggers: soap, bubble-bath, chlorinated pools, skin infections, food or inhalant triggers, stress
   - Family history of eczema, allergic rhinitis, asthma, or food allergy

2. **Examine the skin**, looking for:
   - Age-related patterns:
     - *Infants under 1 year:* widespread rash, often starting on the cheeks; nappy area usually spared
     - *Toddlers and pre-schoolers:* extensor aspects of joints, ankles, knees
     - *Older children:* flexural pattern — elbows, knees, eyelids, earlobes, neck, scalp
   - Eczema can look different on darker skin: may appear hyper-pigmented, dry, scaly, thickened, or bumpy (papular)
   - Signs of infection
   - Features of eczema herpeticum: rapidly worsening painful eczema, fever, clustered blisters or punched-out lesions, failure to respond to treatment

3. **Consider other diagnoses**, such as psoriasis, impetigo, contact or irritant dermatitis, seborrhoeic dermatitis, fungal infection, scabies, or immune deficiency

4. **Assess severity** using the Patient-Oriented Eczema Measure (POEM) questionnaire

5. **Investigations** are only arranged if indicated — a wound swab is only considered if antibiotic resistance is suspected, the infection is not typical of *Staphylococcus aureus*, or there is recurrent infection not responding to antibiotics. Allergy tests are only arranged if a specific allergen has been identified.

## Management

### Everyday Management

The key to managing eczema is the regular, generous use of emollients (moisturisers). Your doctor may consider:

1. **Emollients** — applied at least 4 times per day (e.g., Cetomacrogol, Sorbolene, HealthE Fatty cream)
   - Applied to the entire body, not just affected areas
   - Applied in the direction of hair growth
   - Pump containers or a clean spoon reduce infection risk from open tubs
   - Aim to use around 500 g every 2 weeks
   - Continue daily use even when skin is clear
   - Lotions are not moisturising enough; avoid products containing sodium lauryl sulfate (SLS) or fragrance

2. **Regular baths or showers** to hydrate skin and remove creams:
   - Use a soap substitute (never soap or bubble-bath)
   - Comfortable warm water temperature, up to 15 minutes
   - Pat dry and immediately apply emollient
   - Consider adding a bleach bath once or twice a week to reduce *Staphylococcus aureus* colonisation and prevent infective flares

3. **Preventive measures**:
   - Avoid triggers: overheating, soap, bubble-bath, wool against skin
   - Keep fingernails short and filed to reduce skin damage from scratching
   - Avoid daily antihistamines for itch — address underlying skin dryness instead

4. **Education and action plan** — your doctor will provide an Eczema Action Plan and information on how to manage day-to-day

5. **Food allergy** — most children with eczema do not have food allergy causing the eczema. Restriction diets are not advised as they can be nutritionally harmful in young children. If you suspect a specific food is causing an immediate reaction (within 30–60 minutes of eating), discuss this with your doctor before making any dietary changes.

6. **Follow-up** every 2–3 weeks until symptoms are under control. Bring topical treatments to each appointment so your doctor can check quantities used. If topical steroids are needed daily for more than 4 weeks, your doctor will reassess.

---

### Flaring Eczema

During a flare, your doctor may consider:

1. **Increasing emollient** use to hourly if needed

2. **Topical corticosteroids** (steroid creams) for inflamed, red skin:
   - Applied after a bath and before emollient
   - Used twice a day until redness resolves
   - Not used if eczema herpeticum is suspected
   - Oral steroids are not recommended — they can cause rebound flares when stopped

3. **Topical calcineurin inhibitors (TCIs)** — tacrolimus 0.1% or pimecrolimus 1% — may be considered for eligible patients (e.g., mild to moderate eczema on the face or eyelids where topical corticosteroids are contraindicated). These are second-line treatments and are not used during active infection.

   Topical corticosteroids are contraindicated in: periorificial dermatitis, rosacea, documented epidermal atrophy, documented allergy to topical corticosteroids, cataracts, glaucoma, or raised intraocular pressure.

4. **Bleach baths** once or twice a week to reduce skin bacteria

5. **Treating infection**, if present:

   *Infected eczema* (usually caused by *Staphylococcus aureus*) — your doctor may prescribe oral antibiotics:
   - Cephalexin: 25 mg/kg three times a day (max 1 g per dose) for 5 days — palatable suspension, well tolerated
   - Flucloxacillin: 12.5 mg/kg three times a day (max 500 mg per dose) for 7–10 days — effective against *S. aureus* but the suspension is unpalatable and must be taken on an empty stomach
   - If penicillin allergy or MRSA suspected — co-trimoxazole: 24 mg/kg twice a day (max 960 mg per dose) for 5 days. Not used in infants under 6 weeks.
   - A wound swab is only considered if antibiotic resistance is suspected or the infection is not responding
   - Intravenous antibiotics are not given in the community

   *Suspected minor herpes infection* — your doctor may prescribe oral antiviral medication (aciclovir or valaciclovir) promptly. Steroids and TCIs are stopped while treating herpes infection.

   > **Note (May 2025):** Aciclovir and valaciclovir doses should be reduced in patients with kidney impairment. Your doctor will check kidney function and monitor for signs of toxicity (lethargy, confusion, agitation, hallucination).

6. **Antihistamines** for occasional use during acute flares to reduce itch or aid sleep:
   - Cetirizine — first choice, non-sedating
   - Loratadine — non-sedating alternative
   - Promethazine — sedating, for severe itch for 1–2 nights only; contraindicated in children under 6 years

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer to a specialist or community eczema service if:

- Mild to moderate eyelid or facial eczema where topical corticosteroids are contraindicated
- Severe or recalcitrant eczema not responding to standard treatment
- Recurrent skin infections
- Regularly waking at night more than twice a week
- Missing school or activities due to eczema
- Significant psychosocial impact
- No improvement after 4 visits despite education
- Ongoing daily need for topical steroids
- Concerns about food triggering eczema (without immediate reaction)
- A breastfeeding mother is considering a food exclusion diet
