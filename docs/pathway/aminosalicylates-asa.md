# Aminosalicylates (ASA) for Inflammatory Bowel Disease

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the use of aminosalicylates (ASA) and other medications in inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD). All medication decisions should be made with your doctor.

---

## IBD Medication Overview

There are four main groups of medications used in IBD — for either acute flares or long-term maintenance:

1. **Aminosalicylates (ASA)** — first-line for ulcerative colitis
2. **Steroids** — for acute flares
3. **Immunomodulators** — for maintenance when ASA fails or steroids are needed repeatedly
4. **Biologics** — for moderate-to-severe disease unresponsive to other treatments

> **Important:** Patients on steroids, thiopurines, methotrexate, or biologics are immunosuppressed. Annual flu vaccination is recommended. Discuss any infections or planned surgery with your doctor promptly.

---

## Aminosalicylates (ASA)

ASA medications include **mesalazine** (brand names: Pentasa, Asacol), **olsalazine**, and **sulphasalazine**.

### Indications

- ASA are the core drugs for **ulcerative colitis (UC)** — used first-line for both remission and maintenance
- Effect in active Crohn's disease is probably very limited
- Mesalazine is effective and well-tolerated
- Sulphasalazine and olsalazine are used less commonly due to side effects but are useful in certain circumstances

### Dosing

**Mesalazine:**
- *Asacol* — oral tablets and suppository formulations. Tablets release in the colon only. Standard dose up to **3.2 g per day** in divided doses.
- *Pentasa* — oral tablets, sachets, suppository and enema formulations. Tablets release throughout the tract from duodenum to rectum. Standard dose up to **4 g per day** as divided or single dose.

**Sulphasalazine:**
- Acute disease: 1–2 g four times daily
- Maintenance: 500 mg four times daily
- Note: may cause yellow-orange discolouration of skin, urine, and body fluids, and can stain soft contact lenses

**Rectal therapy** (suppositories, enemas) is effective for distal disease and can be used alone or combined with oral ASA.

### Monitoring

- Monitor every 6 months with **creatinine** and **urine dipstick**

### Safety — Blood Disorders

ASA can cause blood disorders. Your doctor may advise:

- Report any unexplained bleeding, bruising, purpura, sore throat, fever, or malaise during treatment
- A blood count will be performed and the drug stopped immediately if a blood dyscrasia is suspected

## Steroids (e.g., Prednisone)

### Indications

- Used to obtain remission in more severe disease or flare-ups in both UC and CD
- Requires a sufficiently long course (typically 4–8 weeks) with slow tapering to prevent early relapse
- **No role in maintenance therapy**
- Consider the adverse effects of steroids and increased risk of sepsis

### Dosing

Your doctor may consider prednisone at full dose (e.g., 40 mg/day), weaning over approximately 8 weeks by 5 mg per week.

> If steroids are needed more than twice a year, or courses are prolonged, your doctor will refer to gastroenterology to consider immunomodulator or biologic therapy.

### Monitoring

- Your doctor will consider bone protection: vitamin D, calcium intake, and bone density scanning (DXA) for long-term steroid use

---

## Immunomodulators

Two groups:

### Thiopurines (azathioprine, mercaptopurine/6MP)

Both are first-line immunomodulators. Mercaptopurine use in UC is unapproved but used in practice.

**Indications:**
- Maintenance therapy when ASA is ineffective in UC, or when repeated steroid courses are required
- Induction of remission in CD with severe or complex disease
- Only started by gastroenterologists after full risk discussion

**Important safety information:**
- Slow onset — may take **3–6 months** to achieve remission
- Side effects include nausea, vomiting, flu-like illness, myelosuppression, and liver toxicity
- Long-term risks: immunosuppression, small increased risk of skin cancer and lymphoma
- **Avoid co-prescribing with allopurinol** (risk of severe myelosuppression)
- **Live vaccines are contraindicated** (see below)
- TPMT enzyme level is checked before starting to predict risk of toxic effects
- Safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding (discuss with your specialist)

**Monitoring:** FBC and LFTs at least weekly for the first 4 weeks, then every 3 months long-term.

### Methotrexate (second-line)

- Used weekly, orally or subcutaneously
- Useful when patients are intolerant of, or disease is refractory to, thiopurines
- **Absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy** — effective contraception required; delay pregnancy for at least 3–6 months after stopping (men and women)
- Co-prescribed with folic acid 5 mg once weekly (not on the same day as methotrexate) to reduce side effects
- Monitoring: FBC and LFTs every 3 months

## Biologics (Infliximab, Adalimumab)

### Indications

- Potent anti-inflammatory agents (TNF blockers) used for moderate-to-severe IBD unresponsive to other treatments
- Currently available in New Zealand: **infliximab** (Remicade) and **adalimumab** (Amgevita, Humira)
- Adalimumab is funded via Special Authority — initial application by a gastroenterologist only; GPs may apply for renewal

### Monitoring and Safety

- Before starting, patients undergo full screening (varicella zoster, hepatitis, tuberculosis) — medication may reactivate latent conditions
- Withhold dose if signs of active infection
- Withhold before major surgery — discuss with surgeon and gastroenterologist
- Flu and pneumococcal vaccinations may be less effective on biologics
- **Live vaccines are contraindicated** (see below)
- Regular gastroenterology review (at least every 6 months once stable)

---

## Live Vaccines — Contraindicated on Immunosuppressants

Patients on thiopurines, methotrexate, or biologics should **not** receive live attenuated vaccines, including:

- Herpes zoster vaccines
- Varicella
- MMR (measles, mumps, rubella)
- Oral polio
- Intranasal influenza
- BCG, oral typhoid, yellow fever

Inactivated vaccines (e.g., injectable flu, pneumococcal) are safe but may be less effective.
