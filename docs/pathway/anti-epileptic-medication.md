> **Clinical guidance note:** This content is drawn from HealthPathways clinical guidelines for use as a reference tool. Decisions about anti-epileptic medication — including which drug to start, dose titration, and monitoring — should be made by or in close consultation with the prescribing doctor managing the patient's epilepsy. Drug interactions, pregnancy considerations, and psychiatric comorbidities require individual clinical judgement.

## Overview

Anti-epileptic medications (AEDs) are used to prevent seizures in people with epilepsy.

**First-line medication choices by epilepsy type:**

| Epilepsy type | First-line options |
|---|---|
| Primary generalised epilepsies | Sodium valproate*, lamotrigine, or levetiracetam |
| Focal epilepsies | Carbamazepine, lamotrigine, or levetiracetam |

*Sodium valproate is avoided in women of child-bearing potential due to teratogenicity.

## General Principles of Treatment

- **Monotherapy is preferred** — use one drug before adding a second.
- Treatment is usually initiated after 2 unprovoked seizures more than 24 hours apart, or after 1 unprovoked seizure when further seizures are likely.
- The first medication trialled has the best chance of success — choose carefully.
- Ensure an adequate trial of one agent before switching.
- When transitioning to a new AED: introduce it while still on the previous drug, assess effect, then withdraw whichever is not working.
- Start low and increase slowly (typically every 1–2 weeks) to reduce side-effects.
- Prefer slow-release (SR/CR) formulations where available — they minimise peak-concentration side-effects.
- Many AEDs cause significant drug interactions via hepatic enzyme induction (e.g., oral contraception).
- **AED hypersensitivity** is rare but potentially fatal: fever, rash, and lymphadenopathy appearing 1–8 weeks after starting. Withdraw the drug immediately and seek neurology advice.

## Special Considerations for Females

- Carefully weigh benefits and risks of AEDs in women — teratogenicity and hormonal contraception interactions are major concerns.
- Several AEDs interact with the oral contraceptive pill (see individual drug entries below).
- See also the Epilepsy in Pregnancy pathway for pregnancy-specific guidance.

## Individual Drug Profiles

### Carbamazepine

**Role:** First-line for focal epilepsies.

**Initiation — use controlled-release (CR) formulation:**

| Week | Dose |
|---|---|
| Week 1 | 200 mg at night |
| Weeks 2–3 | 200 mg twice a day |
| Weeks 4–5 | 300 mg morning, 200 mg night |
| Week 6 onward | 300 mg twice a day |

- If seizures continue on 300 mg twice daily, increase gradually until seizure-free or toxicity occurs.
- The 200 mg CR tablets can be halved (to 100 mg) without losing the CR effect.
- Due to auto-induction, a dose increase may be needed at 6–8 weeks to maintain serum levels.
- Usual maintenance: 600–800 mg/day in 2 divided doses.

**Withdrawal:** Reduce by 100 mg every 1–2 weeks.

**Key side-effects:**
- Generally well tolerated; relatively safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding.
- Monitor LFTs and WCC fortnightly for first 2 months (early hepatic abnormality and leukopenia risk). Isolated GGT elevation = enzyme induction, not hepatotoxicity.
- Rapid titration can cause allergic rash or Stevens-Johnson syndrome (SJS).
- Increased SJS risk in Han Chinese or Thai patients with HLA B*2502 haplotype — consider haplotype testing before starting in these groups.
- Dose-related: nausea, sedation, dizziness, ataxia.
- **Reduces effectiveness of oral and injectable contraceptives** (enzyme induction).

**Monitoring:**
- First 1–2 months: FBC, LFTs, electrolytes, drug level.
- Ongoing: FBC, LFTs, electrolytes yearly.
- Drug levels not routinely needed — check only for suspected toxicity, poor seizure control, or compliance concerns.

### Clobazam

**Role:** Add-on therapy — not used as monotherapy. Useful in focal epilepsy and some generalised epilepsies (e.g., Lennox-Gastaut syndrome). Can be used for clusters of seizures.

**Dose:**
- Start 10 mg at night.
- If needed after 1–2 weeks: increase to 10 mg twice a day.
- Can increase to 30 mg once daily — limited evidence for higher doses.

**Withdrawal:** Reduce by 5 mg every 2–3 weeks.

**Key side-effects:**
- Sleepiness, behavioural reactions.
- May aggravate obstructive sleep apnoea.
- May cause muscle weakness.

---

### Lamotrigine

**Role:** First-line for both focal and generalised epilepsies.

**Important:** Titrate slowly — rapid titration or use with sodium valproate significantly increases risk of Stevens-Johnson syndrome. Warn patients to stop the drug immediately if a generalised rash develops.

**Initiation — if patient is on sodium valproate** (valproate doubles lamotrigine half-life):

| Weeks | Dose |
|---|---|
| Weeks 1–2 | 25 mg alternate days |
| Weeks 3–4 | 25 mg at night |
| Weeks 5–6 | 25 mg twice a day |
| Weeks 6–7 | 25 mg morning, 50 mg night |
| Weeks 8–9 | 50 mg twice a day |

Up to 2 further 50 mg increases at ≥3-week intervals if seizures continue.

**Initiation — not on valproate:**

| Week | Dose |
|---|---|
| Week 1 | 25 mg at night |
| Week 2 | 25 mg twice a day |
| Week 3 | 25 mg morning, 50 mg night |
| Week 4 | 50 mg twice a day |
| Week 5 | 50 mg morning, 75 mg night |
| Week 6 | 75 mg twice a day |
| Week 7 | 75 mg morning, 100 mg night |
| Week 8 | 100 mg twice a day |

Further 50 mg increases at ≥3-week intervals up to 600 mg/day if seizures continue or drug level is low.

**Withdrawal:**
- Reduce by 100 mg off total daily dose every 1–2 weeks until 200 mg/day.
- Then 50 mg/week until 100 mg/day.
- Then 25 mg/week.

**Key side-effects:**
- Generally well tolerated; may cause irritability, insomnia, rash.
- Dizziness, blurred vision, ataxia = toxicity — reduce dose.
- **Oestrogen-containing oral contraceptives (COCP) increase lamotrigine metabolism:** levels drop on active pill and rise in the pill-free week. If starting COCP, may need to increase lamotrigine dose to prevent seizure breakthrough.

**Monitoring:**
- Drug levels not routinely needed — check for suspected toxicity, poor control, or compliance concerns.
- A level after reaching initial maintenance dose can help estimate final target dose (range: 100 mg in elderly up to 600–800 mg/day in monotherapy).
- **Pregnancy:** lamotrigine metabolism increases significantly in the second trimester — monitor levels every 2 weeks and maintain pre-pregnancy level. After delivery, levels rise rapidly — adjust promptly to avoid toxicity.

### Levetiracetam

**Role:** First-line for both focal and primary generalised epilepsies.

**Initiation:**

| Week | Dose |
|---|---|
| Week 1 | 250 mg at night |
| Week 2 | 250 mg morning, 250 mg night |
| Week 3 | 500 mg morning, 250 mg night |
| Week 4 | 500 mg morning, 500 mg night |
| Week 5 | 750 mg morning, 750 mg night |

- Reduce dose in renal impairment.

**Withdrawal:** Reduce by 500 mg every 1–2 weeks until 1000 mg total daily dose, then by 250 mg per week.

**Key side-effects:**
- Generally well tolerated; may cause dizziness, drowsiness, tremor.
- **Psychiatric symptoms** (depression, irritability) may require withdrawal.
- Use as second-line in patients with significant psychiatric history.

**Monitoring:**
- Drug levels not routinely needed — check for suspected toxicity, poor control, or compliance concerns.
- **Pregnancy:** obtain baseline level pre-pregnancy and every 4 weeks during pregnancy. Levels may fall with seizure breakthrough.

---

### Phenytoin

**Role:** Second-line only — no longer widely used due to narrow therapeutic index and long-term toxicity. **Do not start without specialist advice.**

**Dose:**
- Day 1: oral load 300 mg twice a day.
- Day 2 onward: 300 mg once a day. Dose adjustment after 2 weeks based on drug level.

**Key side-effects:** Hirsutism, gum hyperplasia, osteoporosis. Toxicity: unsteadiness, blurred vision, ataxia.

**Monitoring:**
- Drug levels useful when initiating to finalise maintenance dose.
- Routine levels not needed — check for suspected toxicity, poor control, or compliance concerns.
- **If level is low, seek specialist advice before increasing** — small dose increases can cause disproportionately large rises in plasma levels and toxicity.

---

### Sodium Valproate

> **Avoid in women of child-bearing potential** due to teratogenicity and risk of developmental delay in the child.

**Paternal exposure risk:** Studies suggest increased risk of neurodevelopmental disorders in children whose fathers were taking Epilim (valproate) at the time of conception. Inform patients and consider alternative treatments or effective contraception.

**Role:**
- First-line for primary generalised epilepsy (except in women of child-bearing potential).
- Second-line for focal epilepsy.

**Initiation:** Give twice daily with food. Use enteric-coated preparation if GI side-effects occur.

| Week | Dose |
|---|---|
| Week 1 | 200 mg twice a day |
| Week 2 | 400 mg twice a day |
| Week 3 | 500 mg twice a day |

- After a further month on 500 mg twice daily, can increase to 500 mg morning and 1000 mg night if seizures continue.
- Maintenance: usually 800–1000 mg/day.

**Withdrawal:** Reduce by 200 mg off daily dose at weekly intervals.

**Key side-effects:**
- Weight gain, tremor (dose-related).
- Hair loss — can be prevented with oral zinc.
- GI upset/nausea — use enteric-coated tablets and take with food.
- Thrombocytopenia — common, usually not clinically significant.
- Hepatic dysfunction — more common in children. Advise patients to report fever, nausea, vomiting, jaundice, or unexplained bruising/bleeding.

**Monitoring:**
- First 1–2 months: FBC and LFTs. Then yearly.
- Drug levels not routinely needed — check for suspected toxicity, poor control, or compliance concerns.

---

### Topiramate

> **Use with caution in females of child-bearing potential:** increased risk of fetal malformation (especially cleft lip/palate) and neurodevelopmental disorders (autism spectrum disorder, intellectual disability). Pregnancy testing required before starting. Effective contraception essential.

**Role:** Second-line for generalised and focal epilepsies. Managed by clinicians experienced in its use.

**Initiation:**

| Week | Dose |
|---|---|
| Week 1 | 25 mg at night |
| Week 2 | 25 mg twice a day |
| Week 3 | 25 mg morning, 50 mg night |
| Week 4 | 50 mg twice a day |
| Week 5 | 50 mg morning, 75 mg night |
| Week 6 | 75 mg twice a day |

Further increases of 25 mg if seizures continue and no evidence of toxicity.

**Withdrawal:** Reduce by 50 mg/week until 100 mg/day, then reduce by 25 mg/week.

**Key side-effects:**
- Drowsiness, slowed thinking, word-finding difficulties (dose-related — improve with dose reduction).
- Reduced appetite and weight loss — may require withdrawal long-term.
- Renal stones — advise increased fluid intake.
- Acute glaucoma — seek urgent attention for sudden painful eye.
- **Reduces effectiveness of oral contraceptive pill** — a higher oestrogen dose is recommended.

## Quick Reference: AED Summary

| Drug | First-line use | Key concern | Contraception interaction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Carbamazepine | Focal | SJS risk (esp. Han Chinese/Thai); enzyme inducer | Reduces OCP/injection efficacy |
| Clobazam | Add-on only | Sedation; OSA aggravation | No significant interaction |
| Lamotrigine | Focal + generalised | SJS if titrated too fast or with valproate | COCP reduces lamotrigine levels |
| Levetiracetam | Focal + generalised | Psychiatric side-effects | No significant interaction |
| Phenytoin | Second-line only | Narrow therapeutic index; specialist only | Enzyme inducer — reduces OCP |
| Sodium valproate | Generalised (not WCBP) | Teratogen; paternal risk also | No significant interaction |
| Topiramate | Second-line | Fetal malformation risk; cognitive effects | Reduces OCP efficacy |

WCBP = women of child-bearing potential; OCP = oral contraceptive pill; SJS = Stevens-Johnson syndrome; OSA = obstructive sleep apnoea.
