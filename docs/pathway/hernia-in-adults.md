# Hernia in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of hernias in adults, including when urgent care is needed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department immediately if you have a hernia with:

- Severe pain, tenderness, or signs of peritoneal irritation
- Redness or discolouration over the hernia
- Nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain or distension, or inability to pass wind or stool — suggestive of bowel obstruction
- Fever with a painful hernia
- A hernia that was previously reducible but can no longer be pushed back in, especially with worsening pain

These features may indicate **strangulation** (impaired blood supply to the hernia contents) — a surgical emergency. Do not attempt to push the hernia back in.

---

## Background

A hernia occurs when tissue or part of an organ (usually bowel) protrudes through a weakness in the surrounding muscle or tissue wall.

- Hernias most commonly occur in the groin (inguinal or femoral), around the umbilicus, or at the site of a previous surgical incision.
- They present as a lump, often with varying degrees of discomfort.
- The lifetime risk of a male developing an inguinal hernia may be as high as 1 in 4.

### Types of Hernia

- **Inguinal hernia** — the most common type. A bulge in the groin, above the inguinal ligament. Can extend into the scrotum. Occurs in both men and women.
  - *Indirect*: passes through the inguinal ring; may be congenital.
  - *Direct*: bulges through a weakness in the posterior canal wall; more common with age.
- **Femoral hernia** — bulges below the inguinal ligament through the femoral canal. More common in women. Only 2% of all hernias, but carries a higher risk of strangulation — early treatment is recommended.
- **Ventral hernia** — along the midline of the abdomen:
  - *Umbilical/paraumbilical*: around the belly button. 15% of all hernias.
  - *Epigastric*: between the breastbone and navel.
  - *Spigelian*: through the Spigelian fascia at the lateral edge of the rectus muscle.
- **Incisional hernia** — occurs at the site of a previous surgical scar. 10% of all hernias.

### Hernia States

- **Reducible** — contents can be pushed back into the abdomen (e.g., by lying down or applying gentle pressure).
- **Irreducible** — contents cannot be returned to the abdomen, but blood supply is intact and pain is not severe.
- **Strangulated** — blood supply to the hernia contents is cut off. This is an emergency.

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your hernia with:

**History:**
- Location, onset, and progression of the lump
- What makes it appear or disappear (standing, lying, straining)
- Whether it is painful, and the nature of any pain
- Previous hernia surgery
- Impact on daily activities and work
- Symptoms of obstruction or strangulation (nausea, vomiting, constipation, fever)

**Physical examination:**
- Looking and feeling for a lump — may be more apparent when standing or coughing
- Testing for a **cough impulse**: the lump expands when you cough if it is a hernia
- Assessing whether the hernia is reducible or not
- Checking for signs of strangulation (tenderness, redness, systemic upset)

**Imaging:**
- Hernias are usually diagnosed on clinical examination alone. Imaging (ultrasound) is not routinely required, but may be used if the hernia cannot be felt or the diagnosis is uncertain.

**Differential diagnosis:** Other causes of a groin or abdominal lump include lipoma, lymph node enlargement, abscess, cyst, varicocele, or vascular aneurysm.

---

## Risk Factors

Factors that increase the risk of developing a hernia or hernia recurrence:

- Family history of hernia
- Previous abdominal surgery or previous hernia repair
- Smoking
- Low BMI or malnutrition
- Systemic conditions (cirrhosis, chronic renal failure)
- Raised intra-abdominal pressure from: obesity, pregnancy, ascites
- Increasing age
- Chronic straining: chronic cough, constipation, prostate symptoms

Modifiable risk factors should be addressed to reduce recurrence risk.

## Management

### Emergency

Your doctor will arrange **urgent surgical assessment** if there are signs of strangulation or bowel obstruction.

### Conservative Management (Non-Surgical)

Your doctor may consider conservative management if:

- The hernia is **asymptomatic or minimally symptomatic** (except femoral hernias, which carry a high strangulation risk regardless of symptoms)
- You are **elderly or medically unfit for surgery**

Conservative options include:
- **Watchful waiting** — monitoring the hernia without intervention
- **Supportive therapy** — a truss, corset, or binder can reduce discomfort, particularly for incisional hernias in patients unfit for surgery. Efficacy is generally low; surgery is preferred for symptomatic hernias when safe.

**Lifestyle advice (applies to all hernia patients):**
- Avoid heavy lifting or straining
- Achieve a healthy weight (your doctor may advise weight loss if BMI is above 25)
- Manage underlying conditions that increase abdominal pressure (chronic cough, constipation)
- Quit smoking — nicotine cessation is usually required before elective hernia surgery

### Surgical Management

Surgery is the definitive treatment. Your doctor may refer you for surgical assessment if:

**Criteria that generally favour surgery:**
- Symptomatic hernia with significant impact on daily life
- Femoral hernia (high strangulation risk — early repair recommended)
- Incisional hernia smaller than 5–10 cm (smaller incisional hernias strangulate more readily)
- Stomal hernia
- History of episodic irreducibility or bowel obstruction
- Very large hernia (greater than 20 cm)
- Diagnostic uncertainty (symptoms suggest hernia but none found on examination)

**Before surgery, your doctor will:**
- Consider your overall fitness for surgery and anaesthesia, and any co-morbidities that increase recurrence risk
- Advise that smoking cessation is usually required before elective surgery
- Advise that surgery may not proceed if BMI is 40 or above until weight loss is achieved

**The most common repair** is laparoscopic (keyhole) surgery using a synthetic mesh.

### After Hernia Surgery

Routine specialist follow-up after laparoscopic mesh repair is generally not needed. What to expect:

- Some pain for up to a week after surgery
- Bruising or swelling around the groin or scrotum — this is normal
- Desk workers typically need at least 1 week off work; labourers need 2–3 weeks
- You can drive once you can press the pedals comfortably
- Mesh-related complications are rare

**When to seek review after surgery:**
- A new lump develops, or pain is frequent or severe — contact your surgeon or GP
- Chronic pain lasting more than 3 months — your doctor may refer back to a surgeon
- Fluid collection (seroma) larger than a walnut — may need weekly aspiration
- Signs of wound infection — your doctor may prescribe antibiotics
- Suspected deep mesh infection — seek urgent surgical review (do not wait for imaging)
- Hernia recurrence — your doctor may refer back for surgical assessment
