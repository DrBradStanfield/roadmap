# Fever in Returning Travellers

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of fever in adults and children who have recently returned from overseas travel.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance immediately if:

- Rash with fever
- Neurological impairment (including altered mental state or decreased level of consciousness)
- Cardiovascular compromise (e.g., fast heart rate, low blood pressure, mottled skin)
- Return from a region with an active viral haemorrhagic fever outbreak (e.g., Ebola)

---

## Background

### About fever in returning travellers

There are many possible causes of fever after overseas travel. A detailed travel history — connecting symptoms to specific timelines and locations — is essential.

The most common illnesses in returned travellers include:
- Respiratory tract infections (e.g., COVID-19, influenza, pneumonia)
- Travellers' gastroenteritis
- Skin problems (e.g., cellulitis, rash, insect bites)
- Febrile illnesses including dengue, malaria, typhoid fever, hepatitis A, rickettsial infections

Serious infections to always consider:
- **Malaria** — can be life-threatening and must be excluded in any febrile returned traveller
- **Viral haemorrhagic fever** (e.g., Ebola)

Visiting friends and relatives accounts for about 40% of infections in returned travellers — these travellers often travel to more rural areas, for longer periods, and may not have obtained pre-travel advice, vaccination, or prophylaxis.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will do a rapid initial assessment to exclude serious infections (malaria, severe dengue, typhoid fever, viral haemorrhagic fever) and to evaluate any public health risk.

### Travel history — your doctor will ask about:

- Exact travel itinerary including stopovers, specific areas visited (rural or urban), duration of travel, and date of return
- Travel to areas with an emerging disease or active outbreak
- Type of accommodation (e.g., hotel vs. local housing, presence of bed nets)
- Activities and exposures during travel:

| Exposure | Related illnesses |
|----------|-------------------|
| Fresh water exposure | Schistosomiasis, leptospirosis |
| Raw or undercooked food, contaminated water | Campylobacter, giardia, amoebiasis, typhoid |
| Unpasteurised dairy products | Brucellosis, listeriosis, campylobacter |
| Caving | Histoplasmosis, leptospirosis, viral haemorrhagic fevers |
| Animal bites or direct animal contact | Rabies, wound infections, rat-bite fever, Herpes B virus (monkey bites) |
| Handling carcasses or animal hides | Q fever, leptospirosis, brucellosis, anthrax, Nipah virus |
| Contact with rodents or their excreta | Leptospirosis, murine typhus, Lassa fever (West Africa), hantavirus |
| Bird contact | Psittacosis, avian influenza |
| Mosquito bites | Malaria, dengue, chikungunya, Zika, Japanese encephalitis, yellow fever |
| Flea or fly bites | Murine typhus (fleas), leishmaniasis (sand flies), trypanosomiasis (tsetse fly) |
| Tick or mite bites | Rickettsial infections, Lyme disease, tick-borne encephalitis |
| Soil-skin contact (bare feet) | Cutaneous larva migrans, hookworm, strongyloides |
| Sexual contact | HIV, hepatitis B and C, STIs, syphilis, mpox |

- Medical care or hospitalisation received overseas
- Illness among travel companions
- Pre-travel health consultation, immunisations received, and whether malaria chemoprophylaxis was taken as prescribed

Your doctor will also consider common infections that are not travel-related — such as influenza, COVID-19, or an underlying condition flare — before focusing on tropical diagnoses.

### Fever pattern and associated symptoms

Your doctor will consider the combination of symptoms to narrow down the likely diagnosis:

**Systemic fever with non-specific symptoms (fatigue, headache, myalgia):**
Malaria, dengue, typhoid fever, rickettsial diseases, leptospirosis, acute HIV infection, acute schistosomiasis (Katayama fever), East African trypanosomiasis

**Fever with neurological symptoms:**
Malaria, meningococcal meningitis, viral encephalitis (e.g., herpes simplex, Japanese encephalitis, West Nile virus), rabies, trypanosomiasis, syphilis

**Fever with respiratory symptoms:**
Malaria, influenza, COVID-19, community-acquired pneumonia, legionellosis, MERS, avian influenza (H5N1)

**Fever with skin rash:**
Dengue, measles, chikungunya, Zika, varicella, typhoid fever, HIV, syphilis, Lyme disease, rickettsial disease, Epstein-Barr virus, viral haemorrhagic fevers

**Fever with gastrointestinal symptoms:**
Travellers' diarrhoea (bacterial, viral, or parasitic), malaria, amoebic dysentery, typhoid fever

**Fever with jaundice:**
Malaria, hepatitis A/B/E, leptospirosis, amoebic liver abscess, Epstein-Barr virus, rickettsial diseases, viral haemorrhagic fevers

### Investigations

Your doctor will indicate on the laboratory form that you are a returning traveller, and include your travel dates and destinations. This is important because laboratories need this information to run the correct tests.

**Standard initial tests:**
- Full blood count (FBC), kidney and liver function, CRP, blood cultures
- COVID-19 testing as per current guidance
- Urine test if urinary symptoms
- Stool specimen if diarrhoea (including details such as bloody or "rice water" appearance on the request form)

**Disease-specific tests — selected based on travel destination, incubation period, and symptoms:**

**Avian influenza (H5N1)**
- Regions: countries with ongoing H5N1 activity (check CDC)
- Incubation: 2–8 days
- Symptoms: fever, cough, breathlessness, headache, myalgia — can progress to pneumonia and respiratory failure
- Test: nasopharyngeal PCR swab (requires specialist approval and PPE)

**Chikungunya**
- Regions: Africa, Asia, Central and South America, Indian Ocean islands, Pacific, Caribbean
- Transmission: mosquito-borne
- Incubation: 2–4 days
- Symptoms: fever, symmetrical joint pain (especially distal joints), myalgia, headache, maculopapular rash — may cause prolonged post-infectious arthritis
- Test: mosquito-borne viral PCR panel (up to day 7 of illness — also tests for dengue and Zika)

**Dengue fever**
- Regions: Caribbean, Central and South America, Pacific Islands, northern Australia, South-east Asia, Africa
- Transmission: mosquito-borne
- Incubation: usually 4–7 days (up to 14 days)
- Symptoms: fever, nausea, vomiting, severe muscle and joint pain ("breakbone fever"), pain behind the eyes, rash
- Tests vary by day of illness:
  - Days 0–5: mosquito-borne viral PCR panel
  - Days 6–9: dengue NS1 antigen and urine PCR
  - After day 7: urine PCR and serology (requires specialist discussion)

**Hepatitis A**
- Regions: worldwide, especially lower-income countries
- Transmission: faecal-oral (contaminated food or water)
- Incubation: usually 28 days (range 15–50 days)
- Symptoms: fever, fatigue, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, jaundice
- Test: hepatitis A serology

**Japanese encephalitis**
- Regions: China, Korea, South-East Asia, Western Pacific, parts of Australia
- Transmission: mosquito-borne
- Incubation: 5–15 days
- Symptoms: most infections are asymptomatic; if symptomatic — headache, fever, seizures, focal neurological signs, reduced consciousness
- Test: specialist discussion required before any testing — symptomatic patients need hospital assessment

**Leptospirosis**
- Regions: worldwide including New Zealand, particularly after flooding (e.g., rice fields)
- Transmission: exposure to fresh water, animal urine, or offal
- Incubation: usually 10 days (range 2–30 days)
- Symptoms: fever, rigors, headache, muscle pain, vomiting, abdominal pain, rash
- Tests:
  - Days 0–7: blood PCR and acute serology; repeat serology at 2 weeks
  - After day 7: urine PCR

**Lyme disease**
- Regions: North America, Europe, temperate Asia (not endemic in New Zealand)
- Transmission: tick bites (especially in high grass, bush, and woodland)
- Incubation: 3–30 days
- Symptoms: characteristic expanding rash (erythema migrans), fever, fatigue, headache, joint and muscle aches; later complications include arthritis, facial palsy, meningitis
- Test: usually diagnosed on clinical grounds (rash + tick exposure); serology if weeks or months after initial infection

**Malaria**
- Regions: Sub-Saharan Africa, Asia, Oceania, South America
- Transmission: mosquito-borne
- Incubation: P. falciparum usually 7–10 days (longer if prophylaxis taken); P. vivax and P. ovale can relapse months after return
- Symptoms: non-specific fever, headache, muscle aches, nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, pallor
- Test: blood film for malaria parasites — repeat if initial test negative but suspicion remains; if patient appears unwell, your doctor will arrange urgent specialist assessment rather than waiting for results

**MERS (Middle East Respiratory Syndrome)**
- Regions: Arabian Peninsula (Iran, Jordan, Kuwait, Oman, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Yemen)
- Transmission: respiratory contact with infected humans or camels
- Incubation: 2–14 days
- Symptoms: fever, cough, shortness of breath
- Test: nasopharyngeal PCR (requires specialist approval and PPE)

**Rabies / bat lyssavirus**
- Regions: worldwide (rabies); Australia (bat lyssavirus, closely related)
- Transmission: bite, scratch, or mucous membrane contact with infected animal saliva (dogs most common; also monkeys, bats)
- Incubation: generally 2–8 weeks (range 10 days to 2 years)
- Symptoms: initial fever, weakness, tingling at bite site — progressing to acute encephalitis
- Test: saliva, CSF, or biopsy — only in an inpatient setting

**Rickettsial disease**
- Regions: worldwide (different species in different regions)
- Transmission: tick, flea, mite, or louse bites
- Incubation: 7–10 days (variable)
- Symptoms: fever, severe headache, intense muscle aches, rash, and localised eschar (wound-like sore at the bite site in some types) — can progress to sepsis with multi-organ failure if untreated
- Tests: PCR on eschar and rickettsial serology

**Typhoid fever**
- Regions: common in developing countries; very common in the Indian subcontinent and Pacific (Samoa, Fiji)
- Transmission: contaminated food or drinking water
- Incubation: 5–21 days
- Symptoms: fatigue, fever (may be the only symptom), abdominal pain, constipation (diarrhoea is uncommon), headache, rash
- Test: blood cultures are the only reliable community test

**Viral haemorrhagic fever (VHF)** (e.g., Ebola, Marburg, Crimean-Congo haemorrhagic fever, yellow fever)
- Regions: variable by virus type — check current outbreak data
- Incubation: Ebola 8–10 days (range 2–21 days); Marburg 2–21 days; CCHF 1–12 days; yellow fever 3–6 days
- Symptoms: abrupt fever, malaise, headache, muscle aches — progressing to vomiting, diarrhoea, and haemorrhage
- Test: no community testing — requires hospital admission and specialist management

**Zika virus**
- Regions: sub-Saharan Africa, South-East Asia, Pacific Islands, Americas, Caribbean (check current outbreak data)
- Transmission: mosquito-borne
- Symptoms: often asymptomatic; fever, joint pain, headache, conjunctivitis, rash
- Tests:
  - Within 14 days of symptom onset: blood and urine PCR (mosquito-borne viral PCR panel also covers dengue and chikungunya)
  - 14 or more days after onset: specialist advice required

## Management

> **Important:** Your doctor will always send a sample before starting treatment — especially from wounds and urine — because of the high risk of antibiotic-resistant organisms in travellers.

### Emergency transfer

Immediate ambulance transfer to the nearest emergency department is arranged if any of these are present:
- Neurological impairment (altered mental state or decreased level of consciousness)
- Acute respiratory distress (rapid breathing, low oxygen levels, severe shortness of breath)
- Cardiovascular compromise (fast heart rate, low blood pressure, cyanosis, or mottled skin)

Your doctor will alert the ambulance service and emergency department to the possibility of a transmissible infection so that appropriate precautions can be taken.

### Specialist assessment

Your doctor will arrange urgent specialist assessment (infectious diseases or paediatric medicine) if any of the following are suspected:
- Avian influenza (H5N1)
- Severe dengue or dengue with warning features (persistent abdominal pain, persistent vomiting, fluid accumulation, fall in blood pressure, mucosal bleeding, lethargy, or rapidly falling platelet count)
- Dengue shock syndrome (significant bleeding or bruising, hypotension, shock)
- Malaria (particularly if patient appears unwell)
- MERS
- Typhoid fever
- Viral haemorrhagic fever
- Rabies with symptoms after an animal bite overseas
- Rickettsial disease with severe features (encephalitis, respiratory distress, cardiac arrhythmias, bleeding, skin necrosis)
- Chikungunya with severe complications (respiratory failure, cardiovascular compromise, kidney failure, encephalitis)

### Management by diagnosis

**Chikungunya:**
- Supportive care only — usually self-limiting

**Dengue fever:**
- Your doctor will follow the dengue management pathway; specialist input is sought for warning features or severe disease

**Hepatitis A:**
- Supportive care only (no specific antiviral treatment)
- Public health guidance on exclusion: usually excluded from work, childcare, school, and food handling until 7 days after jaundice onset (or 2 weeks after symptom onset if no jaundice)

**Leptospirosis:**
- Your doctor may consider antibiotic treatment:
  - Adults and children aged 12 and over: doxycycline orally (200 mg loading dose, then 100 mg twice daily for 5 days)
  - Pregnant patients and children under 12: amoxicillin 1 g three times daily for 5 days (or azithromycin if amoxicillin not tolerated)

**Lyme disease:**
- Early-stage (at time of erythema migrans rash) can be treated in the community
- Your doctor may consider doxycycline or amoxicillin after seeking infectious diseases advice
- Specialist advice is also sought if neurological, cardiac, or joint involvement is suspected

**Malaria:**
- Treatment depends on the type of malaria and severity — your doctor will determine this based on blood results and specialist input

**Rabies (asymptomatic, bite wound acquired overseas):**
- Wound cleaned thoroughly
- Your doctor will seek infectious diseases advice for rabies immunoglobulin and post-exposure vaccination (given via the emergency department) — this is urgent and should be considered at any time after an animal bite overseas

**Rickettsial disease:**
- Your doctor may consider antibiotic treatment after infectious diseases discussion:
  - Adults and children aged 12 and over: doxycycline orally (200 mg loading dose, then 100 mg twice daily for 7 days)
  - Children under 12: paediatric infectious disease advice sought
