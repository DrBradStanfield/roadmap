# Weight-related Concerns in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: January 2023.*

This pathway covers assessment and management of weight-related concerns in adults, including overweight, obesity, and unintentional weight change.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Red Flags — Discuss with Your Doctor Promptly

Seek prompt medical review if you experience:

- Unintentional weight change (losing or gaining more than 10% of body weight in the last 6 months without changing food intake or physical activity)
- If your BMI is less than 20, a weight loss of more than 5% is considered significant.

---

## Background

Excess weight is common. People in larger bodies may be at increased risk of:

- High blood pressure
- High LDL cholesterol, low HDL cholesterol, or high triglycerides
- Type 2 diabetes
- Coronary heart disease and stroke
- Gallbladder disease
- Osteoarthritis
- Sleep apnoea and breathing problems
- Most cancers, except oesophageal and prostate cancer
- Mental illness, such as clinical depression and anxiety
- Body pain and difficulty with physical functioning
- Low quality of life

Achieving a healthy weight underpins the prevention and management of many long-term conditions. Finding and addressing psychological barriers to weight loss, and checking readiness to change, will help lifestyle, medicine or surgical treatment work.

## Assessment

Your healthcare provider will typically:

1. **Ask permission** before discussing your body weight, especially if this was not the primary reason for your visit.

2. **Take a history**, including:
   - Diet and eating habits (types of food and drink, meal patterns, speed of eating, fad diets tried, comfort eating, budget constraints)
   - Physical activity (type, duration, and frequency, including work, recreational, transport, home, and incidental activity)
   - Sleep quality and quantity, including shift work and insomnia
   - Smoking status and alcohol use
   - Mental well-being, spiritual beliefs, and cultural practices
   - Physical injuries or disabilities that may have contributed to weight change
   - Underlying medical conditions that may contribute to weight change or affect food intake (e.g., thyroid disease, depression, PCOS, sleep apnoea, COPD, dental or gut problems, cancer, eating disorders)
   - Medications. Some may contribute to weight gain (e.g., insulin, sulfonylureas, epilepsy medicines such as sodium valproate or carbamazepine, antipsychotics, antidepressants such as mirtazapine or amitriptyline, beta blockers, steroids, contraceptives). Others can lead to weight loss or affect appetite (e.g., empagliflozin).
   - Psychological factors (e.g., binge eating, poor body image, low self-worth, self-criticism)

3. **Examine you**, including:
   - Height and weight to calculate **BMI** (weight in kg ÷ height in m²):
     - Less than 18.5 = underweight
     - Between 18.5 and 24.9 = healthy weight
     - Between 25 and 29.9 = overweight
     - Greater than 30 = obese (mild greater than 30, moderate 35 or more, extreme 40 or more)
   - Note: BMI was not designed to be an individual indicator of health. It may overestimate body fat in muscular people and underestimate it in adults aged 70 years or older or those who have lost muscle. It is used as a screening tool as part of the overall assessment, and should not be used in pregnancy or for people aged under 18 years.
   - **Waist circumference** (measured at the midpoint between rib and iliac crest):

     | Population | Men, increased risk | Men, greatly increased risk | Women, increased risk | Women, greatly increased risk |
     |---|---|---|---|---|
     | European | ≥94 cm | ≥102 cm | ≥80 cm | ≥88 cm |
     | South Asian (incl. Chinese, Malay, Asian-Indian) | ≥90 cm | | ≥80 cm | |
     | Māori and Pacific Islander | ≥102 cm | | ≥88 cm | |

     European figures apply to Middle Eastern and Sub-Saharan African people, and South Asian figures to South and Central American people.

4. **Arrange investigations**, which may include:
   - Blood tests: lipids, HbA1c, creatinine/eGFR, FBC, CRP, sodium, potassium, calcium, alkaline phosphatase, thyroid function, coeliac serology, and iron/B12/folate if a nutritional deficiency is suspected
   - Cardiovascular disease risk assessment and diabetes screening (timing depends on age, ethnicity, and risk factors)
   - A same-day chest X-ray if unexplained weight loss and loss of appetite have persisted for more than 3 weeks, or if other criteria are met
   - CT chest/abdomen/pelvis if unintentional weight loss and clinical criteria are met

5. **Assess readiness to change.** Your provider may use tools such as the Readiness Ruler to understand your motivation and confidence around lifestyle changes.

## Management

### For Everyone

Your healthcare provider may recommend:

- Treating any underlying medical conditions and addressing nutritional deficiencies
- Discussing lifestyle factors such as alcohol, smoking, sleep, mental well-being, and social support
- Advice on healthy eating and regular physical activity
- Help with food costs or local food banks if healthy food is hard to afford or access
- Setting goals across three areas: food and drink, physical activity, and behaviour support
- Involving family/whānau, partner, or friends in your plan

**Healthy eating principles** include:
- Plenty of vegetables (especially non-starchy) and fruit
- Mostly wholegrain grain foods
- Some low- or reduced-fat milk products, legumes, nuts, seeds, fish and seafood, eggs, chicken, or lean red meat
- Mostly whole or less processed foods, low in salt, with little or no added sugar
- Make plain water your first choice of drink

For most people, losing 5 to 10% of body weight (e.g., 5 to 10 kg if you weigh 100 kg) will make a big difference to their health and the way they feel. Losing around 0.5 to 1 kg a week is a safe and realistic target.

### If You Are Underweight (BMI < 18.5) or Have Unintentional Weight Loss

Your provider will investigate for underlying causes (e.g., thyroid disease, COPD, eating disorder, malignancy, PCOS, sleep apnoea, Cushing syndrome).

If you appear malnourished, a **Food First approach** may be recommended:
- Three small regular meals with a small nutritious snack in between
- High-energy foods: milkshakes, standard milk, butter, oil, avocado, cream, cheese, sugar, honey
- Milky drinks (milky coffee, hot chocolate, milkshakes, smoothies) or soups instead of water, tea, or coffee
- Over-the-counter nutrition supplement drinks

If there is no improvement after 4 weeks, your provider may recommend oral nutrition supplements, referral to a dietitian, or both.

### If You Are Overweight or Obese

Your healthcare provider may recommend:

- Lifestyle changes as first-line management (diet, exercise, behaviour support)
- Referral to a dietitian, health psychologist, or a structured exercise programme (e.g., Green Prescription)
- Review of any medications contributing to weight gain

**Anti-obesity medication** produces only modest weight loss, is not effective in the long term, and must always be used with lifestyle changes. It may be considered if:
- Lifestyle changes (supervised diet, exercise, and behaviour change) over at least 6 months have not produced significant benefit, and
- Your BMI is ≥30

Only one weight loss medication should be used at a time. Four drugs are registered for weight loss in New Zealand:

| Medication | Type | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Liraglutide (Saxenda) | Subcutaneous injection | Reduces appetite and increases satiety |
| Naltrexone + bupropion (Contrave) | Oral tablet | Suppresses appetite |
| Orlistat (Xenical) | Oral tablet | Reduces absorption of dietary fat |
| Phentermine (Duromine) | Oral tablet | Suppresses appetite |

**Semaglutide (Wegovy) and tirzepatide (Mounjaro)** are also available as unfunded add-on treatments for obesity. They are indicated for adults aged 18 years and older with:
- BMI above 30 kg/m², or
- BMI above 27 kg/m² with at least one weight-related comorbidity

Your provider will review response to medication monthly for the first 3 months, and continue beyond 3 months only if it is well tolerated and has produced at least 5% weight loss.

**Bariatric surgery** may be considered if weight loss has not been achieved. Access in the public system is very limited, and specific criteria and exclusions apply. Private referral is another option. Your doctor can discuss whether you meet criteria and whether a referral is appropriate.

---

## Follow-up

If your BMI is less than 18.5, greater than 25, or you have had unintentional weight change, your provider will typically follow up in 1 month.

At follow-up, your provider will:
- Review progress against goals
- Identify and address any barriers
- Adjust medications if needed (particularly antihypertensives and antidiabetic medications if weight loss has occurred)
- If you were underweight and have not gained weight with the Food First approach, consider a nutrition supplement or a dietitian
- Consider additional support if weight goals have not been met
