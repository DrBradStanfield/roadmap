# Plantar Fasciitis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of plantar fasciitis — the most common cause of heel pain in adults.

---

## Background

The plantar fascia is a thick fibrous band extending from the heel bone (calcaneum) to each toe. It supports the arch of the foot.

- Pain is felt at the attachment of the plantar fascia at the heel, typically on weight-bearing.
- It is usually a degenerative change rather than true inflammation.
- It can result from an acute injury, partial tear, or gradual overload.
- Peak age is 40 to 60 years; it is also common in runners.
- Heel spurs may coexist but are not necessarily the cause of pain.
- In most cases, it is managed without surgery — 80% of cases settle with conservative management within 12 months, though some take up to 2 years.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider risk factors including:

- Being overweight
- Middle age
- Sedentary lifestyle, or repetitive athletic stress
- Occupations involving prolonged standing on hard surfaces in inadequate footwear

### Typical Symptoms

- Gradual onset of heel pain
- Pain is worst with the first steps in the morning or after sitting for a period
- Pain lessens with activity but may worsen by the end of the day
- Pain is localised to the inner (medial) aspect of the heel
- Patients often describe it as "feels like a stone bruise"

### Other Causes of Heel Pain

Your doctor will also consider other causes, including:

**Neurological**
- Tarsal tunnel syndrome (pain, burning, numbness in the medial heel, worse at night)
- Peripheral neuropathy
- S1 radiculopathy

**Soft tissue**
- Subcalcaneal bursitis
- Plantar fascia rupture
- Achilles tendonitis
- Fat-pad atrophy

**Skeletal**
- Calcaneal stress fracture
- Subtalar arthritis
- Inflammatory arthropathies

**Other**
- Neoplasm
- Vascular insufficiency

### Examination

Your doctor will typically check:

- Tenderness over the medial (inner) heel and along the plantar fascia
- Foot and ankle alignment during standing and walking
- For flat feet, high arches, and calf or Achilles tightness
- Spine and lower limbs for a nerve-related pain component
- Body weight

### Investigations

Investigations are not routinely required. Your doctor may consider:

- X-ray if a calcaneal stress fracture or other skeletal cause is suspected, or after 6 months without improvement
- Ultrasound if an acute tear is suspected or to assess the degree of degeneration in long-standing symptoms
- Blood tests if there is no improvement after 6 months and symptoms are bilateral or atypical (to look for an underlying inflammatory condition)

Note: heel spurs visible on X-ray do not correlate with pain, and surgery is not performed to remove them.

## Management

### Self-Management

Your doctor will discuss the following options:

- **Weight loss** if overweight — reduces load on the plantar fascia
- **Activity modification** — reduce load-bearing activities; swimming and stationary cycling are good alternatives
- **Footwear** — supportive shoes; avoid running on hard surfaces (grass or softer surfaces preferred)
- **Heel pads and shoe inserts** — used in both shoes to cushion and raise the heel (use with caution as some products can worsen symptoms)
- **Night resting splints and arch supports** — available online or from some pharmacies
- **Stretching exercises** — your doctor or physiotherapist can guide you on an appropriate programme
- **Ice** applied to the heel
- **Taping** the heel (may help some patients)

### Pain Relief

Your doctor may recommend paracetamol or anti-inflammatory medications (NSAIDs) if tolerated.

### Further Conservative Treatment

If self-management alone is not sufficient, your doctor may arrange:

- **Podiatry assessment** — for customised orthotics and footwear advice
- **Physiotherapy assessment** — for exercise therapy and activity modification

### Steroid Injection

Your doctor may consider a corticosteroid injection if there has been no improvement after 3 to 6 months of consistent conservative management (including orthotics, exercises, weight loss, and anti-inflammatory medications).

Important points about steroid injection:
- May provide short-term pain relief lasting a few weeks to a few months
- The injection can be painful; ultrasound guidance reduces pain and improves accuracy
- Possible complications include plantar fascia rupture (especially with multiple injections or obesity), fat-pad atrophy, skin changes, infection, or bruising
- Your doctor will discuss the risks and benefits and obtain your consent before proceeding

### Specialist Referral

Your doctor may refer you to an orthopaedic specialist if:

- There is no improvement after consistent conservative management
- A steroid injection is being considered and your doctor is not trained in the technique
- There is uncertainty about whether a steroid injection or repeat injection is appropriate
- Atypical symptoms suggest an inflammatory joint condition (rheumatology referral may be considered instead)
