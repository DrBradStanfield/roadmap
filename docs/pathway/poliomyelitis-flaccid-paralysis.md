# Poliomyelitis (Flaccid Paralysis)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the recognition and initial management of suspected poliomyelitis and acute flaccid paralysis (AFP). All suspected cases require urgent hospital assessment — there are no effective treatments for polio once it has caused paralysis.

---

## Important Context

Variant polioviruses have increased globally since COVID-19. Most people with polio are asymptomatic, but travellers can introduce the virus. Vaccination coverage in children under 5 is currently insufficient, and some communities — particularly Māori and Pacific peoples — are at higher risk.

---

## Act Immediately — Suspected Poliomyelitis

**This is a medical emergency requiring same-day hospital assessment.**

If a patient presents with acute flaccid paralysis:

- **Children aged 14 years and under:** Requires acute paediatric medicine assessment
- **Adults and young people aged 15 years and over:** Requires acute neurology assessment

Your doctor will also notify public health — poliomyelitis is a notifiable disease. All cases of flaccid paralysis in patients aged 14 or younger are reported to the New Zealand Paediatric Surveillance Unit (NZPSU).

---

## Recognising Acute Flaccid Paralysis (AFP)

AFP is a clinical syndrome — not a diagnosis in itself. It includes:

- Acute onset of weakness or paralysis, with reduced muscle tone in one or more limbs
- Decreased or absent deep tendon reflexes in the affected limbs
- Possible bulbar-respiratory muscle dysfunction (affecting speech and swallowing)
- No sensory or cognitive loss

Causes include Guillain-Barré Syndrome, transverse myelitis, poliovirus, and other viruses. All require urgent assessment.

---

## Non-Paralytic Poliovirus Infection

Most people infected with polio do not develop paralysis. Non-paralytic infection can cause:

- Fever
- Headache
- Sore throat
- Gastrointestinal disturbances
- Malaise
- Neck and back stiffness
- Pain in the limbs, back, and neck

Meningitis occurs in up to 1 in 25 cases. Permanent paralysis occurs in up to 1 in 200 cases.

---

## Infection Control

Suspected and confirmed cases must be managed in isolation using contact and droplet precautions:

- The patient should be kept isolated and wear a medical mask if able to tolerate one
- Maintain hand hygiene
- Use PPE: gloves and apron, eye protection, and medical mask (staff may choose a P2/N95 mask)
- Clean surfaces with household bleach (0.5% sodium hypochlorite solution)
- Clean toilets and bathroom facilities with standard products before others use them

Your doctor will seek public health advice and specialist advice (infectious diseases or paediatric medicine) to ensure adequate isolation for patients who remain in the community.

---

## Vaccination

Polio vaccination is the primary prevention measure. Vaccines include Ipol, Infanrix-Hexa, Infanrix-IPV, Hexaxim, and Adacel Polio.

Vaccination priorities:

1. Children aged 4 years and younger — including catch-up vaccination
2. Children and young people aged 15 years and younger
3. Unvaccinated or incompletely vaccinated adults
4. Anyone travelling overseas should be up to date with polio and MMR vaccination

Your doctor can advise on your vaccination status and whether catch-up doses are needed.
