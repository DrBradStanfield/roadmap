# Eczema (Dermatitis) in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: May 2024.*

This pathway covers eczema or dermatitis that starts in adulthood, or that continues from childhood atopic eczema. Most adults with eczema can be managed by their GP with irritant avoidance, regular soap substitutes and emollients, and appropriate use of topical corticosteroids.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department if:**
- You may have **extensive eczema herpeticum** (eczema-prone skin infected with the herpes simplex virus). Signs include clusters of "punched out" sores about 2 to 3 mm across that spread rapidly, areas of rapidly worsening painful eczema, and fever, lethargy, or swollen lymph glands. This infection can be severe and often needs hospital admission.
- You may have **eczema herpeticum around the eyes**. This needs urgent hospital eye assessment.
- You have **erythroderma**: intense, widespread redness of the skin.
- You have a **significant bacterial skin infection** with symptoms affecting your whole body (systemic symptoms).

**See your doctor promptly if:**
- Your eczema looks **infected**: usually weeping, crusting, and redness, sometimes with blistering.
- You may have mild, localised eczema herpeticum. Prompt antiviral treatment is given to prevent hospital admission.

## Background

- Dermatitis is a group of itchy, inflammatory skin conditions. "Dermatitis" and "eczema" are often used interchangeably. Atopic dermatitis (atopic eczema) is a common type.
- Atopic eczema is complex and has many causes. In some people, a genetic change in filaggrin, a protein that helps the skin hold moisture, weakens the skin barrier.
- Features of chronic atopic eczema:
  - The skin is usually itchy, dry, and inflamed. During flares it is redder, weepy, and blistered.
  - The face, neck, skin folds, nipples, and hands are commonly affected.
  - In skin of colour, there may be follicular, papular, and inverse patterns. The skin can become darker or lighter as a result, and inflammation can be underestimated.
  - The skin is prone to infection.
  - A personal history of eczema in childhood, and a family history of eczema, hay fever, or asthma, are common.
- Regular use of a soap substitute and emollients improves skin barrier function and prevents flares.
- Atopic eczema is more common in Māori and Pacific children than in European children. It often settles with age, but in a significant proportion it continues or returns in adolescence, early adulthood, and beyond. Severe childhood eczema is a sign of likely prolonged disease.

**Food and eczema:** The relationship is complex. Most people with atopic eczema do not have a food allergy causing it. Food allergies may coexist and may make eczema worse, but avoiding foods seldom cures the condition. Ongoing active eczema treatment is still needed.

## Assessment

### History

Your doctor may ask about:
- Which body sites are affected, the pattern, and severity.
- What triggers flares, e.g. irritants, allergens, infections, or food allergies.
- Current and previous treatments.
- How often you bathe or shower, and your access to a bath or shower.
- Your occupation.
- Personal and family history of eczema, hay fever, or asthma.

### Examination

Your doctor may look for:
- Where the dryness and redness are, and roughly what percentage of the body is affected.
- Signs of infection: usually weeping, crusting, and redness. There may be blistering.
- Erythroderma (intense, widespread redness).
- Thickened, leathery skin (lichenification), a sign of long-standing eczema.
- Eczema herpeticum. If the face is affected, the eyelids and eyes are checked.

### Types of eczema and dermatitis

- **Atopic dermatitis:** itchy, red, scaly patches in skin folds. Often a family history of eczema, hay fever, or asthma. Skin often thickens over time from scratching.
- **Seborrhoeic dermatitis:** affects oily areas such as the eyebrows, between the eyebrows, the folds beside the nose, behind the ears, the scalp, and the central trunk. The role of the yeast *Malassezia* is uncertain.
- **Nummular or discoid dermatitis:** separate round, itchy patches. May occur with long-standing atopic dermatitis and dry skin.
- **Irritant contact dermatitis:** caused by repeated exposure to irritants such as frequent hand washing, friction, or chemicals (e.g. detergents, solvents, acids). Often on the hands, with itchy blisters, scaling, and peeling.
- **Allergic contact dermatitis:** a delayed allergic reaction to substances such as nickel, fragrances, or preservatives. Where the rash appears can give a clue, e.g. earlobes for nickel in jewellery. Patch testing is used to confirm the allergy.
- **Asteatotic (dry skin) dermatitis:** skin dries with age, and this is a common cause of eczema in older people. It often affects the lower legs with a "crazy paving" pattern of fine cracks.
- **Venous eczema (venous stasis dermatitis):** poor blood return from the legs, often made worse by higher BMI and reduced mobility, causes swelling and itchy red, crusted, or scaly patches, with brown staining and hardened skin.
- **Lichen simplex chronicus:** a well-defined patch of thickened, leathery skin caused by chronic scratching.

For work-related contact dermatitis, your occupational history matters. Your doctor may consider an ACC claim, as ACC may provide assistance.

### Other conditions to consider

- Scabies
- Psoriasis
- Fungal infections
- Drug reactions

### Quality of life

Eczema can have a significant psychosocial effect on you and your whānau. Your doctor may use the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI), which has 10 questions with a maximum score of 30. The free DLQI app lets you repeat the questionnaire to track how eczema affects you over time.

## Management

Education matters: understanding how to manage your eczema makes treatment more effective. See the patient guide [how to manage eczema](https://www.healthinfo.org.nz/patientinfo/37247.pdf).

### General measures

- Avoid irritants (e.g. soaps, detergents, perfumed products), scratching, and extreme temperature (hot, cold) or humidity.
- Keep fingernails short.
- Use cotton bedding and underclothes.

### Emollients (moisturisers)

Your doctor may prescribe emollients. Continue them long-term, even when the skin looks good and feels comfortable.

### Bathing

- Once daily bathing or showering is recommended.
- Limit bathing time to 10 to 15 minutes and avoid very hot water.
- Avoid soap and liquid soap.
- Consider a subsidised soap substitute, e.g. cetomacrogol aqueous cream with glycerol, or emulsifying ointment. Apply it before bathing, then wash it off.
- Pat the skin dry and reapply moisturiser straight after bathing.

### Antiseptic washes for recurrent skin infections

If skin infections keep coming back, your doctor may suggest a regular antiseptic wash used in place of soap for body and hair. Currently there are no funded options, and availability changes often, so check with your pharmacist. People with otherwise healthy skin do not need ongoing antiseptic washes.

- **Bleach bath twice weekly:** diluted bleach (sodium hypochlorite), following the [bleach bath instructions](https://media.starship.org.nz/bleach-baths-2019/Bleach_Baths_for_Eczema_May_25.pdf). Without a bath, dilute bleach in a bucket and use the water to wash the body for 10 minutes.
- **Chlorhexidine gluconate 4% solution:** leave on the skin for at least 1 minute before washing off. It can be drying, so a moisturiser may be needed.
- **Octenidine:** leave on the skin for at least 1 minute before washing off. A useful alternative for sensitive or fragile skin, or if chlorhexidine is not tolerated. It is sold over the counter.
- **Vinegar soaks:** apply twice a day to small areas of infected eczema. Add 2 tablespoons of vinegar to 2 cups of warm water. Store extra solution in the fridge.

### Topical corticosteroids

Topical corticosteroids treat skin inflammation (redness, itch) and let the skin barrier heal. Ointments are more effective than creams of the same potency.

**How your doctor may prescribe them:**
- The potency is chosen for the severity and body area, used for a set period, then tapered and stopped.
- In most cases they are applied for a 2-week course. Thickened (lichenified) patches may need 4-week treatment. Anything longer should prompt a review of the diagnosis and treatment.
- Darker patches left after inflammation, especially common in skin of colour, are not a reason to keep using a steroid.
- **Apply liberally once daily**, enough to see a good shiny layer on the skin. Applying "thinly" or "sparingly" is a common cause of treatment failure. Once daily (e.g. at night) is usually enough and minimises side effects.
- Creams may suit people who find greasy ointments hard to tolerate, and very oozy skin, where ointment slides off.
- Steroids can be used on broken skin when there is no infection, and on superficially infected skin. Ointments can be used in skin folds.
- Potency depends on the steroid molecule, not the percentage on the label. Labels marked "mild", "moderate", "potent", or "very potent" help avoid confusion.
- Diluting a steroid with an emollient does not make it weaker or reduce side effects.
- With recurrent flares, your doctor may suggest maintenance use 2 days a week on old and new affected areas until inflammation has settled for 3 to 6 months. This reduces flares and the total amount of steroid used.

**General guide to potency by body area:**

- Face: mild steroid for mild disease, moderate steroid for moderate disease.
- Trunk or limbs: moderate steroid for mild disease; start with a potent steroid for moderate disease.
- Thickened skin (palms, fingers, soles of feet, lichenified skin): potent or very potent steroid usually required.
- Skin folds (neck folds, armpits, groin): mild steroid.

Treatment often fails in primary care because the steroid prescribed was not potent enough or not enough was supplied.

#### Atopic dermatitis

- Moderate and severe atopic dermatitis on the body is treated with a potent steroid, e.g. betamethasone valerate 0.1% or mometasone furoate 0.1%, until redness, inflammation, and itch are controlled. Typically, this takes 5 to 15 days.
- Once controlled, the steroid is stopped and emollients continue.
- If inflammation persists after 15 days, your doctor may reduce how often it is applied and reconsider the diagnosis.
- Steroid courses are repeated for flares.

#### Discoid eczema

Usually treated daily for 2 to 4 weeks with a potent steroid ointment (e.g. betamethasone dipropionate 0.05%) or a very potent one (e.g. clobetasol propionate 0.05%), then tapered over weeks.

#### Venous eczema

- Acutely inflamed venous eczema is treated with potent or ultra-potent topical steroids and emollients.
- The underlying vein problem is also managed: elevate your feet, walk regularly, and use graduated compression stockings once the acute eczema has settled.

### Seborrhoeic dermatitis

Your doctor may consider:
- For flares, a mild steroid combined with an antifungal (e.g. miconazole with hydrocortisone) for 1 to 3 weeks, then maintenance treatment.
- Antifungals as maintenance to reduce *Malassezia*:
  - Miconazole cream twice daily for 2 to 4 weeks, repeated as needed.
  - For the scalp and face, ketoconazole shampoo 2%, used and rinsed off twice a week for at least a month, and indefinitely if needed.
  - For the scalp, other medicated shampoos containing selenium sulfide, zinc pyrithione, coal tar, or salicylic acid, twice a week as maintenance.

### Topical calcineurin inhibitors (facial eczema)

For eczema on the face, your doctor may consider pimecrolimus or tacrolimus. These may be funded in limited situations when steroids are not suitable, or may be self-funded.

- They are second-line for moderate to severe atopic dermatitis flares if steroids cause side effects. They are not more effective than steroids but have different side effects.
- They can be used to prevent flares in people who flare often, i.e. 4 or more times per year.
- They do not thin the skin, so may be preferred on thin skin such as the face and skin folds. They do not cause glaucoma or cataracts, though these can still occur in eczema from chronic rubbing.
- Examples of when steroids on the face are not suitable: periorificial dermatitis, rosacea, documented skin thinning, documented steroid allergy, and (for the eyelids) cataracts, glaucoma, or raised eye pressure.
- **Options:** pimecrolimus 1% cream (mild), approved from age 3 months; tacrolimus 0.1% ointment (potent), approved from age 16 years.
- **Side effects:**
  - Temporary burning that improves with continued use but may return after a break. Keeping the tube in the fridge can reduce stinging. If burning does not settle, your doctor may start with one week of a mild steroid, then switch once the eczema has cleared or partly cleared.
  - Reactivation of herpes simplex. They should not be used on skin with a bacterial or viral infection.
  - Alcohol intolerance (facial flushing, skin irritation).

### Infected eczema

- Your doctor may take bacterial swabs. These help guide antibiotic choice if MRSA (a resistant staph bacterium) is suspected.
- Prompt oral antibiotics cover *Streptococcus pyogenes* and *Staphylococcus aureus*. Your doctor may consider:
  - Flucloxacillin 1 g four times daily for 5 days.
  - If flucloxacillin is not tolerated, or with a mild penicillin allergy (e.g. rash): cefalexin 1 g three times daily for 5 days.
  - With a severe penicillin allergy (e.g. immediate anaphylaxis): doxycycline 200 mg once daily for 5 days, or co-trimoxazole 960 mg twice daily for 5 days, or erythromycin 800 mg twice daily or 400 mg four times daily for 5 days.
- Long antibiotic courses are avoided in eczema. There is little evidence of benefit in most cases and a higher risk of resistance.
- MRSA risk factors include frequent antibiotic courses, infection not responding to usual antibiotics, family history of MRSA, time in a high-risk healthcare setting (e.g. a North Island or overseas hospital), crowded living conditions, and open or broken skin. With known recent MRSA, treatment follows known sensitivities. If there is no improvement after 24 to 72 hours, the severe penicillin allergy options are used.
- If there are signs of cellulitis, it is managed as cellulitis.

### Mild, localised eczema herpeticum

- Your doctor may take a viral swab from the base of a fresh blister.
- Prompt oral antiviral treatment is given to prevent hospital admission. Staying well hydrated is advised.
- Without kidney impairment, options include valaciclovir 1 g three times a day for 7 to 10 days, or aciclovir 800 mg five times a day for 7 days if a dispersible form is needed. If immunocompromised, valaciclovir is given for at least 7 days, and either drug continues for 2 days after crusting of lesions.
- Kidney function is checked first. Aciclovir (also the active form of valaciclovir) is 80% eliminated by renal clearance, and build-up can harm the nervous system. The dose may need adjusting if creatinine clearance is less than 30 mL/minute. Signs of this toxicity include lethargy, confusion, agitation, hallucinations, seizures, and coma.

### Oral corticosteroids

Oral steroids are not used except in very severe cases or emergencies (e.g. sitting an exam). Then a short course of prednisone may start at 0.5 mg/kg once daily and taper over 1 to 2 weeks. Topical steroids and emollients continue: oral prednisone is not a substitute for good topical treatment. A course is not repeated within 6 months without dermatologist advice.

### If treatment is not working

Your doctor may:
- Check how often and how much moisturiser you use.
- Check that the steroid prescribed, and the amount applied, were the right potency and quantity.
- Consider other diagnoses (scabies, psoriasis, fungal infection, drug reaction).

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you to a dermatologist if:
- Treatment fails despite maximal topical treatment and good adherence, and a large area of skin is involved.
- Further treatment is needed, e.g. phototherapy, immunomodulators, or immunosuppressants.
- Patch testing is needed because allergic contact dermatitis is possible.
- The diagnosis is uncertain.

A referral usually includes clear photos showing the sites and extent of eczema, details of past treatments, and how eczema affects daily life.

Some people take specialist-started medicines such as methotrexate, azathioprine, ciclosporin, mycophenolate, or upadacitinib (a JAK inhibitor), with shared care between the specialist and GP. If occupational dermatitis is covered by ACC, your doctor may consider a private dermatology referral.

## Support

- Counselling and cognitive behavioural therapy may help with the psychological effects of atopic dermatitis.
- Extra support may help, especially for groups more likely to face barriers to care, such as Māori and Pacific peoples. This includes healthy homes support, financial support for access to appointments, and Māori and Pacific health services.

## Key Concepts for Patients

- Regular soap substitutes and emollients improve the skin barrier and prevent flares. Keep using emollients even when your skin looks good.
- Apply topical steroids generously once daily for the course your doctor sets. Too weak a steroid, or too little, is a common reason treatment fails.
- Food avoidance seldom cures eczema.
- Rapidly spreading painful sores, fever, or widespread redness need urgent care.
- Always discuss any new symptoms or medication questions with your doctor before making changes.
