# Angio-oedema

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of angio-oedema in adults and children.

---

## Red Flag — Seek Emergency Care

If there are signs of **anaphylaxis** (airway involvement, systemic compromise), this is a medical emergency requiring immediate treatment. Call an ambulance.

---

## Background

Angio-oedema:
- Presents as non-pitting, subdermal swelling that typically affects the face, lips, and tongue.
- Can affect mucous membranes causing laryngeal obstruction or gastrointestinal symptoms.
- May be associated with urticaria (hives).
- Is idiopathic in most cases.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. **Check for anaphylaxis** — look for airway involvement or systemic signs.

2. **Take a careful history** for urticaria and potential triggers.
   - Urticarial crops are usually intensely itchy and/or burning.
   - Angio-oedema may be allergic (IgE-mediated) or non-allergic.

   **Allergic (IgE-mediated):**
   - Usually occurs within 30–60 minutes of a specific trigger.
   - Common triggers: seafood, peanuts, tree nuts; medications such as penicillins; insect venom or stings; latex.
   - Oral allergy syndrome can cause similar intra-oral symptoms after eating fresh fruit or vegetables, but is restricted to the oral cavity.

   **Non-allergic causes include:**
   - Medications (via toxicity, immune complex, or T-cell mediated reactions)
   - Hereditary angio-oedema (HAE)
   - Acquired angio-oedema (AAE)
   - Idiopathic or autoimmune

3. **Physical examination:**
   - Check for non-pitting swelling. If swelling does not resolve after several days, other causes should be considered.
   - Assess blood pressure, pulse, respiratory rate; check for hoarse voice or stridor.
   - Look for signs of hypothyroidism (dry skin, hair thinning, nail ridging).
   - Look for urticaria — raised, erythematous, intensely itchy plaques.
   - Haematological examination, especially in older patients.

4. **Classify by probable trigger:**

   - **Drug-induced** — especially ACE inhibitors, aspirin, NSAIDs, DPP-4 inhibitors.
     - ACE inhibitor-induced angio-oedema can occur at any stage of treatment, even after years of use. It is sporadic (does not occur with every dose). Episodes can occur up to 3 months after stopping the drug.

   - **Idiopathic** — the most common cause. Defined as 3 or more episodes over 6–12 months with no cause found despite thorough evaluation.

   - **Hereditary (HAE):**
     - Rare genetic condition with reduced C1 esterase inhibitor levels or function.
     - Recurrent attacks affecting any body site including airway and gut.
     - Rarely associated with urticaria.
     - Usually presents in childhood — first presentation after age 30 is uncommon.
     - Autosomal dominant — check family history.

   - **Acquired (AAE):**
     - Low C1 esterase inhibitor levels or activity.
     - Usually associated with a haematological malignancy or autoimmune disease (e.g. lupus).
     - Rarely associated with urticaria.

5. **Investigations** — your doctor will arrange FBC, biochemistry, and CRP. IgE testing is generally not helpful unless history suggests a specific allergic trigger.

---

## Management

Your doctor will manage angio-oedema as follows:

1. **If anaphylaxis is suspected** or angio-oedema is accompanied by urticaria, the Anaphylaxis pathway is followed.

2. **Without anaphylaxis or airway involvement:**
   - Close monitoring in general practice or an observation unit until improving.
   - Antihistamines for 5 days:
     - Adults: cetirizine or loratadine 10–20 mg twice a day
     - Children: cetirizine or loratadine — dose per NZ Formulary for Children

3. **If severe and persisting** after several hours of observation or 2 days of antihistamines, corticosteroids are added:
   - Adults: prednisone 20–40 mg/day for 2 days
   - Children: prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg (max 40 mg) once daily for 3–5 days

4. **Trigger avoidance** — discuss and advise about identifying and avoiding known triggers.

5. **Frequent attacks** (2 or more per month): regular antihistamines are prescribed, sometimes at higher doses (up to 20 mg twice daily of cetirizine or loratadine). Note: cetirizine causes sedation in about 10% of people. Mild, well-controlled attacks do not require specialist referral.

6. **NSAID- or aspirin-induced:** avoid all NSAIDs and aspirin (cross-reaction risk). A low-dose COX-2 inhibitor may be considered.

7. **ACE inhibitor caution:** angio-oedema is rare with angiotensin receptor blockers (ARBs), but your doctor will review the indications for ACE blockade if relevant.

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will refer to a specialist if:
- Angio-oedema is **not controlled** by first-line treatment (antihistamines ± corticosteroids)
- **Hereditary or acquired angio-oedema** is suspected (recurrent attacks, no urticaria, family history, or young onset)

Referral pathways:
- Adults: immunology assessment
- Children: paediatric medicine assessment
