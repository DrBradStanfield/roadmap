# Intrauterine Contraception

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: February 2026.*

Intrauterine contraception (IUC) is a form of long-acting reversible contraception (LARC). LARCs (intrauterine contraception and the contraceptive implant) are recommended in New Zealand as a first-line contraceptive choice for all eligible patients because of their excellent efficacy and safety.

> **Always discuss your contraception choices and any new symptoms with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department if you have an IUD, you may be pregnant, and you have symptoms that could mean an ectopic pregnancy:**
- Abdominal or pelvic pain.
- Vaginal bleeding, with or without clots.
- Dizziness, fainting, or collapse.
- Shoulder tip pain.

Pain can come and go and settle for several hours or days. Pain that comes and goes does not rule out an ectopic pregnancy.

If pregnancy happens with an IUD in place, the chance that it is ectopic is about 6% with a copper IUD and 50% with a levonorgestrel intrauterine system (LNG-IUS).

**See your doctor promptly if:**
- You think you may be pregnant with an IUD in place, even without symptoms. Your doctor may arrange a same or next day ultrasound scan to rule out an ectopic pregnancy.
- You have fever, pain, or a new vaginal discharge, which may be a pelvic infection.
- You notice signs that your IUD may have moved or come out (see below).

---

## Background

- An IUD can be used at all ages, including by young people. Being younger than 25 years or never having given birth is not a reason to avoid one.
- It can also be used by people with a high BMI or polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS).
- Types available in New Zealand:
  - **Copper IUDs (Cu-IUDs):** hormone-free. Current devices last 5 years. May be used for both long-term and emergency contraception.
  - **52 mg levonorgestrel IUD (Mirena):** for long-term contraception, heavy menstrual bleeding, and as the progestogen part of menopause hormone therapy (MHT).
  - **13.5 mg levonorgestrel IUD (Jaydess):** a lower dose device for contraception.

---

## Choosing an IUD

### All IUDs

- Efficacy is greater than 99%.
- They are a "fit and forget" option.
- The devices are funded on prescription. There is a cost for fitting, and funding may be available for some patients.
- After removal, the return to fertility is similar to stopping oral contraceptives or barrier methods.
- They must be inserted and removed by an appropriately trained clinician.
- IUDs do not protect against sexually transmitted infections (STIs).

### 52 mg LNG-IUS (hormonal)

- Efficacy is 99.8% at 1 year, and 99.3% at 5 years and 8 years.
- Works by thickening cervical mucus and thinning the womb lining (endometrial atrophy), which prevents implantation. Most users continue to ovulate.
- Duration of use is 8 years. If inserted when aged more than 45 years and not needed for MHT, it can be used for up to 8 years, or until menopause if periods have stopped.
- Periods: 20% of users have no periods after 1 year. At 5 years, 30 to 40% have no periods with a first device, and more with later devices.
- For womb lining protection as part of MHT: may be used for up to 5 years.
- For heavy menstrual bleeding: may be used for 5 years, or longer if symptoms have not returned (maximum 8 years).
- May be associated with a reduced risk of endometrial cancer. Your doctor may discuss this if you have risk factors, such as being aged 45 years or older, or being younger than 35 years with long-term lack of ovulation (including PCOS), obesity, tamoxifen use, never having given birth, or a strong family history of related cancers.
- May reduce the pain of primary dysmenorrhoea (period pain), endometriosis, and adenomyosis.

### 13.5 mg LNG-IUS (hormonal, lower dose)

- Efficacy is 99.6% at 1 year and 99.1% at 3 years.
- Duration of use is 3 years.
- Slimmer and smaller than the 52 mg LNG-IUS, so may be easier to insert in people who have never given birth.
- May make periods significantly lighter, but has a lower rate of periods stopping than the 52 mg LNG-IUS.
- Works the same way as the 52 mg device. Most users continue to ovulate.

### Copper IUD (non-hormonal)

- Efficacy is 99% to 99.9%, depending on the device. The most effective are T-shaped devices with at least 380 mm2 copper and copper bands on the arms.
- All currently available devices last 5 years.
- If inserted after age 40 years, a copper IUD with 300 mm2 or more of copper can stay in until:
  - 1 year after the last period, if this happens after age 50 years.
  - 2 years after the last period, if this happens before age 50 years.
- Works by stopping sperm moving, reducing egg survival, and preventing implantation.
- Effective immediately. Can be used for emergency contraception.
- Can be used when hormonal contraception is not suitable, for example after breast cancer.
- May lead to heavier periods and more period pain. This is more common in the first 3 to 6 months and usually improves with time.
- May not be the best option for people who get recurrent bacterial vaginosis or thrush with a copper IUD in place.

## Possible Side Effects and Risks

### All IUDs

- **Pregnancy** is uncommon (1% or less). If it happens, there is a higher chance it is ectopic, and the IUD will need to be removed urgently. Pregnancy with an IUD in place increases the risk of second trimester miscarriage, preterm delivery, and infection.
- **Pelvic infection:** a small risk in the first 20 days after insertion. After 20 days the risk is no higher than usual. Infection occurs in about 1 in 300 patients.
- **Expulsion (the IUD coming out):** a small risk (5%). It is most common in the first year, especially the first 3 months, and if inserted within 48 hours of birth. It may be more likely in people who have expelled an IUD before, teenagers, people with fibroids or a distorted uterus and heavy bleeding, menstrual cup users, and people with a BMI greater than 25.
- **Displacement or migration** of the device.
- **Perforation of the uterus** during insertion: about 1.4 per 1000 insertions. The risk is 6 times higher when breastfeeding and 3 times higher within 36 weeks after birth, but the absolute risk remains low. Perforation may cause no symptoms, but a misplaced IUD needs surgical removal.
- **Fainting (vasovagal) response** at insertion. Some people may need sedation, for example with a history of fainting during cervical procedures, or anxiety.
- **Partner discomfort during sex** from the threads.

### Copper IUD

- Heavier bleeding and period pain, more common in the first 3 to 6 months and usually improving with time.

### LNG-IUS

- **Bleeding changes:** frequent bleeding or spotting is common in the early months and usually improves. Most people have lighter periods on either device. The 13.5 mg device gives slightly less reduction in bleeding than the 52 mg device.
- **Hormonal side effects** such as acne, breast tenderness or pain, and headache. These often settle with time.
- **Breast cancer:** there may be a small increase in risk in pre-menopausal users. The absolute risk remains small, and there is no evidence of a causal link. Non-hormonal methods are most appropriate for people with a history of breast cancer.

### Other common concerns

- There is no evidence that IUDs affect libido, or that the LNG-IUS causes weight gain.
- There is little or no increased risk of blood clots (venous thromboembolism) or heart attack with an LNG-IUS.
- Routine antibiotics are not needed for insertion, even if you are at risk of infective endocarditis.

## Assessment

**Your doctor may:**
- Discuss all your contraception options before offering an IUD.
- Ask whether you need the IUD for emergency contraception, about any abnormal bleeding, and whether a current IUD has expired.
- Check for reasons an IUD may not be suitable (see below).
- Check your weight and blood pressure.
- Do a speculum examination if you have abnormal bleeding or symptoms of an STI, and an internal (bimanual) examination if needed.
- Arrange cervical screening if due. This can be done at the IUD consultation or at the fitting appointment.
- Do a pregnancy test if needed.
- Offer STI screening if you are at higher risk of STIs or pelvic inflammatory disease (PID). Higher risk includes being younger than 30 years, a new sexual partner in the last 3 months, an STI in the past 12 months, a partner with an STI, genital symptoms, recent procedures inside the uterus, or recent pregnancy. There is no need to screen for bacterial vaginosis if you have no symptoms.

### When an IUD may not be suitable

**An IUD should not be used with:**
- Known or suspected pregnancy.
- Unexplained vaginal bleeding.
- Current PID.
- Current chlamydia, gonorrhoea, or *Mycoplasma genitalium* infection with symptoms or signs.
- Sepsis after childbirth or abortion.
- Gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD) with persistently raised hCG, or malignant disease.
- Cervical cancer awaiting treatment, or endometrial cancer.
- Pelvic tuberculosis known before insertion.
- For an LNG-IUS: current treatment for breast cancer.
- For a copper IUD: allergy to copper or device parts, Wilson's disease, or medical diathermy.

**Your doctor may seek specialist advice first with:**
- 48 hours to up to 4 weeks after birth.
- A distorted uterine cavity, for example fibroids or a uterine abnormality present from birth.
- Current chlamydia, gonorrhoea, or *M. genitalium* infection without symptoms or signs.
- A complicated organ transplant (for example graft failure or rejection).
- HIV infection when clinically unwell and not on treatment.
- Long QT syndrome.
- GTD with falling hCG levels.
- Cervical cancer treated by radical trachelectomy.
- Pelvic tuberculosis developing after insertion.
- For an LNG-IUS: current or previous heart disease or stroke, completed breast cancer treatment, decompensated cirrhosis, or liver tumours (hepatocellular adenoma or carcinoma).
- Significant heart disease (for example single ventricle circulation, Eisenmenger physiology, tachycardia, or pre-existing bradycardia). Cardiology advice is sought before insertion, and the IUD may be fitted in hospital if fainting would be especially risky.
- The presence of *Actinomyces*-like organisms (ALOs), with significant pelvic symptoms.

### Ruling out pregnancy before insertion

You are unlikely to be pregnant if you have no symptoms or signs of pregnancy and any of these apply:
- Within 5 days of the start of a normal period.
- Within 5 days of an abortion, miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, or uterine evacuation for GTD.
- No sex since your last normal period, since giving birth, or since an abortion, miscarriage, or ectopic pregnancy.
- Consistently and correctly using a reliable method of contraception. Condoms have a high failure rate (18%), so may not count when starting a new method.
- Less than 21 days after giving birth.
- Fully breastfeeding, with no periods, and less than 6 months after giving birth.
- No sex for more than 21 days and a negative urine pregnancy test. A test can be falsely negative if you had unprotected sex in the last 21 days.

## Getting an IUD Fitted

### Before insertion

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Treating bacterial vaginosis before or at insertion if it causes symptoms. Bacterial vaginosis without symptoms does not need treatment.
- Managing chlamydia, gonorrhoea, or PID before insertion.
- If STI results are not back and you have no symptoms, there is no need to wait or take antibiotics. If you have symptoms, preventive antibiotics may be given: azithromycin 1 g by mouth for chlamydia as a minimum, plus ceftriaxone 500 mg by injection if gonorrhoea risk is very high.
- If you use testosterone, a short course of vaginal estrogen to make insertion more comfortable.
- If you are waiting for treatment of high-grade cervical changes, an IUD can stay in. The treating specialist may remove it if needed.
- Insertion under general anaesthetic may be appropriate with severe anxiety or mental health concerns, previous trauma or abuse, or for a child. Your doctor may offer another method or refer to gynaecology.

**To prepare:**
- If you need a prescription, take it to the pharmacy at least a few days before your appointment, as pharmacies may need to order an LNG-IUS. Bring the device to your fitting.
- Your doctor may suggest paracetamol 1 g or ibuprofen 400 mg one hour before insertion, if these are safe for you, to reduce cramping.
- You may need someone to drive you home.
- If you are replacing an IUD, avoid sex or use condoms for at least 7 days before the procedure, in case the new device cannot be inserted.

### Timing for an LNG-IUS

- Ideally inserted between days 1 and 7 of your cycle. It is then effective immediately.
- After day 7, it can be inserted at any time if pregnancy is reasonably ruled out. Use extra contraception for 7 days, and do a pregnancy test at least 21 days after the last unprotected sex.
- Replacement: the 52 mg device is effective for 8 years (replace after 5 years if used for MHT). The 13.5 mg device is effective for 3 years. If a device is past this, pregnancy is ruled out before a new one goes in.
- After birth: it can go in between 10 minutes and 48 hours after a vaginal or Caesarean birth, and is effective immediately. Expulsion is more likely, but so is continued use. Otherwise it can go in from 28 days after birth, and becomes effective 7 days after insertion. Use extra contraception for 7 days.
- After miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, abortion, or GTD treatment: if inserted within 5 days, it is effective immediately. If inserted 5 days or more after, it becomes effective after 7 days, pregnancy is ruled out first, and extra contraception is used for 7 days. After GTD, the hCG level must be normal, or your doctor will seek gynaecology advice.
- If the uterus is still soft and not fully recovered after birth, insertion may be delayed because of a higher risk of perforation.

### Timing for a copper IUD

- A copper IUD is effective immediately.
- Pregnancy does not need to be ruled out if it is inserted between days 1 and 12 of a normal cycle, or up to 5 days after stopping another method used consistently and correctly. At other times, pregnancy is ruled out first.
- As emergency contraception, it can be inserted up to 5 days after the first unprotected sex in the current cycle, or up to 5 days after the earliest likely day of ovulation, whichever is later. The earliest likely day of ovulation is your cycle length minus 14 days (for example, day 14 of a 28-day cycle).
- If switching from an LNG-IUS, ideally avoid sex or use condoms for the 7 days before the fitting.
- After birth: within 48 hours, or from 28 days after birth.
- After an abortion within the last 5 days, it can go in any time after a medical abortion, or immediately after a surgical abortion. After miscarriage or ectopic pregnancy, it can go in any time if there is no sign of sepsis (serious infection). After GTD treatment, the hCG level must be normal, or your doctor will seek gynaecology advice. If inserted 5 days or more after any of these, pregnancy is ruled out first.

### After insertion

- Your doctor may consider a check 3 to 6 weeks after insertion, especially if the IUD was inserted within 48 hours of birth. This checks you are happy with the device and rules out infection, perforation, and expulsion.
- If inserted straight after birth, the threads are checked and shortened 4 to 6 weeks later.
- No further routine follow-up is needed.

## Managing Problems

### Bleeding

Not all bleeding is caused by the device. Your doctor may first rule out pregnancy, an STI, and problems of the cervix or uterus.

**Heavy bleeding with a copper IUD.** Your doctor may suggest an anti-inflammatory (NSAID), started just before or at the start of your period and taken regularly for the first 3 to 4 days:
- Ibuprofen 400 mg two to three times a day, or mefenamic acid 500 mg three times a day (not funded, and no more effective than other NSAIDs).
- And/or tranexamic acid up to 1 g three to four times a day, for up to four days during the period.

**Irregular bleeding with an LNG-IUS.** Light, irregular bleeding is common in the first 3 to 6 months and usually improves. The position of the device and other causes are also considered. Your doctor may consider:
- Up to 3 months of the combined oral contraceptive pill, if suitable. Expect a withdrawal bleed when you stop it.
- If the pill is not suitable: an NSAID such as ibuprofen 400 mg two to three times a day, or medroxyprogesterone acetate 10 mg daily for three out of four weeks. The dose may be increased. If bleeding is not controlled on 30 mg, other causes and options are considered.
- If bleeding persists and you want to keep the device, advice from a gynaecologist, who may suggest a pelvic ultrasound.

### Signs the IUD may have moved or come out

Expulsion happens in about 1 in 20 people. It is most common in the first year, especially within 3 months of fitting, and slightly more likely after fitting following birth or abortion. See your doctor if you:
- Cannot feel the threads, or they feel shorter or longer than usual.
- Can feel the plastic stem of the device.
- Have pain, cramping, or discomfort that is getting worse or not helped by pain relief.
- Have abnormal bleeding.
- Have signs of infection, such as fever or new vaginal discharge.
- Have pain during sex.
- Find the expelled device.

If you suspect expulsion, avoid sex or use condoms until the position is confirmed. Emergency contraception is available if needed.

### Lost threads

- Your doctor will first check whether you could be pregnant. If you are, they will seek gynaecology advice and will not try to remove the IUD.
- If you are not pregnant, your doctor may look for the threads in the cervical canal with a small instrument.
- If the threads cannot be found, you will be offered other contraception and a pelvic ultrasound:
  - If the IUD is correctly placed, it can stay until it is due to come out.
  - If it is not in the uterus, an abdominal X-ray checks whether it is in the abdomen. If so, it is removed by keyhole surgery (laparoscopy), and you use other contraception until then.
  - If it is not found on ultrasound or X-ray, it came out without being noticed.

### Low or poorly placed IUD

- A low IUD usually causes symptoms (abnormal bleeding, cramping, or pain during sex), with the stem visible or felt in the cervical canal, or threads much longer than at insertion. A scan is not usually needed. The device is removed, and your doctor may offer a pregnancy test, emergency contraception if appropriate, and discuss ongoing contraception.
- Evidence on IUDs that sit below the top of the uterus is limited. Their protection cannot be guaranteed, especially if the device is more than 2 cm from the top on ultrasound. Whether to remove and replace it is an individual decision with your doctor.

### Suspected pelvic infection (PID)

- The risk is slightly higher in the first 20 days after insertion, but PID can happen at any time.
- Symptoms may include fever, feeling unwell, pain, or discharge.
- In most cases the infection is treated and the IUD stays in. Your doctor may review your response 48 to 72 hours after treatment.
- If the infection is severe, not improving at 72 hours, or you ask, removal may be considered. If the IUD is removed, your doctor may discuss emergency contraception and other contraception.

### Pregnancy with an IUD in place

- Pregnancy with an IUD in place increases risks for the mother and baby, including infection (chorioamnionitis), miscarriage (about 50%), bleeding in pregnancy (antepartum haemorrhage), preterm birth, and placental abruption.
- An ultrasound is done to rule out an ectopic pregnancy. If ectopic pregnancy is confirmed, the IUD is not removed and you are sent to hospital by ambulance.
- If the pregnancy is in the uterus, less than 12 weeks, and the threads are visible: removing the IUD may improve the outcome. It slightly reduces the risks of preterm birth and infection, and lowers the miscarriage risk to about 20%. Removal is advised, with a shared decision.
- If the pregnancy is 12 weeks or more, the threads cannot be seen, or an ultrasound cannot be done in time, your doctor will seek gynaecology advice immediately and will not try to remove the IUD.

### Expired or unwanted IUD

- An IUD that has expired, or is not tolerated despite treatment of symptoms, can be removed by a trained clinician. If you do not want to become pregnant, the timing of removal is planned, and other contraception is discussed.

### *Actinomyces*-like organisms (ALOs)

- These are part of normal vaginal flora but are rarely found without an IUD. If you have significant pelvic symptoms, your doctor will seek infectious diseases advice.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may seek advice or refer you if:
- **Hospital (emergency):** you are pregnant with an IUD in place and clinically unstable, or an ectopic pregnancy is suspected or confirmed.
- **Gynaecology advice immediately:** you are pregnant with an IUD in place and the pregnancy is more than 12 weeks, or the threads are missing.
- **Gynaecology advice:** insertion cannot wait for STI results, you may have an STI, and you are at risk of infective endocarditis; bleeding with an LNG-IUS persists and you want to keep it; or hCG is not normal after GTD.
- **Non-acute gynaecology assessment:** insertion under general anaesthetic is needed; the device has perforated and lies outside the uterus; or threads cannot be found when removal is wanted.
- **Cardiology advice:** you have significant heart disease.
- **Infectious diseases advice:** ALOs are found and you have significant pelvic symptoms.
- **Relevant specialist:** you have a condition that makes an IUD less suitable.

---

## Key Concepts for Patients

- IUDs have an efficacy greater than 99% and are a "fit and forget" option.
- Depending on type, an IUD lasts 3 years (13.5 mg LNG-IUS), 5 years (copper) or 8 years (52 mg LNG-IUS).
- Young age and never having given birth are not reasons to avoid an IUD.
- A copper IUD can also be used as emergency contraception.
- IUDs do not protect against STIs.
- If you might be pregnant with an IUD in place, see your doctor promptly. With pain, bleeding, or fainting, call 111 or go to the emergency department.
