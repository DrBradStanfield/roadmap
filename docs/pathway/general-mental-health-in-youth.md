# General Mental Health in Youth

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to mental health concerns in children and young people — recognising indicators, assessing severity, and understanding the management options your doctor may consider.

---

## Background

- Many mental health disorders — including depression, anxiety, emotional dysregulation, and eating disorders — have their onset in youth.
- Early-onset depression (ages 14–16 years) is associated with increased risk of educational underachievement, unemployment, early parenthood, and later major depression and anxiety disorders.
- Vulnerability to mental health disorders increases when other stressors are present, such as bullying, school difficulties, and family breakdown.
- It can be hard to differentiate between the normal ups and downs of adolescence and a developing mental illness.

Indicators that a mental health problem may be present include:

- Persistent anger, worry, or unhappiness
- Disruptive behaviour, suicidal thoughts, or drug use
- Decline in school achievement or school avoidance
- Becoming unenrolled from school (often a marker of significant distress, neglect, or social chaos)

---

## Warning Signs — Speak to Your Doctor Promptly

Seek an urgent appointment if a young person shows:

- Suicidal thoughts, self-harm, or talk of hopelessness
- Significant behavioural change over a short period
- Refusal to eat, severe weight loss, or purging behaviour
- Alcohol or drug use that is escalating or causing harm
- Complete withdrawal from family, friends, and school

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will usually assess mental health concerns over several appointments and will often involve your family, as family understanding is frequently crucial to resolving problems.

Your doctor may use a structured approach called the **HEEADSSS assessment** to ask about psychosocial well-being, covering home, education, eating, activities, drugs, sexuality, safety, and suicide/self-harm.

If specific mental health concerns arise, your doctor may use validated assessment tools such as:

- **GAD-7** (Generalised Anxiety Disorder Assessment) — for older adolescents with anxiety symptoms
- **K10** (Kessler Psychological Distress Scale) — for older adolescents to measure general distress
- **PHQ-A** (Patient Health Questionnaire — Adolescent version) — to assess depression symptoms
- **SACS** (Substance Use and Choices Scale) — developed and validated in New Zealand for young people to assess substance use and its impact

Your doctor will also consider whether any child protection concerns need to be reported.

---

## Management

Management depends on the nature and severity of concerns. Your doctor may consider:

- **Mild to moderate symptoms:** Referral for counselling or therapy, including:
  - Individual or family therapy
  - E-therapy (online therapeutic programmes)
  - Youthline — provides counselling and transition-to-independence support
- **School-based support:** Referral to school pastoral care and health teams, which can include nurses and visiting psychologists
- **Alcohol or drug concerns:** Referral to youth alcohol and drug support services
- **Gender diversity concerns:** Referral to specialised gender health services if this is a primary or significant concern
- **Suicidal ideation:** Assessment and management via the suicide prevention pathway — your doctor will assess safety carefully
- **Complex mental health concerns:**
  - If aged under 18: referral for non-acute child and youth mental health assessment
  - If aged 18 or over: referral for non-acute adult mental health assessment

All treatment decisions should be made with your doctor, who will determine the most appropriate pathway based on the young person's individual needs, age, and circumstances.
