# ADHD in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of ADHD in adults aged 18 years and older. ADHD diagnosis and treatment is complex — always discuss with your doctor before starting or changing any medication.

---

## Background

- Attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) is a complex neurodevelopmental disorder strongly influenced by genetic factors, associated with psychosocial risk factors, and can significantly affect social, academic, and self-care functioning.
- The main characteristics are inattention, hyperactivity, and/or impulsivity that are excessive, long-term, and occur across multiple settings. There are three subtypes: **inattentive**, **hyperactive/impulsive**, and **combined**.
- Prevalence in adults is 3–5%. ADHD tends to run in families. Adults often present for assessment after their child is diagnosed.
- 50% of those diagnosed in childhood continue to have significant symptoms in adult life. Inattention is more likely to persist than hyperactivity.
- The diagnosis of adult ADHD requires that some symptoms were present before the age of 12 years.
- In adulthood, ADHD is commonly associated with anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, other neurodevelopmental conditions, and substance use disorders.
- Untreated adult ADHD is associated with substance misuse, occupational difficulties, relationship problems, increased accident risk, and justice system involvement.
- ADHD is associated with a 5–10 times increased risk of suicide attempts and self-injurious behaviour, and a 2 times increase in all-cause mortality compared to the general population. These risks normalise with treatment.

## When to Seek Urgent Help

Seek emergency care or contact your doctor urgently if you experience:

- Thoughts of suicide or self-harm
- A new or worsening episode of psychosis or mania
- Chest pain, palpitations, fainting, or significant shortness of breath on exertion (especially if on stimulant medication)
- Sustained resting heart rate above 120 beats per minute while on stimulant medication

## Assessment

Your doctor will need to carry out a comprehensive, culturally sensitive assessment. This process takes significant time and requires specialist competence — many GPs will refer to a psychiatrist for diagnosis if they are not specifically trained in ADHD.

### History

Your doctor will:

- Take a detailed history of your symptoms, including when they started and how they affect your daily life
- Ask about **inattention symptoms**, such as:
  - Difficulty sustaining attention in tasks, conversations, or reading
  - Not following through on instructions or finishing tasks
  - Problems with organisation, time management, or meeting deadlines
  - Being easily distracted by unrelated thoughts or stimuli
  - Losing things necessary for tasks (keys, phone, paperwork)
  - Forgetfulness in daily activities
- Ask about **hyperactivity and impulsivity symptoms**, such as:
  - Fidgeting or restlessness
  - Difficulty staying seated for any length of time
  - Talking non-stop or interrupting others
  - Blurting out answers before questions are finished
  - Self-injurious or risk-taking behaviours
- Ask when symptoms first appeared (some symptoms must have been present before age 12)
- Ask how symptoms affect your functioning at home, work, and in relationships
- Screen for other mental health conditions (anxiety, depression, bipolar disorder, psychosis, substance use)

### Structured Assessment Tools

Your doctor may use standardised tools including:

- **Adult ADHD Self Report Scale (ASRS-V1.1)** — to assess current symptom severity
- **Wender Utah Rating Scale (WURS-25)** — to assess childhood symptoms
- **Diagnostic Interview for ADHD (DIVA)** — a structured clinical interview

Rating scales are helpful but should not be used as the sole basis for diagnosis.

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will typically:

- Record your baseline heart rate, blood pressure, and weight
- Look for tics
- Note any signs of deliberate self-harm

### Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- Blood tests to exclude medical causes (e.g., liver function tests, thyroid function)
- ECG, if there is a family history of serious cardiac disease, history of sudden death in young family members, abnormal cardiovascular findings, or age over 50 years
- Urine drug screen if there is any history of substance misuse

### Diagnostic Criteria

Your doctor may consider a diagnosis of ADHD if you have:

- 5 or more inattention symptoms **and/or** 5 or more hyperactivity-impulsivity symptoms, **and**
- Symptoms that are pervasive across multiple settings and have caused significant impairment in social, academic, or occupational functioning for at least 6 months, **and**
- Some symptoms or impairment were present before age 12 years, **and**
- Symptoms are not better explained by another disorder

### Differential Diagnoses

Conditions your doctor will consider and rule out include:

- Substance misuse or abuse
- Moderate or severe anxiety or depression
- Bipolar disorder
- Autism spectrum, dyspraxia, or specific learning disorders
- Acquired brain injury
- Hearing or vision problems
- Hyperthyroidism
- Sleep apnoea

## Management

ADHD is managed primarily in the community and requires a comprehensive, collaborative, and multimodal approach.

### Lifestyle Interventions

Your doctor will discuss self-management strategies. Key approaches include:

- **Psychoeducation** — Understanding that ADHD has strengths as well as challenges is important. Books such as "Driven to Distraction" can be helpful.
- **Reduce distractions** — Work in a quiet space, use headphones
- **Increase structure** — Use to-do lists, schedules, calendar reminders
- **Healthy diet and regular exercise**
- **Regular sleep routines**
- **Avoid self-medicating** — Including alcohol and recreational drugs

### Psychosocial Management

Your doctor may consider referral for:

- Psychological therapy (cognitive behavioural therapy or similar)
- Social skills training
- Mindfulness
- Work rehabilitation and support

### Address Co-morbidities First

If you have severe anxiety, psychosis, severe depression, bipolar disorder, or a substance use disorder, your doctor will generally treat these conditions first before starting ADHD medication. Safety concerns such as suicidal ideation are always addressed as a priority.

### Medication — What Your Doctor May Consider

ADHD medication can improve focus, self-regulation, and reduce impulsivity and hyperactivity. Medication alone does not confirm a diagnosis, and regular reviews are needed to assess progress.

**Important:** Only a doctor with appropriate training should prescribe ADHD medications. Your doctor will first assess for contraindications before prescribing.

#### Absolute contraindications to stimulant medication include:
- Severe depression, anxiety, or agitation
- Suicidal ideation
- Hyperthyroidism
- Significant cardiovascular disease (cardiomyopathy, arrhythmias, severe hypertension, structural heart disease, cerebrovascular disease)
- Glaucoma
- Concomitant use of monoamine oxidase inhibitors (MAOIs)
- Acute psychosis or manic episode
- Severe hepatic impairment

#### Relative contraindications include:
- Epilepsy
- Tic disorders
- Recreational stimulant use (methamphetamine, MDMA, cocaine)

### Stimulant Medications (First-line)

Stimulant medicines can cause a modest increase in blood pressure (around 2–4 mmHg) and heart rate (around 3–6 beats per minute). Your doctor will monitor these during treatment.

#### Methylphenidate (MPH)

Methylphenidate is typically first-line. Your doctor may consider:

- **Immediate release** (Rubifen, Ritalin): Onset within 30–60 minutes, lasts 4 hours, given 2–3 times daily. Effective dose 0.3–1 mg/kg/day, maximum 60 mg/day.
- **Sustained release** (Rubifen SR): Onset within 1 hour, lasts 4–8 hours, given once or twice daily, maximum 80 mg/day.
- **Extended release** (methylphenidate extended release / Ritalin LA): Once daily in the morning, releases over 12 hours.
- **Modified release / osmotic-controlled** (Concerta, MPH Sandoz XR): Once daily in the morning, preferred if diversion or abuse is a concern.

**Starting dose:** Typically 5–10 mg in the morning, increased by 10–20 mg weekly until a therapeutic dose is reached.

**Common side effects of methylphenidate:**
- Insomnia
- Agitation, nervousness, or irritability
- Headache, tremor, dizziness
- Reduced appetite or weight loss
- Dry mouth

**Potentially serious side effects:**
- Tachycardia, palpitations, or hypertension
- Precipitation of mania or psychosis
- Seizures
- Sudden death (very rare)

Methylphenidate interacts with alcohol — alcohol increases methylphenidate levels and worsens central nervous system effects. Side effects are usually dose-dependent and may improve with a dose reduction.

#### Dexamfetamine

Dexamfetamine is an alternative stimulant, useful if methylphenidate fails or causes adverse effects.

- Starting dose: 5 mg in the morning, titrated in 5 mg steps
- Usual dose: 5–30 mg once or twice daily
- Onset of action is slower than methylphenidate
- Taken 1–3 times per day depending on duration of effect

#### Lisdexamfetamine (Vyvanse)

Lisdexamfetamine is a prodrug to dexamfetamine with extended duration of action.

- Starting dose: 30 mg in the morning, titrated in 20 mg steps per week
- Available in 30, 50, and 70 mg formulations
- Usual dose: 30–70 mg once daily

### Non-stimulant Medications (Second-line)

Your doctor may consider non-stimulant medications if stimulants are not tolerated or not appropriate:

#### Atomoxetine (Strattera)

- Second-line when there has been an inadequate response or serious adverse reaction to stimulants
- May be preferred as first-line in patients with a history of psychosis or first-degree relative with schizophrenia, or when medication abuse or diversion is a concern
- Usual dose: 40–100 mg once daily

#### Clonidine

- Occasionally used off-label, typically initiated by a specialist with ongoing prescribing by the GP once the dose is stabilised

---

### Monitoring on Medication

Your doctor will review you regularly on ADHD medication:

- **Initially:** At 2 and 4 weeks after starting, then every 3–6 months
- **At each review:** Weight, height, pulse, and blood pressure are measured
- **Blood pressure monitoring:** At every dose change and every 6 months
- **Annual:** Lipid check
- **If heart rate exceeds 120 bpm at rest, arrhythmia develops, or blood pressure rises significantly:** Dose reduction and specialist review are needed

Your doctor may also monitor for psychiatric effects (changes in mood, anger, or personality) and, if diversion or misuse is suspected, may arrange a random urine drug screen.

### Managing Common Problems on Medication

| Problem | What your doctor may consider |
|---------|-------------------------------|
| Insomnia | Adjust dose timing, change formulation, consider melatonin |
| Appetite loss / weight loss | Eat before taking medication, late snacks, calorie-dense foods; consider dose or formulation change |
| Irritability | May occur at peak effect or as medication wears off; dose or formulation adjustment |
| Rebound symptoms in afternoon | Small short-acting dose in the afternoon |
| Tics worsening | Monitor over 3 months; consider dose reduction |
| Inadequate response | Review adherence, consider incorrect diagnosis, coexisting conditions, or dose increase |

### Reviewing the Need for Ongoing Medication

Your doctor will review the ongoing need for stimulant medication every 2 years. A trial without medication may be considered after several years of stable improvement — ideally at a time when the patient is undertaking normal daily activities and not at times when impulse control is particularly important (e.g., driving, employment).

## When to See a Specialist

ADHD diagnosis requires specific competence. Your doctor may refer you to a psychiatrist if:

- The diagnosis is uncertain
- Symptoms are moderate to severe and significantly impairing functioning across multiple domains (education, work, relationships)
- You have not responded to several medication trials
- There are complex co-morbid mental health conditions
- There are concerns about medication management, tics, or diversion
