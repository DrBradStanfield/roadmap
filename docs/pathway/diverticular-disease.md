# Diverticular Disease

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of diverticular disease, including acute diverticulitis.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if you have:

- Severe abdominal pain with signs of peritonism (rigid abdomen, severe tenderness)
- Systemic features of sepsis (fever or very low temperature, fast heart rate, low blood pressure, confusion)
- Significant rectal bleeding

---

## Background

**Diverticulosis** is the presence of small pouches (diverticula) in the colon wall. It is uncommon under age 40 but affects about 50% of people by age 60 and 65% by age 85. Most involve the sigmoid colon (lower left colon). It is usually asymptomatic.

**Diverticulitis** is inflammation of one or more of these pouches. About 4–15% of people with diverticulosis will develop diverticulitis. After one episode, 20–40% of patients experience a recurrence.

- **Uncomplicated diverticulitis:** Inflammation without perforation, abscess, bleeding, fistula, peritonitis, or obstruction.
- **Complicated diverticulitis:** Involves perforation, peritonitis, sepsis, or an abscess larger than 5 cm.

**Risk factors:**
- Age over 60
- Low-fibre, high red-meat diet
- Obesity, lack of exercise, smoking
- Use of NSAIDs (anti-inflammatory pain medicines)
- Female sex on hormone replacement therapy
- Significant medical conditions or immunosuppression

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history and examine you. They will ask about:

**Symptoms to describe:**
- Location and character of abdominal pain (most commonly left lower quadrant, may be constant and last several days)
- Fever
- Changes in bowel habit — constipation, diarrhoea, or bleeding
- Nausea or vomiting
- Urinary symptoms: burning, frequency, urgency, air in urine (pneumaturia) — these can indicate a fistula between the bowel and bladder
- Vaginal discharge (can indicate a bowel-vaginal fistula)

**Your doctor will also ask about:**
- Previous diagnoses of diverticular disease, and how it was diagnosed
- Prior episodes of diverticulitis and whether antibiotics or hospital admission were required
- Previous complications (abscess, fistula, perforation, obstruction, or major bleeding)
- Other medical conditions, medications, and immune status

**Examination:** Your doctor will check vital signs, perform an abdominal examination looking for tenderness, a palpable mass, and signs of peritonism (rigidity, rebound tenderness, guarding). A rectal or pelvic examination may be performed if relevant.

**Investigations your doctor may arrange:**
- Blood tests: full blood count, ferritin, kidney function, liver function, CRP
- Urinalysis; pregnancy test for women of childbearing age
- Stool culture if diarrhoea is present
- Pelvic ultrasound if a gynaecological cause is being considered
- CT scan of the abdomen (if complications are suspected — your doctor will seek surgical advice before ordering)

**Possible other diagnoses your doctor will consider:**
- Bowel: colorectal cancer, appendicitis, gastroenteritis, inflammatory bowel disease, irritable bowel syndrome, bowel obstruction, pancreatitis
- Gynaecological: ectopic pregnancy, ovarian cyst or torsion, endometriosis, pelvic inflammatory disease
- Urological: kidney stones, pyelonephritis, urinary tract infection
- Vascular: aortic aneurysm, aortic dissection

## Management

### When Hospital Assessment Is Required

Your doctor will refer you to acute general surgery if you have:
- Any red flags (see above), or suspected abscess, obstruction, perforation, or fistula
- Need for intravenous antibiotics
- Immunosuppression or significant comorbidities (e.g. diabetes, severe liver or kidney disease, pregnancy, frailty)
- Pain requiring strong opioid pain relief (narcotics)
- Difficulty eating or drinking
- No support at home, or inability to seek help if symptoms worsen
- No improvement within 2–3 days of oral antibiotics
- Worsening symptoms at any point

### Community Management (Uncomplicated Diverticulitis)

For patients with uncomplicated diverticulitis who do not require hospital, your doctor may consider:

**Diet:**
- You can eat and drink normally — there is no need for a liquid-only diet.

**Pain relief:**
- Your doctor may consider paracetamol as first-line treatment; tramadol may be added if stronger relief is needed.
- NSAIDs (e.g. ibuprofen) may be considered if there are no contraindications such as a history of gastrointestinal bleeding or ulcers, or possible dehydration. Use with caution.
- Codeine and stronger opioids are generally avoided due to the risk of constipation.

**Antibiotics:**
- Antibiotics are **not routinely recommended** for most patients with acute uncomplicated diverticulitis.
- Your doctor may consider oral antibiotics if you are systemically unwell (but not requiring hospital), symptoms persist or worsen after 48 hours, you have immune problems or significant comorbidities, or right-sided diverticulitis is suspected.
- If prescribed, options your doctor may consider include:
  - **First line:** Amoxicillin + clavulanic acid (Augmentin) 625 mg three times daily for 5 days
  - **Second line:** Cefalexin 1 g three times daily + metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 5 days, OR trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole 960 mg twice daily + metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 5 days

### Follow-Up After Recovery

Once the acute episode has settled, your doctor will arrange follow-up and may:

**Advise on diet and bowel health:**
- A high-fibre diet is usually recommended, introduced gradually to reduce bloating and flatulence.
- You do not need to avoid seeds, nuts, or corn.
- Maintain good hydration and keep stools soft. Bulk-forming laxatives may be considered if you have constipation and cannot increase dietary fibre.
- Weight loss, more physical activity, and smoking cessation may be recommended if relevant.

**Arrange further bowel investigation (colonoscopy or CT colonography) if:**
- You have atypical or prolonged symptoms
- You have had complicated or recurrent diverticulitis
- Imaging showed abnormalities
- You have had pre-existing changes in bowel habit (blood, mucus, or diarrhoea)
- You are at higher risk of bowel cancer
- If arranged, this is booked more than 6 weeks after the acute episode to avoid risk of perforation.

**Bowel cancer screening:**
- If you have a family history of bowel cancer, your doctor will manage this according to bowel cancer screening guidelines.
