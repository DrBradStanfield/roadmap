# Syncope and Presyncope

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: May 2023.*

This pathway covers anyone who has lost consciousness, or felt they were about to lose consciousness (presyncope). A cause is found in only 50% of presentations. The goal is to rule out high-risk causes.

> This is general information from a clinical pathway, not medical advice. Always discuss your symptoms and treatment with your doctor.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

These features can point to a heart cause, which carries a higher risk of serious illness and death:

- Syncope during exercise (exertional syncope)
- Syncope while lying flat
- Syncope with no warning symptoms
- Syncope with palpitations, chest pain, or shortness of breath
- Family history of sudden cardiac death

If you faint with any of these, call 111 or go to the emergency department.

---

## What Is Syncope?

**Syncope** is a transient loss of consciousness (usually with loss of muscle tone) with spontaneous, rapid, and complete recovery, usually in less than 1 minute. There is no focal neurological abnormality.

**Presyncope** is the feeling that you are about to lose consciousness. It comes from the same processes as syncope and carries the same risk, so it is always treated the same way.

Presentations including blackouts, faints, dizzy turns, collapses, and sudden unresponsive episodes are collectively known as "funny turns."

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history and examine you. An account from someone who saw the episode is very helpful. A phone video is even better.

### What Your Doctor Will Ask About

**Around the episode:**
- What were you doing when it happened: exercising, just after exercise, standing up, coughing, in pain, turning your head or shaving?
- Were there any contributing factors: an overheated room, dehydration, another illness, weight loss, medication changes?
- Were there any warning symptoms: palpitations, chest pain, shortness of breath, abdominal or back pain, feeling hot, nausea, sweating, loss of vision, or an aura?
- How long were you unconscious?
- Did you bite the side of your tongue, lose bladder control, or have prolonged muscle jerking or stiffness? (Brief jerks or twitches lasting a few seconds are common with fainting.)
- What colour were you during and after the episode (pale suggests low blood pressure; blue suggests seizure)?
- How long were you confused afterwards? (Less than 5 minutes suggests vasovagal; more than 5 minutes is more likely a seizure)
- Did it happen at night? (Nocturnal episodes suggest seizure)

**Other history:**
- All medications, including prescriptions, over-the-counter medicines, recreational drugs, and any recent changes
- Family history of sudden cardiac death under age 40
- Past medical history, particularly heart failure or coronary artery disease
- Previous fainting episodes as a teenager
- Recent weight loss
- Recent COVID-19 infection

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will typically:
- Check for any injuries from the fall
- Measure blood pressure lying down and standing up (a manual cuff is preferred, as automated machines can miss rapid changes)
- Listen to your heart for murmurs and check your heart rate and rhythm
- Check for neurological signs
- Assess fluid status

**Lying-to-standing blood pressure:** measured three times, after lying and then after standing for at least 1 minute. A drop of more than 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic is significant. Your doctor may consider treatment if your standing blood pressure is less than 90/60 and you have symptoms.

### Investigations

- **ECG**: performed to look for heart block, ischaemic changes, and conduction disorders
- **Blood tests**: only if the cause is unclear or cardiac syncope is suspected: FBC, renal function, electrolytes, blood glucose; consider HbA1c, ferritin, or thyroid function; troponin only if cardiac syncope is suspected

## Possible Causes

### Vasovagal Syncope (Reflex Syncope)

A clear trigger helps with the diagnosis. Triggers include pain, the period just after exercise, a blood test, or another illness. Hidden bleeding or sepsis can also be a precipitant.

Warning symptoms are typical: sweating, feeling hot, nausea, blurred vision, abdominal discomfort.

### Postural (Orthostatic) Hypotension

A drop in blood pressure of more than 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic on standing. It may cause light-headedness on standing or fainting. Fainting from this cause happens within 3 minutes of standing. It is often seen in older people.

Common causes:
- Dehydration (most common)
- Medications, e.g. alpha blockers, diuretics, anticholinergics
- Sepsis
- Autonomic failure (e.g. Parkinson's disease, diabetes)

### POTS (Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome)

POTS is common and usually affects young women. It causes recurrent postural light-headedness with a sustained rise in heart rate on standing (more than 30 bpm above baseline, or more than 40 bpm if under 19), without a drop in blood pressure. Symptoms must be reliably triggered by posture and present for at least 3 months. A variable heart rate suggests hyperventilation rather than POTS.

Your doctor may use a **NASA Lean Test** to assess for POTS:
1. Lie flat for 10 minutes, with pulse and oxygen monitoring attached
2. Stand upright with feet 20 cm from a wall, leaning shoulders back against the wall
3. Blood pressure measured every minute for 5 minutes

Postural tachycardia can also be caused by bed rest, hyperventilation, weight loss, eating disorders, and anticholinergic medications. POTS should not be diagnosed in these situations. Your doctor may check HbA1c, iron studies, B12 and folate, and thyroid function (TSH) to rule out reversible causes. If blood pressure rises on standing, your doctor may check plasma metanephrines to rule out phaeochromocytoma.

### Cardiac Syncope

Less than 10% of cases, but carries a high risk of serious illness and death.

More likely if:
- Syncope occurs during exertion (in fit young people, symptoms just after exercise may be vasovagal)
- No warning symptoms
- No relationship to posture
- Known heart condition (conduction problems, structural disease, reduced heart function)
- First syncope in old age, without postural hypotension or blood-vessel-widening medications
- Family history of sudden death under age 40
- Heart murmur
- Abnormal ECG

### Seizure vs Syncope

| Feature | Syncope | Seizure |
|---------|---------|---------|
| Warning | Light-headedness | Aura |
| Posture when it happens | Usually standing | Any position |
| Incontinence | Rare | Common |
| Tongue biting | Rare (tip of tongue) | Common (side of tongue) |
| Body movements | Brief, usually asymmetric jerks after losing consciousness | Tonic-clonic movements as consciousness is lost, usually symmetric and rhythmic |
| Confusion afterwards | Resolves quickly (less than 5 minutes) | Commonly prolonged (10 to 30 minutes) |
| Heart rate after episode | Usually slow | Commonly fast |
| Time of day | Daytime | Daytime or overnight |

### Other Causes

- **Situational:** triggered by urinating, coughing, straining, or neck movement
- **Carotid sinus syncope:** pressure on the neck triggers the episode
- **Pseudo-syncope:** suspected if the eyes are shut and the loss of consciousness is prolonged (e.g. more than 30 minutes)
- **Metabolic:** hypoglycaemia, adrenal insufficiency (both rare)
- **Vertigo**

### Post-COVID Light-headedness

Postural light-headedness is common when recovering from any significant illness, especially with deconditioning or time in bed. Persistent symptoms beyond 3 months are rare but can occur with long COVID. It usually resolves over time. Helpful measures include a gradual return to fitness, pacing activity, enough fluid, food, and salt (if not hypertensive), standing slowly, and counter-pressure manoeuvres such as calf pumping and buttock clenching.

## Management

Presyncope is always treated the same as syncope.

### If Cardiac Cause Is Suspected

If a serious heart cause is suspected, your doctor will arrange acute cardiology assessment in hospital. If you have red-flag features and are not with a doctor, call 111 or go to the emergency department.

### If First Seizure

Your doctor will follow the First Seizure pathway.

### Vasovagal and Postural Causes — Community Management

Most funny turns are vasovagal or postural and can be managed without hospital admission.

**Education and lifestyle measures:**
- Understand your triggers and avoid them where possible
- For recurrent episodes, increase fluid and salt intake to improve blood volume (unless you have high blood pressure or heart failure)
- Use **counter-pressure manoeuvres**: cross your legs, clench your buttocks, grip your hands, or tense your arms. These can reduce the drop in blood pressure
- Wear support stockings (below-knee, preferably grade 2, from a pharmacy) or tight sports compression garments. Stockings can be hard to pull on. Sports garments are an acceptable alternative, though their compression is lower

**Medications:** Your doctor will review all your medications and may reduce or stop those contributing to your symptoms, including over-the-counter pills and alcohol. The commonest are alpha blockers (e.g. doxazosin, terazosin, tamsulosin), vasodilators (nitrates, calcium channel blockers, ACE inhibitors), diuretics, beta blockers, tricyclic antidepressants, antipsychotics, anticholinergics (e.g. loperamide, oxybutynin, solifenacin), and opioids.

### POTS Management

**Lifestyle (key):**
- High fluid intake: aim for 3 L/day
- High salt intake: at least 3 teaspoons a day in total (6000 mg sodium), unless you are hypertensive. The average New Zealand diet already has about 1.75 teaspoons (3500 mg sodium). Each morning, measure out extra salt to use across the day's meals: start with 0.5 teaspoons/day and increase to 1 teaspoon/day as tolerated. Look for foods with more than 600 mg sodium per 100 g. A dietitian can help. If urinary sodium is already ≥170 mmol/24hr, further increases are unlikely to help.
- Lower-body compression clothing (ideally ankle to waist)
- Counter-pressure manoeuvres (leg crossing, calf pumping, buttock clenching)
- Graduated exercise, which has proven benefit. You may need to start lying down or in water (swimming, recumbent cycle)
- Address associated hyperventilation, anxiety, dietary restriction, and sleep disruption

**If lifestyle measures don't help:**
Your doctor may consider medication: fludrocortisone (usually first line) or a low-dose beta blocker if palpitations are prominent. These are managed by your doctor. If there is still no improvement, your doctor may seek specialist advice.

### Elderly Patients

Management is more complex in older patients due to multiple medications, falls, injuries, and cognitive impairment. Your doctor may consider fludrocortisone if there are no modifiable causes and non-drug measures have not been enough. It is not used in heart failure, and the evidence for it is weak. It should not be stopped suddenly; your doctor will reduce it gradually. Your doctor may also arrange extra support services if you are finding daily life harder. If it is not safe for you to be at home, your doctor may arrange hospital admission.

### Anxiety and Panic

If anxiety, panic attacks, or hyperventilation are contributing, these are addressed directly. Relaxation techniques may help.

### Driving

If the cause of your syncope is unclear, your doctor will advise you not to drive until you have been assessed by a specialist.

General guidance (your doctor will advise based on your licence type and circumstances):

- **Unknown cause / suspected cardiac:** do not drive until symptom-free for 4 weeks (private licence); at least 3 months symptom-free (passenger or heavy goods licence)
- **Known cause:** stand-down until cause is identified, treated, and symptoms resolved. Vasovagal syncope triggered by a one-off event (e.g. blood test, illness) can usually return to driving once the trigger has resolved. The same applies to fainting on standing caused by a new blood pressure medicine once that medicine is stopped
- **Unknown cause where seizure is possible:** private licence: 6 months (or 12 months if two or more blackouts more than 24 hours apart); passenger/heavy goods: 5 years (or 10 years if two or more such blackouts), unless a cause is identified and resolved

## When Your Doctor Will Refer You

Your doctor will refer you to a specialist syncope (funny turns) clinic if:
- The cause remains unclear after initial tests
- You have recurrent episodes not caused by the heart, especially if you are injuring yourself
- You have severe postural hypotension not caused by medications, or caused by medications that may be dangerous to stop
- Your occupation is affected (driving, especially heavy vehicles, piloting, working at heights)
