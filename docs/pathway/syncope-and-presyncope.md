# Syncope and Presyncope

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of any patient who presents with a loss of consciousness, or a feeling that they were about to lose consciousness (presyncope). The goal is to rule out high-risk causes — a diagnosis is found in only 50% of presentations.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

Your doctor needs to assess you urgently if you have:

- Syncope during exercise (exertional syncope)
- Syncope while lying flat (not standing)
- Syncope with no warning symptoms whatsoever
- Syncope accompanied by palpitations, chest pain, or shortness of breath
- Family history of sudden cardiac death

---

## What Is Syncope?

**Syncope** is a transient, complete loss of consciousness (usually with loss of muscle tone) with spontaneous, rapid, and complete recovery — usually within 1 minute. There is no focal neurological abnormality.

**Presyncope** is the feeling that you are about to faint without necessarily losing consciousness. It carries the same risk as syncope and is always treated the same way.

Presentations including blackouts, faints, dizzy turns, collapses, and sudden unresponsive episodes are collectively known as "funny turns."

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history and examination. A witness account or phone video of the episode is extremely helpful.

### What Your Doctor Will Ask About

**Around the episode:**
- What were you doing when it happened — exercising, standing up, coughing, in pain, turning your head?
- Were there any contributing factors — hot environment, dehydration, recent illness, weight loss, medication changes?
- Were there any warning symptoms — palpitations, chest pain, shortness of breath, abdominal/back pain, feeling hot, nausea, visual changes, or an aura?
- How long were you unconscious?
- Did you bite your tongue, lose bladder control, or have prolonged muscle jerking or stiffness?
- What colour were you during/after the episode (pale suggests low blood pressure; blue suggests seizure)?
- How long were you confused afterwards? (Less than 5 minutes suggests vasovagal; more than 5 minutes suggests seizure)
- Did it happen at night? (Nocturnal episodes suggest seizure)

**Other history:**
- All medications including over-the-counter and supplements, and any recent changes
- Family history of sudden cardiac death under age 40
- Past medical history — particularly heart failure or coronary artery disease
- Previous fainting episodes (e.g. as a teenager)
- Recent significant weight loss
- Recent COVID-19 infection

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will typically:
- Check for any injuries from the fall
- Measure blood pressure lying down and standing up (using a manual cuff, which is more accurate for detecting rapid changes)
- Listen to your heart for murmurs and abnormal rhythms
- Check for neurological signs
- Assess fluid status

**Lying-to-standing blood pressure:** measured three times after lying and after standing for at least 1 minute. A drop of more than 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic is significant.

### Investigations

- **ECG** — performed to look for heart block, ischaemic changes, and conduction disorders
- **Blood tests** — only if the cause is unclear or cardiac syncope is suspected: FBC, renal function, electrolytes, blood glucose; consider HbA1c, ferritin, or thyroid function; troponin only if cardiac syncope is suspected

## Possible Causes

### Vasovagal Syncope (Reflex Syncope)

The most common cause. Triggered by a clear precipitant (pain, prolonged standing, heat, emotional stress, venepuncture).

Warning symptoms are typical: sweating, feeling hot, nausea, blurred vision, abdominal discomfort.

### Postural (Orthostatic) Hypotension

A drop in blood pressure of more than 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic when standing. Causes syncope within 3 minutes of standing up.

Common causes:
- Dehydration (most common)
- Medications — alpha blockers (e.g. doxazosin, tamsulosin), diuretics, ACE inhibitors, nitrates, beta blockers, antidepressants, antipsychotics, anticholinergics, opioids
- Sepsis
- Autonomic failure (e.g. Parkinson's disease, diabetes)

### POTS (Postural Orthostatic Tachycardia Syndrome)

Usually affects young women. Causes recurrent postural light-headedness with a significant increase in heart rate on standing (more than 30 bpm above lying rate, or more than 40 bpm if under 19), without a drop in blood pressure. Symptoms must be present for at least 3 months.

Your doctor will perform a **NASA Lean Test** to diagnose POTS:
1. Lie flat for 10 minutes with pulse monitoring attached
2. Stand upright with feet 20 cm from a wall, leaning shoulders back against the wall
3. Blood pressure measured every minute for 5 minutes

Postural tachycardia can also be caused by bed rest, hyperventilation, weight loss, eating disorders, and certain medications — these must be excluded before a POTS diagnosis is made.

### Cardiac Syncope

Less than 10% of cases, but carries the highest risk of serious harm.

More likely if:
- Syncope occurs during exertion
- No warning symptoms
- No relationship to posture
- Known heart condition (conduction problems, structural disease, reduced heart function)
- First syncope at an older age without an obvious cause
- Family history of sudden death under age 40
- Heart murmur
- Abnormal ECG

### Seizure vs Syncope

| Feature | Syncope | Seizure |
|---------|---------|---------|
| Warning | Light-headedness | Aura |
| Posture when it happens | Usually standing | Any position |
| Incontinence | Rare | Common |
| Tongue biting | Rare (tip of tongue) | Common (lateral tongue) |
| Body movements | Brief, asymmetric twitches after falling | Simultaneous, symmetric tonic-clonic movements |
| Confusion afterwards | Resolves quickly (less than 5 minutes) | Prolonged (10–30 minutes) |
| Heart rate after episode | Usually slow | Usually fast |
| Time of day | Daytime | Any, including overnight |

### Other Causes

- **Situational:** triggered by coughing, urinating, straining, or neck movement
- **Carotid sinus syncope:** pressure on the neck triggers the episode
- **Pseudo-syncope:** eyes closed, prolonged episode — often a functional disorder
- **Metabolic:** hypoglycaemia, adrenal insufficiency (rare)
- **Vertigo**

### Post-COVID Light-headedness

Postural light-headedness is common after any significant illness, especially with deconditioning or bed rest. Persistent symptoms beyond 3 months can occur with long COVID. Generally resolves over time with gradual return to activity, adequate fluid and food intake, salt (if not hypertensive), and counter-pressure manoeuvres.

## Management

### If Cardiac Cause Is Suspected

Your doctor will arrange urgent cardiology assessment.

### If First Seizure

Your doctor will follow the First Seizure pathway.

### Vasovagal and Postural Causes — Community Management

Most funny turns are vasovagal or postural and can be managed without hospital admission.

**Education and lifestyle measures:**
- Understand your triggers and avoid them where possible
- Rise slowly from lying or sitting
- Increase fluid intake to improve blood volume
- Increase salt intake (unless you have high blood pressure or heart failure)
- Perform **counter-pressure manoeuvres** when you feel a faint coming on: cross your legs, clench your buttocks, grip your hands, or tense your arms — these can prevent or reduce a faint
- Wear compression stockings (below-knee, preferably grade 2) or sports compression garments

**Medications:** Your doctor will review all your medications and may reduce or stop those contributing to your symptoms — particularly alpha blockers, diuretics, ACE inhibitors, nitrates, beta blockers, antidepressants, and anticholinergic drugs.

### POTS Management

**Lifestyle (key):**
- High fluid intake — aim for 3 L/day
- High salt intake — at least 3 teaspoons per day (6000 mg sodium), unless you are hypertensive. Start with 0.5 teaspoons/day and increase as tolerated. Look for foods with more than 600 mg sodium per 100 g. If urinary sodium is already ≥170 mmol/24hr, further increases are unlikely to help.
- Lower-body compression clothing (ideally ankle to waist)
- Counter-pressure manoeuvres (leg crossing, calf pumping, buttock clenching)
- Graduated exercise — may need to start lying down or in water (swimming, recumbent cycle)
- Address associated hyperventilation, anxiety, dietary restriction, and sleep disruption

**If lifestyle measures don't help:**
Your doctor may consider medication — fludrocortisone (first line) or a low-dose beta blocker if palpitations are prominent. These are managed by your doctor.

### Elderly Patients

Management is more complex in older patients due to multiple medications, fall risk, and cognitive impairment. Your doctor may consider fludrocortisone if non-pharmacological measures have failed and there is no heart failure.

### Anxiety and Panic

If anxiety, panic attacks, or hyperventilation are contributing, these are addressed directly. Relaxation techniques may help.

### Driving

If the cause of your syncope is unclear, your doctor will advise you not to drive until you have been assessed by a specialist.

General guidance (your doctor will advise based on your licence type and circumstances):

- **Unknown cause / suspected cardiac:** do not drive until symptom-free for 4 weeks (private licence); at least 3 months symptom-free (passenger or heavy goods licence)
- **Known cause:** stand-down until cause is identified, treated, and symptoms resolved. Vasovagal syncope triggered by a one-off event (e.g. blood test, illness) — can usually return to driving once the trigger has resolved
- **Unknown cause where seizure is possible:** private licence: 6 months (or 12 months if two or more episodes); passenger/heavy goods: 5 years (or 10 years if two or more episodes)

---

## When Your Doctor Will Refer You

Your doctor will refer you to a specialist (syncope/funny turns clinic via general medicine) if:
- The cause remains unclear after initial tests
- You have recurrent episodes, especially if you are injuring yourself
- You have severe postural hypotension that cannot be managed in primary care
- Your occupation is affected (driving heavy vehicles, piloting, working at heights)

A recent ECG (within 6 months) is required with all referrals to the syncope clinic.
