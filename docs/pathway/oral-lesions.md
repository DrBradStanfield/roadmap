# Oral Lesions

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of lesions inside the mouth, lips, and throat — distinguishing benign causes from those that may require urgent specialist assessment.

---

## Red Flags — See a Doctor Promptly

See your doctor promptly if you have any of the following:

- A lesion that has not healed after 3 weeks, especially in a high-risk site or with lifestyle risk factors
- New throat pain or pain referred to the ear
- Persistent numbness of the mouth, throat, or lip
- Unexplained tooth mobility or a socket that will not heal after a tooth extraction
- A previous biopsy that showed atypia or dysplasia
- A lump in the neck

---

## Background

There are many causes of lesions inside the mouth — most are benign. However, early diagnosis of oral cancer leads to the best outcomes, as most malignant lesions are curable if caught before they spread.

Viruses, especially HPV, may play a role in some oral cancers. Pre-malignant lesions do exist, but many malignant lesions arise without any preceding warning signs.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess several factors to determine whether a lesion is at higher risk of being cancerous.

### High-Risk Locations

Lesions in these areas of the mouth carry higher cancer risk:

- Floor of the mouth
- Lateral margin (side edge) of the tongue
- Tonsillar fossa and retro-molar region (behind the back teeth)
- Inside of the cheek (buccal mucosa)
- Lip (particularly from sun damage)

### High-Risk Patient Factors

Cancer risk is higher if you:

- Are older
- Smoke or use tobacco in any form (including chewing tobacco, betel nut, pan masala, or gutkha)
- Drink alcohol heavily
- Have poor oral hygiene
- Are immunosuppressed
- Have certain genetic conditions (e.g. Fanconi anaemia)

### Appearance That Raises Suspicion

Your doctor will be particularly concerned if a lesion has been present for more than 3 weeks **and** has any of the following features:

- **Leukoplakia** (a white patch) — particularly if nodular, swollen, or bleeding. Flat white patches are usually benign.
- **Erythroplakia** (a red patch) — uncommon, but more likely to be malignant, especially in a high-risk site
- Unexplained tooth mobility or a non-healing socket
- Persistent numbness of the chin, lip, palate, or tongue

Your doctor will also ask about:

- How long the lesion has been present and whether it has changed in size or texture
- Any contact bleeding from the lesion
- Whether the lesion is on one side only
- Lesion size (> 5 mm is more significant)

### Other Possible Causes of White or Red Oral Patches

Not all white or red patches are dangerous. Your doctor will consider:

- Trauma (physical, chemical, or heat-related)
- Skin conditions affecting the mouth (e.g. lichen planus)
- Systemic conditions (e.g. inflammatory bowel disease)
- Developmental changes — often appear symmetrically on both sides

---

## Management

### If Cancer Is Suspected

Your doctor will consider whether the lesion is in a high-risk site, whether you have high-risk factors, or whether the appearance meets criteria for high suspicion of oral, throat, or lip cancer.

If cancer is suspected, your doctor will arrange an urgent specialist referral for biopsy and assessment. Early diagnosis — before any spread — gives the best outcomes.

The specialist involved will depend on where the lesion is:

- Oral or mouth lesion — oral health or maxillofacial surgery
- Lip lesion — general surgery, dermatology, or plastic surgery
- Throat or tonsillar lesion — ENT (ear, nose, and throat) surgery

### If Cancer Is Not Suspected

Your doctor may consider:

- Patient education about reducing risk for mouth and throat cancer
- Referral to a general dental practitioner if dental trauma is the likely cause
- Further assessment for a neck lump if one is present
