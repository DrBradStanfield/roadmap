# Carpal Tunnel Syndrome

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) — compression of the median nerve at the wrist causing numbness, tingling, and pain in the hand.

---

## Background

Carpal tunnel syndrome is the most common peripheral nerve compression disorder:

- Caused by compression of the median nerve at the level of the wrist
- Produces variable symptoms including numbness, tingling, hand and arm pain, and muscle weakness
- In more than 90% of cases no specific underlying cause is found
- More common in women (approximately 2.4:1 female to male ratio), people over 55, and those with certain medical conditions

**Underlying or contributing conditions** (not common, but worth considering):

- Pregnancy
- Obesity
- Diabetes
- Hypothyroidism
- Acute fluid retention
- Connective tissue diseases (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis)
- Previous wrist fractures or local wrist abnormalities

---

## Assessment

Diagnosis is primarily clinical, based on characteristic symptoms and signs.

### Symptoms

Classic symptoms include:

- Pain or pins-and-needles (paraesthesia) in the thumb, index finger, middle finger, and half of the ring finger (median nerve distribution)
- Symptoms typically **worse at night**, often waking from sleep — patients may shake the hand or dangle it out of bed for relief
- Symptoms may also occur in the morning
- Pain or tingling provoked by activities involving wrist flexion or extension (e.g., reading, driving)
- Weakness of grip, particularly thumb abduction and pincer grip
- Symptoms can be localised to the wrist, involve the whole hand, or radiate up into the forearm (rarely above the elbow)

### Examination

Your doctor will examine for:

- **Sensory changes:** Reduced pinprick sensation in the median nerve territory (fingertips and radial digits); the thenar eminence and palm are usually spared
- **Motor changes:** Weakness is usually seen only with advanced CTS — may involve thumb abduction weakness or, if severe, wasting of the thenar eminence (the muscle pad at the base of the thumb)
- **Provocative tests:**
  - *Phalen test* — holding the wrist in acute flexion for 30–60 seconds to reproduce symptoms
  - *Tinel test* — tapping over the median nerve at the wrist to reproduce or worsen symptoms
  - Neither test is highly sensitive or specific; a negative test does not exclude CTS

### Nerve Conduction Studies

If there is diagnostic uncertainty, nerve conduction studies can:

- Confirm the diagnosis and assess severity
- Identify alternative diagnoses
- Detect underlying peripheral neuropathy

Your doctor can arrange a referral if these are needed. A negative nerve conduction study does not completely exclude CTS if symptoms are clearly present.

## Management

### Conservative Treatment

When there is no sensory loss or muscle wasting, a trial of non-operative treatment is usually recommended first.

**Your doctor may consider:**

**Wrist splinting**
- Splints hold the wrist in a neutral position to relieve nerve compression
- Night splints are the main approach; full-time splinting has not been shown to be better than night-time use
- Available from pharmacies and online; a custom-made splint from a hand therapist is generally more beneficial
- Particularly useful during pregnancy, as CTS often resolves after delivery

**Hand therapy**
- A hand therapist can provide graded exercises, rehabilitation, and custom splinting
- May reduce or delay the need for surgery with good patient engagement

**Steroid injection into the carpal tunnel**
- Can provide effective short-term symptom relief (up to approximately 3 months)
- In mild to moderate CTS of less than 6 months' duration, can be as effective as surgery
- Less likely to be effective if symptoms are severe or have been present for more than 6 months
- Typically limited to no more than 3 injections per year
- Common agents used: **triamcinolone** (Kenacort) or **methylprednisolone acetate** (Depo-Medrol), combined with lignocaine

**Treating underlying conditions**
- Managing contributing conditions (e.g., diabetes, hypothyroidism) may help symptoms

### When Surgery May Be Considered

Your doctor may refer for surgical assessment if:

- There is muscle wasting or sensory loss (referral is recommended even before conservative treatment has been trialled)
- Conservative treatment has been tried and symptoms persist

Surgery — **carpal tunnel release (CTR)** or **carpal tunnel decompression (CTD)** — is most commonly performed as a day procedure under local anaesthetic. It has a high rate of complete and permanent cure.

If a neurological deficit is already present, surgery can prevent further progression but may not result in complete recovery.
