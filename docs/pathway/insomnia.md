# Insomnia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: July 2025.*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of insomnia in adults, including non-pharmacological treatments (CBT-I) and when medication is appropriate.

> **Important:** This information is for education only. It does not replace advice from your own doctor. Talk to your doctor before starting, stopping, or changing any treatment.

---

## About Insomnia

Insomnia means difficulty falling or staying asleep, or waking earlier than desired, despite adequate opportunity and conditions for sleep. This negatively affects daytime functioning.

Insomnia symptoms are common: 22 to 55% of people report insomnia symptoms at some point in their life. Insomnia disorder is less common and affects around 13% of New Zealand adults.

The causes are multifactorial:
- **Predisposing factors:** e.g., psychological factors
- **Precipitating factors:** e.g., stress events
- **Perpetuating factors:** unhelpful sleep habits, cognitive hyperarousal (worrying about sleep)

Older age is associated with less total sleep time and less deep sleep. Problems staying asleep and early morning waking may also increase with age. Advanced sleep phase disorder (wanting to sleep and wake earlier than normal) tends to occur in older people. Teenagers and young adults may experience delayed sleep phase (wanting to sleep and wake later than normal).

## What Your Doctor Will Assess

Your doctor will take a detailed history, and may check with your partner or whānau if possible. It covers:

### Sleep history

- Sleep patterns and quality as a child and teenager
- When your sleep pattern changed and what triggered it
- Current sleep and wake times, time taken to fall asleep, quality of sleep
- Whether you feel sleepy (not just tired) at bedtime
- Time spent in bed versus actual sleep time: sleeping less than 80% of the time in bed is a good indicator of a clinically significant problem
- Regularity of sleep routine (night-to-night variation is typical of insomnia)
- Daytime symptoms: sleepiness, irritability, poor concentration, low energy, memory problems
- Whether sleep changes on weekends or holidays

### Lifestyle and behavioural factors

- Napping during the day
- Screen use in the evening or in bed
- Caffeine (including energy drinks), alcohol, or nicotine use
- Heavy meals near bedtime
- Worrying or racing thoughts at night
- Spending long periods in bed without sleeping
- Shift work (any work pattern that shifts your preferred sleep and wake times, such as early starts, late finishes, or night work)

### Environmental factors

- Light, noise, or temperature in the bedroom
- Electronic devices in the evening
- Partner disturbance, pets in the bedroom

### Medical factors

Your doctor may consider medical causes such as:
- Pain, breathlessness (heart failure, COPD), acid reflux, frequent nocturia
- Restless legs, chronic fatigue, thyroid overactivity
- Post-traumatic brain injury or concussion, itch conditions (e.g., eczema)

And medications that may disrupt sleep:
- Stimulants (e.g., pseudoephedrine, phenytoin, caffeine)
- Antidepressants (e.g., SSRIs)
- Levodopa (for Parkinson's disease)
- Beta blockers (especially lipid-soluble ones like bisoprolol, metoprolol, or carvedilol): associated with bad dreams and nightmares
- Diuretics (causing night-time urination), corticosteroids
- Stimulant laxatives, appetite suppressants
- Long-term benzodiazepine use, or recently stopping sleeping tablets (which can cause rebound insomnia)
- Alcohol and nicotine

Psychological factors including depression, anxiety, and stress are also assessed.

### Assessment tools

Your doctor may ask you to complete:
- **Insomnia Severity Index:** an easy-to-use questionnaire, useful to monitor progress
- **Epworth Sleepiness Scale:** to assess daytime sleepiness
- **Two-week sleep diary:** records your actual sleep patterns
- **Wearable device** (e.g., smart watch): for self-assessment of sleep duration

If relevant and not done before, blood tests may be arranged to look for other causes of fatigue (FBC, ferritin, creatinine, HbA1c, TSH).

### Types of insomnia

**Short-term insomnia:** usually lasts a few days or weeks, and occurs in response to an identifiable stressor (physical, psychological, or social). May progress to chronic insomnia if unhelpful sleep habits develop.

**Chronic insomnia:** symptoms at least 3 nights per week, for at least 3 months. May include:
- *Psychophysiological insomnia:* worrying, including about lack of sleep, causes heightened arousal and anxiety before and during sleep. This further reduces the ability to sleep. They are more likely to respond to CBT.
- *Paradoxical insomnia:* a mismatch between the amount of sleep reported (e.g., in a sleep diary) and the sleep actually measured. People commonly overestimate the time spent awake in bed.

Excessive daytime sleepiness is always taken seriously, as it is a safety concern. Your doctor may assess for:
- Sleep-restricted insomnia (sleep of less than 6 hours, measured by a movement monitor or a sleep study): thought to carry a higher risk of high blood pressure, cognitive impairment, and death, and more likely to need medication
- Sleep apnoea (can coexist with insomnia)
- Other significant sleep disorders (e.g., narcolepsy)

Your doctor may also assess your fitness to drive, especially if you hold a commercial licence.

## Management

The goal is to reduce daytime impairment and improve sleep quality (e.g., falling asleep, wake times). Any co-existing conditions (pain, depression, anxiety) are treated at the same time. If obstructive sleep apnoea is a concern, your doctor may assess for it.

### CBT for Insomnia (CBT-I)

Your doctor may use parts of cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) that can be delivered in general practice. CBT has five major components, each of which may be effective on its own:

#### 1. Sleep hygiene education

- Go to bed and get up at the **same time every day**. Get into bed when you feel drowsy, tired, and ready to sleep.
- Make a consistent **wake-up time** the priority. It increases the chance of bright morning light.
- **Avoid napping** if possible. If unavoidable, nap before 3:00 pm and for no more than 30 minutes.
- Wind down before bed with relaxing activities, such as music or a warm (not hot) bath. Writing things down can help your mind let go and relax.
- Avoid caffeine and alcohol before bed.

Patient resources: Sleep Health Foundation – Good Sleep Habits; Centre for Clinical Interventions – Sleep Hygiene.

#### 2. Sleep restriction

Sleep restriction reduces the time spent in bed to more closely match actual sleep time.

*Important precautions. Discuss with your doctor before starting:*
- May not be appropriate with epilepsy or bipolar disorder, where sleep loss may trigger seizures or mania
- With significant other health conditions, a slow and cautious approach is advisable
- With significant sleep deprivation, other strategies may be better to start with
- May cause temporary increased daytime drowsiness. If drowsiness is a risk in your job (e.g., surgeon, commercial driver), your doctor may suggest another approach or doing the programme during a holiday.

**How it works:**
1. Use a sleep diary to find out how much you actually sleep (not just time in bed).
2. Reduce time in bed by half the time you spend awake. For example, if you're awake 4 hours per night, reduce time in bed by 2 hours. Keep a minimum of 5 hours in bed.
3. Stick to this for 2 weeks. Keep the sleep diary. Do not drive or use tools or machines if drowsy.
4. Review: calculate average sleep efficiency (time asleep ÷ time in bed)
   - Target: 80–85% sleep efficiency
   - Still spending a long time awake? Reduce time in bed by another 30 minutes, keeping a minimum of 5 hours in bed
   - Sleeping efficiently but still sleepy during the day? Extend time in bed by 15–30 minutes

#### 3. Stimulus control

- **Avoid caffeine and energy drinks** for several hours before bed
- **Avoid alcohol** near bedtime
- **Cut down smoking**, including nicotine-containing vaping
- **Keep the bedroom for sleep and intimacy only:** no TV, electronic devices, or reading in bed
- If you are not asleep after about 20 minutes, **get out of bed** and do something non-stimulating (reading, quiet chores, relaxation). Return when tired enough to sleep.
- **Bedroom environment:** keep it dark and at a comfortable temperature. Limit noise or use earplugs. Avoid watching the clock at night; ideally place it out of reach.
- Avoid screens and devices for at least **2 hours before bed**
- Avoid **bright light** for several hours before bed
- **Regular daytime physical activity**

#### 4. Cognitive therapy

This addresses worries and beliefs about sleep:
- Unrealistic expectations (e.g., thinking your challenges would disappear after a good night's sleep)
- Fear of missing out on sleep
- Overestimating the consequences of poor sleep

**Online CBT-I programmes:**
- **Just a Thought** (justathought.co.nz): free for people aged 16+; includes a Managing Insomnia course (4–6 modules); monitored
- **CALM:** free, unmonitored; includes audio modules such as meditation and mindfulness
- **CBT-I: CBT for Insomnia** (cbtforinsomnia.com): a cost is involved
- **Sleepio:** six-week self-directed programme; may be covered by insurance or employer, otherwise a cost applies
- **SleepSchool:** uses acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT); a cost is involved

#### 5. Relaxation techniques

- Yoga, t'ai chi, and relaxation
- Breathing and distraction techniques
- Progressive muscle relaxation
- Mindfulness and meditation apps
- Sleep apps (to aid relaxation, provide distraction, and help soothe to sleep)

### Shift work

If you work shifts, your doctor should always discuss this. Safety comes first: impaired driving is dangerous, so plan a safe way home, such as public transport or being picked up.
- Aim for 7 to 9 hours of sleep in each 24-hour period, with consistent sleep and wake times across a run of similarly timed shifts.
- Keep the bedroom dark, cool, and quiet.
- Keep naps to 40 minutes or less, and allow time to fully wake before driving or safety-critical tasks.
- Changing work hours, even for a while, may help with diagnosis.

For shift work sleep disorder, your doctor may consider medication only if non-drug approaches were unsuitable or did not work:
- **Melatonin:** e.g., 2 mg taken at home one to two hours before sleep time
- **Caffeine:** only when needed, as tolerance can develop. Start with 100 mg (about an average cup of coffee) early in the shift, and avoid more than 300 mg a day from all sources.
- **Modafinil:** only for excessive sleepiness with moderate to severe shift work sleep disorder. It is not subsidised for this use, and only a specialist should start it.

## Medication

Short-term medication may be used to supplement CBT-I. *Always discuss medication options with your doctor.*

**General principles:**
- Use the lowest effective dose
- Taper as soon as the condition allows
- Avoid long-term use: there is significant evidence of harm and little evidence of long-term benefit or safety
- Ask how the medication may affect driving

### Prescription medications

**Melatonin** (unsubsidised in NZ):
- Modified-release melatonin 2 mg, taken 1 hour before target sleep time
- Approved for short-term use in patients over 55 years
- Taking melatonin at a different time can upset sleep patterns further, including causing daytime sleepiness. Timing matters.

**Hypnotics** (e.g., zopiclone, temazepam):
- Short-acting options are preferred: zopiclone 3.75–7.5 mg or temazepam 10 mg
- Avoid or use lower doses in older people (risk of falls, fractures, car accidents, confusion, reduced energy)
- All hypnotics carry a risk of withdrawal effects, such as rebound insomnia, if used for more than a few weeks. Some tolerance develops with prolonged use.

**Tricyclic antidepressants** (e.g., amitriptyline):
- Amitriptyline 10–25 mg at night
- May be useful if there is also neuropathic pain, depression, or anxiety
- The sedative effect can prevent night-time waking, so it may help people who have trouble staying asleep
- Not addictive, but has more side-effects than other options at doses above 10 mg

**Low-dose quetiapine** (not approved for insomnia):
- Used with caution in people without psychosis, as there is little evidence of benefit for insomnia
- Associated with next-day drowsiness and long-term adverse effects such as significant weight gain, diabetes, tardive dyskinesia, QT prolongation, and sudden cardiac death
- Your doctor would consider other medications first, and discuss the risks with you before prescribing

### Over-the-counter options

- **Equivocal evidence:** magnesium, tart cherry and its extracts
- **No evidence of efficacy:** vitamin B, herbal remedies (valerian, chamomile, kava, hops), 5-HTP
- **Avoid sedating antihistamines:** little to no evidence of benefit, and they can cause next-day drowsiness

## Referral

Your doctor may arrange a specialist sleep assessment if:
- There is concern about a significant sleep disorder (e.g., narcolepsy)
- Unexplained daytime sleepiness raises safety concerns, especially falling asleep while driving. Your doctor may advise you not to drive, or recommend driving restrictions.
- You are a shift worker being considered for modafinil

If insomnia does not respond to primary care management and significantly affects your life or work, your doctor may:
- Reassess possible causes and diagnoses
- Consider private referral to a clinical psychologist with CBT-I expertise
- Consider a private sleep disorder assessment
