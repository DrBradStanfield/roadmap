# Insomnia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of insomnia in adults, including non-pharmacological treatments (CBT-I) and when medication is appropriate.

---

## About Insomnia

Insomnia means difficulty falling or staying asleep, or waking earlier than desired, despite adequate opportunity and conditions for sleep. This negatively affects daytime functioning.

Insomnia symptoms are common — 22 to 55% of people report insomnia symptoms at some point in their life. Insomnia disorder (more persistent and impairing) affects around 13% of New Zealand adults.

The causes are multifactorial:
- **Predisposing factors** — e.g., psychological vulnerability
- **Precipitating factors** — e.g., stress events
- **Perpetuating factors** — unhelpful sleep habits, cognitive hyperarousal (worrying about sleep)

Older age is associated with less total sleep time and less deep sleep. Early morning waking tends to increase with age. Teenagers and young adults may experience delayed sleep phase (wanting to sleep and wake later than normal).

## What Your Doctor Will Assess

Your doctor will take a detailed history covering:

### Sleep history

- Sleep patterns and quality as a child and teenager
- When your sleep pattern changed and what triggered it
- Current sleep and wake times, time taken to fall asleep, quality of sleep
- Time spent in bed versus actual sleep time — sleeping less than 80% of time in bed suggests a significant problem
- Regularity of sleep routine (night-to-night variation is typical of insomnia)
- Daytime symptoms: sleepiness, irritability, poor concentration, low energy, memory problems
- Whether sleep improves on weekends or holidays

### Lifestyle and behavioural factors

- Napping during the day
- Screen use in the evening or in bed
- Caffeine, alcohol, or nicotine use
- Heavy meals near bedtime
- Worrying or racing thoughts at night
- Shift work (different sleep/wake demands from work schedule)

### Environmental factors

- Light, noise, or temperature in the bedroom
- Electronic devices in the evening
- Partner disturbance, pets in the bedroom

### Medical factors

Your doctor may consider medical causes such as:
- Pain, breathlessness (heart failure, COPD), acid reflux, frequent nocturia
- Restless legs, chronic fatigue, thyroid overactivity
- Post-traumatic brain injury, itch conditions (e.g., eczema)

And medications that may disrupt sleep:
- Stimulants (pseudoephedrine, some antidepressants/SSRIs)
- Beta blockers (especially lipid-soluble ones like bisoprolol, metoprolol) — associated with bad dreams
- Diuretics (causing nighttime urination), corticosteroids
- Stopping sleeping tablets — can cause rebound insomnia
- Alcohol and nicotine

Psychological factors including depression, anxiety, and stress are also assessed.

### Assessment tools

Your doctor may ask you to complete:
- **Insomnia Severity Index** — a quick questionnaire useful to monitor progress
- **Epworth Sleepiness Scale** — to assess daytime sleepiness
- **Two-week sleep diary** — records your actual sleep patterns
- **Wearable device** (e.g., smart watch) — for self-assessment of sleep duration

Blood tests may be arranged to rule out other causes of fatigue (FBC, ferritin, creatinine, HbA1c, TSH).

### Types of insomnia

**Short-term insomnia** — lasts days to weeks, usually linked to an identifiable stressor. May progress to chronic insomnia if unhelpful sleep habits develop.

**Chronic insomnia** — symptoms at least 3 nights per week, for at least 3 months. May include:
- *Psychophysiological insomnia* — worrying about sleep causes heightened arousal and anxiety, which makes sleep worse. These patients tend to respond well to CBT-I.
- *Paradoxical insomnia* — the amount of sleep reported feels much less than what is actually measured. Patients often overestimate time spent awake.

If daytime sleepiness is a significant concern, your doctor will assess for:
- Sleep-restricted insomnia (actual sleep <6 hours) — associated with higher risks of hypertension and cognitive impairment
- Sleep apnoea (can coexist with insomnia)
- Other significant sleep disorders (e.g., narcolepsy)

## Management

The goal is to reduce daytime impairment and improve sleep quality. Any co-existing conditions (pain, depression, anxiety) are treated at the same time.

### CBT for Insomnia (CBT-I)

CBT-I is the recommended first-line treatment. It includes five components, each of which can be effective on its own:

#### 1. Sleep hygiene education

- Go to bed and get up at the **same time every day** — even on weekends. Only go to bed when you feel genuinely sleepy (not just tired).
- A consistent **wake-up time** is the most important anchor. Morning light exposure helps.
- **Avoid napping** if possible. If unavoidable, nap before 3:00 pm and for no more than 30 minutes.
- Wind down before bed: relaxing activities such as music, a warm (not hot) bath, writing things down to clear your mind.
- Avoid caffeine and alcohol before bed.

Patient resources: Sleep Health Foundation – Good Sleep Habits; Centre for Clinical Interventions – Sleep Hygiene.

#### 2. Sleep restriction

Sleep restriction reduces the time spent in bed to more closely match actual sleep time — this consolidates sleep and builds sleep pressure.

*Important precautions — discuss with your doctor before starting:*
- May not be appropriate for epilepsy or bipolar disorder
- Not appropriate if you have very severe sleep deprivation
- May cause temporary increased daytime drowsiness — avoid if this is dangerous for your work or situation (e.g., heavy machinery, commercial driving). Consider doing the programme during a holiday period.

**How it works:**
1. Use a sleep diary to find out how much you actually sleep (not just time in bed)
2. Reduce time in bed by half the time you spend awake — e.g., if you're awake 4 hours per night, reduce time in bed by 2 hours. Minimum 5 hours in bed.
3. Stick to this for 2 weeks. Keep the sleep diary. Do not drive or use machinery if drowsy.
4. Review: calculate sleep efficiency (time asleep ÷ time in bed × 100)
   - Target: 80–85% sleep efficiency
   - Still spending lots of time awake? Reduce time in bed by another 30 minutes
   - Sleeping efficiently but still tired? Extend time in bed by 15–30 minutes

#### 3. Stimulus control

This retrains the brain to associate the bedroom with sleep.

- **Avoid caffeine and energy drinks** for several hours before bed
- **Avoid alcohol** near bedtime
- **Cut down smoking** and nicotine-containing vaping
- **Keep the bedroom for sleep and intimacy only** — no TV, phones, tablets, or reading in bed
- If you can't sleep within about 20 minutes, **get out of bed** and do something calm and non-stimulating (reading, light chores, relaxation). Return only when sleepy.
- **Bedroom environment:** keep it dark, cool, and quiet. Use earplugs if needed. Move the clock out of sight so you don't watch it.
- Avoid screens/devices for at least **2 hours before bed**
- Avoid **bright light** in the hours before sleep
- **Regular daytime exercise** supports better sleep

#### 4. Cognitive therapy

This addresses unhelpful thoughts and beliefs about sleep:
- Unrealistic expectations (e.g., thinking all problems will be solved after a good night's sleep)
- Fear of missing out on sleep
- Overestimating the consequences of poor sleep

**Online CBT-I programmes:**
- **Just a Thought** (justathought.co.nz) — free for patients 16+, includes a Managing Insomnia course (4–6 modules), monitored with support
- **CALM** — free, unmonitored; includes audio modules for meditation and mindfulness
- **Sleepio** — six-week self-directed programme; may be covered by insurance or employer, otherwise a cost applies
- **SleepSchool** — uses acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT); a cost is involved

#### 5. Relaxation techniques

- Breathing exercises and distraction techniques
- Progressive muscle relaxation
- Yoga, t'ai chi
- Mindfulness and meditation apps
- Sleep apps (for relaxation, distraction, soothing)

## Medication

Medication can supplement CBT-I for short-term use, but is not recommended as a standalone long-term solution. *Always discuss medication options with your doctor.*

**General principles:**
- Use the lowest effective dose
- Taper and stop as soon as the condition allows
- Long-term use carries significant risk of harm and has little evidence of sustained benefit

### Prescription medications

**Melatonin** (unsubsidised in NZ):
- Modified-release melatonin 2 mg, taken 1 hour before target sleep time
- Approved for short-term use in patients over 55 years
- Taking melatonin at the wrong time can worsen sleep patterns or cause daytime drowsiness — timing matters

**Hypnotics** (e.g., zopiclone, temazepam):
- Zopiclone 3.75–7.5 mg or temazepam 10 mg — short-acting options are preferred
- Avoid or use lower doses in older patients (risk of falls, fractures, accidents, confusion)
- All hypnotics carry risk of withdrawal and rebound insomnia if used for more than a few weeks; tolerance develops with prolonged use

**Tricyclic antidepressants** (e.g., amitriptyline):
- Amitriptyline 10–25 mg at night
- Useful if there is also neuropathic pain, depression, or anxiety
- Sedative effect helps with night-time waking
- Not addictive, but has more side-effects at doses above 10 mg

**Low-dose quetiapine** (not approved for insomnia):
- Not recommended without first trying other options — limited evidence of benefit for insomnia alone
- Associated with weight gain, diabetes risk, tardive dyskinesia, QT prolongation, and next-day drowsiness
- Your doctor will only consider this after discussing risks carefully

### Over-the-counter options

- **Equivocal evidence** (some studies positive, results mixed): magnesium, tart cherry extract
- **No evidence of efficacy**: vitamin B supplements, herbal remedies (valerian, chamomile, kava, hops), 5-HTP
- **Avoid sedating antihistamines** — little to no evidence of benefit and can cause next-day drowsiness

## Referral

Your doctor may arrange specialist sleep assessment if:
- There is concern about a significant sleep disorder (e.g., narcolepsy)
- Unexplained daytime sleepiness raises concerns about safety (especially falling asleep while driving)
- You are a shift worker being considered for modafinil

If insomnia does not respond to primary care management and significantly affects your life or work, your doctor may consider:
- Private referral to a clinical psychologist with CBT-I expertise
- Private sleep disorder assessment

> **Note:** Insomnia is not managed in the public specialist sector in Auckland — referrals are to private providers.
