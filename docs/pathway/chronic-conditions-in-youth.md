# Chronic Conditions in Youth

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to supporting young people who live with a long-term health condition — including how assessments are structured, what management strategies your doctor may consider, and how to prepare for the transition to adult services.

---

## Background

- Approximately 17% of young people have one or more chronic conditions — for example, diabetes, epilepsy, asthma, or cystic fibrosis.
- Most chronic conditions start during childhood. Advances in medical care mean around 80% of affected young people survive into adulthood.
- Many young people will be transitioning their healthcare from hospital-based paediatric (children's) services into primary care — this can be a stressful time for both the young person and their family or caregivers.
- Young people with chronic conditions may face additional challenges, including:
  - Being less likely to be employed.
  - Being more likely to experience bullying or stigmatisation.
  - Being more likely to experience depressive symptoms.
  - Being more likely to experiment with cigarettes, alcohol, or marijuana.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will focus on your needs as a young person first, and your condition second. A comprehensive assessment may include:

1. **A full adolescent health assessment** — often using a structured approach (such as the HEeADSSSSS framework) that covers home, education, activities, drugs, sexuality, suicide/self-harm, safety, sleep, and screen time.

2. **How your condition and development interact** — your chronic condition may affect:
   - Physical growth and development.
   - Your sense of identity, body image, and thinking.
   - Peer and family relationships, independence, sexuality, and school achievement.
   - Conversely, normal teenage behaviours and physical changes can sometimes make a chronic condition less stable or harder to manage.

3. **Confidentiality** — your doctor will explain what is kept private. In general:
   - Your health information is kept confidential within your treating team, unless there are safety concerns or you give consent to share it.
   - If safety concerns arise, others may need to be involved.
   - Parents or caregivers do not automatically have access to your health information.

4. **Your psychological response to the condition** — your doctor will consider:
   - Adaptive responses (e.g., denial, intellectualisation, regression) — normal and common.
   - Maladaptive responses (e.g., extreme hostility, panic, suicidal behaviour, or withdrawal) — these may need targeted support.

5. **Specific factors that affect how distressing a condition feels**, including:
   - Visibility — a more visible condition is often associated with less psychological distress.
   - Degree of functional impairment — even a seemingly minor limitation can cause significant distress.
   - Prognosis — uncertainty about the future is more stressful than a known outlook.
   - Course of illness — a stable, predictable condition is less distressing than one that fluctuates unpredictably.

## Management

Your doctor will focus on your capacity for healthy functioning, not just your condition. Management may include:

1. **Promoting positive coping** — concentrating on what you *can* do: interests, activities, social connections, and lifestyle. Your doctor may encourage you to:
   - Maintain a network of friends (both with and without chronic conditions).
   - Participate in sports and social activities where possible.
   - Get involved in household chores or part-time work where possible.
   - See yourself as a whole person, not defined by a disability or condition.

2. **Supporting treatment compliance** — your doctor may help you understand your condition and the reasons for any medication or treatment, and may:
   - Offer some choice about treatments where possible.
   - Set short-term, achievable goals.
   - Address associated problems (physical, emotional, or social).
   - Maintain regular contact to support ongoing management.

3. **Balancing your priorities with medical needs** — your doctor will consider that your concerns (normal teenage priorities) may differ from those of your parents or health team (symptoms and treatment). Both perspectives matter.

4. **Limiting unnecessary examinations** and providing clear, honest information about your condition and what to expect.

5. **Supporting your family** — your doctor may work with your parents or caregivers to reduce over-protectiveness, anxiety, and over-attentiveness, helping you build independence.

6. **Referral for additional support** — a referral to a psychologist or social worker may be recommended if you are struggling emotionally or practically with your condition.

7. **Preparing for transition to adult services** — your doctor will help you and your family understand and plan for the move from child/youth health services to adult services.

## Notes for Specific Conditions

### Asthma
- A common condition in young people.
- Often poorly self-managed due to competing priorities — your doctor can help develop a practical management plan that fits your lifestyle.

### Cancer
- Specialised keyworkers are available in New Zealand to support young people diagnosed with cancer.
- **Canteen** ([canteen.org.nz](https://www.canteen.org.nz/)) provides support for young people with cancer.

### Cystic Fibrosis
- Specialist support and information is available through **Cystic Fibrosis New Zealand** ([cfnz.org.nz](https://www.cfnz.org.nz/)).

### Diabetes
- Normal teenage behaviours (e.g., eating habits, alcohol use) can make diabetes harder to manage.
- A multidisciplinary team is typically involved in care.
- **Diabetes Youth New Zealand** ([diabetes.org.nz](https://www.diabetes.org.nz/diabetes-youth)) provides additional support.

### Obesity
- Your doctor will raise weight in the context of how you feel and your energy levels — not as a lecture about future risk.
- A full picture of your diet, exercise, and daily life will be part of the assessment.
- Management strategies are adapted to your developmental stage.

### Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD — Crohn's or Colitis)
- Your doctor may involve an IBD nurse specialist or gastroenterologist for ongoing support.
- **Crohns and Colitis New Zealand** ([crohnsandcolitis.org.nz](https://crohnsandcolitis.org.nz/)) provides peer support and information.
