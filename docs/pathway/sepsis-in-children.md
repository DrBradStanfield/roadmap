# Sepsis in Children (Age 0–11 Years)

> **This information supports clinical decision-making. Always use your own clinical judgement — sepsis is time-critical and warrants immediate physician involvement. If you suspect sepsis in a child, act now and get senior help.**

This covers assessment and management of sepsis in children aged 0–11 years.

---

## What Is Sepsis?

Sepsis is a life-threatening complication of infection in which the immune system causes organ failure and circulatory impairment. Morbidity and mortality improve significantly with prompt recognition, resuscitation, source control, and antibiotics.

Sepsis is commonly **under-recognised or recognised late**. Early recognition is vital.

---

## Who Is at Higher Risk?

Consider sepsis in any child with suspected infection, especially if risk factors are present:

- Age <12 months
- Incomplete immunisation
- Previous sepsis
- Māori or Pacific ethnicity (higher rates of sepsis and in-hospital death)
- Socio-economic deprivation
- Chronic medical conditions
- Immunosuppression
- Recent hospital admission, trauma, surgery, or invasive procedure (within 6 weeks)
- Neonatal risk factors (mother): group B strep colonisation/infection, prolonged membrane rupture >18 hours, preterm birth <37 weeks, intrapartum fever, chorioamnionitis

## Assessment — Ask: Could It Be Sepsis?

### Step 1 — Examine the Child

| What to assess | What to look for |
|---|---|
| **Mental state** | Reduced consciousness, lethargy, irritability, floppiness. Use AVPU: Alert / Voice / Pain / Unresponsive |
| **Skin** | Non-blanching rash (petechial/purpuric). Mottled, pale, or cool skin. Prolonged capillary refill |
| **Temperature** | Fever OR hypothermia — both can indicate sepsis. Normal temperature does not exclude sepsis |
| **Heart rate** | See tachycardia thresholds below |
| **Respiratory rate** | See tachypnoea thresholds below |
| **Blood pressure** | Measure if appropriate cuff available — see hypotension thresholds below |
| **Source of infection** | Signs of meningitis, pneumonia, peritonitis, cellulitis, osteomyelitis, septic arthritis |

**Signs of meningitis in children:** May be minimal or nonspecific, especially early or in infants. Look for: bulging fontanelle, excessive irritability, non-blanching rash. Kernig's and Brudzinski's signs are often absent in infants.

### Step 2 — Apply the Sepsis Criteria

#### Red Flag Sepsis (requires all three):

1. **Confirmed or suspected infection** *(systemic symptoms more common than local; fever not always present)*
2. **Patient appears seriously unwell, OR carer/whānau is very concerned** *(caregiver concern often precedes vital sign changes)*
3. **One or more Red Flag features:**

| Red Flag Feature | Detail |
|---|---|
| Severe or persistent tachypnoea | <1 yr: ≥70/min; 1–4 yr: ≥55/min; 5–11 yr: ≥45/min |
| Severe or persistent tachycardia | <1 yr: ≥180/min; 1–4 yr: ≥160/min; 5–11 yr: ≥155/min |
| Fever >38.5°C AND age <6 weeks | — |
| Purpuric rash | — |
| Perfusion changes | Mottled skin, central capillary refill >3 seconds |
| Reduced mental status | Confusion, responds only to voice or pain (AVPU ≤V), inconsolable |
| SpO2 <90% | (<85% in known chronic lung disease) |
| Hypotension | <1 yr: ≤65 mmHg; 1–4 yr: ≤75 mmHg; 5–11 yr: ≤79 mmHg systolic |

#### Amber Flag Sepsis (requires all three):

1. Confirmed or suspected infection
2. Patient appears very unwell, OR carer/whānau is very concerned
3. One or more Amber Flag features (no red flags present):

| Amber Flag Feature |
|---|
| Persistent carer/whānau concern |
| SpO2 <92% |
| Rigors OR temperature >39°C |
| Moderate tachypnoea: <1 yr 55–69/min; 1–4 yr 45–54/min; 5–11 yr 35–44/min |
| Moderate tachycardia: <1 yr 170–179/min; 1–4 yr 150–159/min; 5–11 yr 140–154/min |
| Acute leg pain |
| Significant cardiac, respiratory, or neurological disability |

> More than one amber flag = increased concern. Lower your threshold for treating as Red Flag Sepsis.

### Alternative Diagnoses to Consider
- Adrenal insufficiency
- Anaphylaxis
- Diabetic ketoacidosis

## Management

### Red Flag Sepsis — Act Immediately (Sepsis Six)

Perform as many steps as possible — do not delay:

1. **Arrange urgent transfer to ED.** Phone St John healthcare professional line (0800-262-665, not 111) and state "Red Flag Sepsis." Arrange acute paediatric or emergency assessment; seek senior help.
2. **Give oxygen** via non-rebreather mask.
3. **Secure IV access** and take urgent investigations to send with patient. Do not delay antibiotics for IV access difficulties.
4. **Blood cultures** (if bottles available) — see technique below.
5. **Give antibiotics** (see below).
6. **IV fluid resuscitation** if poor perfusion (mottled/cool skin, altered mental state) or hypotension — see below.

#### Urgent Investigations

- **Blood cultures:**
  - Use vacutainer-compatible butterfly technique (preferred)
  - Single set acceptable for most children; two sets for paediatric oncology patients
  - Volume: <30 kg → 1–5 mL into paediatric aerobic (pink) bottle; >30 kg → 8–10 mL aerobic + 8–10 mL anaerobic if possible
  - Strict aseptic technique; 30 seconds drying time after disinfection
- **Blood glucose (BGL):** If BGL <3.3 mmol/L:
  - IV/IO bolus 2 mL/kg 10% glucose, OR
  - Glucagon IM (if no IV/IO): ≤25 kg → 0.5 mg; >25 kg → 1 mg
- **Urine sample** before antibiotics if possible — do not delay treatment for this

#### Antibiotics

**First-line: Ceftriaxone 50 mg/kg IV (maximum 2 g).**
- If IV not yet established: give IM instead. Divide doses >1 g across multiple sites.
- **Penicillin/cephalosporin allergy:**
  - Do not delay treatment based on penicillin allergy history — delay causes greater harm.
  - Ceftriaxone is safe in the vast majority of patients with a penicillin allergy label, including penicillin anaphylaxis. **Give ceftriaxone.**
  - **Exception:** If clearly documented serious delayed hypersensitivity to penicillin or cephalosporin (Stevens-Johnson syndrome / toxic epidermal necrolysis) — seek urgent specialist advice before giving.

#### IV Fluid Resuscitation

**20 mL/kg bolus of IV sodium chloride 0.9%:**
- Use a three-way tap: saline bag → giving set → three-way tap → cannula. Withdraw with 20 mL syringe and push, repeating until dose given.
- Can start with pre-filled 10 mL saline syringes while setting up.
- Reassess after bolus. If hypotension or poor perfusion persists → give further 10–20 mL/kg bolus.

### Amber Flag Sepsis

- If >1 amber flag → lower threshold for treating as Red Flag Sepsis.
- Consider seeking urgent paediatric or emergency advice.
- Manage underlying infection source per relevant pathway.
- Community management (if appropriate):
  - Period of observation with urgent investigations and treatment
  - Planned review at 24–48 hours
  - Written advice to parents/carers specific to presentation

## Post-Recovery Support

- Debrief patient and family about sepsis. Key messages:
  - Most children recover completely, but some have ongoing challenges (more likely after ICU or prolonged admission)
  - Physical symptoms may include: headaches, pain, fatigue, poor sleep, appetite changes, weakness
  - Cognitive/psychological symptoms may include: behaviour changes, concentration difficulties, fear of medical treatment
  - Recovery takes time; support is available (Sepsis Trust NZ)
- **Follow-up appointment at 1 month post-discharge**
- **Consider post-sepsis syndrome (PSS)** — lasts 6–18 months in some. No specific treatment; address each symptom individually:
  - Physical: fatigue, poor appetite, insomnia, repeated infections, joint/muscle pain, sweating, hair loss
  - Psychological: poor concentration, mood swings, memory loss, anxiety, depression, PTSD, nightmares
- If significant ongoing symptoms: consider referral for non-acute paediatric medicine assessment
- Offer Health Improvement Practitioner or Health Coach support if available
