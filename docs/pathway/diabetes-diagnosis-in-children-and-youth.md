# Diabetes Diagnosis in Children and Youth

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial diagnosis and acute management of suspected diabetes in children under 15 years of age. Early recognition is critical — undiagnosed diabetes can progress rapidly to the life-threatening complication of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA).

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately if your child has a high blood glucose (above 11 mmol/L) combined with any of:

- Blood ketones above 0.6 mmol/L (finger-prick test)
- Breath that smells like nail polish remover (ketone smell)
- Nausea and/or vomiting
- Signs of dehydration
- Fast heart rate (tachycardia)
- Abdominal pain
- Fast or laboured breathing — rapid and shallow breathing, or deep sighing breaths (this can be mistaken for asthma)
- Confusion, reduced alertness, or loss of consciousness

These are signs of diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), which requires urgent ambulance transfer to hospital.

---

## Background

- **Diabetes mellitus** is diagnosed when blood glucose is above 11.1 mmol/L (non-fasting, random) or above 7 mmol/L (fasting).
- **Type 1 diabetes** makes up approximately 90% of childhood diabetes. It affects about 1 in 750 children and is increasing by 3–5% per year. It can occur from infancy through adulthood — the peak age of diagnosis is during childhood.
- Most children diagnosed with type 1 diabetes have no family history of the disease, although family history does increase risk.
- **Diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA)** occurs in around 25% of new diagnoses, and is especially common in children under 5 years and in teenagers.
- **Type 2 diabetes** in children is increasing alongside childhood obesity, particularly in Māori, Pacific, and South Asian children. It is typically diagnosed between ages 11 and 20, usually after the onset of puberty.
- **Other causes of high blood glucose** in children include:
  - Transient hyperglycaemia with acute illness or stress (may indicate prediabetes — follow-up is important, especially with a family history of diabetes)
  - Medications (e.g., corticosteroids)
  - Cystic fibrosis (which may progress to cystic fibrosis-related diabetes)
  - Maturity onset diabetes of the young (MODY) — a rarer inherited form caused by a single-gene mutation. Usually presents before age 25 and is often misdiagnosed as type 1 or type 2 diabetes. Genetic testing guides treatment, and insulin may or may not be needed.

## Assessment

Your doctor will suspect diabetes in any child with unexplained weight loss, excessive thirst, frequent urination, new bedwetting, or waking at night to drink or urinate.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

**Symptoms of diabetes:**
- Excessive thirst (polydipsia)
- Frequent urination (polyuria)
- Weight loss
- Drinking or passing urine during the night
- New or returning bedwetting
- Increased appetite (polyphagia)
- Excessive tiredness or general unwellness
- Recurrent infections (e.g., thrush, urinary tract infections)
- In infants or toddlers: poor weight gain may be the earliest sign

**Symptoms of DKA** (as listed above under Red Flags)

**Risk factors for type 2 diabetes:**
- Overweight (BMI at or above 85th percentile) or obesity (BMI at or above 95th percentile)
- Mother had diabetes or gestational diabetes during the pregnancy
- First-degree relative with type 2 diabetes
- Physical signs of insulin resistance (e.g., acanthosis nigricans — darkened, thickened skin in body folds)
- Related conditions: polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS), high blood pressure, high cholesterol, fatty liver disease, small size at birth, recurrent thrush or UTI
- Use of antipsychotic medications
- High-risk ethnic background: Māori, Pacific Islander, South Indian, Filipino or other South East Asian, North African, Latino, Middle Eastern, Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander

### Examination

Your doctor will assess:

- If the child is unwell: hydration status, circulation, and vital signs (heart rate, breathing rate, blood pressure)
- If the child is well: growth measurements (height and weight)

**Typical vital sign ranges by age:**

|-----|-----------------|-------------------------------|---------------------|
| Term newborn | 120–170 | 25–60 | 60–95 |
| 1 year | 105–150 | 20–45 | 70–105 |
| 2 years | 95–150 | 20–40 | 70–105 |
| 4 years | 80–150 | 17–30 | 75–110 |
| 6 years | 75–140 | 16–30 | 80–115 |
| 8 years | 70–130 | 16–30 | 80–115 |
| 10 years | 60–130 | 15–25 | 85–120 |
| 12 years | 65–120 | 15–25 | 90–120 |
| 14 years | 60–115 | 14–25 | 90–125 |

*Adapted from The Royal Children's Hospital, Melbourne.*

**Signs of dehydration to look for:**

| Severity | Symptoms | Signs |
|----------|----------|-------|
| Mild | Thirsty, restless | Slightly dry mouth |
| Moderate | Lethargic, irritable | Dry mucous membranes, sunken eyes, decreased skin elasticity, reduced urine output, ketone smell on breath |
| Severe | Limp, drowsy | Altered consciousness, shock (rapid weak pulse, cool skin, low blood pressure) |

Note: In DKA, typical signs of dehydration may not be obvious.

### Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- **Finger-prick blood glucose** — the most important and immediate test. Your child should wash and dry their hands before the test. Waiting for a laboratory result can cause dangerous delays in diagnosis.
- **Urine test** — checked for glucose (above 1 mmol/L is abnormal) and ketones.
- **HbA1c** — only if type 2 diabetes is suspected, the child appears well, and the finger-prick test does not show elevated blood glucose.

Your doctor will not delay referral for additional blood tests unless specifically advised by a specialist.

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following approach depending on what is found:

1. **If DKA is suspected** (high blood glucose plus any DKA symptoms): Your doctor will arrange urgent ambulance transfer to hospital. Intravenous fluids are only given before transport if the child is in shock. Management before transfer follows national DKA guidelines.

2. **If new-onset diabetes is suspected** (finger-prick glucose above 10 mmol/L non-fasting, or above 7 mmol/L fasting; or glucose and ketones both present in urine): Your doctor will refer urgently to the hospital paediatric diabetes team, even if the child does not appear severely unwell.

3. **If the child is well and the finger-prick glucose is normal, but HbA1c is 48 mmol/mol or higher**: Your doctor will arrange a non-urgent specialist paediatric diabetes assessment.

4. **If uncertain or considering other causes of high blood glucose**: Your doctor will seek specialist phone advice from the paediatric diabetes team before deciding next steps.

All children and young people newly diagnosed with diabetes will be fully investigated and have their ongoing management arranged by the specialist paediatric endocrinology and diabetes service.
