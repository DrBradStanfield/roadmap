# Undifferentiated Neurological Symptoms

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach when a patient has neurological symptoms that don't yet have a clear diagnosis — helping determine whether a significant underlying condition is present.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if you have:

- Progressive neurological symptoms that are worsening rapidly
- Altered or reduced level of consciousness
- Evolving neurological signs

---

## Background

Neurological symptoms that don't fit a clear pattern are common and can be challenging to diagnose. Taking time — often across several appointments — is important. Patients who feel heard and are given compassionate care tend to have better long-term outcomes.

**Functional neurological disorder (FND)** accounts for 20–30% of new patients seen in neurology clinics. In FND, the nervous system is structurally normal but not functioning correctly — investigations are normal, yet symptoms are real and can be disabling. Past or ongoing trauma can be a trigger.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history — this is central to diagnosis and may take more than one appointment.

**Symptoms your doctor will ask about:**

- Headache, facial pain, back pain, or neck pain
- Loss of consciousness — seizures (fits), fainting, or "funny turns"
- Altered thinking — memory problems, difficulty concentrating, or reduced alertness
- Sensory changes:
  - Paraesthesia — reduced or absent sensation
  - Unusual sensations — pins and needles, tingling, prickling, electric shocks
  - Numbness (your doctor will clarify exactly what you mean: loss of sensation, heaviness, tingling, or loss of function)
- Loss of coordination or dexterity
- Motor changes — paralysis, weakness, involuntary movements, or tremor
- Abnormal gait (the way you walk)
- Vertigo or dizziness
- Changes in vision, hearing, smell, speech, or swallowing
- Fatigue
- Sleep disturbance
- Bladder or bowel problems

**Your doctor will also ask about:**

- When symptoms started, how long they last, what makes them better or worse, and whether they are constant, variable, or come and go
- Whether symptoms are **non-variable** (progressing steadily — more likely to suggest a structural neurological condition) or **variable/intermittent** (affecting multiple areas with normal examination findings — may suggest functional neurological disorder)
- Pre-existing neurological conditions or other chronic conditions (e.g. fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, irritable bowel syndrome)
- Family history of neurological disorders
- Medications (including over-the-counter and supplements), chemical exposures, animal contact, or recent overseas travel
- Impact on work, daily activities, and ability to care for others
- Past or current trauma — this may be difficult to raise, but is clinically relevant

**Physical examination typically includes:**

- Vital signs — temperature, pulse, blood pressure
- Level of consciousness and signs of meningism (neck stiffness)
- Fundoscopy (looking into the eyes) to assess for raised pressure in the brain
- Full neurological examination:
  - Muscle tone, reflexes, power, and coordination
  - Cerebellar signs — nystagmus (eye movements), ataxia, and coordination tests
  - Sensation testing
  - Tremor assessment
  - Gait and posture
- Cognitive assessment using the Mini-Addenbrooke's Cognitive Examination (Mini-ACE) if indicated — scored out of 30, with lower scores suggesting cognitive impairment

**Concerning examination features your doctor will look for:**

- Sudden-onset neurological symptoms
- Rapidly progressive symptoms (faster progression = more concerning)
- Symptoms affecting basic functions — breathing, swallowing, gait, bladder or bowel control
- Bilateral (both-sided) limb weakness

## Possible Diagnoses

### Common Conditions

- **Stress or anxiety** — can cause a wide range of physical neurological-type symptoms
- **Epilepsy** — seizures with a wide variety of presentations
- **Migraine** — can cause neurological symptoms beyond headache
- **Peripheral neuropathy** — nerve damage causing numbness, tingling, or weakness, often in hands and feet
- **Parkinson's disease** — tremor, rigidity, and movement slowness
- **Functional neurological disorder (FND):**
  - 3 times more common in adult females than males; also occurs in children and adolescents
  - Symptoms are real but arise from how the nervous system is functioning, not from structural damage
  - FND is **not** a diagnosis of exclusion — positive signs include symptoms that vary with distraction and are inconsistent with recognized neurological conditions (e.g. "collapsing weakness," Hoover's sign)
  - Often coexists with other functional conditions (chronic pain, fibromyalgia, chronic fatigue syndrome, IBS)
  - May be linked to past or ongoing trauma
- **Hyperventilation syndrome** — can cause profound neurological symptoms (tingling, dizziness, weakness); responds well to breathing retraining and education
- **Sleep deprivation or sleep disorders** — poor sleep can mimic brain disorders, causing memory and concentration problems; consider obstructive sleep apnoea, insomnia, or shift work patterns

### Rarer Conditions

- **Multiple sclerosis (MS)** — consider if two episodes of focal neurological symptoms consistent with demyelination occur at least 1 month apart. Symptoms typically worsen over days, stabilise, then gradually resolve. May involve visual symptoms (optic neuritis: monocular vision loss with eye movement pain), motor weakness, sensory impairment, vertigo, balance problems, or bladder/bowel/sexual dysfunction.
- **Motor neurone disease (MND)** — progressive weakness affecting speech, swallowing, and limb movement
- **Guillain-Barré syndrome (GBS)** — progressive, fairly symmetric ascending muscle weakness with absent or reduced deep tendon reflexes; can follow viral illness or Campylobacter infection
- **Myasthenia gravis** — chronic autoimmune condition with subacute to acute muscle weakness and fatigability; early symptoms often include drooping eyelids and double vision; approximately 80% have positive antibodies (AChR-Ab or MuSK-Ab)
- **Myositis** (including dermatomyositis, polymyositis, inclusion body myositis, drug-induced) — progressive muscle weakness, sometimes with pain or tenderness; elevated CK on blood tests
- **Myelopathy** — spinal cord dysfunction from trauma, malignancy, infarction, haemorrhage, infection, inflammation (e.g. MS), or B12 deficiency

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following approaches based on whether your symptoms are non-variable or variable/intermittent:

**If symptoms are non-variable and progressively worsening:**

- Your doctor will refer you for a non-urgent neurology or general medicine assessment.

**If symptoms are variable or intermittent:**

- Your doctor will arrange follow-up appointments to reassess over time.
- If no serious neurological condition is identified, your doctor will reassure you accordingly.
- If FND is the likely diagnosis, your doctor may:
  - Validate your experience — "Your symptoms are real and your level of distress is understandable."
  - Name the diagnosis: "You have a functional neurological disorder."
  - Explain that the nervous system is capable of functioning normally, but isn't doing so right now — and that because there is no structural damage, symptoms can improve. A "hardware vs. software" analogy is sometimes used.
  - Refer you for a non-urgent neurology assessment (which allows access to allied health treatment services).
- For hyperventilation syndrome: education and breathing retraining techniques are effective.
- Chronic pain management if relevant.
- Psychological support may be considered (typically private options).

**Allied health services your doctor may refer you to:**

- **Occupational therapy** — for safety concerns, independence at home, or daily activity difficulties
- **Physiotherapy** — for mobility problems
- **Speech language therapy** — for speech or swallowing difficulties

**Driving:**

- Your doctor will consider whether your symptoms affect driving safety, and may request a formal driving assessment if concerned.
