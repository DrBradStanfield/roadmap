# Elder Abuse and Neglect

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the identification and initial management of elder abuse and neglect in older adults, including how to recognise signs, assess risk, and access support.

---

## Background

Elder abuse is a single or repeated act — or failure to act — within any relationship where there is an expectation of trust, that causes harm or distress to an older person.

Under the Crimes Amendment Act 2011, a person who has frequent contact with a vulnerable adult and knows they are at risk of harm must take reasonable and necessary steps to protect them from that risk.

Research suggests 2 to 5% of older people may be victims of elder abuse. Very few people volunteer information about their abuse, and most do not have obvious signs or symptoms.

### Types of elder abuse

- **Partner abuse** — abuse or neglect within a life-long or recent partnership
- **Institutional abuse** — policy, practice, or lack thereof in residential care or hospital settings resulting in abuse or neglect
- **Abuse by discrimination** — behaviour perceived as dishonouring or insulting, including ageist attitudes and disrespect
- **Structural or systemic abuse** — social or economic policies that marginalise older people
- **Self-neglect** — failure to provide basic necessities, or refusal to accept help, resulting in physical, psychological, material, or social harm
- **Intentional or unintentional neglect**

Older people with dementia or difficulty communicating are more vulnerable to abuse.

---

## Key Risk Factors

**In the older person:**
- Aged over 75 years
- Has dementia
- Is dependent on a carer for personal care

**In the carer:**
- Has depression or other mental illness
- Is financially dependent on the older adult

**Other:**
- Substance abuse by the older person or the carer

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will be alert to the possibility of abuse, particularly when carer stress is present. The patient should be interviewed alone or with a support person of their choice.

### General questions your doctor may ask

- How are things going at home or in residential care?
- How are you spending your days?
- How do you feel about the amount of help you are getting?
- How do you feel your close relatives or caregivers are managing?
- Do you have everything you need to take care of yourself?

### More direct questions

- Has anyone at home ever hurt you?
- Are you afraid of anyone?
- Are you alone a lot?
- Has anyone ever taken anything of yours without your consent?
- Has anyone ever touched you without your consent?
- Has anyone ever scolded or threatened you?
- Have you ever signed documents you didn't understand?
- Has anyone ever failed to help you take care of yourself when you needed help?

## Signs and Symptoms

### Behavioural signs in the older person

- Appears afraid of a particular person
- Worried or anxious for no obvious reason
- Irritable or easily upset
- Depressed, withdrawn, or has lost interest in activities
- Suicidal thoughts
- Sleep disturbances or changed eating habits
- Frequent shaking, trembling, or crying
- Rigid posture
- Uses contradictory statements (not due to mental confusion)
- Reluctant or hesitant to talk openly; waits for the caregiver to answer
- Avoids physical, eye, or verbal contact with caregiver

### Signs the abuser may exhibit

- Blames the older person for their behaviour (e.g., wandering, incontinence)
- Unwilling to let the older person be interviewed alone
- Refuses treatment on the older person's behalf
- Seeks medical attention from multiple different doctors or centres
- Responds defensively, makes excuses, is hostile or evasive
- Is excessively concerned or seemingly unconcerned
- Minimal eye, physical, or verbal contact (noting this can vary with culture)
- Treats the older person like a child
- Uses threats, insults, or harassment
- Controls the older person's money or resources
- Has difficulty managing their own life

### Physical injuries

In the absence of a verifiable cause:

- Central injuries to the head, face, neck, chest, breast, abdomen, or genitals
- Bilateral injuries, injuries to multiple sites, or grip marks
- Bruises, burns, lacerations, abrasions, stab wounds, human bites, fractures (particularly nose, orbits, and spiral wrist fractures)
- Complaints of acute or chronic pain without evidence of tissue injury
- Multiple injuries in different stages of healing
- Tufts of hair pulled out
- Pressure injuries
- Delay in seeking treatment for an injury

### Sexual abuse

- Sexual assault, including unwanted sexual contact by a partner
- Bruising, bleeding, pain, or itching in the genital area
- Sexually transmitted disease
- Recoiling from being touched
- Fear of bathing or toileting

### Psychological abuse

- Resignation, fear, or shame
- Mental confusion
- Marked passivity
- Anger
- Insomnia

### Financial or material abuse

- Failure to pay rent or bills on behalf of the older person
- Sale of property by an older person who seems confused about the reasons
- Apparent inability to afford food, clothing, housing, or social activities
- Depletion of savings or disappearance of possessions
- Finances of a seemingly competent older person managed by another without clear reason
- Signs of an enduring power of attorney being misused for personal gain
- Signatures on documents or cheques that don't resemble the older person's signature
- The older person is reluctant to make a will or accept budget advice

### Active or passive neglect

- Malnourishment, dehydration, or hypothermia
- Weight loss with no apparent medical cause
- Pallor, sunken eyes or cheeks
- Injuries that have not been properly cared for
- Poor personal hygiene
- Clothing in poor repair or inappropriate for the weather
- Lack of safety precautions or supervision
- Absence of appropriate dentures, glasses, or hearing aids
- Abandoned or left unattended for long periods
- Medicines not purchased or administered
- No social, cultural, intellectual, or physical stimulation

### Self-neglect

- Absence of basic hygiene and personal care; body odour
- Reclusive behaviour
- Inappropriate eating habits
- Malnourished or dehydrated
- Unhygienic or unhealthy living environment
- Collecting or hoarding
- Inappropriate or unusual clothing
- Inability or refusal to pay bills
- Fiercely guards independence and privacy

## Risk Stratification

If abuse is disclosed, your doctor will assess the level of risk:

- **Low risk** — complaint of abuse or strong evidence, but no immediate danger
- **Medium risk** — low-level neglect or abuse that is increasing; few external supports available but currently safe
- **High risk** — evidence of life-threatening injuries, or risk of homicide, suicide, or self-harm

Your doctor will also consider risk to other individuals in the same household or institution.

---

## Management Overview

**If there is immediate danger, call 111 (police/emergency services).**

Your doctor will:

- Reassure the patient that strict confidentiality will be maintained unless there is risk to them or others
- Provide emotional support — listening, acknowledging, and validating the patient's experience ("this is not your fault")
- Respect the patient's right to refuse assistance; if help is declined, support continues
- Consider prevention if key risk factors are present
- Offer carer education and support
- Discuss respite care options where appropriate

### Managing by risk level

- **High risk** — urgent referral or hospital assessment if the patient cannot safely return home
- **Medium risk** — non-acute older person's health assessment; may include referral to Age Concern
- **Low risk** — referral to Age Concern; ongoing GP support

### If abuse is occurring in a rest home or care facility

Your doctor may discuss concerns with the facility manager, consult Age Concern, or make a complaint to the [Health and Disability Commissioner](https://www.hdc.org.nz/making-a-complaint/).

### If abuse is not disclosed

Your doctor will reassure you that the question was asked because elder abuse affects health and is asked of many patients — the practice remains a safe place to discuss this at any time.

### Documentation

Your doctor will document the patient's health and functional status, the relationship of the alleged abuser to the patient, current supports, and a detailed history and injury record (which may be required for medico-legal purposes).

**Who can be an abuser?** Analyses of elder abuse cases found family members are most often identified — particularly adult sons or daughters (40% of abusers) or spouses/partners (15%). However, abusers can include any person in contact with an older person: partners, relatives, friends, neighbours, health care providers, care workers, facility managers, volunteers, or legal guardians.
