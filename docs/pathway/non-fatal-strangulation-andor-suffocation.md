# Non-Fatal Strangulation and/or Suffocation

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways (last reviewed December 2024)*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of patients who have experienced strangulation (pressure to the neck) and/or suffocation (obstruction to the upper airway). It is not equivalent to a forensic assessment by a specialist examiner.

---

## When to Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department immediately (call 111) if:

- Reduced level of consciousness, confusion, or a compromised airway
- Possible or actual loss of consciousness (LOC) at the time of the event
- Current symptoms related to possible or actual loss of consciousness
- Significant neck pain, difficulty swallowing (dysphagia), or difficulty speaking (dysarthria) — seek emergency advice about management
- Any other serious injuries or acute medical conditions requiring urgent attention

---

## Background

Strangulation (sometimes called choking) and suffocation are common forms of intimidation, particularly in family and intimate partner violence (IPV). In New Zealand, non-fatal strangulation is a separate criminal charge under the Crimes Act 1961.

Patients who have been strangled face a high risk of serious injury and a significantly increased risk of future homicide. **The risk of homicide increases by 800% for victims who have previously been strangled by a partner.**

Māori are overrepresented as victims of physical and/or sexual IPV. The lifetime risk of physical and/or sexual IPV is 49% for Māori women and 31% for NZ European women.

Patients may self-present, be referred from other agencies, or be seen after police involvement. Medical records documenting injuries may be important to support any future legal proceedings.

---

## Assessment

### What to Expect

Primary care clinicians provide care to patients who have experienced strangulation and/or suffocation. They are not expected to be forensic experts. If legal or criminal proceedings result, the health practitioner's role is as a generalist, not as a forensic expert.

Patients may be withdrawn or irritable, with poor recall of events. It is common for patients not to recognise their symptoms or to minimise them. Your doctor will:

- Create a confidential and safe space for discussion
- Define the limits of confidentiality (information may be shared to prevent serious harm)
- Determine what support you have and whether you can be safely managed in the community
- Discuss any risks around patient portal access to notes

Patients aged 18 and older have the right to decide whether to make a police report or seek counselling, and when or if to do so.

### History

Your doctor will ask about the incident, including:

- How pressure to the neck or obstruction to the airway was applied (e.g., one or two hands, forearm, object, face covering)
- Whether the attack was from the front or behind
- Whether there was any pressure on the neck or throat, and whether you could breathe properly
- How long the episode lasted (recall is often imprecise) and how many times it occurred
- Symptoms at the time or since: neck pain, headache, voice changes, hearing or vision changes, difficulty swallowing, breathing, or speaking
- Any loss of consciousness, feeling faint, memory gap, confusion, drowsiness, or loss of bladder or bowel control
- Any attempted self-protection (hitting, scratching, biting)

If the patient is pregnant, fetal well-being will be considered and obstetric advice sought if needed.

### Examination Findings

Your doctor will examine you carefully in good lighting. Important to know: **only 50% of strangulation cases have visible findings on initial examination.** A normal-appearing exam does not rule out injury.

Findings that may be recorded include:

- Hoarse or raspy voice, cough
- Difficulty or pain on swallowing
- Redness or swelling of the neck
- Pinpoint bruises above the neck (scalp, face, eyelids, whites of the eyes, palate, ears)
- Scratches, grazes, or bruising around the neck, under the chin, or behind the ears
- Any other injuries

### Documentation

Accurate documentation is essential and may be used in future legal proceedings. Your doctor will:

- Note the time and date of examination
- Record your description of the incident in your own words
- Use body diagrams to map injuries
- Consider taking photographs (with your consent) if secure digital storage is available; if police are involved, police photography is recommended

### Risk Assessment

Your doctor will consider your safety, particularly if the event involved intimate partner violence, family violence, or elder abuse. They may ask:

- Where the abuser is now
- Whether you feel afraid to go home or stay home
- Whether the physical violence has escalated in frequency or severity
- Whether children have witnessed or experienced violence
- Whether the abuser has made threats of homicide or suicide
- Whether there is alcohol or substance use involved
- Whether there are weapons in the home

## Management

### Emergency Department Referral

Your doctor will refer you to the emergency department if you have:

- Reduced consciousness, confusion, or airway compromise
- Possible or actual loss of consciousness related to the event
- Significant neck pain, difficulty swallowing, or difficulty speaking
- Other injuries requiring urgent care

### Community Management

If you are alert, oriented, and have no signs of airway compromise (with or without superficial neck injuries), your doctor will:

- Record and document your injuries
- Check whether your home environment is safe
- Provide information on managing strangulation and/or suffocation injuries (MEDSAC: Strangulation and Suffocation Discharge Information)
- Offer referrals to community support services

### If Sexual Assault Was Also Involved

If the strangulation or suffocation occurred in the context of sexual assault, your doctor will discuss additional specialist assessment options with you. Specialist non-acute medical forensic assessments are available in some cases through services located throughout New Zealand.

### Ongoing Care

Your doctor will:

- Offer referrals to community supports and culturally appropriate services (e.g., Hauora Māori, Pacific Health Services)
- Reassess your safety if the event was related to family violence; if there are safety concerns, immediate intervention will be sought
- Seek obstetric advice if you are pregnant

### Police Reports and Your Notes

If police request a copy of your clinical notes, your doctor will seek your consent before releasing any records. Your doctor is only expected to report facts, not to give expert forensic opinion. Your privacy is the primary obligation.
