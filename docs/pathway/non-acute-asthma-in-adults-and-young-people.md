# Non-acute Asthma in Adults and Young People

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway applies to patients aged 12 years and older. It covers how doctors diagnose and manage asthma outside of acute attacks — including which inhalers to use, how to step up or down treatment, and when specialist input is needed.

---

## Background

- The use of **salbutamol (SABA) alone without an inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) is no longer recommended** — it results in poorer outcomes.
- **Anti-inflammatory reliever (AIR) therapy** uses a combined ICS/formoterol inhaler that provides both maintenance and acute relief, regardless of severity. A separate SABA is not required.
- The **SMART protocol** uses an AIR inhaler as both regular maintenance and reliever therapy for moderate to severe asthma. The only inhalers licensed for AIR therapy (as-needed or SMART) are dry powder inhalers (DPI) containing budesonide and formoterol.

## Assessment

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- **Typical asthma symptoms**: wheeze, breathlessness, chest tightness, and cough
- Features that make asthma more likely:
  - Intermittent symptoms with diurnal, seasonal variation
  - Triggers: allergens, irritants, exercise, medications or supplements
  - Positive response to treatment in the past
  - Family history of asthma
  - History of atopy (hay fever, eczema), rhinosinusitis
  - Childhood asthma symptoms
- Smoking history — including vaping, cannabis, or other drugs
- Occupational exposures (high-risk work includes baking, woodwork, spray painting, welding, laboratory animal work, healthcare, farming, food/chemical/textile processing)
- Factors affecting access to care (e.g. socio-economic situation, housing)

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will check:

- **Cardiorespiratory system** — looking for atypical findings (crackles, clubbing, cyanosis, persistent non-variable breathlessness, monophonic wheeze or stridor, systemic features like weight loss or fever)
- **Signs of atopy** — swollen turbinates, transverse nasal crease, mouth breathing, under-eye swelling, eczema
- **Peak expiratory flow rate (PEFR)** — serial measurements varying by >15% over time suggest asthma; also used as a marker of control

### Asthma Control

- If symptoms or reliever use occurs more than twice a week, control can be improved
- The Asthma Control Test (Adult 12+) can be used for detailed assessment

### When Asthma Is Less Likely

A diagnosis of asthma is less likely if you have:

- Chronic productive cough without wheeze or breathlessness
- No wheeze when symptomatic
- Normal spirometry or PEFR when symptomatic
- Symptoms starting later in life (especially in smokers)
- FEV1 increase after bronchodilator <12%
- PEFR variability over time <15%
- No response to a trial of asthma treatment
- Features suggesting an alternative diagnosis:
  - Obesity or poor fitness, COPD, bronchiectasis, OSA or vocal cord dysfunction
  - Heart failure, anxiety/depression, breathing pattern disorder or hyperventilation
  - ACEI-induced cough, GORD, allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis, diffuse lung disease

### Risk Factors for Severe or Fatal Asthma

**Frequent or severe exacerbations:**
- One or more exacerbations requiring oral corticosteroids in the past 12 months
- Previous ICU admission or ventilation for asthma
- Hospital admission or ED visit for asthma in the last year
- Precipitous asthma (rapid onset to severe within 4 hours)
- Raised eosinophil count
- High SABA use (>3 inhalers per year)

**Social factors:**
- Underuse of ICS treatment
- Disconnection from regular medical care
- Socio-economic disadvantage
- Poor housing (cold, damp, mouldy, or unflued gas heaters)
- Occupational asthma

**Co-morbidities:**
- Smoking, psychiatric illness (psychosis, depression), alcohol/drug abuse
- Aspirin or NSAID sensitivity
- Anaphylaxis, food allergy

### Investigations

- **Spirometry** — gold standard for airflow obstruction; perform if diagnosis is uncertain. Normal spirometry in a symptomatic patient most likely indicates an alternative diagnosis.
- **Blood eosinophil count** — check at diagnosis and if control is poor (ideally before systemic steroids):
  - High eosinophils (>0.3): correlates with eosinophilic airway inflammation; predicts response to corticosteroids; higher risk of exacerbations. Prompts: check adherence and technique, increase ICS dose, consider ultra-fine particle inhalers (e.g. Qvar) for small airway disease.
  - Low eosinophils (≤0.3) at exacerbation: suggests neutrophilic process — more likely to respond to antibiotics than corticosteroids.
  - Suspect allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis if: eosinophils >1.0, total IgE >1000, raised *Aspergillus*-specific IgE.
- **Chest X-ray** — if diagnosis unclear, poor control, or frequent exacerbations (to assess alternative diagnoses or co-morbidities)
- **Bronchial challenge tests** — consider if: diagnosis uncertain; specific employment (armed forces, fire service, police); scuba diving assessment; investigating exercise-induced bronchospasm

## Management

> **Key principle: All people with asthma should be treated with an ICS.** Salbutamol alone without ICS is no longer recommended.

### Inhaler Step Therapy — AIR Therapy (Preferred Approach)

Use a **budesonide + formoterol** dry powder inhaler licensed for as-needed symptom relief:

- **Budesonide 200 mcg + formoterol 6 mcg** — first line
- **Budesonide 100 mcg + formoterol 6 mcg** — if increased risk of steroid side-effects

| Step | Maintenance | Symptom Relief |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| **Step 1** | None | 1 actuation as required (max 8–12 per day) |
| **Step 2** | 1 actuation twice daily, or 2 actuations once daily | 1 actuation as required (max 8–12 per day) |
| **Step 3** | 2 actuations twice daily | 1 actuation as required (max 8–12 per day) |

*Dosages based on budesonide 200 mcg + formoterol 6 mcg AIR inhaler.*

- Start at **Step 1** for mild, intermittent asthma; **Step 2** for poorly controlled asthma
- **Step up** if control remains poor — after reviewing: adherence, inhaler technique, co-morbidities, alternate diagnoses
- **Step down** when low risk of flare-ups and sustained good control for ≥ 3 months
- Provide a personalised AIR asthma action plan

### Inhaler Step Therapy — Traditional ICS + SABA (Alternative)

Standard daily ICS doses:
- Budesonide 400 mcg
- Fluticasone propionate 200–250 mcg
- Fluticasone furoate 100 mcg
- Beclometasone dipropionate 400–500 mcg
- Beclometasone dipropionate extra fine 200 mcg

Plus a SABA (e.g. salbutamol) for symptom relief as needed.

| Step | Maintenance | Symptom Relief |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| **Step 1** | Standard dose ICS | SABA 1–2 puffs via spacer as required |
| **Step 2** | Standard dose ICS + LABA | SABA 1–2 puffs via spacer as required |
| **Step 3** | High dose ICS + LABA | SABA 1–2 puffs via spacer as required |

Provide a personalised asthma action plan (4 Stage for standard-dose ICS; 3 Stage for high-dose ICS).

## Self-Management

At every review, your doctor will:

- Ask about inhaler use — when, why, how often, and how often forgotten
- **Check inhaler technique** — rinse mouth after ICS to reduce oral thrush risk; consider alternative device if persistent difficulty
- Update and review your asthma action plan
- Encourage regular physical exercise and healthy nutrition
- Support a smoke-free environment

---

## Trigger Avoidance and Education

- **Workplace exposures**: avoiding or protecting against occupational triggers is essential
- **Home heating**: unflued gas heaters can worsen asthma — electric heat pumps preferred
- **Hand and cough hygiene** protect against respiratory infections
- **Allergens**: house dust mite avoidance is labour-intensive and may not significantly alter control; cat removal evidence is unclear; food sensitivities unlikely to affect control unless associated with anaphylaxis; reducing mould exposure may be worthwhile
- **Exercise-induced asthma**: generally a symptom of poor asthma control; elite athletes should be aware of anti-doping requirements
- **Breathing techniques**: Buteyko and yoga exercises can improve symptoms and quality of life and may reduce reliever use — consider respiratory physiotherapy if chronic breathing dysfunction is suspected

---

## If Occupational Asthma Is Suspected

- Lodge a gradual process ACC claim
- Request non-acute respiratory assessment

---

## Allergen Management

Consider for patients with significant allergen sensitivities:

- **Anaphylaxis risk**: food-related anaphylaxis is a risk for fatal asthma — manage appropriately (EpiPen)
- **Aspirin/NSAID sensitivity**: treat nasal polyposis; consider aspirin desensitisation via immunology
- **Allergen immunotherapy**: sublingual or subcutaneous for confirmed inhaled IgE allergies (note: time-consuming and expensive; specific aeroallergen IgE testing is unfunded)

---

## If Control Remains Poor Despite Good Management

Your doctor will:

1. **Recheck inhaler technique**
2. **Consider alternative diagnoses** and co-morbidities — especially if atypical findings (crackles, clubbing, cyanosis, monophonic wheeze, weight loss, fever, never had raised eosinophils despite exacerbations)
3. **Request respiratory nursing** for community-based support and education
4. **Seek respiratory advice** if uncertain about management
5. **Request non-acute respiratory assessment** for possible add-on therapies:
   - Montelukast (aspirin-sensitive asthma)
   - Additional high-dose ICS, oral corticosteroids, theophylline, azithromycin
   - LAMA (e.g. tiotropium — not funded unless COPD-Asthma; glycopyrronium; aclidinium)
   - Biologicals (omalizumab, mepolizumab) for high IgE or eosinophils — **specialist and Special Authority required**

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor will refer you to a respiratory specialist if:

- You've had a **severe exacerbation or hospitalisation**
- Diagnosis is **unclear** or clinical findings are atypical
- **Spirometry is unexpectedly abnormal**
- **Chest X-ray shows abnormal shadowing**
- **Allergic bronchopulmonary aspergillosis** is suspected
- Marked peripheral blood eosinophilia (>1 × 10⁹/L)
- **Control remains poor** despite optimal management
- Bronchial challenge tests are needed (specific employment, scuba diving)
- Immunology assessment for aspirin desensitisation or allergen immunotherapy

---

## Important Notes

- This pathway reflects local New Zealand clinical guidelines and is intended to support conversations with your own doctor.
- Treatment decisions — including which inhaler step to use, whether to refer, and whether to add-on therapies — should be made by your doctor based on your full clinical picture.
- If you are having an **acute asthma attack**, this pathway does not apply — seek emergency care.
