# Persistent Wet Cough in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway applies to children with a persistent cough (most days for more than 4 weeks) and does not apply to children with acute respiratory symptoms.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Care

Your doctor will arrange urgent assessment if:

- Tuberculosis (TB) is suspected — requires same-day paediatric assessment
- There are signs of significant respiratory distress

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Whether the cough has been persistent or is recurring episodes of acute cough (young children may normally have 4–10 respiratory infections per year)
- History of significant respiratory illness — respiratory admission aged 2 years or under, pneumonia, recurrent respiratory admissions, or focal changes on chest X-ray
- Possible postnasal drip from allergic or infective rhinosinusitis
- Home environment — whether the home is warm, dry, and not overcrowded, and whether there is tobacco smoke exposure in the home or car

Physical examination typically includes:

- Temperature, pulse, and respiratory rate
- Ears, nose, and throat
- Chest auscultation (listening with a stethoscope)
- Peak expiratory flow rate if wheeze is detected
- Growth assessment — checking the child is thriving
- Looking for significant signs such as stridor (noisy breathing), cyanosis (bluish discolouration), and finger clubbing
- Asking the child to cough to assess whether it is wet, dry, or barking

Your doctor may arrange a sputum culture if a good sample can be obtained (sometimes possible in children over 6 years).

---

## Possible Diagnoses

### Protracted Bacterial Bronchitis

A prolonged wet cough lasting more than 4 weeks without an identified underlying cause. There is evidence it may be a precursor to bronchiectasis in some children. Treated with a broad-spectrum antibiotic course of at least 2 weeks.

### Bronchiectasis

- More common in Pacific and Māori children than European children
- May result from a severe viral or bacterial chest infection, or frequent infections with only short symptom-free periods
- Associated with recurring wet cough after waking, following infections, and on exertion
- Chest hyperexpansion and finger clubbing are important suggestive signs, though their absence does not exclude bronchiectasis
- Immunodeficiency is a rare cause (suggested by multiple infections, especially of skin and gut, and poor growth)
- Primary ciliary dyskinesia is a rare cause (suggested by nasal discharge from early infancy, respiratory distress at birth, dextrocardia, and recurrent ear, sinus, and chest infections)

### Cystic Fibrosis

- More common in European children
- A small proportion of children with cystic fibrosis are not detected by newborn screening (the Guthrie card test)
- These children may have an asthma-like picture or bronchiectasis with clubbing
- Sputum cultures growing *Staphylococcus aureus* or *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* are suspicious for cystic fibrosis (though these can also occur in bronchiectasis)

### Aspirated Foreign Body

Consider if there is a history consistent with inhalation of an object.

### Tuberculosis

Consider in children with possible TB exposure and a cough, particularly in high-risk families. Children with active or latent TB are usually identified during contact tracing after diagnosis in an adult. Most children develop latent TB (treated with isoniazid alone). Children under 5 years with TB exposure are at higher risk of developing TB disease and are treated more aggressively.

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following:

**Antibiotic treatment** — For a genuine persistent wet cough lasting more than 4 weeks, your doctor will typically treat with antibiotics for 2 weeks before arranging further investigations (unless TB is suspected):

- First-line: amoxicillin for 2 weeks
- Second-line: amoxicillin + clavulanic acid for 2 weeks
- If penicillin-allergic: co-trimoxazole for 2 weeks (not used in infants under 6 weeks due to risk of jaundice)

Your doctor will reassess at the end of the antibiotic course.

**If cough continues or relapses after antibiotics**, your doctor may:

- Arrange a chest X-ray
- Seek paediatric specialist advice about a further antibiotic course
- Refer for non-urgent paediatric assessment

**If wheeze is also present**, your doctor will assess for asthma.

**General advice your doctor may give:**

- Annual influenza vaccination for children under 5 years
- Smoking cessation support for household members
- Frequent hand-washing by all family members during respiratory viral season
- Consider alternatives to childcare centres for very young children with recurrent infections

---

## When Your Doctor May Refer

Your doctor will consider referring to a paediatric specialist if:

- TB is suspected (urgent same-day assessment)
- No response to a 2-week antibiotic course, or the cough relapses as soon as antibiotics finish
- Suspected bronchiectasis or cystic fibrosis
- Recurring pattern of prolonged wet cough after each minor infection
- Persisting chest signs
- Finger clubbing
- Sputum cultures repeatedly positive for pathogenic bacteria, or positive for *Staphylococcus aureus* or *Pseudomonas aeruginosa*
