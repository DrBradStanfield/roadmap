# Peri-procedural Management of Warfarin, DOACs, and Antiplatelets

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers how anticoagulants and antiplatelet medicines are managed around procedures and surgery. The approach depends on the type of medicine, the risk of bleeding from the procedure, and the patient's underlying risk of blood clots.

**Always discuss any planned procedure with your doctor before stopping or changing your anticoagulant or antiplatelet medicine.** Stopping these medicines without medical guidance carries a risk of stroke, blood clot (thromboembolism), or other serious events.

---

## The Planning Approach

Your doctor will weigh up three things when planning around a procedure:

1. **Bleeding risk** — based on the type of procedure and individual patient factors (e.g., history of bleeding, supratherapeutic INR, HAS-BLED score)
2. **Thromboembolism risk** — based on why you are on anticoagulation and the nature of the surgery
3. **Type of anticoagulant** — warfarin, DOAC (direct oral anticoagulant), or antiplatelet agent

---

## Minor Procedures

For minor procedures, anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents **usually do not need to be stopped**.

Minor procedures include:
- Cataract surgery
- Diagnostic endoscopy (e.g., gastroscopy without therapeutic intervention)
- Dental extractions
- Skin lesion removal with primary closure

The clinician performing the procedure has primary responsibility for the antithrombotic management plan.

For patients on DOACs, your doctor will check that kidney function has been tested recently and that you are on the correct dose. If possible, the procedure should be timed to avoid peak drug levels.

### Dental Extractions

- **Antiplatelets (aspirin, dipyridamole):** These should not be stopped before dental procedures.
- **Warfarin:** Most dental extractions do not require stopping warfarin if local haemostatic measures are used. An INR check within 24 hours of the extraction is needed — if INR ≥ 4.0, the procedure should be deferred and INR managed first.
- **DOACs:** Evidence-based guidelines are not yet established. Studies suggest bleeding outcomes are comparable to warfarin, and DOAC interruption may be unnecessary if the procedure is at least 6 hours after the last dose.

### Skin Lesion Excision (Under Local Anaesthetic)

Patients may continue aspirin, warfarin, DOACs, or clopidogrel before minor skin surgery. The risks of stopping these medicines generally outweigh the minor bleeding risks of skin procedures. For patients on warfarin, the INR should be within the therapeutic range the day before surgery.

### Catheter Changes

If INR is in the therapeutic range, warfarin does not need to be changed. DOACs may be continued.

### Endoscopy

Anticoagulation does not generally need to be stopped for endoscopy unless the proceduralist is planning a therapeutic intervention (e.g., polypectomy, dilation, PEG insertion). The vast majority of endoscopic procedures are diagnostic only.

## Major Surgery

Before major surgery, the surgeon and anaesthetist take primary responsibility for anticoagulation management. Your GP may assist with monitoring and follow-up care.

Any operation more significant than minor surgery requires normal coagulation. The management plan depends on the anticoagulant type and the individual's bleeding and clot risk.

### Warfarin — Major Surgery

Your doctor will document a plan covering warfarin cessation, any bridging therapy, and when warfarin is restarted. This plan should be clearly communicated to you.

**If the procedure has a high bleeding risk, warfarin will usually be stopped.**

Whether bridging therapy (temporary injectable anticoagulant) is needed depends on your clot risk:

**Low clot risk:**
- Bridging therapy is unlikely to be needed.
- If you have a mechanical heart valve, your cardiologist will be consulted.

**High clot risk:**
- Warfarin will typically be substituted with unfractionated heparin (UFH) or low molecular weight heparin (LMWH) injections during the window when warfarin is stopped.
- An example plan (your surgical team will individualise this):
  1. Stop warfarin 5 days before the procedure (last dose given 6 days before)
  2. Start bridging therapy when INR falls below 2.0
  3. Stop LMWH at least 24 hours before surgery (or earlier if kidney function is impaired); stop UFH 4–6 hours before surgery
  4. Recheck INR within 24 hours of the procedure — it should be below 1.5

**Bridging therapy options (prescribed by your doctor):**

*Enoxaparin (LMWH):*
- Dose depends on kidney function (eGFR) and body weight
- If kidney function is significantly reduced (eGFR < 30) or weight > 120 kg, specialist haematology input is needed
- Standard dosing: 1.5 mg/kg subcutaneously once daily (if eGFR > 50), or 1 mg/kg once daily (if eGFR 30–50)

*Unfractionated heparin (UFH):*
- 80 units/kg intravenous loading dose, then 18 units/kg/hour by continuous infusion, adjusted to APTT
- Usually given in hospital

After surgery, warfarin should be restarted once the surgeon confirms it is safe. Your doctor will seek surgical advice on timing if there is any uncertainty.

### DOACs — Major Surgery

DOACs have a shorter half-life than warfarin, which allows for shorter periods off the medicine. However, the timing must be carefully planned.

Your doctor will document and communicate to you:
- When to take your last dose of DOAC before surgery
- When to restart your DOAC after surgery

**Timing of last dose before surgery** depends on:
- Which DOAC you take (half-life varies)
- Your kidney function
- Whether the procedure has high or low bleeding risk

**Timing of restart after surgery:**

*After higher bleeding risk surgery:*
- Therapeutic anticoagulation (full dose DOAC) can usually restart 48–72 hours after surgery
- If your clot risk is high, a lower dose DOAC or LMWH injections will be given from the evening after surgery until it is safe to restart full-dose anticoagulation
- If switching from LMWH to DOAC, start the DOAC at the time the next LMWH dose would have been due

*After lower bleeding risk surgery:*
- DOACs can usually restart about 24 hours after surgery

**Bridging therapy** is generally not required for DOAC patients unless you cannot take oral medicines for a period after surgery. If bridging is needed, the same enoxaparin or UFH options apply as for warfarin patients.

After surgery, your DOAC should be restarted as soon as the surgeon confirms it is safe.

## How Long Should You Stay on Anticoagulation?

Your doctor will review the benefit of anticoagulation against bleeding risk at least annually. The table below is a general guide — your individual circumstances may differ.

| Indication | Typical duration |
|---|---|
| DVT prevention after knee replacement | 2 weeks |
| DVT prevention after hip replacement | 5 weeks |
| Isolated distal DVT | 2 weeks to 3 months (specialist discussion) |
| Provoked DVT or PE | 3 months |
| Unprovoked DVT or PE (low–moderate bleeding risk) | Usually lifelong |
| Unprovoked DVT or PE (high bleeding risk) | 3 months |
| Recurrent unprovoked DVT or PE | Usually lifelong |
| DVT or PE with active cancer | Usually LMWH or rivaroxaban (warfarin less effective in cancer) |
| Acute coronary syndrome | Dual antiplatelet therapy for 12 months, then single antiplatelet lifelong |
| Percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) | Dual antiplatelet therapy for 3–12 months, then single antiplatelet lifelong |
| Atrial fibrillation | Usually lifelong |
| Mechanical heart valve | Lifelong — do not stop unless advised by a specialist; do not use dabigatran or rivaroxaban |
| Arterial embolism | Lifelong |

## Important Notes

- **Never stop anticoagulants or antiplatelets before a procedure without discussing it with your doctor first.**
- If you have a mechanical heart valve, specialist cardiology advice is required before any changes to anticoagulation.
- Kidney function affects DOAC dosing and bridging therapy decisions — your doctor will check this before major procedures.
- If you are on anticoagulation for cancer-related blood clots, your oncology or haematology team should be involved in the perioperative plan.
