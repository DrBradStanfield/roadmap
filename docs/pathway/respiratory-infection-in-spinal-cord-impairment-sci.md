# Respiratory Infection in Spinal Cord Impairment (SCI)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of respiratory infections in adults with spinal cord impairment (SCI). People with cervical or high thoracic SCI have very limited respiratory reserve and can deteriorate quickly. This information is a guide only — always seek medical assessment promptly.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

People with cervical or high thoracic SCI who have any of the following need emergency assessment immediately:

- Orthopnoea (difficulty breathing when lying flat)
- Tachypnoea (rapid breathing)
- Shallow or paradoxical breathing
- Use of accessory muscles (neck and shoulder muscles used for breathing)

These patients have little or no respiratory reserve. **Decompensation can happen quickly** and can lead to an urgent need for respiratory support or intubation.

---

## Why Respiratory Infection Is Serious in SCI

Respiratory complications (pneumonia, respiratory failure, pulmonary embolism) are the **leading causes of death** in people with cervical or high thoracic SCI with complete injury.

Respiratory muscle function is compromised in cervical and high thoracic SCI — this reduces the ability to breathe adequately, expand the chest, and cough. As a result:

- Hypoventilation and respiratory infection risk is increased
- Normal chest infection symptoms are worsened by weak respiratory muscles and difficulty clearing secretions

**DVT and pulmonary embolism (PE) risk** is also elevated in SCI and persists over time. Any change in mobility (e.g., bed rest due to a pressure injury or fracture) can increase this risk. Even a small PE can significantly worsen lung function.

**Important:** Oxygen saturation readings may appear adequate even when dangerous carbon dioxide retention (hypoventilation) is occurring. Your doctor will consider this.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Previous chest infections and hospital admissions
- Previous need for assisted ventilation
- Response to antibiotics in the past

Your doctor will examine:

- Respiratory rate and depth, use of accessory muscles, paradoxical breathing, orthopnoea
- Oxygen saturation (with awareness that CO₂ retention can occur even with normal saturation)
- Lung sounds — crepitus (crackling sounds) may be widespread and bi-basal if a chest infection is present

If you can produce a sputum sample, it will be sent for microscopy, culture, and sensitivity.

**If there are no signs of infection:** Your doctor will consider pulmonary embolism as a cause — especially if there has been a recent change in mobility.

---

## Management

### When Hospital Assessment Is Needed

Your doctor will arrange urgent hospital assessment and ambulance transfer if:

- You are acutely unwell
- You need supplemental oxygen to keep saturation above 92%
- You have cervical or high thoracic SCI with any red flag sign (see above)

**If you use BiPAP or other specialised respiratory equipment, take it with you to hospital.**

Your advance care plan and wishes regarding active management (including intubation and mechanical ventilation) should be clearly documented and accessible to after-hours services and ambulance.

### Antibiotic Treatment

Standard antibiotics for community-acquired pneumonia will be prescribed as appropriate. Your doctor will select treatment based on your history and any previous culture results.

### Physiotherapy and Cough Assist

Early chest physiotherapy and assisted cough (within 24 hours if needed) can help prevent hospital admission. Your doctor will arrange physiotherapy referral urgently if required.

Therapists will ensure carers and support workers are trained in the correct cough assist technique.

### Vaccinations

Your doctor will check if you are up to date with influenza and pneumococcal vaccinations.

### Planning Ahead

Your doctor may discuss developing an acute deterioration plan — so you and your support network know what to do if symptoms worsen. If advance care planning has not been done, this is worth discussing so your wishes are clear.

---

## When to Seek Help

Contact your doctor promptly if you have symptoms of a chest infection. Seek emergency care immediately if you have:

- Any of the red flag symptoms (orthopnoea, tachypnoea, shallow/paradoxical breathing, accessory muscle use)
- Oxygen saturation below 92%
- Rapidly worsening breathlessness or exhaustion

Recovery from chest infection is likely with appropriate treatment — but prompt assessment is essential.
