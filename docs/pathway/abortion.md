# Abortion

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: February 2024.*

This pathway covers early medical abortion (EMA), referral for other types of abortion, and care after an abortion.

> **Always discuss your options, symptoms, and any medicines with your doctor or abortion provider.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department if:**
- You have heavy vaginal bleeding and feel faint, dizzy, or collapse.
- You have signs of a possible **ectopic pregnancy** (a pregnancy outside the womb): abdominal or pelvic pain, vaginal bleeding, shoulder tip pain, dizziness, or fainting. Pain can come and go. Intermittent pain does not rule out an ectopic pregnancy.
- You feel very unwell with severe or persistent pelvic pain after an abortion, with or without fever or bleeding. This may be a serious infection. Sepsis is life-threatening, and early recognition and treatment are vital.
- You have significant abdominal or pelvic pain after a surgical abortion. This may mean an injury to the womb or cervix.

**Contact your doctor or abortion provider if** you have any of the concerning bleeding features listed under Follow-Up and Aftercare, ongoing pregnancy symptoms, persistent pain or cramping, or a bad-smelling vaginal discharge.

## Background

- Abortion was decriminalised in New Zealand in 2020. Key points of the law:
  - Abortion up to 20 weeks with no restrictions.
  - Self-referral to abortion providers up to 20 weeks.
  - Abortion at 20 weeks or later if clinically appropriate.
  - Pre-abortion counselling must be offered, but you do not have to accept it.
  - Abortion is not allowed for the purpose of sex selection.
- There is no lower legal age limit for having an abortion.
- A doctor who conscientiously objects to abortion must, at the earliest opportunity, make sure you know how to contact the closest abortion provider yourself.
- In 2022, 50.6% of abortions were early medical abortions (EMA) and 47.8% were surgical abortions.
- 1.6% of abortions were associated with a complication.
- People who have to travel to reach abortion services tend to have their abortion later in pregnancy.

## Assessment

### Getting care quickly

- An EMA is only offered in the community up to 10+0 weeks (≤ 70 days) of pregnancy, so timing matters.
- You can usually choose a telehealth or in-person appointment. An in-person consultation is not required for an EMA.
- If a practice cannot see you urgently (ideally within 24 hours), or will not provide the abortion, you can go directly to a local abortion provider or the [national abortion telehealth service](https://www.decide.org.nz/).
- Support people and whānau are welcome at appointments. Your doctor can discuss karakia (blessing), rongoā Māori (Māori herbal medicine), and returning fetal tissue.

### Confirming the pregnancy

- Pregnancy is confirmed with a urine or blood test, or a point-of-care ultrasound.
- Gestation (how far along the pregnancy is) is estimated from the first day of your last period, how certain that date is, your cycle length, recent hormonal contraception, and sometimes an examination or point-of-care ultrasound.
- In September 2026, some point-of-care pregnancy tests were recalled because of a small risk of false-positive results. If a positive result is unexpected, your doctor may confirm it with a laboratory test.
- Your doctor will ask about symptoms of ectopic pregnancy and examine you if needed.

### Tests your doctor may consider

A formal ultrasound scan before abortion is usually unnecessary and can delay care. Your doctor may arrange one if:
- the date of conception or your last period is uncertain
- gestation is estimated at 14 weeks or more
- your menstrual history and examination do not match
- you have a higher risk of ectopic pregnancy, such as one-sided abdominal pain with vaginal bleeding, an intrauterine device (IUD) in place when you conceived, a previous ectopic pregnancy, or previous tubal damage or sterilisation.

There are no blood tests that are essential before an abortion. Your doctor may consider:
- haemoglobin and ferritin if there is concern about anaemia
- blood group and antibody screen if the pregnancy is estimated at more than 10 weeks
- cervical screening, if you are not up to date
- a sexual health check.

Tests should not delay abortion care or referral.

**Anaemia and iron:** For women, haemoglobin lower than 115 g/L (including in pregnancy) indicates anaemia. Ferritin less than 20 micrograms/L indicates iron deficiency. Your doctor may recommend an IV iron infusion if haemoglobin is less than 100 g/L and you are iron deficient.

### Supplements while you decide

Until a decision is made about the pregnancy, your doctor may recommend:
- **Folic acid:** 0.8 mg once daily if at low risk, or 5 mg once daily if you have a higher risk, for example a personal or family history of neural tube defect, a BMI greater than 30, diabetes, coeliac disease or other malabsorption, sickle cell anaemia, epilepsy or anti-seizure medicine, or a folate antagonist medicine such as sulfasalazine.
- **Iodine:** potassium iodate 150 micrograms daily. Seaweed and kelp tablets are not recommended because their iodine levels vary and can be toxic. People who are hyperthyroid or already taking thyroxine may not need iodine, so check with your doctor.
- **Vitamin D:** colecalciferol drops (400 units/drop), 1 to 2 drops (400 to 800 units) once a day.

### Counselling and choosing an option

- Counselling must be offered, but it is not mandatory to accept it.
- Your doctor can talk through all pregnancy options with you, including the [types of abortion](https://www.decide.org.nz/en/abortion-services/abortion-types/compare-abortion-procedures/).
- Most abortion services are free for people eligible for publicly funded health care. There may be prescription or imaging costs. Others can still have an abortion but may need to pay.
- Your doctor will check that the abortion is your own wish and that you are not being pressured.

## Management

### Early medical abortion (EMA)

An EMA is an abortion at home induced by medication, up to 10+0 weeks (70 days) of pregnancy. The pregnancy must be under 10+0 weeks (70 days) at the time you take the misoprostol.

**Reasons an EMA may not be suitable (absolute contraindications):**
- Allergy to mifepristone or misoprostol
- Adrenal failure
- Poorly controlled severe asthma
- Steroid dependency
- Hereditary porphyria
- An intrauterine contraceptive device in place (the EMA can go ahead once it is removed)
- Known or suspected ectopic pregnancy

**Situations needing extra care (relative contraindications):**
- Severe anaemia
- Serious or unstable health conditions, such as ischaemic heart disease, uncontrolled epilepsy, kidney failure, or liver failure
- A known bleeding disorder, or taking anticoagulant (blood-thinning) medicines

**Precautions:** An EMA at home may not be the best choice if you have no access to a toilet, no phone or transport for emergency help, no adult support at home, or difficulty communicating with health professionals in an emergency.

**Before you start:** The medicines used in an EMA can harm a developing baby. If an EMA fails, completing the abortion is recommended.

### Taking the medicines

Your doctor or abortion provider will give you written instructions. A typical schedule is:

**Day 1:**
- Mifepristone 200 mg by mouth, ideally in the morning.
- If you already have pregnancy-related vomiting, an anti-sickness medicine (such as ondansetron) may be taken 30 to 60 minutes before the mifepristone.
- If you vomit within 1 hour of taking mifepristone, the dose is repeated.

**Day 2 to 3 (at least 24 hours, ideally 36 to 48 hours, after mifepristone):**
- One hour before the misoprostol, take paracetamol 1 g, ibuprofen 400 mg, and ondansetron 4 mg by mouth to reduce side effects and discomfort.
- Misoprostol 800 micrograms is placed buccally (between the upper gum and cheek, 2 tablets on each side) and left to dissolve for 30 to 60 minutes. Any softened tablet left can then be swallowed. Under the tongue or vaginal use is acceptable, but buccal is preferred.
- If bleeding has not started within 6 hours of the first dose, a second dose of misoprostol 400 micrograms is swallowed whole with water. Not everyone needs a second dose.
- Stronger pain relief (tramadol or codeine) may be needed.

Your provider may call you 24 hours after the planned misoprostol date to check you have taken it and bleeding has started.

See the abortion telehealth service's guide to [what to expect during an early medical abortion](https://www.decide.org.nz/en/abortion-services/abortion-types/early-medical-abortion-the-procedure/#section_what-to-expect-during-an-early-medical-abortion-nbsp).

### Other types of abortion

Your doctor may refer you, or you can refer yourself, to a local abortion provider for:
- **Later medical abortion:** similar to an EMA but for pregnancies more than 10 weeks (70 days), provided in hospital or a specialist clinic, not at home.
- **Surgical abortion:** there are virtually no absolute contraindications.
- **Abortion at more than 20 weeks:** may be provided if a qualified health practitioner reasonably believes it is clinically appropriate.
- **Abortion for fetal anomalies or genetic conditions.**
- An EMA that is not suitable for you, or that you or the practice choose not to do.

Referral should not wait for test results. Abortion services can do the full pre-abortion assessment, including tests.

### Contraception after an abortion

- A contraceptive implant (Jadelle) or injection (Depo Provera) can be given on the day mifepristone is taken. Depo Provera is associated with slightly higher EMA failure rates.
- The contraceptive pill can be started the day after the abortion.
- An intrauterine device (IUD) can be inserted as soon as it is reasonably certain you are no longer pregnant, for example after a beta-hCG drop of more than 80% 7 to 14 days after mifepristone, or a negative low-sensitivity urine pregnancy test at 3 to 4 weeks. Use another method while you wait.
- If you have a new IUD, your doctor can show you how to check for the strings.

## Follow-Up and Aftercare

### Confirming the abortion is complete

Completion after an EMA is confirmed by one of:
- A blood test showing a serum beta-hCG drop of more than 80% 7 to 14 days after administration of mifepristone.
- A negative low-sensitivity urine pregnancy test 3 to 4 weeks after the mifepristone. Do not use a standard home pregnancy test instead. Low-sensitivity tests register beta-hCG more than 1000 mIU/mL, while most commercial pregnancy tests measure to approximately 25 mIU/mL.
- An ultrasound showing no pregnancy sac (not routinely recommended).

Routine follow-up and examination are not required. Your doctor may offer follow-up if you are at risk of complications, still need contraception, may need mental health support, are young, or ask for it.

### What bleeding to expect

- Bleeding after an EMA is common 1 to 2 weeks after an abortion and can take 5 weeks to settle.
- Bleeding after a surgical abortion should generally stop by day 10.

**Concerning features (contact your doctor or abortion provider):**
- At any time: heavy bleeding (2 or more soaked pads per hour for 2 consecutive hours, or passing large clots), or feeling unwell.
- At 7 days: bleeding heavier than a normal period, bleeding with clots, or cramps not helped by simple pain relief.
- At 14 days: heavy or persistent bleeding that has not reduced.
- At 4 to 5 weeks: ongoing bleeding after your expected period.

### Emotional support

Your doctor can ask how you feel about the abortion and offer counselling or support, for example for relationships, existing mental health issues, family violence, or housing. Support is available through the [abortion telehealth service](https://decide.org.nz/en/abortion-services/get-support/) and [Whetūrangitia](https://wheturangitia.services.govt.nz/miscarriage/support-resources).

## Complications

Most complications cause ongoing bleeding and lower abdominal cramps or pelvic tenderness. Your abortion provider can often help assess and manage these.

**Symptoms:** persistent pain or cramping, vaginal bleeding, ongoing pregnancy symptoms, dizziness or fainting, and a bad-smelling vaginal discharge.

**Possible causes:**
- Retained products of conception (RPOC), also called an incomplete abortion
- Retained blood clot
- Infection, such as endometritis or pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)
- Ongoing pregnancy (failed abortion)
- Ectopic pregnancy
- Uterine injury

**Assessment:** Your doctor may check your pulse, temperature, and blood pressure, examine your abdomen and pelvis, and do a speculum examination. Tests may include vaginal swabs if infection is suspected, and a beta-hCG blood test and ultrasound if an ongoing pregnancy is possible. If beta-hCG is not falling as expected, ectopic pregnancy is considered. An ultrasound is of limited value for suspected RPOC and may look abnormal for several weeks after an abortion.

### Bleeding

- Heavy bleeding with signs of shock needs emergency hospital care. Call 111 or go to the emergency department.
- Ongoing "nuisance" bleeding in someone who is otherwise well (for example, bleeding up to 4 to 5 weeks that is getting lighter, without clots or cramping) may be retained clot or tissue, or normal recovery. Your doctor may consider:
  - waiting to see whether it settles
  - misoprostol 400 micrograms by mouth or buccally
  - surgical management through the abortion provider
  - a blood count to check for anaemia.
- If you take the combined contraceptive pill, small amounts of retained tissue may pass with the next period or withdrawal bleed.

### Infection

- Severe, persistent pelvic pain, a tender womb, and feeling very unwell (with or without fever or bleeding) may be sepsis. This needs hospital care. Call 111 or go to the emergency department.
- Milder infection (no fever and no signs of sepsis) is treated like pelvic inflammatory disease. Your doctor may consider:
  - ceftriaxone 500 mg in 2 mL of 1% lidocaine as a single injection, **and** doxycycline 100 mg twice a day for 14 days, **and** metronidazole 400 mg twice a day for 14 days.
  - If you are pregnant, breastfeeding, or may find the full course hard to complete: ceftriaxone as above, **and** azithromycin 1 g repeated 1 week later, **and** metronidazole 400 mg twice a day for 14 days.
  - For infection that is not sexually acquired after a procedure: amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 500 + 125 mg three times a day, for 14 days. With a penicillin allergy, metronidazole 400 mg twice a day plus trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole 160 + 800 mg twice a day may be used instead.

### Ongoing pregnancy

If a viable pregnancy continues, your doctor will talk through your options: repeat the EMA (if under 10 weeks), have a surgical abortion, or continue the pregnancy. The EMA medicines are considered teratogenic, and continuing carries risks such as miscarriage and malformations. If you choose to continue, your doctor will seek specialist advice and help you [find a midwife](https://www.findyourmidwife.co.nz).

### Uterine injury

Significant abdominal or pelvic pain after a surgical abortion may mean a perforation or other injury to the womb or cervix. This needs hospital assessment.

## When to Refer

**Hospital (call 111 or go to the emergency department):**
- Possible ectopic pregnancy.
- Heavy vaginal bleeding with signs of shock.
- Suspected infection with severe, persistent pelvic pain, a tender womb, and feeling very unwell.
- Significant pain after a surgical abortion suggesting uterine injury.

**Specialist gynaecology advice:**
- Troublesome vaginal bleeding for more than 2 weeks in someone who is otherwise stable with no signs of infection.
- An ongoing viable pregnancy that you wish to continue.
- An abnormal scan, or difficulty getting a scan, when complications are suspected.

**Abortion provider (urgent, and you can self-refer):**
- If your practice cannot see you quickly or will not provide an EMA.
- Later medical abortion, surgical abortion, or abortion at more than 20 weeks.
- Contraindications to EMA.
- Pre-abortion counselling.
