# Acute Bipolar Disorder

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to a new, acute presentation suggestive of bipolar disorder.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department if any of the following are present:

- Risk of harm to self, including suicidal ideation, damage to reputation, relationships, finances, or employment
- Vulnerability or sexual risk
- Risk of harm to others
- Psychosis
- Acute mania

---

## Background

Bipolar disorder is a mood disorder characterised by episodes of mania or hypomania, acute bipolar depression, and mixed episodes. Key features:

- Generally begins between the ages of 15 and 40 years and continues lifelong
- Occurs equally across all cultures
- Can be challenging to diagnose and is often initially misdiagnosed as unipolar major depression
- Most people experience multiple episodes at an average of 1 episode every 2–3 years, each lasting about 3–6 months
- Stressful life events (money or relationship worries, lack of sleep, unemployment) can trigger an episode
- Most people return to their usual level of functioning after periods of illness

### Types of Bipolar Disorder (DSM-5 / ICD-11)

- **Bipolar I** — at least one lifetime manic episode or mixed episode; depressive episodes usually occur as well
- **Bipolar II** — periods of major depression with at least one hypomanic episode
- **Cyclothymic disorder** — numerous subsyndromal hypomanic and depressive episodes over a 2-year period, none of which meet full criteria for either mania or depression

### Mania

Mania is characterised by:
- Elated mood, elevated out of keeping with circumstances
- Emotional lability (unstable affect)
- Overactivity, decreased sleep, significant distractibility, and pressured speech
- Inflated self-esteem and loss of inhibitions
- Psychosis may occur (mood-congruent or mood-incongruent), characterised by delusions or hallucinations
- Duration: ≥ 1 week, or any duration if hospitalisation is required

### Hypomania

Hypomania is characterised by:
- Persistent mild elevation of mood
- Increased energy and activity, decreased need for sleep
- Feelings of wellbeing and physical and mental efficiency
- No hallucinations or delusions
- Duration: ≥ 4 days, with less severe elevated mood than mania and minimal or no functional impairment

Hypomania does not significantly disrupt work or cause social rejection.

### Acute Bipolar Depression

The criteria are the same as for major depression, but patients are more likely to present with severe, melancholic, and/or psychotic features. There is a risk that bipolar disorder may have been previously misdiagnosed as unipolar depression.

### Mixed Episode

The criteria for a mixed episode are the same as for both mania and depression simultaneously:
- The patient may present as hopeless and suicidal but also highly energised
- Symptoms occur nearly every day for ≥ 1 week

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess for bipolar disorder through several steps:

### 1. Consider the Diagnosis

Consider bipolar disorder in any patient with acute mania or hypomania who has one or more previous affective episodes (e.g., bipolar depression, hypomania, mania, or mixed episode).

### 2. ACE Model for Mania or Hypomania

If mania or hypomania is suspected, your doctor may use the ACE model — in mania, all three of these aspects of functioning are increased:

- **Activity** — e.g., feeling energised and "wired", seeking excessive stimulation, overly goal-driven, needing less sleep
- **Cognition** — e.g., making excessively creative and grandiose plans, having difficulty discussing issues rationally
- **Emotionality** — e.g., irritable if stopped from carrying out ideas, disinhibited, offensive or insensitive to others, spending money unusually, disregarding social boundaries

### 3. Mental Health Screening

Your doctor will assess:

- Severity of symptoms
- Degree of functional impairment
- Degree of insight into current illness
- Presence of other mental health conditions (e.g., anxiety, depression)
- Risk to self or others, including:
  - Financial, sexual, and reputational risks
  - Suicide and self-harm (including from command hallucinations or delusions)
  - Accidents, driving safety, poor self-care
  - Exploitation and harm from others
  - Risk to others: threats, aggression, or violence
- Medication compliance
- Use of alcohol, supplements, prescription drugs, or drugs of abuse
- Stressors and level of support from family and community

### 4. Medical and Social History

If the patient is co-operative, your doctor will consider:

- Co-morbid disorders: alcohol and other drug (AOD) issues, anxiety, eating disorders, personality disorders (particularly borderline personality disorder), ADHD, metabolic syndrome
- Social and personal factors: family history of mental illness, developmental history, education and work history, current social situation (dependants, supports, housing, finance), recent life events, premorbid personality

### 5. Organic Causes

Your doctor will consider and examine for underlying organic (physical) causes of the presentation:

- Substance use (particularly amphetamines and cocaine)
- Medications (e.g., antiparkinsonian drugs, corticosteroids, thyroxine)
- Hyperthyroidism
- Stroke, brain tumour, encephalitis, temporal lobe epilepsy, Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome
- Frontotemporal dementia
- Delirium

### 6. Investigations

To exclude underlying causes, your doctor may arrange:

- Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)
- Urine drug screen
- STI screening including syphilis and HIV serology
- Pregnancy test
- Vitamin B12 and folate
- Brain imaging if an organic cause is suspected

To guide safe medication choices:

- Kidney and liver function
- Full blood count (FBC)
- Fasting lipid profile
- Glucose and HbA1C
- Serum prolactin (if specific indications are met)
- Calcium, phosphate, parathyroid hormone
- ECG

### 7. Corroborative Information

Where possible (especially if psychosis is suspected or there is risk of harm), your doctor may seek information from family or others about:

- Trajectory and nature of recent behaviour changes
- Pre-morbid functioning
- Level of functional impairment

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following steps depending on the severity of the presentation:

- **If there is immediate danger** to the patient, the clinician, or others — call **111** and request police assistance
- **Compulsory assessment** under the Mental Health Act may be considered if the patient poses a serious danger to health or has severely diminished capacity to care for themselves
- **Acute mental health assessment** is typically sought if there is:
  - An acute mental health crisis (moderate to high suicide risk, serious self-harm, acute mania, severe depression, or psychosis that is a danger to the patient or others)
  - Suspected mixed episode or rapid cycling bipolar disorder
  - Mania or severe depression that is a danger to the patient or others
  - Severe functional compromise affecting work or reputation
  - Co-morbid drug and alcohol use
- **Mental health specialist advice** is typically sought if:
  - Newly suspected diagnosis and the patient is not an immediate risk
  - Diagnostic uncertainty
  - Considering starting medication in the community
  - Difficult medication or co-morbid physical problems that may complicate management
- **Dependants** — your doctor will consider the needs of any dependent children who may be at risk of harm

> All decisions about diagnosis, medication, and referral should be made by your doctor. This summary is for information only.
