# Oral Iron

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This page covers oral iron supplementation for treating iron deficiency — including which preparations to use, how to take them effectively, and what to do if treatment isn't working.

> **Note:** Always discuss your diagnosis and treatment plan with your doctor. Investigating the cause of iron deficiency is essential before or alongside treatment.

---

## How Long Does Treatment Take?

Your doctor will typically prescribe oral iron for **six months** in total:

- **First 3–4 weeks:** Haemoglobin (Hb) should rise by roughly 20 g/L (about 1–2 g/L per day). This confirms the treatment is working.
- **Once Hb is back in the normal range:** Continue iron for another 3 months to fully replenish your body's stores.

If there is no improvement after 3 months, your doctor will review whether you are taking the iron correctly, check for interactions with other medications or foods, and consider other causes.

---

## Which Iron Preparation

Your doctor will choose a preparation that provides **100–200 mg of elemental iron per day**. Over-the-counter (OTC) iron products typically don't contain enough elemental iron to treat iron deficiency anaemia (IDA) and are not recommended for treatment.

| Preparation | Amount | Elemental Iron | NZ Subsidy |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ferrous fumarate | 200 mg | 65 mg | Fully funded |
| Ferrous fumarate + folic acid * | 310 mg | 100 mg | Fully funded |
| Ferrous sulphate (sustained release) | 325 mg | 105 mg | Fully funded |
| Ferrous sulphate with folic acid (sustained release) * | 325 mg | 105 mg | Partially funded |
| Ferrous sulfate + ascorbate sodium (sustained release) ** | 325 mg | 105 mg | Not funded |
| Ferrous sulphate liquid | 150 mg / 5 ml | 30 mg / 5 ml | Fully funded |

\* Contains 350 micrograms folic acid
\** Contains 562.4 mg ascorbate sodium

**Enteric coated or sustained release preparations** tend to cause fewer side effects, but are absorbed less well than standard tablets. Your doctor will weigh up the trade-offs.

**Ferrous fumarate** is often preferred because it can be taken 2–3 times per day, which improves absorption.

---

## How to Take Iron for Best Absorption

- Take iron tablets **on an empty stomach**, or with **vitamin C-rich food or juice** — this significantly improves absorption.
  - Good vitamin C sources: orange, mandarin, grapefruit, kiwifruit, blackcurrants, strawberries, raspberries, orange juice.
- Avoid the following for **at least 1 hour before or 2 hours after** taking iron, as they reduce absorption:
  - Dairy products
  - Tea, coffee, wine
  - Wholegrain breads, cereals, nuts, and legumes (phytate-containing foods)
  - Calcium supplements
  - Zinc supplements
  - Antacids

---

## Drug Interactions

Take iron **at least 3 hours apart** from these medications, as iron reduces their absorption:

- Quinolone antibiotics (e.g., ciprofloxacin)
- Tetracycline antibiotics
- Levodopa

Also check with your doctor or pharmacist if you are taking PPIs (proton pump inhibitors), as these reduce stomach acid and may impair iron absorption.

---

## Managing Side Effects

Common side effects include: nausea, vomiting, constipation, diarrhoea, dark-coloured stools, and abdominal bloating.

To reduce side effects:
- Start at **half the recommended dose** and slowly increase to the full dose.
- Take iron with vitamin C-rich food (see above).
- Switch to a **divided dose preparation**.
- If sustained release tablets cause diarrhoea (particularly with IBD), discuss alternatives with your doctor.
- Use sustained release preparations **with caution** if you have diverticular disease or intestinal strictures.

**If you cannot tolerate tablets:** Ask your doctor about a liquid iron preparation. Use a straw when drinking ferrous sulphate liquid and brush your teeth afterwards to prevent tooth discolouration.

---

## If Oral Iron Isn't Working

Your doctor may consider:

- **IV (parenteral) iron infusion** — if you cannot tolerate oral iron, are not absorbing it, or are not responding adequately.
- **Blood transfusion** — in cases of symptomatic anaemia. Oral iron will still be needed afterwards to replenish stores.

---

## When to See a Specialist

If your iron deficiency anaemia does not respond to treatment or becomes transfusion-dependent, your doctor may arrange a **non-urgent gastroenterology assessment** to investigate further (e.g., capsule endoscopy).
