# Subcutaneous Fluid Administration in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway applies to adults in palliative care. Decisions about fluid administration in palliative care require careful discussion with your doctor and palliative care team.

---

## Overview

Oral fluids are always the preferred method of hydration in conscious patients. Subcutaneous fluid administration (also called hypodermoclysis) is occasionally used in palliative care when a patient cannot take oral fluids, but it is rarely appropriate in the last days of life.

---

## When Subcutaneous Fluids May Be Considered

Your doctor may consider subcutaneous fluids when:

- There are signs and symptoms of dehydration
- The patient is unable to tolerate oral fluids
- Hospital admission is not appropriate or not wanted
- Rehydration is likely to improve comfort and reduce distress

---

## Important Limitations

Subcutaneous fluids have real limitations that your doctor will explain:

- **Unlikely to improve prognosis** — they do not change the course of serious illness
- **Do not usually relieve dry mouth** — mouth care (moistening lips and oral mucosa) is more effective for this symptom
- **May make some symptoms worse** — they can increase lung secretions ("death rattle") and cause peripheral swelling
- **Volume is limited** — typically 500 mL to 1,000 mL can be absorbed per 24 hours through the subcutaneous space
- **Site limitations** — administration sites can be restricted, especially in very thin (cachectic) patients
- **Local discomfort** — fluid can pool at the site and cause swelling or discomfort

---

## How It Is Given

If your doctor decides subcutaneous fluids are appropriate:

- **Fluid used:** Sodium chloride 0.9% (normal saline)
- **Starting dose:** Usually a trial of 500 mL over 24 hours for 1 to 2 days — can increase to 1 litre per 24 hours
- **Review:** If there is no clear benefit, fluids are not continued beyond the agreed review date
- **Frequency:** Can be given intermittently (1 to 2 times per week) or up to daily

**Sites used:** Areas with loosely bound tissue tolerate fluids best — typically the abdomen, inner thigh, or over the shoulder blade. Sites are rotated to prevent tissue damage. Sites with skin damage, swelling, lymphoedema, previous radiation, or near a mastectomy or stoma are avoided.

---

## Involvement of the Palliative Care Team

Your doctor will ensure close collaboration with your palliative care team throughout this process. Palliative care support will be sought for:

- Planning ahead and managing any acute changes
- Arranging a joint visit from a palliative care specialist
- Considering admission to hospice if appropriate

---

## Working with Your Doctor

Subcutaneous fluid administration in palliative care is a decision made carefully, with clear goals and a planned review date. The goal is always comfort — not prolonging life at the cost of distress. Your doctor and palliative care team will guide this decision based on your individual circumstances, values, and wishes.
