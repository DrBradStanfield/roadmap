# Intoeing

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of intoeing (pigeon-toed gait) in children.

---

## Background

One in five children aged 2–5 years will intoe as they walk. There are three common causes, each associated with a different age group:

- **Age 1–2 years:** Metatarsus adductus (also called "banana foot") — medial deviation of the forefoot. Usually reduces significantly by age 5.
- **Age 2–3 years:** Internal tibial torsion — inward twisting of the shinbone. The most common cause of intoeing in toddlers. Often affects both legs. Usually resolves by around age 5; a small number of children have persistent tibial torsion.
- **Age 3 years and older:** Increased femoral anteversion — increased internal rotation of the hip. More common in females. Children often prefer "W sitting". Most cases resolve spontaneously by age 7–8.

Benign intoeing gait continuously improves as the child grows. Surgical treatment is significant and rarely required, and is only performed at skeletal maturity — referral is not needed before age 8 unless there are specific concerns (see below).

Braces, special shoes, and orthotics do not make a difference to physiological intoeing.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Prompt Medical Assessment

Your doctor should assess your child promptly if intoeing is associated with:

- Acute onset, especially with a limp, pain, fever, or inability to bear weight
- Asymmetrical intoeing (one side noticeably different from the other)
- Reduced hip movement or pain in the hip
- Signs of scoliosis or neurological abnormality (e.g., spasticity, weakness, ankle clonus)
- Child aged over 8 years with persisting or worsening intoeing

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will review your child's:

- Developmental milestones and general medical history
- Pregnancy and birth history (e.g., breech delivery, oligohydramnios, neonatal asphyxia)
- Family history of persistent intoeing
- Any concerns about developmental dysplasia of the hip (DDH) or cerebral palsy
- Sitting habits (frog-leg or W sitting)

Physical examination typically includes:

- Observing gait — both walking and running — looking for a limp or inward-facing ("squinting") kneecaps
- **Hip assessment:** Internal and external rotation with the child lying prone and knees flexed. Significantly more internal than external rotation suggests femoral anteversion.
- **Tibial assessment:** Thigh-foot angle measured with the child lying prone, knees flexed at 90 degrees. If the foot points toward the midline, internal tibial torsion is present. Normal range is 10 degrees internal to 15 degrees external rotation.
- **Foot assessment:** If the forefoot deviates medially while the hindfoot is in a normal position, metatarsus adductus may be the cause.
- Spine examination — looking for scoliosis or a hairy patch (which can indicate an underlying spinal anomaly)
- Targeted neurological assessment if there is a limp, asymmetry, or progressive deformity

Your doctor may arrange an AP pelvis X-ray if there is a limp or reduced hip range of motion.

---

## Management

Management depends on findings. Your doctor may consider:

- **Reassurance and observation** for children who are healthy, developing normally, and have no abnormal findings. Most intoeing resolves without treatment by age 7–8.
- **Acute orthopaedic assessment** if there is acute onset with a limp, pain, fever, or inability to bear weight.
- **Non-acute paediatric orthopaedic assessment** if the child is over 8 years, has asymmetrical intoeing, intoeing with a limp, metatarsus adductus (at any age), reduced hip abduction or hip pain, or scoliosis.
- **Non-acute general paediatric assessment** if there are concerns about developmental history or neurological examination findings.

For children managed with observation, your doctor may advise:

- Intoeing does not usually cause functional disability — the child can take part in all physical activities with no need to modify walking or sitting.
- Intoeing will continuously improve as the child grows.
- Braces, special shoes, and orthotics make no difference to physiological intoeing.
- Mild intoeing may persist into adulthood but is not linked to functional disability or a predisposition to arthritis.
- Taking a series of short videos of your child's gait over time can be useful to track improvement.
- Tripping and falling is very common in children under 5 years and is not a concern on its own, unless accompanied by other developmental or neurological abnormalities.
