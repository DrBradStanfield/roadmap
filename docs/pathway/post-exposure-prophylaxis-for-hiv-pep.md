# Post-exposure Prophylaxis for HIV (PEP)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of PEP after potential HIV exposure in a non-occupational setting. PEP is time-critical — it must be started within 72 hours of exposure, ideally within 24 hours. Always discuss urgently with your doctor.

---

## What is PEP?

Post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP) is the use of antiretroviral medications (ARVs) by HIV-uninfected individuals to reduce the risk of acquiring HIV after a potential exposure. It is a 28-day course that must be started as soon as possible, and within 72 hours of exposure.

---

## When PEP Is Never Recommended

PEP is not appropriate if:

- The patient presents more than 72 hours after exposure
- The exposure consisted solely of:
  - Receptive or insertive oral intercourse
  - Blood or body fluid splashed onto intact skin
  - Bite injury or saliva onto skin or mucous membrane (e.g., eye)
- Needle-stick from a discarded needle (e.g., park, beach) of uncertain age and unknown source, or from known source with unknown HIV status
- The patient has been taking PrEP as prescribed (if poorly adherent to PrEP, PEP could be considered)
- The source has been taking PrEP adherently
- The source is HIV-positive but on treatment with an undetectable viral load

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess the following:

### Exposure History

- Date and time (was the exposure less than 72 hours ago?)
- Type of exposure: blood or body fluid involvement, trauma, first aid taken
- If sexual: vaginal, anal, or oral sex; ejaculation; condom use; whether sex was consensual
- If needle-stick: blood contamination of needle/syringe, estimated age of needle, depth of injury

### Patient History

- Most recent HIV test and result
- Potential exposures within last 3 months
- Previous PEP use; current PrEP use
- Current STI symptoms
- Hepatitis B and C status
- Immunisation history (HAV, HBV, HPV)
- Pregnancy risk, contraception, or breastfeeding
- Medical history: renal disease, current medications, allergies, psychiatric history, drug and alcohol use

### Source Person

Your doctor will seek information about the source person (where available):
- HIV status — if positive: current treatment and viral load
- Current STIs, hepatitis B and C status
- High-prevalence country origin (prevalence > 1%)
- Intravenous drug use
- Men who have sex with men (MSM) status

### Factors That Increase Transmission Risk

- STI in source or patient (especially genital ulcer disease or gonococcal infection)
- Breach in genital or oral mucosal integrity
- Direct IV or intra-arterial injection with HIV-infected blood
- Uncircumcised insertive HIV-negative partner
- Recent contact or shared equipment with person from high-prevalence country

---

## Risk of Transmission and PEP Recommendation

PEP is recommended when the risk of transmission exceeds 1 in 1,000. PEP may be considered when risk is between 1 in 1,000 and 1 in 10,000.

Your doctor will use a risk table to determine the appropriate regimen. Key examples:

| Exposure type | Source HIV+ (detectable/unknown VL) | Source unknown, MSM or high-prevalence |
|---|---|---|
| Receptive anal sex | 3-drug PEP | 2-drug PEP |
| Insertive anal sex (uncircumcised) | 3-drug PEP | Consider 2-drug |
| Insertive anal sex (circumcised) | 3-drug PEP | Consider 2-drug |
| Receptive vaginal sex | 3-drug PEP | Consider 2-drug |
| Insertive vaginal sex | 3-drug PEP | Not recommended |
| Shared injecting equipment | 3-drug PEP | Consider 3-drug |
| Occupational needle-stick | 3-drug PEP | 3-drug (seek specialist) |

When the source has an **undetectable viral load** or is from a **low-prevalence population**, PEP is generally not recommended.

---

## Baseline Investigations

Your doctor will arrange:

- STI screen (as per Sexual Health Check pathway)
- Blood tests: HIV serology, syphilis serology, hepatitis B (HBsAg, anti-HBs, anti-HBc), hepatitis C serology, FBC, creatinine and eGFR, LFT
- Pregnancy test if at risk

---

## Contraindications and Cautions

Your doctor will check for:

- Known or suspected antiretroviral drug resistance in the source
- Significant renal impairment in the patient (tenofovir is contraindicated if eGFR < 50)
- Pregnancy or breastfeeding
- Drug interactions (especially with dolutegravir): cytochrome P450 inducers (phenytoin, phenobarbital, rifampicin, St John's wort, carbamazepine), metformin, antacids, calcium or iron products
- Chronic hepatitis B infection
- Previous allergy or adverse reaction to PEP components

If contraindications are present, specialist advice must be sought urgently.

---

## PEP Regimens

### Three-Drug Regimen (preferred when indicated)

- Tenofovir disoproxil + emtricitabine 245 mg/200 mg — one tablet daily for 28 days
- Plus dolutegravir 50 mg — one tablet daily for 28 days

If dolutegravir is not immediately available, start tenofovir/emtricitabine alone — a two-drug regimen is non-inferior and it is more important to start PEP promptly.

### Two-Drug Regimen

- Tenofovir disoproxil + emtricitabine 245 mg/200 mg — one tablet daily for 28 days

### Side Effects

- Early side-effects (headache, nausea, diarrhoea) are usually mild and settle after the first few weeks
- Longer-term effects (renal toxicity, lowered bone density) are unlikely to be significant with a one-month course

---

## Funding

PEP is funded by Pharmac (via Special Authority) in these situations:

- Condomless anal or receptive vaginal intercourse with a person known to be HIV-positive with detectable or unknown viral load (> 200 copies/mL)
- Shared IV injecting equipment with a known HIV-positive person
- Non-consensual intercourse where risk assessment indicates prophylaxis
- Condomless anal intercourse with a person from a high-prevalence country (unknown HIV status)

If not eligible for funded PEP, self-funded options exist (approximately $30 for 2-drug course, $1,345 for 3-drug course, depending on pharmacy).

---

## Follow-Up

Your doctor will arrange follow-up at 1 month and 3 months after PEP to:

- Review baseline HIV test results
- Reinforce preventive practices (safe sex and injecting behaviour)
- Support any psychological distress
- Offer vaccinations (HAV, HBV, HPV) as appropriate
- Arrange STI and blood-borne virus (BBV) testing
- Discuss transition to PrEP if the patient remains HIV-negative but has ongoing exposure risk
- Ensure awareness of HIV seroconversion symptoms

### Signs and Symptoms of HIV Seroconversion (1–6 weeks after exposure)

- Fever, rash, myalgia, arthralgia, sore throat, mouth ulcers, headache
- Approximately 50% of patients have no symptoms
- Signs: fever, lymphadenopathy, pharyngitis, aphthous ulcers, splenomegaly

Later immunodeficiency symptoms include: weight loss, oral candidiasis, skin infections, shingles, Kaposi's sarcoma, pneumonia, meningitis.

---

## When to Seek Specialist Input

Your doctor should seek sexual health or infectious diseases advice urgently if:

- They do not feel competent prescribing PEP (this is time-critical)
- The patient has contraindications to standard PEP regimens
- The source has known or suspected antiretroviral drug resistance
- Follow-up after PEP cannot be arranged in primary care (request non-acute infectious diseases assessment)
