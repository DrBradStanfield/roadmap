# Eyelid Lesions

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of lumps and lesions on the eyelids, including how to tell benign from malignant lesions.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

Your doctor will arrange urgent ophthalmology assessment for:

- Malignant lesions in the medial canthus (inner corner of the eye) — these are seen more urgently as they are more likely to invade the lacrimal drainage system and orbit

---

## Background

Many benign and malignant lesions can occur on the eyelids. Common types include:

- **Benign:** Chalazion (meibomian cyst), hidrocystoma (cyst of Moll), naevus (mole), papilloma, stye (hordeolum), xanthelasma, dacryocystitis, lacrimal mucocoele
- **Malignant:** Basal cell carcinoma (BCC), squamous cell carcinoma (SCC), melanoma, and other rare adnexal malignancies

Common benign lesions can be irritating and cosmetically unacceptable but can often be treated in general practice. Optometrists can also assess and manage some eyelid conditions.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will examine the eyelid lesion and determine whether it is likely to be benign or malignant.

### Signs of a Benign Eyelid Lesion

- Minimal growth or change
- Non-destructive to surrounding tissue
- Often present for months to years
- Eyelashes usually intact

**Types of benign eyelid lesions:**

**Chalazion (meibomian cyst / tarsal cyst)**
- Caused by inflammation in a blocked meibomian gland in the eyelid
- Usually subacute and painless, but can become acutely inflamed
- Most settle and disappear over a few weeks

**Eyelid skin papillomas**
- Can be smooth, rounded, or pedunculated (on a stalk)
- Clearly benign in appearance with no lash loss or tissue destruction

**Xanthelasma**
- Yellow plaques, most commonly near the inner corner of the eyelid (medial canthus), more often on the upper lid
- Can be soft, semi-solid, or calcareous (chalky)
- Tend to progress, coalesce, and become permanent
- About half are associated with high cholesterol (hyperlipidaemia)

**Dacryocystitis (infected lacrimal sac)**
- Acute bacterial infection of the lacrimal sac, usually from a blocked nasolacrimal duct
- Can affect all age groups but is most common in infants and post-menopausal women
- Can progress to cellulitis requiring hospital admission

**Lacrimal mucocoele**
- Chronic swelling with or without mucopurulent discharge from the lacrimal puncta, without cellulitis

**Cyst of Moll**
- Benign swelling from a blocked gland of Moll at the base of an eyelash
- Appears as a solitary dome-shaped papule or nodule filled with clear fluid

**Stye (hordeolum)**
- Usually caused by a staphylococcal infection of the eyelash follicles
- Usually self-limiting, rupturing after 2 to 4 days

### Signs of a Malignant Eyelid Lesion

- Progressive enlargement over months
- Distortion or destruction of normal lid architecture — missing lashes, notch or dip in lid contour (may cause ectropion)
- Bleeding with minor trauma
- Recurrent crusting and ulceration
- A typical basal cell carcinoma (BCC) is a pearly solid lump, sometimes with a depression in the centre (rodent ulcer) — more commonly affects the lower lid and medial canthus
- Squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) and some aggressive BCCs are less well defined, may be inflamed with tissue distortion and ulceration

## Management

Your doctor will determine the most appropriate management based on the type of lesion.

**If a malignant eyelid lesion is suspected:**
- Your doctor will arrange ophthalmology assessment for biopsy or excision
- Eyelid lesions on the lid margin should not be excised in general practice, as lid notching or distortion can cause chronic discomfort or corneal damage
- Any lesion that is excised will be sent for histology

**For benign eyelid lesions, your doctor may consider:**

**Chalazion:**
- Most resolve on their own within 6 to 12 months
- Your doctor may advise warm compresses and gentle massage of the cyst towards the lid margin, twice daily until fully resolved
- A topical antibiotic eye ointment (chloramphenicol or fusidic acid) may be applied for localised inflammation
- If redness around the eye extends significantly beyond the chalazion, your doctor will assess for periorbital cellulitis — this may require urgent ophthalmology review and intravenous antibiotics

**Dacryocystitis:**
- *Acute dacryocystitis* (swollen lacrimal sac with overlying cellulitis): always requires urgent ophthalmology assessment — treatment often includes antibiotics (sometimes intravenous) and may require aspiration or incision and drainage
- *Chronic dacryocystitis* (swollen lacrimal sac, no cellulitis): your doctor will refer to ophthalmology; warm compresses and massage are advised while awaiting review; antibiotics are generally avoided as they do not resolve the underlying nasolacrimal duct obstruction and may select resistant bacteria
- *Lacrimal mucocoele*: your doctor will refer to ophthalmology

**Eyelid skin papillomas:**
- Your doctor may consider excision or cryotherapy (for lesions outside the orbital rim)
- The specimen will be sent for histology, as a small number of benign-looking lesions can contain areas of dysplasia or malignancy

**Stye (hordeolum):**
- Self-management with warm compresses, eyelid massage, and eyelid cleaning

**Xanthelasma:**
- Excision is usually for cosmetic purposes only
- Your doctor will advise that excision is associated with high rates of recurrence, especially if there is underlying high cholesterol
- Excision is generally only considered when lesions are very small, due to the risk of complications such as ectropion
