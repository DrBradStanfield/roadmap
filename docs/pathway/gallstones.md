# Gallstones

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of gallstones in adults, including biliary colic and its complications.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Attention

Contact your doctor urgently or go to the emergency department if you have:

- Jaundice (yellow skin or eyes)
- Symptoms suggesting acute cholecystitis, cholangitis, or pancreatitis (severe persistent pain, fever, or feeling systemically unwell)

---

## Background

About 5–25% of adults in the Western world develop gallstones. The majority are asymptomatic and found incidentally on imaging.

Common presentations include **biliary colic** and **acute cholecystitis**. Less common presentations include acute pancreatitis, acute cholangitis, and obstructive jaundice.

Asymptomatic gallstones do not usually require intervention unless stones are in the common bile duct (which carries a higher risk of pancreatitis or cholangitis). Patients with a partially calcified **porcelain gallbladder** are at an increased risk of gallbladder cancer (up to 15%) and may need surgery even without symptoms.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a full history and examination. Information they will ask about includes:

### Risk Factors

- History of pancreatitis or jaundice
- Increasing age
- Female sex
- Positive family history of gallstones
- Obesity
- Rapid weight loss (e.g. after weight-loss surgery)
- Loss of bile salts (e.g. ileal resection, terminal ileitis)
- Diabetes (as part of metabolic syndrome)
- Pregnancy
- Oral contraception, HRT, or other oestrogen treatment
- Crohn's disease
- Haemolysis

### Symptoms

- **Pain location:** Usually right upper quadrant, but may be epigastric
- **Pain character:** May be colicky or constant; band-like or tight feeling across the upper abdomen
- **Pain radiation:** May radiate around or through to the back, or to the tip of the right shoulder
- **Triggers:** Often brought on by ingesting fatty food
- **Systemic symptoms:** Fever, malaise, loss of appetite, nausea, vomiting, sweating, rigors, jaundice, pruritus, weight loss

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will check for:

- Fever, tachycardia, and jaundice
- Abdominal tenderness and hepatomegaly
- A palpable gallbladder
- **Murphy's sign** — inspiration inhibited by pain on pressing the right upper quadrant (highly specific for acute cholecystitis)

### Possible Diagnoses

**Biliary colic:**
- Epigastric or right upper quadrant pain, moderately severe, lasting minutes to hours
- May be associated with nausea, bloating, and vomiting
- Often triggered by fatty foods; usually self-limiting but recurs
- Intervals between attacks are variable (days to months)

**Acute cholecystitis:**
- Biliary colic pain that is persistent and severe
- May radiate to right shoulder or back
- Associated fever, nausea, vomiting, and anorexia

**Acute cholangitis:**
- Diffuse right upper quadrant or upper abdominal pain
- Associated with fever and jaundice
- May have itch, pale stools, low blood pressure, or confusion

**Acute gallstone pancreatitis:**
- Constant, severe epigastric or right upper quadrant pain radiating to the back
- May be partially relieved by sitting up or bending forward
- Associated vomiting, may deteriorate quickly

**Non-biliary causes to consider:**
- GORD or peptic ulcer disease
- Acute hepatitis
- Appendicitis
- Bowel obstruction
- Irritable bowel syndrome
- Cardiac ischaemia
- Pneumonia (right lower lobe)
- Pyelonephritis
- Renal colic
- Ruptured abdominal aortic aneurysm

### Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- **Blood tests:** Full blood count (FBC), CRP, urea and electrolytes, creatinine, and liver function tests (LFTs). Amylase if pancreatitis is suspected.
- **Ultrasound abdomen:** To confirm gallstones and assess the gallbladder and bile ducts

## Management

### If You Have Symptoms

**Emergency referral** — your doctor will arrange urgent hospital assessment if you have:
- Jaundice, or suspected cholecystitis, cholangitis, or pancreatitis
- Biliary colic pain not relieved by appropriate analgesia
- Biliary pain with raised bilirubin or alkaline phosphatase (suggesting bile duct obstruction)

**For biliary colic without red flags**, your doctor may consider:

- **Pain relief:**
  - First line: paracetamol or NSAIDs (e.g. ibuprofen, naproxen)
  - Second line (for severe pain): intramuscular diclofenac 75 mg, or opioids such as morphine

- **Ultrasound** to confirm the diagnosis if not already done

- **Surgical referral** (non-acute) for consideration of cholecystectomy (gallbladder removal) once gallstones are confirmed on ultrasound

- **Lifestyle advice** to reduce symptoms and perioperative risk:
  - Low fat diet
  - Weight reduction if overweight
  - Smoking cessation
  - Reduced alcohol intake

### If You Have No Symptoms

Most asymptomatic gallstones do not require intervention. However, your doctor may consider surgical referral if you have:

- **Common bile duct stones** — increased risk of pancreatitis, cholangitis, or obstructive jaundice
- **History of pancreatitis or obstructive jaundice** from gallstones — increased risk of recurrent complications
- **Partially calcified porcelain gallbladder** — up to 15% risk of gallbladder cancer; surgery may be recommended even without symptoms

Lifestyle advice (low-fat diet, weight reduction, smoking cessation, reduced alcohol) may help reduce the risk of developing symptoms.

---

## Surgical Treatment

The standard treatment for symptomatic gallstones is **laparoscopic cholecystectomy** (keyhole surgery to remove the gallbladder). This is performed by a general surgeon. Your doctor will refer you to a surgical team for assessment if indicated.
