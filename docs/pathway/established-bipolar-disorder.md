# Established Bipolar Disorder

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway is for people with a **confirmed diagnosis of bipolar disorder**. It covers ongoing management — assessment, medications, monitoring, and supports. If you are experiencing an acute episode, please contact your doctor promptly.

> **Note:** This information is a general guide. Your doctor will tailor assessment and management to your individual circumstances.

---

## When to Seek Emergency Help

Contact emergency services (111) or go to your nearest emergency department if:

- You have thoughts of harming yourself or others
- Someone is at immediate risk due to a manic episode (e.g., dangerous behaviour, severe impulsivity)
- You are experiencing severe depression with risk of self-harm or suicide

---

## Red Flags — Contact Your Doctor Urgently

Your doctor needs to know promptly if:

- Mania or severe depression is significantly affecting your daily life (work, relationships, self-care, driving)
- You are using alcohol or drugs alongside a mood episode
- You feel you may be relapsing after stopping or reducing your medication
- You are pregnant or trying to become pregnant (some mood stabilisers carry risks in pregnancy and your medications may need to be reviewed)
- You or your family feel your carer is becoming overburdened

---

## Background

Bipolar disorder is a mood disorder characterised by episodes of mania or hypomania, depression, and mixed episodes.

- It generally begins between ages 15 and 40 and continues lifelong.
- It occurs equally across all cultures.
- It can be challenging to diagnose — it is often initially misdiagnosed as unipolar depression.

Most people experience multiple episodes (on average one episode every 2–3 years, each lasting around 3–6 months). Stressful life events — money or relationship worries, lack of sleep, unemployment — can trigger episodes.

Most people return to their usual level of functioning between episodes. Effective treatments are available and can significantly reduce the long-term impact of illness.

**Types of bipolar disorder (DSM-5 / ICD-11):**

- **Bipolar I** — at least one lifetime manic or mixed episode; depressive episodes usually also occur.
- **Bipolar II** — periods of major depression with at least one hypomanic episode.
- **Cyclothymic disorder** — numerous subsyndromal hypomanic and depressive episodes over a 2-year period, none meeting full criteria for mania or depression.

## Assessment

Your doctor will carry out a complete assessment regularly, especially during stable (euthymic) periods. This typically includes:

### History

- Your personal history of mental illness
- Nature, onset, and impact of any current symptoms — on relationships, sleep, appetite, work, parenting
- General mental health screening: symptom severity, functional impairment, insight into your condition, presence of other mental health conditions, psychotic symptoms, or thought disorder
- Risk to yourself (financial, sexual, accidents, self-harm, suicide) or to others
- Alcohol, prescription drug, and substance use
- Support from family/whānau and community services
- Stressful circumstances or life events

### Medication Review

Your doctor will check compliance with your current medications. **Non-compliance is the most common factor in relapse.** Stopping or reducing medications too soon is a frequent cause of depressive or manic relapse.

### Physical Examination

- General physical health check, including weight
- If you are currently unwell, your doctor may examine for underlying organic causes (e.g., thyroid problems, substance use, medications that can trigger mood episodes such as corticosteroids or antiparkinsonian drugs)

### Investigations

Your doctor will check serum levels of mood stabilisers and arrange relevant blood tests, which may include:

- Full blood count (FBC)
- Liver function tests (LFTs)
- Thyroid function tests (TFTs)
- Lipids and HbA1c
- Renal function (eGFR)
- Electrolytes

Annual blood tests help identify any co-existing medical conditions.

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following approaches, tailored to your situation.

### Long-Term Mood Stabilisers

Your doctor may consider starting a long-term mood stabiliser if you have had more than 2 episodes of depression or mania within 2 years. This can be started at any point in the condition.

Lifelong treatment may be considered after discussion with you — it can lower the risk of relapse by 50–70%.

Withdrawal of mood stabilisers requires careful discussion and close monitoring. Your doctor may seek specialist mental health advice before making changes.

**Common mood stabilisers include:** lithium, sodium valproate, carbamazepine, lamotrigine.

**Common antipsychotics used in bipolar disorder include:** quetiapine, olanzapine, risperidone, paliperidone, chlorpromazine.

> Your doctor will always review the New Zealand Formulary for current prescribing advice.

### Medication Monitoring

Regular monitoring is essential for people on mood stabilisers or antipsychotics. Your doctor will arrange this as part of your ongoing care.

**Antipsychotics** (e.g., quetiapine, olanzapine, risperidone):

- Weight — weekly for the first 6–8 weeks, then at 3 months, 6 months, and annually. Rapid weight gain early in treatment can predict longer-term weight gain.
- Waist circumference, blood pressure, and heart rate — at 3 months, 6 months, then annually.
- Fasting lipids, fasting glucose, HbA1c, LFTs, FBC — at 3 months, 6 months, then annually.
- Prolactin — only if specific symptoms are present (e.g., galactorrhoea, oligomenorrhoea lasting more than 6 months, or male secondary hypogonadism).
- ECG — at baseline, 6 months, then annually.

**Carbamazepine:**

- Drug levels and LFTs every 6 months.
- FBC, sodium, and potassium annually.

**Lamotrigine:**

- Tell your doctor immediately if you develop a rash, fever, or signs of hypersensitivity — lamotrigine can cause serious skin rashes including Stevens-Johnson syndrome, usually within the first 2–8 weeks after starting.
- Watch for signs of bone-marrow problems (unusual bruising, infections, anaemia) — rare but reported.

**Lithium:**

- Lithium level, renal function, and electrolytes every 3–6 months.
- Thyroid function, serum calcium, and weight every 6 months.
- ECG, lipids, and HbA1c annually (especially if aged over 40 or with obesity).

**Sodium valproate:**

- Drug levels and LFTs every 6 months.
- FBC annually.
- **Important:** Sodium valproate and carbamazepine are associated with risks of birth defects and should generally be avoided in early pregnancy. If you are pregnant or planning pregnancy, discuss this with your doctor urgently.

### Lifestyle

Your doctor will likely discuss:

- **Routine:** Maintaining regular daily routines supports mood stability.
- **Sleep:** Regular sleep patterns are important — disrupted sleep is a known trigger for episodes.
- **Alcohol:** Avoid alcohol — it can destabilise mood and interact with medications.
- **Smoking:** Smoking cessation is recommended.
- **Exercise and diet:** Regular physical activity and healthy eating support overall wellbeing.
- **Contraception:** Planning ahead for contraception is important given medication risks in pregnancy.
- **Vaccination:** Hepatitis B vaccine may be considered.

### Psychological Treatments

Psychological therapies can reduce relapse, improve day-to-day functioning, and help manage side effects. Your doctor may refer you to:

- **Cognitive Behavioural Therapy (CBT)** — helps identify and change unhelpful thought and behaviour patterns.
- **Interpersonal Psychotherapy (IPT)** — focuses on communication, relationships, and roles.
- **Peer support or occupational therapy** — helps with returning to meaningful activity, planning, and relaxation.

Mood tracking tools may also help:

- Smartphone apps: imoodJournal, eMoods, DBSA Wellness Tracker
- [Downloadable mood chart (Black Dog Institute)](https://www.blackdoginstitute.org.au/docs/default-source/psychological-toolkit/18-moodchartforbipolardisorder.pdf)

### Social and Legal Considerations

Your doctor may raise:

- **Driving:** Mania or severe depression can affect driving safety — your doctor has obligations to consider this.
- **Social stressors:** Accommodation, employment, finances, and relationships — your doctor can connect you with community support services.
- **Advance care planning:** It can be valuable to plan ahead for acute presentations, including considering an Enduring Power of Attorney (EPA).

---

## When Your Doctor May Refer or Seek Advice

Your doctor may seek specialist mental health input if:

- Mania or severe depression poses a risk of harm to you or others
- Your functioning is severely compromised (work, self-care, driving)
- There are medication complexities or physical health problems that complicate management
- Early attempts to manage mood and sleep are not effective
- You are pregnant or considering pregnancy
- Your mood stabiliser needs to be withdrawn or changed
