# Health and Frailty Assessment for Older Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway is for assessing daily function and frailty level in older adults. Frailty is not an inevitable outcome of ageing — the path of decline can be modified by identifying and managing a range of factors.

---

## Background

Frailty is not an inevitable outcome of ageing. Deciding who is frail is important, as frailty is a predictor of continuing independence. The path of decline can be modified by assessing and managing a range of contributing factors.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider or suspect frailty in all older adults and use the **Clinical Frailty Scale** to guide further assessment.

### If score is 1 to 3 (not frail)

Your doctor will ask what your health concerns are and assess key factors regularly to potentially delay the onset of frailty:

- **Dentition:** loose dentures, dry mouth (xerostomia), ulceration, toothache, halitosis, broken-down teeth, oral candida, high sugar intake
- **Nutrition and weight**
- **Mobility and falls**
- **Hearing**
- **Sight**
- **Skin**
- **Incontinence**
- **Pain**
- **Sleep**
- **Mood**
- **Social support:** social connectedness with family, friends, community groups; isolation due to mobility or transport issues; screening for elder abuse and neglect
- **Alcohol, smoking, and other drugs**
- **Warm and safe housing**
- **Driving:** your doctor may consider crash risk factors including advancing age, cognitive impairment, visual or hearing impairment, medications (e.g. sedatives), alcohol, cardiovascular or neurological disease (e.g. Parkinson disease, stroke), locomotor limitations, sleep apnoea, and fatigue. Assessment may include visual acuity, hearing, cognition, coordination, neck range of movement, arm strength, and foot proprioception.
- **Medications:** your doctor may review continued indications, consider deprescribing, check for side effects and interactions, and assess whether additional medications are needed (e.g. vitamin D, pain medication)

### If score is 4 to 6 (pre-frail)

Your doctor will ask what your health concerns are and assess:

**Instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs)** — complex activities needed to function in the community, which are often lost before basic self-care:
- Food preparation
- Shopping
- Using the telephone
- Driving and transportation (same crash risk assessment as above)
- Handling finances
- Housekeeping
- Laundry
- Medication self-management
- Using a computer

**Activities of daily living (ADLs)** — basic self-care (patients are often sensitive about needing help):
- Eating and nutrition (including unintentional weight loss — defined as ≥10% at any BMI, or ≥5% at BMI 20 or below)
- BMI calculation (BMI 20–22 is treated as underweight in older adults; if BMI >30 with high cardiovascular risk or arthritis-related limitations, your doctor may advise weight loss via diet, exercise, and behaviour modification)
- Dentition (as above)
- Bathing
- Dressing
- Toileting
- Transfer and walking
- Continence

**Cognitive screening:** your doctor may perform a brief GPCOG screen. A score of 8 or below may prompt further assessment for cognitive impairment.

**Other factors:**
- Social support (as above, including elder abuse screening)
- Hearing, sight, skin, pain, sleep, mood
- Alcohol, smoking, and other drugs
- Warm and safe housing
- Medications (review as above)

### If score is 7 to 9 (frail)

Your doctor will ask what your health concerns are and assess all ADLs, social support, and the full list of other factors as described above for pre-frail patients.

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following:

1. **Discussing your care preferences** — including advance care planning (ACP) and involving family or whānau in decisions about your care.

2. **Optimising any identified conditions**, such as:
   - Alcohol misuse
   - Cognitive impairment
   - Urinary incontinence
   - Dental problems
   - Depression
   - Elder abuse or neglect
   - Falls risk
   - Hearing impairment
   - Medication management
   - Obstructive sleep apnoea
   - Nutritional issues or unintentional weight loss
   - Smoking
   - Visual impairment

3. **Immunisations** — your doctor may offer or recommend:
   - *Tetanus and diphtheria booster* — recommended at age 65; older adults are prone to minor skin injuries and tetanus immunity is an important precaution
   - *Annual influenza vaccination* — recommended for all adults over 65; influenza can be severe or fatal in frail older adults
   - *Herpes zoster (shingles) vaccine* — recommended at age 65; by age 80, 50% of adults have had shingles. The most common complication is postherpetic neuralgia, which tends to be more severe with increasing age
   - *Pneumococcal vaccine* — your doctor may consider this, particularly for more vulnerable older adults
   - *Pertussis (whooping cough) booster* — may be considered if you will have contact with newborn babies

4. **Personal alarm** — if you have ongoing or age-related health problems, you may be eligible for a disability allowance to help cover the cost of a medical alarm.

5. **Mobility parking and transport** — your doctor may assist with mobility parking permits or disability parking identification if your ability to walk is significantly impaired.

6. **Specialist assessment** — if your diagnosis is uncertain or you may need additional long-term home support, your doctor may refer you for a specialist older person's health assessment.

7. **Social participation** — if social isolation is a concern, your doctor may suggest local interest groups, support groups, or day centres.

8. **Advance care planning and legal documents** — if you are considering residential care or have significant health needs, your doctor may discuss:
   - Advance care planning (ACP) — recording your wishes for future care
   - Enduring power of attorney (EPA) — a legal arrangement allowing a trusted person to make decisions on your behalf if you become incapacitated. There are two types:
     - *EPA Welfare* — covers care and welfare decisions
     - *EPA Property* — covers financial and property decisions
   - EPA is activated by a formal assessment once a person becomes mentally incapable of managing their affairs
   - Guardianship and an acute emergency plan
