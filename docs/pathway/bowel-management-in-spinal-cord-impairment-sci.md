# Bowel Management in Spinal Cord Impairment (SCI)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of bowel problems in people with spinal cord impairment (SCI). Bowel dysfunction is common in SCI and significantly affects quality of life. Always discuss management with your doctor.

---

## Background

Many people with SCI experience bowel management problems, including:

- Constipation
- Faecal incontinence ("accidents")
- Abdominal pain or discomfort
- Haemorrhoids
- Periodic rectal bleeding
- Rectal prolapse

These issues tend to worsen with age as gut motility declines. Bowel incontinence in particular affects self-esteem, independence, and participation in daily life.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- The specific bowel issues you are experiencing
- Your current bowel regimen:
  - Timing and duration of bowel care routines
  - Whether "accidents" occur before or after bowel care is complete
  - Any bleeding
  - Whether your pattern has changed recently or is long-standing
  - Your stool consistency (Bristol Stool Chart number — aim for Type 4)
  - Diet: fibre, fruit and vegetable intake; whether you eat a meal and have a hot drink before starting bowel emptying
  - Fluid intake — a minimum of 1.5 litres per 24 hours is recommended (unless medically specified otherwise)
  - Alcohol or cannabis use — both can significantly alter bowel activity and stool consistency
  - Current medications that may affect the bowel

Your doctor will also determine which type of bowel dysfunction applies to you:

### Reflex Bowel (Upper Motor Neuron — injury at or above the conus)

Reflex bowels retain some peristaltic action (confirmed by the presence of bulbocavernosus and anal reflexes). The standard approach uses stimulant laxatives, glycerine or bisacodyl suppositories, and digital rectal stimulation. **Important:** faecal retention and rectal stimulation can trigger autonomic dysreflexia in people with SCI at T6 or above.

### Flaccid Bowel (Lower Motor Neuron — injury at or below the conus, or cauda equina)

Flaccid bowels have little or no peristaltic action (confirmed by absence of reflexes). Management relies on regular digital or manual evacuation with gentle abdominal massage.

**Physical examination** includes checking the abdomen (for distension, constipation, obstruction) and rectum (for impaction, prolapse, haemorrhoids, fissures).

If there are ongoing problems, your doctor will consider whether other pathology may be contributing and may arrange an abdominal X-ray or, if needed, a colonoscopy (with extended bowel preparation and carer involvement).

## Management

### Following Your Established Bowel Regimen

Your doctor will encourage you to follow the regimen you have been taught. If you have not yet established a regimen, specialist guidance from a spinal cord rehabilitation team is recommended.

**Reflex bowel evacuation programme** (as recommended by the Auckland Spinal Rehabilitation Unit):

1. Take a laxative — docusate sodium (softener) and sennoside B (stimulant) — the evening before morning evacuation or 8 hours before evening evacuation
2. After a meal, perform lower bowel evacuation using a double-gloved, lubricated finger in a circular motion against the anorectal wall
3. Insert 2 glycerine or bisacodyl suppositories (if prescribed), pointed end first, pressing firmly against the rectal wall
4. If no reflex action: perform digital stimulation — insert a lubricated finger 2–4 cm into the rectum, move in circular motion for 20–30 seconds, remove to allow contractions, apply clockwise abdominal massage, and remove stool. Repeat every 5 minutes up to 3 times
5. Clean the anal area when complete
6. The procedure should take no longer than 30–45 minutes

**Flaccid bowel evacuation:**

- Daily digital evacuation using a well-lubricated, double-gloved finger with circular motion
- Stool should be formed (Bristol Stool Chart Type 4) — psyllium husk powder (Metamucil or equivalent) helps maintain consistency
- Laxatives may be needed for stool consistency
- Soft or loose stool increases the risk of accidents; constipation causes overflow loose stools

**Note:** Conus-level injuries may produce a "mixed" bowel pattern — trials of both reflex and flaccid techniques may be appropriate.

### Constipation

Your doctor will:

- Step up laxatives according to the Constipation in Adults pathway
- Consider macrogol (Movicol or equivalent) for more severe constipation
- Assess whether additional home carer support is needed

### Loose Bowel Motions

Your doctor will:

- Exclude infection
- Exclude faecal loading or impaction (abdominal X-ray) — if confirmed, treat with sodium picosulphate or 8 sachets of macrogol
- If infection and impaction are excluded: add a bulking agent (e.g. psyllium husk powder)

### Fluid and Dietary Habits

Changes in fluid intake, diet, or daily habits are reviewed and adjusted as part of bowel management.

### Haemorrhoids, Fissures, and Prolapse

Managed with standard treatments. Surgical referral is arranged if needed.

### Advanced Options for Long-Term Wheelchair Users

If bowel management is significantly affecting quality of life despite conservative measures, your doctor may consider:

- Colostomy
- Antegrade colonic enema (ACE) procedure
- Trans-anal irrigation

Gastroenterology assessment is requested first to exclude non-SCI causes. Surgical assessment follows if non-surgical options are exhausted.

### Continence Support

Your doctor can refer you to community nursing for continence assistance, products, and practical support.

---

## When to Seek Specialist Advice

Your doctor will seek spinal cord impairment specialist advice if:

- Bowel symptoms persist despite standard management
- There is uncertainty about the appropriate approach
- Surgical options are being considered
