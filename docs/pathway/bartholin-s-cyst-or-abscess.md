# Bartholin's Cyst or Abscess

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of Bartholin's cyst or abscess in women.

---

## Background

Bartholin's cysts and abscesses account for 2% of all gynaecological visits each year, most commonly in women aged 20–30.

- **Bartholin's glands** are a pair of pea-sized mucous-secreting glands located at the 4 and 8 o'clock positions in the vulvar vestibule.
- **Bartholin's cyst:** a painless (or mildly painful) fluid-filled swelling that develops when the gland duct becomes blocked. May resolve without intervention.
- **Bartholin's abscess:** an acute, painful swelling with possible surrounding redness and swelling — develops when a cyst or the gland itself becomes infected. Three times more common than Bartholin's cyst. May discharge spontaneously.
- Cultures often grow *E. coli* or are polymicrobial; STIs are a rare cause. MRSA incidence is increasing.

---

## Suspicious Features — Refer for Gynaecology Assessment

Your doctor will refer for biopsy to exclude Bartholin's gland carcinoma (less than 5% of vulvar carcinomas) if any of the following are present:

- Mass with a solid component
- Persistent mass despite treatment
- Painless enlarging mass in a woman older than 40 years
- Mass in a postmenopausal woman

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Time since onset of current mass
- Pain character:
  - Cysts are painless or mildly painful
  - Abscesses typically cause acute, rapidly progressive pain
- Discharge:
  - Cysts may have clear or white fluid
  - Abscesses typically have purulent (yellow or green) discharge
- Fever
- Previous vulvar mass and response to treatment
- Menopausal status
- Sexual health history and STI risk factors

Risk factors for Bartholin's abscess include a history of STIs, previous Bartholin's abscesses, and increasing age up to menopause.

Physical examination checks for:

- Whether the site corresponds to Bartholin's gland location (4 and 8 o'clock positions)
- Fever
- Cellulitis
- Tenderness and fluctuation of the mass
- Any solid component
- Surrounding erythema and oedema

If the lesion is discharging, your doctor will send a swab for microbiology, culture, and sensitivity.

**Differential diagnoses to consider:** labial abscess, perianal abscess, inclusion cyst, epidermal (sebaceous) cyst, Gartner's duct cyst, haematoma, lipoma, Skene's duct cyst, vaginitis, vestibular mucous cyst, hidradenoma, fibroma, leiomyoma, vulvar Crohn's disease, vulval cancer.

---

## Management

Management depends on the type and severity of presentation:

### Asymptomatic Bartholin's Cyst

Your doctor will monitor and advise you to return if symptoms develop.

### Symptomatic Bartholin's Cyst

Your doctor will refer for non-acute gynaecology assessment.

### Spontaneously Discharging Bartholin's Abscess

Your doctor may:

- Provide simple analgesia
- Recommend **sitz baths** (soaking the hips and buttocks in warm water or saline solution)
- Consider oral antibiotics if there are signs of systemic infection, extensive cellulitis, immunocompromise, pregnancy, or recurrent abscess:
  - **First-line:** Amoxycillin + clavulanic acid 625 mg three times daily for 5 days
  - **Second-line:** Cefalexin 1000 mg three times daily with Metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 5 days
- If no improvement after 3 days of antibiotics, refer for acute gynaecology assessment for marsupialisation or word catheter placement (both have comparable recurrence rates of around 10%)

### Non-Discharging Bartholin's Abscess

Your doctor may:

- If pain is manageable and there are no systemic features: consider oral antibiotics (same regimens as above)
- If severe pain with systemic features, or no improvement after 3 days of antibiotics: refer for acute gynaecology assessment for marsupialisation or word catheter placement

Note: simple incision and drainage is not performed in the primary care setting as recurrence is common and it can make subsequent definitive treatment more difficult.

### Procedures (performed by gynaecology specialists)

- **Word catheter placement:** Incision of the cyst or abscess, placement of a word catheter to allow drainage and formation of a fistulous tract over 4–6 weeks. Can be performed under local anaesthetic.
- **Marsupialisation:** Incision of the cyst or abscess, with eversion and suturing of the epithelium to create a new ductal opening. Performed under general anaesthetic.
