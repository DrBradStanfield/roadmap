# Impalpable Thyroid Nodule

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers thyroid nodules found incidentally on imaging (CT, MRI, PET scan, or ultrasound done for another reason) that cannot be felt on physical examination.

---

## What Is an Impalpable Thyroid Nodule?

Thyroid nodules found on imaging are very common. Most are completely benign and require no treatment. The goal is to identify the small number that need further investigation — without over-investigating the majority.

Ultrasound uses a classification system called **TI-RADS** to risk-stratify nodules:

| TI-RADS Grade | Meaning |
|---|---|
| TI-RADS 1 | Normal |
| TI-RADS 2 | Benign |
| TI-RADS 3 | Mildly suspicious |
| TI-RADS 4 | Moderately suspicious |
| TI-RADS 5 | Highly suspicious |

The TI-RADS grade combined with nodule size determines whether a biopsy (FNA) is needed.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history to check for:

- **Thyroid hormone symptoms** — weight changes, sweats, tremor, fast pulse
- **Thyroid eye disease symptoms** — gritty or irritable eyes, eyelid swelling, double vision, visual disturbance, or bulging eyes (proptosis)
- **Thyroid cancer risk factors:**
  - Neck irradiation in childhood
  - Personal or family history of thyroid adenoma
  - Hashimoto's thyroiditis (small risk of lymphoma)
  - Cowden's syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis, or familial thyroid cancer
  - Family history of multiple endocrine neoplasm (MEN syndrome)
- **Nodule duration and growth** — sudden increase in size or tenderness may indicate bleeding into a nodule
- **Compressive symptoms** — hoarseness, difficulty swallowing, facial swelling, or breathing difficulty

Your doctor will also examine your neck to check whether the nodule has become palpable and whether any lymph nodes are enlarged. If the nodule can now be felt, a different pathway applies.

### Blood Test

Your doctor will arrange a **TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone)** blood test. This is the key initial investigation.

---

## Management

Management depends on the TSH result and the TI-RADS grade of the nodule on ultrasound.

### If TSH Is Normal or Elevated

Your doctor will arrange a **thyroid ultrasound** (if not already done) to get a TI-RADS grade.

Whether a **biopsy (FNA)** is needed depends on the TI-RADS grade and nodule size:

| TI-RADS Grade | FNA Referral Threshold |
|---|---|
| TI-RADS 5 | Nodule ≥ 10 mm |
| TI-RADS 4 | Nodule ≥ 15 mm |
| TI-RADS 3 | Nodule ≥ 25 mm |
| Any grade | FDG-avid on PET/CT scan |
| Any grade | Enlarged or atypical cervical lymph nodes |

If FNA is indicated, your doctor will refer you to an endocrinologist who will organise the biopsy.

**Note:** FNA is not always appropriate. For patients with significant other health conditions, ultrasound surveillance alone may be more suitable — your doctor will make this judgement.

### If TSH Is Low (Reduced)

A reduced TSH suggests the nodule may be producing excess thyroid hormone. Your doctor will arrange a **radionuclide (nuclear medicine) scan** to assess this.

- If the nodule is "hot" (overactive), it is managed as hyperthyroidism
- If the nodule is "cold" or "warm" (not overactive), further assessment with ultrasound and possible FNA follows
- If you are pregnant, nuclear scans are contraindicated — your doctor will seek specialist advice

### If FNA Result Is Benign

There is no evidence that continued ultrasound surveillance of a nodule proven benign by FNA is beneficial. Your doctor will discuss whether further monitoring is needed.

### Nodules Not Requiring FNA

Most incidentally found nodules do not require biopsy. For these:

- Follow-up ultrasound is arranged only as recommended in the radiology report (typically at 1–5 year intervals depending on TI-RADS grade)
- Your doctor will re-examine your neck every **6 to 12 months**
- You should return promptly if you notice any new neck lumps or a lump that seems to be growing

---

## When to Seek Specialist Input

Your doctor will seek specialist advice (endocrinologist or radiologist) in the following situations:

- The nodule meets size/TI-RADS thresholds for FNA referral
- A PET/CT scan showed a FDG-avid thyroid nodule
- Abnormal neck lymph nodes are found
- TSH is low and it is unclear whether a nuclear scan is appropriate
- You are pregnant and TSH is low (nuclear scans are contraindicated)
- A surveillance nodule changes in size or appearance at any review
- You have subclinical hyperthyroidism plus co-morbidities such as heart disease, or the low TSH persists beyond 12 months
- You have a history of thyroid cancer and are on thyroxine suppression therapy
- Uncertainty remains at any stage

---

## Key Points

- Most incidentally found thyroid nodules are benign and do not require treatment
- TSH is the key initial blood test
- TI-RADS grade (from ultrasound) combined with nodule size determines whether biopsy is needed
- A low TSH triggers a nuclear medicine scan rather than ultrasound as the first investigation
- Surveillance ultrasound intervals are guided by the radiology report
- Notify your doctor promptly if a new palpable lump appears in your neck

*Always discuss your results and next steps with your doctor. This information is a guide to how doctors approach this condition — it is not a substitute for clinical assessment.*
