# UTI in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: August 2021.*

This pathway covers the assessment and treatment of urinary tract infections (UTIs) in adults. Separate pathways cover UTIs in pregnancy, recurrent UTIs in women, UTIs in children, and pyelonephritis (kidney infection).

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When Urgent Advice May Be Needed

**Your doctor may seek urgent specialist advice if you have a UTI and:**
- You have a kidney transplant.
- You have a urinary tract abnormality, such as hydronephrosis (a swollen kidney), stones, or a bladder that does not empty properly.
- You have a spinal cord impairment.

**In a frail older person** with delirium (sudden confusion), a fever greater than 38º C, or rigors (shaking chills), your doctor may first rule out other causes, then treat on a positive urine dipstick and send the urine for culture.

Symptoms of a kidney infection (pyelonephritis or upper UTI) are managed under a separate pathway.

## Assessment

### History

Your doctor may ask about:
- Recent antibiotics, including trimethoprim supplied by a community pharmacist.
- The possibility of pregnancy (in women). Your doctor may suggest a pregnancy test.
- Obstructive symptoms (in men).
- Any history of urinary tract abnormality.
- Previous UTIs.
- Previous pelvic radiation or surgery.
- Your sexual history.

**Pharmacist supply:** accredited pharmacists may supply a single course of 3 trimethoprim tablets for uncomplicated UTIs in women aged 16 to 65 years. This supply is not subsidised.

### Examination

Your doctor may check your temperature, heart rate, abdomen, and flank (the side of your back).

### Urine tests

- **Routine urine cultures are unnecessary.** Your doctor may send a midstream urine (MSU) sample for culture only if you have lower UTI symptoms (such as pain passing urine, frequency, urgency, or pain above the pubic bone) and one of the following:
  - You are male.
  - You have an anatomical or functional urinary tract abnormality.
  - You have a kidney transplant.
  - You have poorly controlled diabetes.
  - Your symptoms are recurrent or persist despite treatment.
  - You have symptoms of pyelonephritis or upper UTI.
- Urine culture is not recommended without symptoms or signs of UTI, unless you are pregnant or are planned to have an invasive urological procedure.
- **Dipstick urinalysis** may be useful if the diagnosis is in doubt:
  - Classic symptoms in uncomplicated UTIs are more predictive of a UTI (90% probability) than a positive dipstick result.
  - A positive dipstick is sensitive but not specific. If it is positive for either nitrites or leukocytes, the probability of a UTI increases to about 80%.
  - Dipstick testing is not done in people with a urinary catheter.

### Older adults

- Urine is tested (dipstick or culture) in an older adult only if there are clinical signs and symptoms.
- Asymptomatic bacteriuria (bacteria in the urine without symptoms) is common in older adults and is not associated with increased illness. There is no benefit in treating it.
- In a frail older person, new or worsening frequency, urinary incontinence, pain above the pubic bone, visible blood in the urine, or general malaise may be treated. Ideally, your doctor waits for culture results before starting antibiotics, but this depends on the individual.

### Other causes of painful urination

Your doctor may consider causes other than infection:
- Vaginal trauma.
- Irritant or allergic reactions to soaps, douches, vaginal lubricants, spermicidal jellies, contraceptive foams and sponges, tampons and sanitary napkins, and perfumed toilet paper.
- **Interstitial cystitis** (also called bladder pain syndrome): a chronic bladder condition with pelvic pain, painful urination, frequency, urgency, and pressure in the bladder and pelvis, without proven infection or other obvious cause. Your doctor may consider urine cytology and ask you to fill in a bladder diary, including comments about pain.

### Sterile pyuria and sexually transmitted infections

If you have sterile pyuria or are at increased risk of a sexually transmitted infection (STI), your doctor may consider testing for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and mycoplasma genitalium urethritis.

**Sterile pyuria** means raised numbers of white cells in a urine sample that appears sterile on standard culture. Raised white cells can come from contamination of the urine with cells from the lining (mucosal cells), so a clean sample is important before making this diagnosis. Possible causes include:
- A treated UTI within 2 weeks of treatment, or an inadequately treated UTI.
- A UTI with bacteria that are hard to grow in culture.
- Kidney stones.
- Prostatitis.
- Urethritis due to STIs.
- Renal papillary necrosis (for example, from analgesic excess).
- Tubulo-interstitial nephritis.
- Interstitial cystitis.
- Urinary tract cancer.
- Polycystic kidney.
- Genitourinary tuberculosis. This is tested for only if you have been diagnosed with tuberculosis and have sterile pyuria. Testing requires 3 early morning specimens. It is an uncommon diagnosis in New Zealand.

**STI risk:** STIs affect some groups more (young people, men who have sex with men, Māori, and Pacific peoples), but risk behaviours matter more than assumptions about groups. Increased risk includes:
- Multiple sexual contacts.
- A recent change in sexual contact.
- A sexual partner or contact with an STI.
- Being sexually active and younger than 30 years.
- Men who have sex with men.

## Management

### Antibiotics for non-pregnant women

For routine symptoms (painful urination, frequency, and urgency), your doctor may consider:
- **First line:** nitrofurantoin 100 mg modified release tablets twice daily for 5 days.
  - Not used in significant kidney impairment (CrCl less than 30 mL/min).
  - Avoid using urinary alkalising agents (such as Ural sachets) at the same time. They reduce how well nitrofurantoin works.
- **Second line:** cefalexin 500 mg twice daily for 3 days.
- Quinolones (such as norfloxacin) are reserved for more serious infections, due to increasing antimicrobial resistance.
- Treatment may be adjusted according to the urine culture susceptibility results.
- If the UTI is due to a multi-resistant *Escherichia coli*, and standard oral options are unsuitable, your doctor may give fosfomycin 3 g as a single dose.

If symptoms are not routine, your doctor may follow the Recurrent UTIs in Women pathway.

### Antibiotics for men

The antibiotic options for men are the same as for women above. If a man has recurrent or persistent lower urinary tract infections, or a single episode of pyelonephritis, your doctor may request a kidney (renal tract) ultrasound.

### UTI with blood in the urine (haematuria)

- **Visible blood:** treated for a longer course, as for uncomplicated pyelonephritis. A repeat urine test (cell count) is done two weeks after treatment.
- **Blood seen only under the microscope:** treated as usual. A repeat urine test (cell count) is done one month after treatment.
- If the infection has cleared but blood is still present, your doctor will follow the haematuria pathway.

### UTI with a urinary catheter

- A suspected catheter-acquired UTI is treated only if you have fever or pain, or are unwell in yourself (systemically unwell).
- A change of catheter is not required.
- Treatment is adjusted according to susceptibility results.
- With a catheter left in place, bacteria in the urine become almost inevitable (5% per day, 50% by 1 week, almost all by 1 month).
- If there are no symptoms, there is no reason for concern or treatment.
- A change in the appearance or smell of the urine is not a reason for antibiotics.
- Over time, the bacteria form a "biofilm" on the catheter. Antibiotics will not clear an infection while the catheter remains in place, but they do increase resistance.

### Symptoms without evidence of infection (women)

If a woman has symptoms of a lower UTI but testing shows no infection, your doctor may advise:
- Avoiding possible irritants.
- Drinking plenty of water.
- Passing urine after sex.
- Using a urinary alkalising agent (such as Ural sachets). Do not combine it with nitrofurantoin.

Your doctor may also consider managing it as interstitial cystitis, and seek urology advice if there is no improvement.

**Interstitial cystitis management:**
- There is no known cause or agreed treatment. Management is generally symptomatic and supportive.
- Avoid irritants that may make symptoms worse, such as caffeine, alcohol, artificial sweeteners, and hot pepper. There is some evidence that certain exclusion diets may help, but this is not conclusive.
- There is limited evidence for any oral medications.
- Your doctor may consider medications for chronic pain, such as tricyclic antidepressants.
- If not contraindicated, your doctor may consider a trial of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) or oxybutynin.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may seek advice or refer you if:

- **Infectious diseases advice:** antibiotic treatment has failed, symptoms persist, or symptoms come back within 1 week.
- **Non-acute urology assessment:**
  - An abnormal upper urinary tract ultrasound (for example, hydronephrosis, stones, scarring, a soft tissue lesion, or post-void residual greater than 200 mL).
  - A woman with past radiation treatment or pelvic cancer.
  - A man with more than 1 UTI.
  - A woman with recurrent UTIs that have not responded to preventive (prophylactic) antibiotics.
- **Urology advice:** persistent UTI symptoms without evidence of infection that do not respond to management.

See "When Urgent Advice May Be Needed" above for urgent kidney and infectious diseases advice.

---

## Key Concepts for Patients

- Routine urine cultures are unnecessary.
- Bacteria in the urine without symptoms is common in older adults and people with catheters, and does not need treatment.
- Do not take urinary alkalising agents at the same time as nitrofurantoin.
- Always discuss any new symptoms or medication questions with your doctor.
