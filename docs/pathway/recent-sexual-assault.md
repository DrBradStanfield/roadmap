# Recent Sexual Assault

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of sexual assault in adults or adolescents that occurred within the last month. Management differs by time from assault to presentation and by the age of the patient.

For assaults that occurred more than one month ago, a different pathway applies (Previously Undisclosed Sexual Assault).

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to an emergency department or call an ambulance if:

- Severe pain or significant injuries
- Mental health crisis (including suicidality)
- High risk of HIV exposure (e.g., anal assault within 72 hours)

---

## Background

### How Patients May Present

Patients may not always disclose the assault directly. Common presentations include:

- A complaint or history of sexual assault
- Sexual or genital health concerns
- Requests for emergency contraception
- Physical assault injuries
- Being intoxicated or drugged with no recollection of events
- Presenting unconscious or injured

### Time Needed

It can take up to an hour to sensitively manage a disclosure, depending on the patient's needs.

---

## Assessment

### Triage Approach

Your doctor or nurse will:

- Provide privacy and a supportive, non-judgemental approach
- Ask a few key questions to determine next steps — they will not pressure you for full details of the assault at this stage
- Key questions typically asked: how long ago the assault occurred, current symptoms, whether police involvement is being considered, safety of you and your children, and the best way to contact you

### If the Patient is an Adolescent

**Confidentiality:**

- Health information is kept private within the treating team unless there are safety concerns or you give consent to sharing
- If safety concerns arise, others may need to be involved — promising not to tell can place people in unsafe situations
- Parents or caregivers do not have automatic access to health information

**Safety assessment:**

- For adolescents under 17 years, a safety assessment is a major factor in deciding about reporting
- Physical safety (from the alleged offender or family), emotional safety (suicidality, self-harm, pressure from others), and safety of others (e.g., siblings) are all assessed
- The HEeADSSSSS Assessment tool may be used
- All discussions about reporting are documented

### Talking About Police Reporting

Your doctor will talk with you about reporting to the police. This decision is important and can be difficult. A crisis counsellor can also help you think through your options.

**Four options are available:**

1. Undergo a forensic medical examination and report to police — includes therapeutic medical care
2. Undergo a forensic medical examination and decide about reporting later — the forensic report and samples are stored while you decide, and therapeutic medical care is included
3. Undergo a therapeutic medical examination only (at a specialist clinic or a clinic of your choice) and not report
4. Not undergo an examination and not report

**Reasons to involve police:**

- Police are the only authority that can investigate and provide protection
- Public safety — likelihood of previous and further offending
- Being believed and having the offender charged may be therapeutic for some survivors

**Reasons some people are reluctant to involve police:**

- Emotional state, cognitive issues, or physical injury affecting decision-making
- Fear of retaliation
- Stigma or shame if more people learn about the assault
- Past negative experiences with police or other undisclosed factors
- Fear of the judicial process and the possibility of re-traumatisation
- No guarantee of conviction (approximately 10% of cases reported lead to conviction)
- Loss of sense of control within the judicial process

## Assessment by Time Since Assault

### Assault Less Than 7 Days Ago

Further management depends on your preference regarding police involvement.

If you are unsure, a crisis counsellor can help you work through the options.

**Preserving forensic evidence**

Forensic evidence is important and time-sensitive. DNA evidence is retrievable for up to 10 days.

- Do not clean wounds unless part of essential repair of a major injury
- DNA, semen, and trace evidence is destroyed over time by washing, contact with other people, and medical intervention
- Used sanitary pads and tampons can be an excellent source of DNA after ejaculation — do not discard them
- Menstruation may accelerate DNA loss from the vagina
- If you need to pass urine, collect the urine sample and note the date and time — do not send it to a lab, keep it to hand to police

If the assault occurred less than 36 hours ago:

- If at all possible, do not wash, change clothes, urinate, defecate, smoke, drink, eat, brush hair or teeth, or rinse your mouth
- If you must urinate, collect the sample and do not wash hands or wipe the genital area
- Retain any used sanitary pads or tampons

**If you choose to involve police and are medically stable:**

- Your doctor will not examine you (to preserve forensic evidence)
- Police will arrange a forensic medical examination at a specialist sexual assault clinic
- Police can usually transport you to the clinic
- Even if you initially choose police involvement, you can withdraw at any time

**If you choose not to involve police:**

- Your doctor will advise you about the options: forensic examination with deferred reporting, therapeutic examination only through a specialist clinic, or treatment in general practice
- If unsure about reporting, your doctor can seek advice from a sexual assault specialist clinician

### Assault 7 to 28 Days Ago

Your doctor will take a full history, including:

- Brief details of the alleged event: when, where, who was involved (if known), context
- Usual medical history, medications, and allergies
- Gynaecological, contraceptive, and anogenital history
- Relevant sexual health history

**At-risk groups** (who may face increased STI risk or additional considerations) include:
- Men and women aged 15–30 years
- Men who have sex with men (MSM)
- Sex workers
- Intravenous drug users
- Pregnant women
- Transgender people
- Māori and Pacific people
- Immigrants and visitors from developing countries

**Examination:**

- Your doctor will assess any injuries and document them accurately — this is important in case the information is needed for medico-legal purposes
- With your consent, photographs may be taken to assist injury documentation
- A genital examination will be offered

**Investigations:**

- Pregnancy test
- Baseline STI screening (see below), repeated in 2–3 weeks

## Management

All management decisions are made together with your doctor. The guidance below outlines what your doctor is likely to discuss or offer.

### Assault Less Than 7 Days Ago

**Injuries:**

- Your doctor will treat any significant injuries
- If immediate medical intervention is required (e.g., head injury, profuse vaginal bleeding, mental health crisis), emergency department assessment is needed

**Basic medical care (if you prefer treatment in general practice):**

- **Emergency contraception** — if the assault happened less than 5 days ago, emergency contraception must be offered (this is a legal requirement under the Contraception, Sterilisation, and Abortion Act 1977)
- **STI screening** (as below)
- **Genital examination** if you have any genital symptoms
- **Antibiotic prophylaxis** for STI prevention

**Emergency contraception:**

- Your doctor will ask about your last menstrual period, usual cycle length, and other recent unprotected sexual intercourse in the current cycle
- Oral emergency contraception works primarily by inhibiting ovulation
- For women with BMI > 30, levonorgestrel may be less effective — a copper IUD is strongly recommended in this situation
- A copper IUD is the most effective emergency contraception available and can be inserted within 5 days of the earliest estimated ovulation date, or up to 120 hours after unprotected sex
- A copper IUD can be left in place for ongoing contraception or removed at your next period
- Mirena (hormonal IUD) cannot be used as emergency contraception
- Ongoing contraception will be discussed after prescribing oral emergency contraception

**Standard antibiotic prophylaxis:**

For females:
- If assault was < 5 days ago: levonorgestrel 1.5 mg, or copper IUCD if appropriate
- Azithromycin 1 g immediately
- Metronidazole 2 g immediately
- Hepatitis B immunisation (if not immune or status unknown)

For males:
- Azithromycin 1 g immediately
- Hepatitis B immunisation (if not immune or status unknown)

**HIV prophylaxis:**

- Not given routinely
- If HIV prophylaxis may be indicated (e.g., anal assault less than 72 hours ago), your doctor will seek specialist infectious diseases assessment urgently

**STI screening:**

- Vaginal, anal, or pharyngeal swab (self-collected or clinician-collected) for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and trichomonas — repeated in 2–4 weeks
- Self-taken swabs are not advised if you have vaginal or anal symptoms
- First-catch urine sample for chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and trichomonas (for those who do not want a vaginal swab)
- MC+S swab only if symptomatic
- Blood tests: HIV, syphilis, hepatitis B serology
- Repeat serology at 3 months

**ACC sensitive claims:**

- If a mental health injury arising from sexual abuse is suspected, your doctor can lodge a claim with ACC
- While the claim is being assessed, you can access up to 16 hours with an ACC-registered counsellor (Sensitive Claims Support Sessions)
- Your doctor submits an ACC 45 form on your behalf, noting "Sensitive claim" as injury cause
- You can also go directly to a counsellor who can submit the ACC claim
- ACC's website lists registered counsellors and psychologists who provide funded support

**Crisis support:**

Crisis counsellors offer:
- Support regardless of whether you choose to involve police
- 24-hour crisis support and advice by phone
- Availability to be present during the forensic medical examination and police interview
- Assistance making contact with police if you choose to

Your doctor will provide you with crisis support contact details and patient information resources.

**Follow-up:**

Your doctor will arrange follow-up in general practice or with a specialist sexual assault service.

### Assault 7 to 28 Days Ago

- Your doctor will treat any injuries, medical symptoms, or concerns
- STI screening will be offered (as above)
- If you have genital symptoms, a genital examination will be offered
- Antibiotic prophylaxis for STI prevention may be offered
- If there is ongoing uncertainty about police involvement or a more comprehensive medical assessment would be beneficial (especially between 7–10 days post-assault), your doctor may refer you to a specialist sexual assault medical service
- Information about the ACC claims process and crisis support will be provided
- Follow-up will be arranged
