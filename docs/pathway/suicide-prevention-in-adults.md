# Suicide Prevention in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of suicide risk in adults (aged 19 years and over). For young people aged 18 years or under, different considerations apply — discuss with your doctor.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Help Now

If any of the following apply, contact your doctor, go to the emergency department, or call a crisis line immediately:

- A plan with or without preparations to act on it
- Recent suicide attempt
- Unable to identify any good supports
- Loss of friends or family members from suicide
- Uncontrolled substance abuse

---

## Background

- Suicide kills more than 500 people in New Zealand every year.
- Asking directly about suicidality does not increase the risk or create a risk — it is safe and important to ask.
- The priority is to give the patient hope and to find ways to keep them safe. Involving family/whānau/friends in care is a key part of this.
- Suicide risk is higher in those with comorbidities, especially substance abuse, depression, and hopelessness.
- The more risk factors present, the higher the risk of self-harm or death.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider a range of factors when assessing suicide risk.

### Suicide Risk Factors

Your doctor may ask about suicide risk if any of the following apply:

- Previous suicide attempts or self-harm
- Mental illness (especially depression, substance abuse, schizophrenia, eating disorders, personality disorder such as borderline or antisocial type)
- Māori ethnicity, especially young Māori
- Older adults (especially males with recent bereavement, poor health, living alone, isolated, or self-neglect)
- Recent loss or isolation (e.g., relationship breakdown, custody, probation, family court)
- Family history of suicide, or a close friend or family member who has died by or attempted suicide
- Chronic pain or chronic illness

### Vulnerability Factors

Your doctor will also consider factors that may increase vulnerability:

- Low self-esteem or poor social skills
- Family conflict, lack of warmth or affection
- Bullying or peer rejection
- Frequently moving home
- Homelessness
- Parents with mental illness or problematic substance use
- Coming from a low socioeconomic background
- Experience of abuse or neglect

These factors are relevant regardless of the immediate level of risk, because suicides occur across all risk groups.

### Protective Factors

Factors that make it less likely someone will consider, attempt, or die by suicide include:

- Family connections, particularly children
- Strong cultural identity
- Spiritual beliefs that discourage suicide
- Belonging to a community
- Able to make a valued contribution
- Supportive friendships
- Developing a skill, volunteering, or contributing to a cause

### Key Questions Your Doctor Will Ask

Your doctor will ask about suicidality directly. Common questions include:

- Have things been so bad lately that you have felt you would be better off dead, or that life is not worth living?
- Have you ever thought about suicide?
- Do you have a plan about how you would do this?
- Do you have access to anything you could use to carry out a plan?
- Have you made any preparations?

They will also ask about:

- **Current circumstances:** relationships, employment, alcohol and drugs, sleep, criminal offences, mental health, abuse, recent loss, isolation, social supports
- **Previous attempts:** method used, whether they believed they would die, why the attempt didn't succeed
- **Current plan:** lethality, access to means, preparation details, prevention of discovery
- **Positive aspects:** things that give a sense of purpose, satisfaction, or connection — family, religion, community, good problem-solving

### Levels of Concern

Your doctor will assess the overall risk level.

**Features of high concern:**
- A current plan and access to means
- Cannot describe protective factors or has few supports
- Hopeless, withdrawn, or poorly engaged
- Not willing to engage in care and treatment
- Multiple psychosocial factors
- Substance abuse
- Previous serious attempt
- Violence

**Other features of concern:**
- May have a plan but limited access to that method, or access can be removed (verified with a support person)
- Can describe only a few protective factors
- Has an acute stressor but it may be manageable in the community with support

**More positive features:**
- Good supports and protective factors
- Cooperative and willing to accept treatment
- Can focus on the future
- Suitable for community management (corroborated with support people)

### Risk Factors: Constant vs. Changeable

**Constant risk factors** include:
- History of mental illness (severe depression, psychosis, eating disorder, personality disorder)
- Previous suicide attempts
- Previous family dysfunction
- Māori ethnicity (highest risk), refugee or immigrant status
- Male gender
- Substance abuse
- Bullying or social isolation
- History of sexual or other abuse
- Knowing someone who has died by suicide
- Identity issues (cultural or sexual orientation)
- School or employment failure
- Poor coping skills
- Lack of protective factors

**Variable (changeable) risk factors** include:
- Suicidal intent — presence of plan, lethality, access to means, degree of determination to die
- Hopelessness and helplessness
- Interpersonal or family crisis, bereavement (especially by suicide)
- Alcohol and drug use, impulsivity when intoxicated
- Peer group influences, including through social media
- Acute or chronic housing issues
- Stressful life events

Your doctor will reassess these regularly as circumstances change.

## Management

### By Level of Concern

Your doctor will manage the situation based on the assessed level of risk:

- **High concern or red flags:** Your doctor will arrange urgent mental health assessment or seek specialist mental health advice. Compulsory assessment may be arranged where indicated.
- **Moderate concern or uncertainty:** Your doctor may arrange a non-acute mental health assessment or seek advice. If needed, respite care or semi-urgent appointments can be arranged.
- **Older adults (over 65 years):** Your doctor will seek specialist advice for older persons' health.

### Encouraging Hope and Support

Your doctor will:

- Focus on protective factors (e.g., children, partner, parents, religion, community)
- Involve family or friends in the patient's care and safety
- Ensure support people are available, particularly after any hospital discharge

### Reducing Impulsivity and Access to Means

Your doctor may recommend:

- Discouraging use of alcohol and other substances — these increase impulsivity
- If the patient is currently intoxicated, keeping them safe with a family member until sober, then reassessing
- Referral to alcohol and drug support services if needed
- Working with family/whānau to minimise access to alcohol and other substances
- Removing access to lethal means (e.g., safeguarding ropes, dog leads, firearms)

### Safety Planning

Your doctor will work with you and your support people to create a safety plan. A safety plan typically includes:

- Making the environment safe (reducing access to means)
- Identifying and engaging immediate personal supports (family/whānau, friends)
- Recording 24-hour emergency contact details

Consider the **Manawa My Own Survival Plan** (available as an app or in print). This tool includes early warning signs of deteriorating mental health, reasons to live, actions to improve safety, and emergency contact details. It was developed specifically for people in New Zealand.

The safety plan should be regularly reviewed and updated.

### Ongoing Management

Your doctor may also:

- Address any identified psychosocial or mental health issues
- Arrange counselling or mental health support through your Primary Health Organisation (PHO) or practice
- Arrange follow-up appointments
- Refer to community mental health support services
- Consider whether any changes in health status affect responsibilities under firearms licence legislation

## Crisis and Emergency Contacts

If you or someone you know is in crisis:

- **1737** — Free call or text, 24/7 (mental health support)
- **Lifeline Tautoko Suicide Crisis Helpline** — free call **0508 828 865**
- **Emergency department** — go to your nearest ED or call 111
