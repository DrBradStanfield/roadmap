# Fungal Nail Infections in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of fungal nail infections (onychomycosis or tinea unguium) in adults.

---

## Background

Onychomycosis (fungal nail infection) is common in adults. One or more nails may be affected. Toenails are affected 7 times more often than fingernails.

Fungal nail infections are commonly caused by dermatophyte organisms including *Trichophyton rubrum*, *Trichophyton interdigitale*, and *Trichophyton mentagrophytes*. Infection is typically insidious in onset.

Fungal nail infections can also be caused by non-dermatophyte yeasts (*Candida albicans*) and moulds. The primary pathology is often chronic paronychia (nail fold inflammation and loss of cuticle) due to repeated exposure to wet-work.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will look for typical features of infected nails:

- **Hyperkeratosis** – thickening of the nail plate
- **Discolouration** – complete, patchy, or linear areas of brown, yellow, or white

Your doctor will also check for symptoms — many people have no symptoms beyond difficulty trimming the nails. Some affected nails can be painful, interfere with walking, or spread infection to other parts of the body.

The skin between the toes and the groin will be examined for co-existing fungal skin infections (tinea pedis, tinea cruris, tinea corporis).

### Risk Factors for Poor Treatment Outcome

Your doctor will assess whether any of the following apply:

- Diabetes mellitus
- Immunosuppression
- Previous history of fungal nail infection
- Older age
- Nail trauma (e.g. from sports)
- Psoriasis
- Peripheral vascular disease
- More than 50% of the nail plate involved

### Differential Diagnoses

Your doctor may consider other causes for nail changes, including:

- Bacterial infection (*Pseudomonas aeruginosa* — black or green nail)
- Eczema of the distal fingers
- Lichen planus — scarring of the proximal nail fold
- Malignancy — squamous cell carcinoma or melanoma (amelanotic or acral)
- Nail dystrophy from systemic disease
- Onychogryphosis — age-related nail thickening with yellowing and friability
- Onycholysis (nail lifting from the nail bed) — can be caused by skin disease, infections, medications (tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, chlorpromazine, some anti-cancer drugs, oral contraceptives), or contact dermatitis
- Psoriasis — may co-exist with fungal nail infection
- Trauma — from injury or rubbing of footwear

### Laboratory Testing

Your doctor may collect nail clippings and subungual debris for laboratory testing to identify the organism and confirm the diagnosis. The sample is taken from the juncture of the nail bed and nail plate — full-thickness clippings from the end of the nail, or scrapings from the nail surface if the top of the nail is involved.

Key points about testing:

- Samples should be taken before starting any treatment, or at least 3 weeks after stopping topical nail treatments
- If culture is performed, results take 3 to 6 weeks
- Microscopic detection is more sensitive than culture
- Culture is not routinely performed for toenail onychomycosis unless there is a history of treatment failure with oral antifungals
- False positive and false negative results can occur; repeat sampling may be needed

## Management

### Lifestyle Measures

Your doctor will advise reducing fungal growth by avoiding moisture, heat, occlusion, and tight-fitting shoes. Treating the infection without addressing these environmental factors can lead to failure.

Any underlying conditions that may be contributing — such as diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, psoriasis, or eczema of the fingers — will also be considered.

### Whether to Treat

Your doctor will weigh the risk of side-effects against the potential symptomatic and cosmetic benefit. Treatment:

- May not be necessary
- May be inappropriate for older people or those on multiple medications, particularly with oral antifungals

Even with treatment, patients should be aware that:

- It can take up to 12 months to confirm successful treatment, due to slow nail growth
- 25% of patients — particularly elderly patients — fail to achieve a cure with any treatment
- The nail may never look completely normal

### Oral Antifungals

Oral antifungals are recommended as first-line therapy for most patients, especially if the entire nail plate is involved. They have cure rates of 70 to 80%, though relapse is common.

Your doctor may consider oral antifungals to be inappropriate in elderly patients or those on multiple medications due to the risk of side-effects and drug interactions.

**Terbinafine** (first-line):

- 250 mg once daily for 6 weeks for fingernails, 3 months for toenails (4 months may be required in diabetes)
- Most effective for dermatophyte organisms (*Trichophyton* species)
- Not recommended in liver disease; dose reduction required if eGFR < 50 mL/minute
- Baseline LFTs and FBC are performed, repeated after 4–6 weeks of treatment
- Stop and seek medical assessment if side-effects develop: rash, gastrointestinal upset, flu-like illness, pruritus, jaundice

**Itraconazole** (second-line):

- Pulsed regimen: 200 mg twice daily for 7 days, followed by 21 days off treatment
- Treat for 2 months for fingernails, 3 months for toenails
- More effective for non-dermatophyte yeasts (*Candida albicans*) and moulds
- Not recommended for patients at increased risk of heart failure, including those with known heart disease, COPD, or renal failure
- Be aware of interactions with calcium channel blockers and other medications

### Topical Antifungal Nail Lacquers

Topical lacquers may be considered for limited disease with partial (but not complete) nail plate involvement, or alongside oral antifungals.

- **Amorolfine** 5% — applied 1 to 2 days weekly for 24 to 52 weeks
- **Ciclopirox** 8% — applied every second day for the first month, then twice weekly for the second month, then once weekly for up to 6 months

These lacquers:

- Require good mobility and eyesight, and time to file and clean the nail before application
- Have low cure rates
- Are not effective for full-thickness nail infection
- Require at least 6 to 12 months of treatment

### Monitoring

Your doctor may photograph the nail or mark the proximal end of the infection to monitor response. The infection should not spread further if treatment is effective.

### If Treatment Fails

Your doctor may:

- Collect new nail samples to re-confirm the diagnosis
- Check the technique for applying topical lacquers and review treatment adherence
- Reconsider the differential diagnosis
- Switch to an alternative drug or combine oral and topical treatments

### When Specialist Input May Be Needed

Your doctor may seek dermatology advice if there is:

- Uncertain diagnosis following investigation
- Suspicion of neoplastic nail disease (e.g. squamous cell carcinoma, amelanotic melanoma, or acral melanoma)

Referral to private podiatry services may be offered. Filing the nail down can help. Other treatments (laser, photodynamic therapy, iontophoresis, ultrasound) have minimal supporting evidence.
