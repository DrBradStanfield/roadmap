# Hypercalcaemia of Malignancy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of high blood calcium (hypercalcaemia) caused by cancer in adults. It is relevant in palliative care settings.

---

## Background

Hypercalcaemia is one of the most common complications of cancer. It occurs more frequently in:
- Lung cancer (primary)
- Breast cancer
- Prostate cancer
- Kidney (renal) cancer
- Myeloma
- Lymphoma

**How does cancer cause high calcium?**
- The cancer releases a substance similar to parathyroid hormone (PTH), which raises calcium levels.
- Direct destruction or invasion of bone by cancer also releases calcium.

**Prognosis:** The development of hypercalcaemia of malignancy is a sign of advanced disease and is a poor prognostic indicator.

---

## Symptoms

Hypercalcaemia symptoms often develop gradually and can be easy to miss. A key clinical clue is the triad of:

- **Polydipsia** — excessive thirst (despite this, the patient is often clinically dehydrated)
- **Polyuria** — passing more urine than usual
- **Constipation**

Other symptoms include:
- Increasing pain
- Fatigue
- Loss of appetite and nausea, with or without vomiting
- Vagueness, confusion, delirium, or psychosis

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Ask about symptoms, particularly the triad above.
- Examine the patient, checking hydration and the mouth (oral mucosa).
- Arrange blood tests:
  - **Corrected serum calcium** — levels above 3.0 mmol/L almost always cause symptoms.
  - Creatinine, electrolytes, and eGFR (kidney function).

Your doctor will also consider the patient's stage of illness and goals of care:
- If the patient cannot communicate their wishes, advance care planning documents or the enduring power of attorney will be consulted.
- If the patient's short-term prognosis is very poor, comfort care only may be the most appropriate approach.

---

## Management

Management is guided by the patient's symptoms, the calcium level, their goals of care, and the overall stage of their palliative illness.

### Initial Management (All Patients)

- **Stop** thiazide diuretics, calcium supplements, and vitamin D supplements (these raise calcium further).
- **Encourage oral fluids** to help with hydration.
  - If oral fluids are not tolerated, specialist palliative care advice will be sought regarding subcutaneous (under the skin) fluids.
- **Treat nausea and vomiting** with haloperidol if needed (also useful for agitation):
  - Haloperidol works centrally to block nausea signals. It is particularly effective for nausea caused by chemical changes such as high calcium, opioids, or kidney failure.
  - It is **contraindicated** in Parkinson's disease.
  - Given orally or subcutaneously at a low dose, with a maximum of 5 mg daily. Doses above 5 mg do not offer additional benefit for nausea.

### Is Active Treatment Appropriate?

Active treatment (IV fluids and bisphosphonates) may not be appropriate for everyone. It is generally **not pursued** if:
- The patient or their enduring power of attorney declines active treatment.
- The hypercalcaemia has not responded to previous treatment (refractory).
- The calcium has returned rapidly after previous treatment (within 3 weeks).
- The patient is expected to enter the terminal phase shortly, regardless of the calcium level.

For **asymptomatic patients** with a corrected calcium below 3.0 mmol/L, the benefit of active treatment is uncertain.

### Active Treatment (When Appropriate)

If the patient has significant symptoms or a corrected calcium above 3.0 mmol/L, and active treatment is appropriate, your doctor will seek advice from the patient's known clinical team (general medicine, haematology, oncology, or palliative care) for:
- **Intravenous (IV) rehydration**
- **Bisphosphonates** (medications that reduce calcium release from bone, e.g. zoledronic acid or pamidronate)

This typically requires hospital-based treatment.

### If Symptoms Persist

If symptoms continue despite initial management, your doctor will seek specialist palliative care advice or arrange a non-acute palliative care assessment.

---

## When Specialist Palliative Care Advice is Sought

Your doctor will seek specialist input if:
- Oral fluids are not tolerated (consideration of subcutaneous fluids).
- Symptoms persist after rehydration.
- Active treatment with IV fluids and bisphosphonates is being considered.
- Symptoms persist despite attempted management.
