# Substance Use in Pregnancy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of substance use in pregnancy, including alcohol, tobacco, and illicit or prescribed drugs.

---

## Background

Substance use in pregnancy is a common and important issue in maternity care. It carries significant risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes, as well as ongoing health, legal, social, and economic problems for mother and child.

Alcohol and tobacco use are most common, but cannabis, opioid analgesics, heroin, amphetamines, and synthetic drugs are also significant. Polysubstance use is common, and patterns of use before pregnancy often continue into pregnancy.

Detecting substance use in pregnancy can be a window of opportunity — motivation to change and willingness to engage with support is often higher during pregnancy. Sensitive counselling and referral to appropriate multidisciplinary services is important once substance use is identified.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if:

- Signs of acute withdrawal (e.g., seizures, severe agitation, confusion)
- Suspected opioid overdose
- Collapse, respiratory compromise, or altered consciousness

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will screen for substance use at every antenatal visit. Assessment typically includes:

**Substance use history:**
- Alcohol and tobacco use
- Prescribed and illicit drug use — type, route of administration, frequency, pattern, and timing of most recent use

**Medical history:**
- Current physical symptoms (e.g., withdrawal symptoms)
- Previous medical conditions related to substance use (overdoses, seizures, cardiomyopathy)

**Social history:**
- Housing, phone access, transport
- Children under 3 years in the household
- Financial and legal situation
- Support systems (partner, family, formal supports) — including whether the partner also uses substances
- Family violence
- Previous child protection notifications

**Mental health screening:**
- Depression: *"During the past month, have you been bothered by feeling down, depressed, or hopeless?"* and *"During the past month, have you been bothered by little interest or pleasure in doing things?"*
- Anxiety: *"During the past month, have you been worrying a lot about everyday problems?"*

**Blood-borne virus screening:**
- Hepatitis B and HIV are part of routine antenatal bloods in the first trimester
- Hepatitis C bloods are arranged if there is a history of intravenous drug use or unknown hepatitis C status
- Repeat screening may be done later in pregnancy based on ongoing risk

**Barriers to care:** Your doctor will be aware of common barriers including stigma, fear of losing custody of children, practical issues (transport, childcare), and a substance-using partner.

## Substance-Specific Risks

### Alcohol

There is no safe level of alcohol use in pregnancy or breastfeeding. Fetal effects are thought to be dose-related and depend on quantity, timing, maternal metabolism, and other risk factors.

**Possible adverse outcomes:**
- Maternal/pregnancy: miscarriage, stillbirth
- Fetal/neonatal: intrauterine growth restriction, low birth weight, preterm birth, fetal alcohol spectrum disorders (FASD)
- Childhood: global developmental delays, attention problems, motor and visuospatial delays, poor social skills

Patient resource: Healthify He Puna Waiora — [Pregnancy and Alcohol](https://healthify.nz/hauora-wellbeing/p/pregnancy-alcohol)

---

### Tobacco

**Possible adverse outcomes:**
- Maternal/pregnancy: miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, poor placental development, placental abruption, premature rupture of membranes; also increased risk of cancer, cardiovascular disease, and pulmonary disease
- Fetal/neonatal: fetal growth restriction, low birth weight, preterm birth
- Childhood: increased risk of sudden unexpected death in infancy (SUDI), bronchiolitis, asthma, ear infections; conduct disorder, reduced IQ, ADHD

Patient resource: Healthify He Puna Waiora — [Smoking and Pregnancy](https://healthify.nz/hauora-wellbeing/s/smoking-and-pregnancy)

---

### Vaping

Vaping is not harmless but is likely less harmful than smoking while pregnant. Nicotine-containing vapes carry the same nicotine-exposure risks to the developing fetus as tobacco. Non-nicotine vapes still contain heavy metals, ultra-fine particles, volatile organic compounds, and some flavouring chemicals that may be harmful when inhaled. Nicotine replacement therapy is the preferred smoking cessation strategy in pregnancy.

Patient resource: New Zealand College of Midwives — [Smoking, Vaping, and Drugs](https://www.midwife.org.nz/women/pregnancy/smoking-and-drugs)

---

### Cannabis

Cannabis is the most widely used illicit drug in New Zealand. Adverse outcomes are more likely with heavy and sustained use.

**Possible adverse outcomes:**
- Maternal/pregnancy: difficulties with self-care, parenting, and maintaining relationships (moderate to heavy use)
- Fetal/neonatal: mild withdrawal signs, sleep disturbances, altered responses to visual stimuli, increased startles and tremors
- Childhood: neurodevelopmental deficit or delay, executive function impairment, impulsivity and inattention, depression

Patient resource: The Royal Women's Hospital — [Using Cannabis During Pregnancy and Breastfeeding](https://thewomens.r.worldssl.net/images/uploads/fact-sheets/Cannabis-2021.pdf)

---

### Opioids (heroin and prescribed opioids)

**Possible adverse outcomes:**
- Maternal/pregnancy: impaired self-care and safe parenting, increased risk of blood-borne viruses (hepatitis B, C, HIV) and infection (bacteraemia, endocarditis, injection site infection), miscarriage, pre-eclampsia, antepartum and postpartum haemorrhage, premature labour
- Fetal/neonatal: intrauterine growth restriction, low birth weight, intrauterine infection, hypoxia or anoxia, neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS), sudden unexpected death in infancy (SUDI)

**Opioid substitution treatment (OST):** Your doctor may consider that pregnant women with opioid dependence generally remain on OST during pregnancy, as OST is associated with lower risks to mother and fetus compared to illicit opioid use or failed detoxification. OST supports access to antenatal care, reduces illicit drug use, lowers risk of blood-borne viruses, and improves maternal social functioning.

Patient resources — The Royal Women's Hospital:
- [Using Heroin During Pregnancy and Breastfeeding](https://thewomens.r.worldssl.net/images/uploads/fact-sheets/Heroin-2021.pdf)
- [Using Prescription Opioids During Pregnancy and Breastfeeding](https://thewomens.r.worldssl.net/images/uploads/fact-sheets/Prescription_opioids.pdf)

---

### Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs (e.g., zopiclone)

Tolerance and dependence can develop easily; abrupt withdrawal can cause seizures. Short-term use for specific medical reasons is unlikely to be harmful to the fetus. Benzodiazepines should be avoided during breastfeeding.

**Possible adverse outcomes:**
- Maternal/pregnancy: tolerance and dependence; abrupt withdrawal can cause withdrawal syndrome and seizures
- Fetal/neonatal: crosses the placenta; sedation, decreased muscle tone, and breathing problems in the newborn; risk of neonatal abstinence syndrome (irritability, sleep issues, and in rare cases, seizures)

Patient resource: The Royal Women's Hospital — [Using Benzodiazepines During Pregnancy and Breastfeeding](https://thewomens.r.worldssl.net/images/uploads/fact-sheets/Benzos-2021.pdf)

---

### Methamphetamine (P)

Other unknown substances may be present with effects that are unpredictable.

**Possible adverse outcomes:**
- Maternal/pregnancy: tachycardia, arrhythmia, elevated body temperature, increased blood pressure, antepartum haemorrhage, increased risk of anxiety and depression, increased risk of blood-borne viruses and infection
- Fetal/neonatal: increased fetal blood pressure, reduced fetal oxyhaemoglobin saturation, decreased uterine blood flow, preterm birth, lower birth weight, fetal abnormalities (including cardiac), altered fetal nervous system and brain development, neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS)

Patient resource: The Royal Women's Hospital — [Using Amphetamines During Pregnancy and Breastfeeding](https://thewomens.r.worldssl.net/images/uploads/fact-sheets/Amphetamines-2021.pdf)

---

### Cocaine

**Possible adverse outcomes:**
- Maternal/pregnancy: placental abruption, premature rupture of membranes
- Fetal/neonatal: crosses the blood-brain barrier; intrauterine growth restriction and low birth weight; fetal or neonatal cerebrovascular events; case reports of malformations (genitourinary tract, heart, limbs, face)
- Childhood: attention problems, disruptive behaviours, language deficits, executive function abnormalities, lower height and weight trajectories

---

### Ecstasy (MDMA)

**Possible adverse outcomes:**
- Maternal/pregnancy: miscarriage
- Fetal/neonatal: crosses the placenta; preterm birth; poor infant mental and motor development

Patient resource: The Royal Women's Hospital — [Using Amphetamines During Pregnancy and Breastfeeding](https://thewomens.r.worldssl.net/images/uploads/fact-sheets/Amphetamines-2021.pdf)

---

### Gamma Hydroxybutyrate (GHB)

Limited data exists on GHB effects in pregnancy. Withdrawal symptoms include anxiety, agitation, sleep disturbances, hallucinations, tremors, hyper-sedation, and in severe cases, seizures and delirium. Symptoms occur mainly in the first 24 hours after stopping but can last up to 3 weeks.

---

### Gabapentinoids (gabapentin, pregabalin)

Use in pregnancy has increased significantly. Gabapentinoids may be prescribed for neuropathic pain or seizures, but also have potential for misuse. Exposure during pregnancy does not appear to be associated with congenital malformation, though there is some evidence of a small increased risk of preterm birth and spontaneous abortion.

## Management

Your doctor will coordinate care across multiple areas:

**Immediate concerns:**
- If there are signs of acute withdrawal, your doctor will arrange emergency assessment
- Withdrawal in pregnancy is considered high risk — management involves balancing the health of mother, baby, and the physiological dynamics of pregnancy

**Withdrawal considerations:**
- *Alcohol and benzodiazepine withdrawal* can cause seizures and is high risk — your doctor will seek specialist input before attempting withdrawal
- *Opioid withdrawal vs. maintenance:* Your doctor may consider that opioid substitution treatment (OST) is generally safer than attempted detoxification in pregnancy; risks include miscarriage in the first trimester and fetal distress in the third trimester with opioid withdrawal

**Specialist referral:**
- Your doctor will arrange referral to alcohol and drug services for pregnant patients using alcohol or other substances — especially if there is difficulty stopping, risk of withdrawal, or complex needs
- All pregnant patients with substance use issues are typically referred early to obstetric care and encouraged to find a lead maternity carer (midwife) as soon as possible

**Mental health:**
- Co-occurring depression and anxiety are common and will be assessed and managed alongside substance use

**Child protection:**
- Your doctor will consider whether a pre-birth child protection notification is indicated, taking into account the patient's ability to provide a safe environment, available support, history of family violence, and engagement with care

**Neonatal abstinence syndrome (NAS):**
- NAS is most common following opioid use in pregnancy; signs typically appear within the first 24–72 hours after birth but may be delayed up to 10 days
- Signs include tremors, irritability, hypertonicity, vomiting, high-pitched cry, sneezing, respiratory distress, fever, diarrhoea, sweating, and convulsions
- Recurrent sneezing and yawning in a newborn should raise the possibility of withdrawal
- Your doctor will ensure the obstetric team is aware of NAS risk ahead of delivery

**Infant feeding:**
- Your doctor will discuss the individual risks and benefits of breastfeeding versus formula feeding, including blood-borne virus status
- HIV-positive women are advised not to breastfeed; hepatitis C-positive women may breastfeed
- If continuing substance use while breastfeeding, specific guidance applies:
  - *Benzodiazepines:* avoid breastfeeding immediately after short-acting benzodiazepines; avoid long-acting benzodiazepines
  - *Amphetamines:* avoid breastfeeding for 24 hours after use; express and discard milk
  - *Alcohol:* avoid before breastfeeding; limit to 2 standard drinks per day and express before drinking

**Follow-up:**
- Regular follow-up is arranged for both mother and baby, including the standard 6-week postpartum check
- Additional appointments may be needed to review NAS signs, infant weight gain, postnatal depression screening, and contraception planning

**Relapse prevention:**
- Ongoing monitoring of substance use and infant safety planning
- Community alcohol and drug pregnancy and parental services can provide ongoing support
