# Enteral Feeding Tubes in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers community management of adults who have a long-term enteral feeding tube (PEG, RIG, PEGJ, or surgical gastrostomy/jejunostomy), including routine monitoring and how to identify and manage complications.

---

## Types of Enteral Feeding Tubes

Long-term enteral feeding (beyond 6 weeks) requires a gastrostomy or jejunostomy tube. Short-term feeding via nasogastric (NG) or nasojejunal (NJ) tubes is used in hospital but is not suitable long term.

- **PEG** — percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy tube, inserted via endoscopy into the stomach.
- **PEGJ** — percutaneous endoscopic gastro-jejunal tube; allows gastric access/decompression and jejunal access for feeding.
- **RIG** — radiologically inserted gastrostomy tube; can be extended into the jejunum (RIG-J).
- **Surgical gastrostomy or jejunostomy** — performed when less invasive procedures are not appropriate; may be laparoscopic or open.
- **Low profile device (button)** — sits just above the skin; feeds into stomach (gastrostomy) and/or jejunum (jejunostomy).

---

## Routine Monitoring

Your doctor will review you every 3 to 6 months, or more often if there is a change in clinical condition (e.g., significant weight change) or if you are transitioning to oral intake. Reviews typically include:

- Checking the external flange (rim) position — it should sit 2 to 5 mm off the skin.
- Confirming the tube can be rotated 360 degrees and pulled about 1 cm out through the stoma.
- Assessing nutritional state — significant weight change may cause an ill-fitting device.
- 6-monthly weight or BMI, and blood tests (iron studies, vitamin D, renal function, liver function, complete blood count, zinc, copper, B12, folate).
- Asking about gastrointestinal function: nausea, vomiting, reflux, bowel habit, urine output.
- Reviewing the emergency action plan.

**Patient and carer responsibilities:**
- Keep the stoma site clean and dry.
- Rotate the tube daily.
- Check and note the cm markings on the tube.
- Maintain an emergency replacement kit and know the emergency action plan.

## Complications — Identification and Management

### Accidental Tube Dislodgement or Removal

**Early removal (within 6 weeks of insertion) is an emergency** due to risk of peritonitis — contact your doctor immediately.

If the tube falls out at any stage, it needs to be replaced promptly. Your doctor will contact the specialist team to arrange replacement. A Foley catheter may be placed temporarily through the stoma to keep it open while awaiting specialist review.

---

### Aspiration

Aspiration of feed or saliva can cause aspiration pneumonia, which can be serious. Patients with a prior history of pneumonia are at particularly high risk.

Management:
- Always feed at 30 degrees or higher, and remain elevated for 1 hour after feeding.
- If aspiration of feed continues to be a problem, your doctor will contact your dietitian to review the feeding method (e.g., switching from bolus to pump feeding, or altering the feeding regime).
- If systemically unwell, seek acute medical assessment.
- In patients with terminal illness or advanced frailty and recurrent pneumonia, palliative care input may be appropriate.

---

### Diarrhoea

Watery or frequent stool may be caused by:
- Medications (especially antibiotics, antacids, sorbitol-containing liquid medications)
- Infection
- Faecal impaction with overflow
- Contaminated equipment or feeds
- Intolerance to the feeding formula (especially hyperosmolar feeds)
- "Dumping syndrome" — feed given too cold, too hot, too fast, or in excessive quantity
- Bolus feeding into a jejunal tube (jejunal feeding must use a pump)

Your doctor will:
- Ensure adequate hydration.
- Consider an abdominal X-ray to exclude faecal overload.
- Obtain a stool specimen.
- Review medications for a potential cause.
- Liaise with your dietitian if formula intolerance is suspected.
- For unresolved diarrhoea, seek gastroenterology assessment.

---

### Fixed Tube or 'Buried Bumper Syndrome'

This occurs when the internal bumper migrates into the gastrostomy tract or peritoneum, usually from a tight external flange. Signs include:
- Induration (hardness) around the stoma
- Localised pain
- Partial or complete loss of gastrostomy patency
- Leakage around the stoma
- Inability to advance or rotate the tube

**Do not use the tube** if it cannot be freely rotated and moved in or out. Your doctor will seek acute gastroenterology assessment.

---

### Hypergranulation Tissue

An excessive healing reaction at the stoma site — appears as a fleshy, easily bleeding lump. A small amount of granulation is normal. Causes include moisture, infection, and excessive tube movement from an ill-fitting device.

Treatment:
- Topical corticosteroid ointment (e.g., Pimafucort) twice daily for 5 days.
- If no response, cauterisation with silver nitrate 2 to 3 times per week until tissue has completely sloughed.

---

### Leaking Around the Tube

Leaking is common and can cause dermatitis. Some serous ooze is normal. Blood-stained ooze may be related to hypergranulation. Large-volume leaking around feeding times suggests gastric fluid leak. Faeculent (faecal-smelling) leakage suggests faecal impaction with overflow.

Potential causes include: split or damaged tube, under-inflated balloon, poor stoma seal, faecal impaction, decreased gastric motility, tube migration or blockage, buried bumper.

Your doctor will contact the specialist nurse for advice and to arrange a tubogram (contrast X-ray) to check tube position and integrity. Occasionally a CT scan is needed to rule out buried bumper. Protect the surrounding skin with barrier creams or absorbent dressings until resolved. If not tube-related, your dietitian may review volume tolerance.

---

### Local Dermatitis

Caused by persistent leakage of gastric fluid. The skin around the stoma will be red, moist, and painful. Superficial fungal infection may also be present.

Management:
- Address any underlying leakage.
- Ensure the external flange is appropriately positioned and not too loose.
- Position the tube end above the exit site to prevent gravity drainage.
- Protect the skin with barrier creams or absorbent dressings.
- Your doctor may consider a proton pump inhibitor to reduce gastric acid secretion, or a regular prokinetic medication (metoclopramide or domperidone).

---

### Peristomal Infection or Cellulitis

A small amount of peristomal redness is normal. More significant signs include spreading erythema with warmth and pain. Patchy red itchy papules with satellite lesions suggest candidiasis (fungal infection).

Your doctor will:
- Take a swab to identify the organism.
- While awaiting the result, prescribe antibiotics:
  - Flucloxacillin 1 g orally four times daily, or
  - Cefalexin 500 mg orally four times daily (mild penicillin allergy), or
  - Clindamycin 450 mg orally three times daily (severe penicillin allergy).
- Treat suspected or confirmed candidiasis with topical antifungal cream (miconazole 2% or clotrimazole 1%).
- If febrile or systemically unwell, consider hospital admission and intravenous antibiotics.
- If multi-resistant organisms are identified, seek infectious diseases or general medicine advice.

---

### Tube Blockage

Causes include: inadequate flushing, inappropriate medication administration (particularly magnesium, most PPIs, or mixed medications), inappropriate material in the tube, solidified gastric fluid, or ageing device materials.

Management:
1. Check for kinks in tubing and confirm the clamp is off.
2. Massage (milk) the tube.
3. Flush with sterile warm water using a pulsatile (back-and-forth) motion to dislodge coagulated formula.
4. If unable to unblock, contact the specialist nurse to arrange tube replacement.

---

### Tube Migration

Tubes can migrate into the oesophagus or through the pylorus into the small bowel, potentially causing obstruction. The stoma level (cm marking at skin) is documented in the patient's notes. If the tube appears to have migrated, your doctor will seek specialist nurse advice.

---

### Systemic Infection

Signs of systemic infection from the stoma: spreading wound erythema, induration or discolouration spreading into the peri-wound area, lymphangitis, red streak following a blood vessel, malaise, fever, raised pulse.

Signs of sepsis: increased respiratory rate, increased heart rate, confusion or disorientation, fever or shivering, muscle pain, low urine output, blue or blotchy skin/lips/tongue, haemorrhagic rash, gastrointestinal symptoms.

- **Clinically stable systemic infection** — your doctor will seek acute gastroenterology assessment.
- **Signs of sepsis, clinically unstable, or deteriorating** — seek acute general medicine assessment (emergency).

---

### Planned Tube Removal

All tubes must be removed by the service that inserted them. Tubes must not be removed until at least 6 weeks from initial insertion (risk of stomach contents leaking into the peritoneum). Replacement of PEG or RIG tubes is also referred back to the inserting service.

---

## When to Seek Help

Your doctor will contact the specialist team in these situations. This is a guide to what to expect:

| Situation | Action |
|-----------|--------|
| Tube dislodged within 6 weeks of insertion | Emergency — acute assessment |
| Signs of sepsis or clinically unstable | Acute general medicine assessment |
| Systemic infection, clinically stable | Acute gastroenterology assessment |
| Tube cannot rotate or move freely (buried bumper) | Acute gastroenterology assessment |
| Unresolved diarrhoea | Gastroenterology assessment |
| Accidental tube removal, recalcitrant leaks, tube migration | Enteral feeding nurse specialist |
| Copious leakage, refeeding syndrome risk, aspiration problem, formula intolerance | Dietitian |
| Aspiration in terminal illness or advanced frailty with recurrent pneumonia | Palliative care |
| Multi-resistant organism in peristomal infection | Infectious diseases or general medicine |
| Medication suitability via enteral route | Community pharmacist |
