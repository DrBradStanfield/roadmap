# Medications for Perinatal Depression and Anxiety

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers medication use for depression and anxiety during pregnancy and the postpartum period.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Contact emergency services or go to the emergency department if:

- Suicidality (thoughts of suicide or self-harm)
- Risk of harm to the fetus or baby

---

## Background

Depression and anxiety disorders are common during pregnancy and the postpartum period. Left untreated, they are associated with significant and potentially long-lasting adverse effects for mothers, babies, and the mother-baby relationship.

**When is antidepressant medication indicated?**

- Moderate depression or anxiety that has not responded to non-pharmacological treatments
- Severe depression or anxiety

In mild depression or anxiety, there is little evidence for the benefit of antidepressants. Other first-line treatments should be considered: lifestyle changes (exercise, relaxation), talking therapies, and community supports.

**Are antidepressants safe in pregnancy?**

The most recent evidence supports that most antidepressants carry low risk in pregnancy and breastfeeding, including the first trimester. The risks and benefits to both mother and baby must be balanced. Typically, the benefits of medication treatment for moderate to severe depression and anxiety outweigh the risks. The risks of untreated maternal depression — including effects on pregnancy outcomes and infant attachment — should always be factored into the decision.

In most situations, antidepressant treatment can be started and managed within primary care.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider several factors:

### 1. Trimester and teratogenesis risk

If in the first trimester, your doctor will assess the risk of teratogenesis (harm to the developing baby):

- They will ask about alcohol use, recreational drugs, and other medications
- **Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs)** — especially sertraline — are considered low risk in relation to teratogenesis, including in the first trimester
- **Paroxetine** may be associated with a small increase in cardiovascular abnormalities
- **Venlafaxine** and **tricyclic antidepressants** (notably nortriptyline and amitriptyline) are also reasonable choices in pregnancy when needed for sustained wellness
- **Sodium valproate and carbamazepine** are highly teratogenic and should not be prescribed as mood stabilisers in pregnancy. If you are currently pregnant and taking either of these, your doctor will seek urgent specialist advice
- **Lamotrigine** is a much lower-risk antiepileptic/mood stabiliser and may be appropriate to continue during pregnancy with specialist guidance

### 2. Breastfeeding status

If breastfeeding:

- SSRIs during breastfeeding are generally considered low risk
- Premature or unwell babies are more likely to be adversely affected — specialist advice will be sought
- Babies aged 6 months or older are less likely to be adversely affected due to a maturing drug metabolism
- **Lithium:** It is recommended that patients taking lithium do not breastfeed
- **Fluoxetine** is excreted into breast milk at a higher rate than other SSRIs and is not a first-line choice during breastfeeding, particularly at high doses or when breastfeeding a premature infant

### 3. Patient factors

Your doctor will also consider:

- Your personal preferences
- Your ability to cope with untreated or sub-threshold symptoms
- Your past response to particular antidepressant medications
- The risk of relapse if medication is reduced or stopped (related to severity of the current episode, past episodes, how long symptoms have been controlled, and social factors)

## Management

Your doctor will always discuss the benefits and risks of antidepressant treatment with you and document this discussion.

### If suicidality or risk of harm to the fetus or baby

Your doctor will arrange urgent specialist mental health assessment.

### General principles your doctor will follow

- Involve you (and your partner, where appropriate) in all decisions
- Use the lowest effective dose
- Minimise the number of medications
- Use the drug with the lowest known risk that also provides benefit
- Ensure routine antenatal screening is done — no additional screening is currently required for pregnant women on SSRIs or other antidepressants
- Be prepared to adjust the dose as pregnancy progresses, particularly in the third trimester when blood volume expands by around 30% and liver metabolism increases
- Be aware of potential transitional problems for newborns (e.g., respiratory problems, jitteriness, feeding difficulties) — these are usually mild, self-limiting, and typically do not require intervention
- Inform your lead maternity carer (LMC) and obstetric team of potential neonatal considerations

### If you are already on medication for depression or anxiety

Your doctor will:

1. Always include non-pharmacological approaches (counselling, therapy, community supports) in any treatment plan
2. Discuss the risks and benefits of continuing, switching, or stopping your current medication
3. Make decisions based on your condition:
   - **Milder depression or anxiety:**
     - If considering pregnancy and wanting to stop medication — your doctor may slowly reduce and aim to discontinue 3 to 6 months before trying to conceive, with close monitoring
     - If already pregnant and wanting to stop — your doctor may consider a cautious withdrawal with close monitoring. Be aware that stopping after a short period on medication (e.g., 6 months) may increase the risk of relapse
   - **Serious or complicated depression or anxiety:**
     - Your doctor will likely maintain antidepressants during and after pregnancy, as the risk of relapse is very high
     - If you are already responding well to an antidepressant, your doctor will generally continue the same medication — switching may not reduce fetal exposure and may lead to relapse
     - Exception: if you are taking **paroxetine** in early pregnancy, your doctor will seek specialist advice
   - **Low-dose quetiapine (100 mg/day or less):** Can generally be continued in pregnancy as it is considered low risk
   - **Lithium:** If you are on lithium and considering pregnancy, already pregnant, or breastfeeding, your doctor will always seek specialist advice
   - **Antiepileptic medications (especially sodium valproate):** Should not be started for any reason in women who are pregnant or planning a pregnancy. If you are currently taking these, your doctor will seek urgent specialist guidance

### If you have developed depression or anxiety during pregnancy or postpartum

**Mild depression or anxiety:**

- Your doctor will prioritise non-pharmacological treatments as the first step (counselling, therapy, community supports) and provide regular follow-up

**Moderate or severe depression or anxiety:**

- Non-pharmacological treatments will still be part of your plan
- Your doctor will discuss the risks and benefits of antidepressant medication
- If you have responded well to a specific medication in the past, your doctor will be guided by that history
- If you have not previously tried antidepressants, your doctor may consider prescribing an SSRI — **sertraline** is generally considered a good first choice in pregnancy

### Follow-up

Your doctor will:

- Review you in 1 to 2 weeks after any change to medication, then regularly thereafter to assess risk and adjust dosage
- Discuss your medication with your lead maternity carer (LMC)
- Consider scheduling regular GP appointments every 4 to 6 weeks during pregnancy
- Avoid reducing your dosage before delivery, as this is associated with an increased risk of relapse
- Consider a dose increase later in pregnancy, particularly in the third trimester
- Continue to encourage non-pharmacological approaches alongside any medication

---

## When Specialist Referral May Be Needed

Your doctor may seek perinatal mental health specialist advice or referral if:

- There is uncertainty about the best antidepressant choice, or concerns about switching
- You are breastfeeding a premature or unwell baby and considering starting a new medication
- You have severe or complicated depression that may be difficult to manage in the community
- Your depression is associated with risk concerns (e.g., new self-harm, suicidal or infanticidal thoughts, rejection of the baby, difficulties with bonding)
- Your depression is complicated by other mental health conditions (e.g., bipolar disorder, psychosis)
- There is medication complexity (e.g., polypharmacy)
- You do not respond to an initial trial of medication
- You are on lithium, paroxetine in early pregnancy, lamotrigine, or sodium valproate
