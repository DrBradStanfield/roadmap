# Transient Monocular Vision Loss

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to transient monocular vision loss — a temporary loss of vision in one eye lasting seconds to minutes.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Attention

Transient monocular vision loss may be an early sign of **giant cell arteritis**, a serious condition that can cause permanent vision loss if not treated promptly. Seek urgent care if you have:

- Sudden vision loss (especially unilateral)
- Headache with vision symptoms
- Jaw pain when chewing (jaw claudication)
- Scalp tenderness
- Age over 50 with new visual symptoms

---

## Background

### What is transient monocular vision loss?

- Vision loss lasts from a few seconds to minutes
- Can affect the upper or lower half of one eye's vision, or the whole field of one eye
- Important causes include:
  - **Amaurosis fugax** (retinal transient ischaemic attack)
  - **Giant cell arteritis**
  - **Optic neuropathy**
  - **Raised intracranial pressure**
  - **Migraine or retinal migraine**

### Amaurosis fugax (retinal TIA)

- Caused by embolic retinal disease, usually originating in the carotid artery
- Also known as a **retinal transient ischaemic attack (retinal TIA)**
- Results in painless transient vision loss
- May be the only symptom of carotid artery disease
- Can precede sustained vision loss from central retinal artery or vein occlusion

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you by asking about:

**History of vision loss:**
- Was the vision loss in one eye or both?
- Did it affect the whole field or just part of it?
- How quickly did it come on, and how long did it last?
- Did vision return completely, or is there any ongoing change?

**If vision loss happened while bending down**, your doctor will consider raised intracranial pressure.

**In people under 45 years**, your doctor may ask about:
- Contraceptive pill use
- Childhood cardiac history
- Migraine headaches
- Personal or family history of clotting disorders (thrombophilia)

**Relevant medical history:**
- Cardiovascular risk factors (high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol, smoking)
- Known cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease
- Polymyalgia rheumatica
- Eye conditions (macular degeneration, glaucoma)
- Previous eye surgery or trauma

---

## Possible Causes

### Amaurosis Fugax (Retinal TIA)

Features that suggest this cause:
- Vision loss descends or ascends like a curtain or shutter over a few seconds
- Usually painless
- Monocular — affects one eye only
- Typically reverses after a few minutes (may take up to 20 minutes)
- Vision may be slightly altered for a time but eventually clears completely

### Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA)

Features that suggest this cause:
- Sudden unilateral vision loss
- Headache
- Jaw claudication (jaw pain with chewing)
- Scalp tenderness
- Past history of GCA or polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR)
- Age over 50

### Transient Ischaemic Attack (TIA)

Features that suggest this cause:
- Unilateral weakness
- Unilateral altered sensation
- Difficulty with speech (dysphasia)
- Transient, painless monocular blindness or loss of half the visual field (homonymous hemianopia)

### Migraine and Retinal Migraine

**Migraine (visual aura):**
- Vision loss is binocular but may feel monocular
- May involve typical scintillations (shimmering or zigzag patterns)
- Usually lasts 20–30 minutes
- Usually followed by other migraine symptoms (headache, nausea, light and sound sensitivity)

**Retinal migraine** (caused by retinal artery vasospasm):
- Vision loss is truly monocular
- A headache may occur before, during, or after the vision loss
- Not associated with other symptoms

Vision loss typically resolves completely between episodes.

### Raised Intracranial Pressure (ICP)

Your doctor may consider this if you have:
- Vision loss when bending over
- Recent onset of headaches present on waking
- Pulsatile tinnitus (a rhythmic noise in your ears)

Vision loss in this condition is usually a transient blur or patchy loss over seconds.

### Intracranial Tumour

A less common cause. Vision loss in only one direction of gaze may suggest this.

---

## Examination and Tests

Your doctor will typically:

- **Test your vision** (with glasses or pinhole)
- **Assess pupil responses** (swinging flashlight test for relative afferent pupillary defect)
- **Check visual fields** (visual field to confrontation test)
- **Examine the back of the eye (fundus)** — looking for disc swelling, haemorrhage, and retinal vessel changes. If amaurosis fugax is suspected, they will look for bright emboli in the retinal vessels.
- **Check blood pressure and heart rhythm** (cardiovascular risk factors and atrial fibrillation)

**Blood tests:** In people over 50, your doctor will check **CRP and ESR** to screen for giant cell arteritis.

**Imaging:** If amaurosis fugax is suspected, your doctor may consider carotid ultrasound or CT head. If an intracranial tumour is suspected, CT head may be arranged.

---

## Management

Your doctor will consider the following based on the most likely cause:

- **Giant cell arteritis (age >50, elevated CRP/ESR, or GCA symptoms):** Your doctor will manage this urgently — high-dose steroids are typically started promptly to protect vision.
- **Amaurosis fugax (retinal TIA):** Your doctor will manage this as per the TIA pathway — this typically involves antiplatelet medication (such as aspirin or clopidogrel), cardiovascular risk factor management, and urgent specialist review.
- **Migraine with typical scintillations:** Your doctor will follow migraine management guidelines.
- **Retinal migraine:** Your doctor will manage the underlying migraine and may refer you to optometry or ophthalmology if episodes are becoming more frequent or prolonged.
- **Suspected papilloedema or raised ICP:** Your doctor will arrange urgent ophthalmology assessment.
