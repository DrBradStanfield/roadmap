# Transient Monocular Vision Loss

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: September 2023.*

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

This pathway covers transient monocular vision loss: a temporary loss of vision in one eye lasting seconds to minutes.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department** if your vision has not fully recovered, or if you also have face drooping, arm or leg weakness or numbness, or trouble speaking. These can be signs of a stroke.

If your vision recovered fully, **see a doctor straight away: your doctor today, or urgent care or the emergency department after hours**. A brief loss of vision in one eye can be a warning sign of a stroke (a transient ischaemic attack, or TIA) and needs prompt assessment.

Transient monocular vision loss may be an early sign of **giant cell arteritis**. See your doctor the same day, especially if you also have any of these features of giant cell arteritis:

- Sudden vision loss in one eye
- Headache
- Jaw pain when chewing (jaw claudication)
- Scalp tenderness
- Past history of giant cell arteritis or polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR)
- Age older than 50 years

Giant cell arteritis needs prompt treatment to protect your sight.

## Background

### What is transient monocular vision loss?

- Vision loss lasts from a few seconds to minutes
- Can black out the upper or lower half of one eye's vision, or the whole field of one eye
- Causes include:
  - **Giant cell arteritis**
  - **Amaurosis fugax** (retinal transient ischaemic attack)
  - **Optic neuropathy**
  - **Raised intracranial pressure**
  - **Migraine or retinal migraine**

### Amaurosis fugax (retinal TIA)

- Caused by embolic retinal disease, usually originating in the carotid artery. Some cases are linked to narrowing of the carotid artery (carotid stenosis).
- Also known as a **retinal transient ischaemic attack (retinal TIA)**
- Results in painless transient vision loss
- May be the only symptom of carotid artery disease
- Episodes can come before sustained vision loss from a blockage of the central retinal artery or vein

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you by asking about:

**History of vision loss:**
- Was the vision loss in one eye or both?
- Did it affect the whole field or just part of it?
- How quickly did it come on, and how long did it last?

**If vision has not fully recovered**, your doctor will assess you as for sudden or recent vision loss.

**If vision loss happened while bending down**, your doctor will consider raised intracranial pressure.

**In people younger than 45 years**, your doctor may ask about:
- Contraceptive pill use
- Childhood cardiac history
- Migraine headaches
- Personal or family history of blood clotting disorders

Your doctor may also consider a thrombophilia screen (blood tests for clotting disorders).

**Relevant medical history:**
- Cardiovascular risk factors
- Known cardiovascular or cerebrovascular disease
- Polymyalgia rheumatica
- Eye conditions (macular degeneration, glaucoma)
- Previous eye surgery or trauma

## Possible Causes

### Amaurosis Fugax (Retinal TIA)

Features that suggest this cause:
- Vision loss descends or ascends like a curtain or shutter over a few seconds
- Usually painless
- Monocular: affects one eye only
- Usually reverses after a few minutes, but may take up to 20 minutes
- Vision may be altered for some time afterwards, but it eventually clears

### Giant Cell Arteritis (GCA)

Features that suggest this cause:
- Sudden vision loss in one eye
- Headache
- Jaw claudication (jaw pain with chewing)
- Scalp tenderness
- Past history of GCA or polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR)
- Older than 50 years

### Transient Ischaemic Attack (TIA)

Features that suggest this cause:
- Weakness on one side of the body
- Altered sensation on one side of the body
- Difficulty with speech (dysphasia)
- Transient, painless monocular blindness or loss of half the visual field (homonymous hemianopia)

### Migraine and Retinal Migraine

**Migraine (visual aura):**
- Vision loss is binocular but may feel monocular
- May involve typical scintillations (shimmering or zigzag patterns)
- Usually lasts 20 to 30 minutes
- Usually followed by other migraine symptoms (headache, nausea, light and sound sensitivity)

**Retinal migraine** (caused by retinal artery vasospasm):
- Vision loss is monocular
- A headache may occur before, during, or after the vision loss
- Not associated with other symptoms

Vision loss typically resolves completely between episodes.

### Raised Intracranial Pressure (ICP)

Your doctor may consider this if you have:
- Vision loss when bending over
- Recent onset of headaches present on waking
- Pulsatile tinnitus (a rhythmic noise in your ears)

Vision loss in this condition is usually described as a transient blur, or a patchy loss of the visual field over seconds. If you have these features, see your doctor the same day.

### Intracranial Tumour

Vision loss in only one direction of gaze may suggest this.

## Examination and Tests

Your doctor will typically:

- **Test your vision** (with glasses or pinhole)
- **Assess pupil responses** (swinging flashlight test for relative afferent pupillary defect)
- **Check visual fields** (visual field to confrontation test)
- **Examine the back of the eye (fundus):** the optic discs for swelling or haemorrhage, the blood vessels, the peripheral retina and the macula. If amaurosis fugax is suspected, they will look for bright emboli in the retinal vessels.
- **Check blood pressure and heart rhythm** (cardiovascular risk factors and atrial fibrillation)

**Blood tests:** In people older than 50 years, your doctor will check **CRP and ESR** and ask about symptoms of giant cell arteritis.

**Imaging:** If amaurosis fugax is suspected, carotid ultrasound and CT head may be needed. If an intracranial tumour is suspected, your doctor may arrange a CT head or ask a neurologist for advice first.

---

## Management

Your doctor will consider the following based on the most likely cause:

- **Giant cell arteritis:** if you are older than 50 years and have either raised ESR and CRP or symptoms of giant cell arteritis, your doctor will manage this as giant cell arteritis.
- **Amaurosis fugax (retinal TIA):** your doctor will manage this as a transient ischaemic attack (TIA).
- **Migraine, or typical scintillations and a visual field defect that resolve completely:** your doctor will follow headache guidance for adults.
- **Retinal migraine:** your doctor will follow headache guidance for migraine management. If symptoms are becoming more frequent and prolonged, they may arrange a community optometry assessment or a non-acute ophthalmology assessment.
- **Suspected papilloedema (optic disc swelling seen on eye examination) or concerns about raised ICP:** your doctor will request an acute ophthalmology assessment in hospital.
