# Trauma in Eyes

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: July 2020.*

Eye injuries are grouped as closed globe injuries or open globe injuries. This pathway covers chemical burns, corneal abrasions, foreign bodies, penetrating injuries, globe rupture, eyelid cuts, and the injuries that can follow severe blunt trauma. It also covers serious eye injuries in children.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## First Aid for Chemical Burns

A chemical injury to the eye is an eye emergency. Alkalis such as cement or lime can cause severe chemical injury.

- Rinse the eye **immediately** with large amounts of the nearest suitable fluid, such as water or saline. Use the nearest available water, even by putting the head under a tap.
- Do not waste time checking the eye's pH or looking for bags of fluid. Even a delay of seconds can make a difference to the outcome.
- Best practice is to rinse for at least 20 minutes.
- Note the chemical's name, what it contains, and whether it was a liquid or powder.
- Then call 111 or go to the emergency department.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

Eye injuries like these should be assessed by a doctor, not an optometrist.

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department if:**
- The injury may have penetrated the eye, for example from high-velocity fragments (such as metal striking metal), an explosion, or a sharp or high-energy object thrown or forced into the eye.
- A direct blow has left the eye obviously deformed, with significant loss of vision.
- The pupil looks distorted, for example tear-drop shaped.
- Vision is poor.
- There is an alkali or strong acid burn.
- There is blood in the front of the eye (hyphaema) or pus in the front of the eye (hypopyon).
- After trauma there is pain and decreased vision with tight eyelids, a bulging eye, or limited eye movements. These can be signs of bleeding behind the eye (retrobulbar haemorrhage).
- A fracture of the eye socket (blow-out fracture) is strongly suspected.
- An eyelid cut goes through the full thickness of the lid margin or involves the inner corner of the eye.
- A child has an obviously abnormal eye, a history suggesting direct trauma, or is too distressed to be examined when there is eyelid bruising, cuts, punctures, or bleeding on the white of the eye.

**While waiting for help with a possible penetrating injury:**
- Do not touch the eye, turn the eyelid, or try to remove anything stuck in it, as this may cause further injury.
- Do not put any drops in the eye, including numbing drops. Their preservatives can damage structures inside the eye.
- A shield (such as a shallow plastic container taped over the eye) is used rather than a pad.
- Do not eat or drink until assessed. If a child will be seen within 6 hours, the child should have nothing to eat or drink.

## Background

- **Open globe injuries** are full-thickness defects of the eye wall that go right through the white of the eye (sclera) or the clear front window (cornea). They come from blunt trauma or a cut:
  - **Globe rupture:** blunt trauma briefly raises the pressure inside the eye, and the eye bursts from the inside out.
  - **Lacerations** are usually caused by a sharp object. They include a penetrating injury (an entrance wound with no exit wound), an intraocular foreign body (an object left inside the eye), and a perforating injury (an entrance and an exit wound from the same object, usually a sharp object or projectile).
- **Closed globe injuries** are commonly due to blunt trauma. The wall of the eye stays intact, but there may be damage inside the eye. They are caused by burns, blunt trauma or bruising, or partial-thickness cuts. Examples are corneal abrasions, foreign bodies, and acid or alkali burns.
- **Severe blunt trauma** can also cause bleeding behind the eye (retrobulbar haemorrhage) or a "blow-out" fracture of the eye socket wall.
- **Hyphaema** is blood in the front chamber of the eye. Sometimes it is only a haze. It needs urgent review because there is a 50% incidence of further bleeding and a 7% incidence of glaucoma.

## Assessment

### History

Your doctor will ask about:
- **Chemical injury:** whether it was an alkali or acid, how strong, how much, and whether the eye was rinsed.
- **Possible penetrating injury:** high-velocity impact, an explosion, or a sharp or high-energy object, and whether safety glasses were worn.
- **Direct impact** with obvious deformity of the eye and significant loss of vision, which suggests globe rupture.
- A simple foreign body on the surface of the eye.

### Examination

- If an open globe injury is strongly suspected, your doctor will limit the examination and will not touch the eye, turn the lid, or try to remove foreign material.
- **Signs of a penetrating injury** include brown or black tissue (uvea) pushing out through the cornea or sclera, a full-thickness cut, an abnormal iris such as a tear-drop shaped pupil, an unusual red reflex, and an obviously deformed eye.
- **Vision** is tested in each eye with a chart, using your distance glasses if you wear them, and with a pinhole if vision is reduced.
- **The eye itself** is checked for:
  - injury to the white of the eye. A full-thickness injury may be hidden under the lids.
  - blood in front of the iris (hyphaema), an irregular pupil, or a black speck or rounded bulge on the cornea or its edge.
  - the red reflex. A poor or black reflex points to hyphaema, and sometimes lens damage or bleeding into the gel of the eye.
  - bleeding on the white of the eye (subconjunctival haemorrhage), which can hide an open globe or penetrating injury.
  - eyelid cuts, looking for deep cuts that may reach into the eye socket and full-thickness cuts across the lid margin.
  - corneal abrasions, using fluorescein dye.
- If there are no clear signs but the history is suspicious, your doctor may consider an X-ray of the eye sockets, especially if a metal foreign body is suspected, and will seek eye specialist advice.

### After significant blunt trauma

Your doctor will look for:
- **Retrobulbar haemorrhage** (bleeding behind the eye), which can follow trauma or surgery to the eye or socket. Symptoms are pain and decreased vision. Signs include reduced vision, an abnormal pupil response when a light is swung between the eyes (relative afferent pupillary defect), widespread bleeding on the white of the eye, tight eyelids, a bulging eye (proptosis), and limited eye movements.
- **Orbital wall "blow-out" fracture**, usually from blunt trauma such as a punch or a squash ball. Features include pain, especially when looking up or down, double vision, eyelid swelling and a crackling feel after blowing the nose, hyphaema, restricted eye movements, and reduced feeling in the area supplied by the infraorbital nerve.
  - If a fracture is strongly suspected, a CT scan of the facial bones is arranged through hospital assessment.
  - If suspicion is low, your doctor may consider plain X-rays first.

## Management

### Chemical burns

After the immediate rinse, your doctor may:
- use numbing drops (such as oxybuprocaine) and rinse the eye further with Ringer's solution, sterile saline, or sterile water while holding the lids open.
- turn the eyelid to clear any foreign material with a moist cotton bud.
- give pain relief, including more numbing drops as needed.
- wait 5 minutes after rinsing, then check the pH of your tears if pH strips are available. If it is still abnormal, rinsing continues. Severe burns may need rinsing for over 30 minutes.
- seek urgent eye specialist advice.

**Follow-up depends on the chemical:**
- **Alkali burn:** your doctor will seek urgent eye specialist advice, and you will be assessed in hospital if the specialist directs.
- **Acid burn with a small corneal defect** (≤ 3 mm across) and an otherwise clear cornea: your doctor may give chloramphenicol ointment and pad the eye, with a general practice check the next day. You cannot legally drive or operate machinery while your eye is patched. If there are ongoing concerns, your doctor will seek eye specialist advice.
- **Acid burn with a larger corneal defect** (more than 3 mm) or a hazy cornea: hospital eye assessment.

### Open globe injury

If an open globe injury is suspected, you will be sent for hospital eye assessment. Before transfer, your doctor may:
- limit any examination of the eye.
- avoid all eye drops, including numbing drops.
- cover the eye with a shield, not a pad.
- give pain relief and anti-nausea medicine.
- check your tetanus immunisation status.
- keep you nil by mouth.
- rely on the eye specialist team to advise whether antibiotics are needed if there will be any delay.

### Hyphaema

Hyphaema needs same-day hospital eye assessment. Your doctor will check that the eye is not ruptured. You should sit up (ideally with the head raised 30 degrees) and be moved by stretcher or wheelchair. Do not drive or go home.

### Other injuries

- **Retrobulbar haemorrhage:** hospital eye assessment if suspected.
- **Orbital wall fracture:** hospital assessment for a CT scan if strongly suspected. If your doctor is unsure, they will seek urgent specialist advice.
- **Corneal foreign body** (including rust rings): managed as for a foreign body in the eye.
- **Eyelid cuts:** if the cut goes through the full thickness of the lid margin or involves the inner corner of the eye, you will be sent to hospital for surgery. The tear ducts are often cut and need surgical repair. Other eyelid cuts can be stitched in general practice by an experienced clinician.
- Your doctor will complete an ACC form for every eye injury.
