# Unintentional Weight Loss in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: December 2020.*

Unintentional weight loss means losing greater than 5% of total body weight over 6 to 12 months, when the loss is not an expected result of a known treatment or intervention. It is often caused by eating less, but it may point to a serious medical or mental health condition.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Red Flags

**See your doctor if you have:**
- Lost greater than 10% of your body weight over 3 to 6 months without trying.
- Weight loss where your doctor is concerned about a cancer that has not yet been diagnosed.

---

## Background

Common causes of unintentional weight loss include:
- Mental health conditions, e.g. depression, anxiety, alcohol dependency.
- Hormone (endocrine) causes, e.g. an overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism), diabetes.
- Gut (gastrointestinal) disease.
- Cancer (malignancy).
- Infections.
- End-stage heart, lung, and kidney disease.
- Neurological disease, e.g. dementia, Parkinson's disease, stroke.
- Inflammatory diseases.
- Side effects of medicines.

---

## Assessment

### History

Your doctor may ask about:
- **How much weight you have lost, and over what time.**
  - A BMI less than 18.5 is of concern.
  - Losing more than 10% of body weight within the last 3 to 6 months can lead to malnutrition at any BMI, including in people who are overweight or obese.
- **Mental health, memory and thinking, alcohol intake, smoking, and your social situation.** Depression is a common cause of unintended weight loss.
- **All your medicines**, including over-the-counter and illicit drugs.
- **Symptoms of an overactive thyroid or diabetes.**
- **Appetite:**
  - Causes of weight loss with an *increased* appetite include an overactive thyroid (thyrotoxicosis), diabetes, and medicines.
  - Causes of weight loss with a *decreased* appetite include upper gut problems, pancreatic and liver disease, chronic infection, tuberculosis (TB), infection of the heart valves (subacute bacterial endocarditis), cancer (e.g. leukaemia), kidney failure, heart failure, respiratory failure, pituitary-adrenal problems, depression, and medicines.
- **Diet:** whether you limit or avoid foods (especially one of the main food groups: vegetables and fruit; grain foods; milk and milk products; lean meats, chicken, seafood, eggs, legumes, nuts and seeds), follow a special diet or cultural practices that affect what and when you eat, skip meals, have difficulty getting, preparing, or cooking food, or eat alone.
- **Eating behaviours.** If an eating disorder is suspected, your doctor may use the SCOFF questionnaire. It asks whether you:
  - make yourself sick because you feel uncomfortably full,
  - worry you have lost control over how much you eat,
  - have recently lost more than one stone (6.4 kg) in a 3-month period,
  - believe yourself to be fat when others say you are too thin,
  - would say that food dominates your life.
  - A score of 2 or more "yes" answers indicates a likely eating disorder (anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa), and your doctor will ask further questions.
- **Gut and mouth symptoms**, especially difficulty swallowing, gum disease, poor teeth, loss of taste, or diarrhoea.
- **Neurological symptoms**, which may point to an undiagnosed condition such as dementia or Parkinson's disease.
- **General health:** a full review of body systems, and signs of end-stage heart, lung, or kidney disease.
- **Food security:** whether you have money for food, what you eat, who does the shopping and prepares meals, and whether you are ever hungry or miss meals.
- In older adults, signs of elder abuse.

### Examination

- Weight, height, and body mass index (BMI). BMI is your weight in kilograms divided by your height in metres squared:
  - Less than 18.5: underweight.
  - Between 18.5 and 24.9: healthy or normal weight.
  - Between 25 and 29.9: overweight.
  - Greater than 30: obese (35 or more is moderate, 40 or more is extreme).
- Features of poor nutrition, e.g. anaemia, iron deficiency.

### Investigations

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Blood and urine tests: FBC, CRP, creatinine, potassium, sodium, calcium, ferritin, a urine test (MSU), HbA1c, glucose, thyroid function tests, LFTs, INR, coeliac serology (while you are still eating gluten), B12, and folate.
- Heart, lung, or kidney tests, guided by your history.
- A **CT scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis** if, after a full assessment, examination, and initial tests, there is a strong suspicion of a cancer that has not yet been found.

## Management

### Treating the cause

- Any cause found is treated. Better management of long-term conditions may help with weight gain.
- Physical causes should be ruled out before weight loss is put down to mental health issues.
- If there is memory or thinking impairment in an older person, your doctor will assess this further.
- If loneliness or depression is suspected, your doctor may suggest counselling or community mental health support.

### Watch and wait

Your doctor may initially "watch and wait" if:
- your examination and initial tests are normal,
- the weight loss is gradual, i.e. over more than 3 to 6 months, and
- you have lost less than 10% of your body weight (or less than 5% if your BMI is less than or equal to 20) in the past 3 to 6 months.

### Food First approach

Your doctor may give nutrition advice using the Food First approach:
- Eat more often: 3 regular small meals with a small nutritious snack in between.
- Choose high-energy foods, e.g. milkshakes, standard milk, butter, oil, avocado, cream, cheese, sugar, honey.
- Have milky drinks (milky coffee, hot chocolate, milkshakes, or smoothies) or soups instead of water, tea, or coffee, which have few calories and no nutritional value.
- Use over-the-counter nutrition supplement drinks.

### Follow-up

Your doctor may arrange follow-up after 4 weeks to re-weigh you and reassess your history, examination, and tests. If a significant underlying cause is suspected, they will arrange specialist assessment.

If there is no improvement on the Food First approach, your doctor may consider:
- oral nutrition supplements,
- community nutrition support,
- dietitian assessment, if you meet the criteria below.

If you are struggling to afford food, or other social concerns are affecting your nutrition, your doctor can help you find support through community agencies or social work.

---

## When to See a Specialist or Dietitian

**Specialist:**
- If cancer or another significant cause is suspected, your doctor will arrange the appropriate specialist assessment.
- If a significant cause is suspected but the right specialty is not obvious, your doctor may request a non-urgent general medicine assessment.

**Dietitian** (malnutrition or nutrition risk):
- **Aged 65 years or older:** Food First advice, with a dietitian referral at the same time if you:
  - are "at risk" on a nutrition risk screen for older adults and have lost 5 kg or more in the past 6 months, or
  - have a BMI of 20 kg/m2 or less, or unintentional weight loss (greater than 10%, or greater than 5% with a BMI less than 20 kg/m2, in the past 3 to 6 months).
- **Aged 16 to 64 years:** Food First advice first. Referral only if there is no improvement after 4 weeks and you have:
  - unintentional weight loss (as defined above), or
  - a BMI of 18.5 kg/m2 or less with a long-term condition, e.g. chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD) or cancer.
- **After gut surgery:** if you have lost 5% or more of your weight in the past 3 to 6 months because you are not eating enough, Food First advice and a referral.
- People with disordered eating or alcohol or drug dependence are directed to eating disorder or addiction services instead.
