# Failure to Thrive (Faltering Growth)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and initial management of failure to thrive in children aged under 5 years.

---

## Background

Failure to thrive refers to children aged under 5 years who have weight below the third centile on two or more separate occasions, or weight which crosses 2 centile lines downward over time.

Important context:
- At least 5% of healthy normal children will meet these criteria
- Many healthy children do not match chart growth trajectories — the centile lines use cross-sectional data, not longitudinal growth data
- A well-looking child with normal neurodevelopmental progress has an excellent prognosis despite isolated poor weight gain
- Nutrition is the main driver for growth in the first two years of life
- If the child was born prematurely (before 37 weeks), correct for gestational age until 24 months

**Average weight gain per week** (these are averages, not minimum thresholds):
- 0–3 months: 180 g per week
- 3–6 months: 120 g per week
- 6–9 months: 80 g per week
- 9–12 months: 70 g per week
- Weight gain slows further after 12 months

**Note:** Avoid weighing the child too frequently. No more than once a week for babies under 3 months, and no more than fortnightly after that. Weighing too often records normal fluctuations and can cause unnecessary parental anxiety.

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history including:

**Feeding history:**
- Infants: breast feeding and attachment, formula feeding, timing and volumes of feeds, presence of vomiting, and timing of introduction of solids
- Toddlers: meal time battles, distractions, coercive feeding, food refusal, milk volume over 24 hours, parental attitude towards food and mess

**Medical and developmental history:**
- Perinatal and birth history, including antenatal complications and maternal health
- Significant illnesses coinciding with onset of weight loss
- Any developmental regression or features suggesting a syndromal cause

**Family background:**
- Family history of growth patterns (mid-parental height gives a prediction of expected final height)
- Childhood weight gain in parents

**Social factors:**
- Financial situation and support networks — neglect and poverty can directly affect a child's nutrition
- Maternal depression
- Family violence (screened for sensitively)

Physical examination will include:
- Height, weight, and head circumference plotted on a growth chart (including previous measurements to show the pattern over time)
- Signs of concern: appearing sick, scrawny, irritable, or lethargic; loss of subcutaneous fat on the upper arm, buttocks, or thighs; pallor; facial dysmorphism; jaundice, bruising, or pruritus; rashes; changes in hair and nails; persistent vomiting
- ENT, gastrointestinal (especially abdominal distension), cardiac and respiratory, neurological, and endocrine (goitre) examination
- Assessment of muscle tone — low tone can reduce sucking ability in infants

## Possible Causes

### Inadequate Caloric Intake or Retention

- Inadequate amount of food provided
- Poor breastfeeding technique
- Structural causes of poor feeding (e.g., cleft palate)
- Persistent vomiting
- Anorexia of chronic disease
- Oral aversion

### Inadequate Absorption

- Coeliac disease
- Chronic liver disease
- Pancreatic insufficiency (e.g., cystic fibrosis)
- Chronic diarrhoea
- Immunodeficiency

### Excessive Caloric Utilisation

- Prolonged fever
- Renal problems or urinary tract infection
- Chronic respiratory disease (e.g., cystic fibrosis)
- Congenital heart disease
- Diabetes mellitus
- Hyperthyroidism
- Malignancy or chronic anaemia
- Inflammatory disorder

### Other Medical Causes

- Genetic syndromes or fetal alcohol spectrum disorder
- Inborn errors of metabolism

### Psychosocial Factors

- Parental depression
- Coercive feeding
- Distractions at meal times
- Poverty
- Behavioural disorders
- Poor social support
- Neglect or abuse

Note: While there has historically been an arbitrary division into "organic" or "non-organic" failure to thrive, this distinction has not been found clinically useful — there is significant overlap between categories.

---

## Management

Your doctor will begin with primary care assessment and management:

1. **Confirm the growth pattern** by re-weighing the child. Depending on findings, they may investigate further or provide reassurance.

2. **No investigations are required** if the child is otherwise healthy, developing normally, and there are no suggestive features in the history or examination.

3. **Reassure the family.** Faltering growth can cause significant stress. Some families benefit from additional support.

4. **If there is considerable concern but no clear medical cause,** your doctor may arrange first-line investigations:
   - Full blood count (FBC) and CRP
   - Sodium, potassium, creatinine, liver function tests (LFT), ferritin, calcium
   - Urine for microscopy and culture
   - Coeliac screen (if the child is on solid foods containing gluten)
   - Stool examination for fat globules, fatty acid crystals, and microscopy and culture

5. **If there is significant faltering growth,** your doctor may refer for paediatric assessment. They may also consider referral to a dietitian, speech language therapist, lactation consultant, or psychologist depending on the likely cause.
