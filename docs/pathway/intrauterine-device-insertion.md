# Intrauterine Device (IUD) Insertion

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers intrauterine devices (IUDs) — including the copper IUD (Cu-IUD) and levonorgestrel intrauterine systems (LNG-IUS, e.g., Mirena 52 mg and Jaydess 13.5 mg). It outlines what to expect before, during, and after insertion, as well as warning signs to watch for.

---

## Device Types and Duration

| Device | Duration | Approximate failure rate |
|---|---|---|
| LNG-IUS 52 mg (Mirena) | 8 years | 0.7% at 8 years |
| LNG-IUS 13.5 mg (Jaydess) | 3 years | 0.9% at 3 years |
| Cu-IUD (CHOICE LOAD 375) | 5 years | ~1% |
| Cu-IUD (CHOICE TT380 short) | 5 years | 0.1–1% |
| Cu-IUD (TCu 380 Plus normal) | 5 years | ~1% |

## Side Effects and Complications

### All IUDs

- Low pregnancy risk (1% or less), but if pregnancy does occur:
  - Risk of ectopic pregnancy is increased — 6% with Cu-IUD, 50% with LNG-IUS
  - The IUD will need urgent removal, as pregnancy with an IUD in place increases the risk of miscarriage, preterm birth, and infection
- Small risk of pelvic infection in the first 20 days after insertion (approximately 1 in 300 patients); risk returns to baseline after 20 days
- Expulsion (device coming out) — around 1 in 20 patients; most common in the first 3 months
- Displacement or migration of the device
- Perforation of the uterus during insertion — rare (approximately 1.4 per 1,000 insertions)
- Vasovagal response (feeling faint) during the procedure

### Copper IUD specifically

- Heavier periods and more painful periods (dysmenorrhoea), especially in the first 3–6 months; usually improves with time

### LNG-IUS (Mirena, Jaydess) specifically

- Irregular bleeding or spotting in the early months — usually settles with time
- About 20% of people become period-free after 1 year on the 52 mg Mirena
- Lighter periods overall; slightly less so with Jaydess than Mirena
- Hormonal side effects such as acne, breast tenderness, or headache — often settle with time
- Possible small increase in breast cancer risk in pre-menopausal users; absolute risk remains small

### Common concerns addressed

- Being young (under 25) or having never been pregnant (nulliparity) are **not** contraindications to an IUD
- No evidence of an effect on libido
- No proven causal link between LNG-IUS and weight gain
- Little or no increased risk of blood clots (VTE) or heart attack with LNG-IUS
- Routine preventive antibiotics are not required, even if you are at risk of infective endocarditis

## Pain Management Before Insertion

Your doctor may recommend:

- Paracetamol 1 g or ibuprofen 400 mg taken one hour before insertion to reduce uterine cramping
- A local anaesthetic injection at the cervix if dilation is needed or insertion is expected to be difficult
- Verbal reassurance, distraction, and positive reinforcement during the procedure

---

## Excluding Pregnancy Before Insertion

Your doctor will need to reasonably exclude current pregnancy before fitting an IUD. Pregnancy can generally be considered excluded if you have no pregnancy symptoms and one of the following applies:

- Within 5 days of the start of your normal period
- Within 5 days of an abortion, miscarriage, or ectopic pregnancy
- No intercourse since your last normal period, childbirth, or abortion/miscarriage
- Consistently using a reliable contraceptive method
- Less than 21 days postpartum
- Fully breastfeeding, no periods, and less than 6 months postpartum
- No intercourse for more than 21 days and a negative urine pregnancy test

---

## Copper IUD as Emergency Contraception

A copper IUD can be used as emergency contraception if inserted:

- Within 5 days of the earliest likely date of ovulation, **or**
- Within 5 days (120 hours) of unprotected sex

The earliest likely day of ovulation = cycle length minus 14 days (e.g., day 14 for a 28-day cycle).

---

## After Insertion — What to Do

Your doctor will give you post-insertion instructions. Generally:

- Avoid tampons and intercourse for 48 hours to reduce infection risk
- Take NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen) or paracetamol for pain after insertion, if not contraindicated
- Check that the IUD threads are present before relying on it for contraception
- Self-check the threads once a month
- Take care when removing menstrual cups — there is a small risk of accidentally dislodging the IUD

### Additional contraception needed?

- **Cu-IUD:** Effective immediately — no extra contraception required
- **LNG-IUS (Mirena/Jaydess):** Extra contraception needed for 7 days if fitted after the first 7 days of your cycle, 21 or more days after giving birth, or 5 or more days after an abortion or miscarriage

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Help

### Ectopic pregnancy — seek emergency care immediately if you are pregnant and have any of:

- Abdominal or pelvic pain
- Vaginal bleeding (with or without clots)
- Dizziness, fainting, or collapse
- Shoulder tip pain
- Passage of tissue
- Rectal pressure or pain on defecation
- Abdominal tenderness or distension
- Rapid heart rate (> 100 bpm) or low blood pressure (< 100/60 mmHg)
- Pallor or shock

**If you think you may be pregnant with an IUD in place, seek medical advice immediately.**

### Return to your doctor if:

- Pain or abnormal discharge more than 48 hours after insertion — may indicate pelvic infection
- You suspect the device has been expelled — use extra contraception until position can be confirmed

### Signs of possible device expulsion:

- Unable to feel the threads, or threads feel shorter or longer than usual
- You can feel the plastic stem of the device
- Worsening or persistent pain, cramping, or discomfort not responding to analgesia
- Abnormal bleeding
- Signs of infection (fever, new vaginal discharge)
- Pain during intercourse
- Finding the device

If you suspect expulsion, avoid intercourse or use condoms until the device position has been confirmed. Emergency contraception is available if needed.

---

## Follow-Up

Your doctor will typically arrange:

- A follow-up check 3–6 weeks after insertion, particularly if the IUD was inserted within 48 hours of giving birth
- This may be with the inserting clinician or your GP
