# Thrombocytopenia (Low Platelets)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of a low platelet count in adults.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Review

Seek urgent medical attention if:

- Significant active bleeding
- Severe thrombocytopenia (platelets < 10 x 10⁹/L)

---

## Background

Thrombocytopenia is a reduced platelet count (< 150 x 10⁹/L).

- **Mild thrombocytopenia** (100–150 x 10⁹/L) is common and often not clinically significant.
- **More significant thrombocytopenia** (especially platelets < 30 x 10⁹/L) can be associated with bleeding.
- Bleeding in thrombocytopenia is usually mucocutaneous: nose bleeds, bleeding gums, easy bruising, heavy periods (menorrhagia).
- Anticoagulants and antiplatelet agents increase bleeding risk at any given platelet level.

### Causes

- **Spurious/artefactual** — EDTA-induced platelet clumping, clotted sample.
- **Physiological** — pregnancy (gestational thrombocytopenia).
- **Reduced production** — post-viral illness, drugs, alcohol, B12 or folate deficiency, myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), chronic viral infections (HIV, viral hepatitis).
- **Increased destruction** — drugs, idiopathic thrombocytopenic purpura (ITP), autoimmune causes, disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), hypersplenism in liver cirrhosis.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess thrombocytopenia. This typically involves the following.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- **Bleeding symptoms** — easy bruising, petechiae, heavy periods, melaena, profuse bleeding from minor cuts. Bleeding is usually only present when platelets are < 10 x 10⁹/L.
- **Pregnancy** — thrombocytopenia occurs in up to 10% of pregnancies. The most common cause is gestational thrombocytopenia (70–80% of cases), which is benign. Thrombocytopenia in pregnancy always requires specialist input.
- **Medications** — including prescription, over-the-counter, herbal remedies and supplements. Medications most commonly implicated include: quinine, trimethoprim/sulfamethoxazole, vancomycin, chemotherapy agents, heparin, aspirin, ibuprofen, penicillins, cephalosporins, thiazide diuretics.
- **Diet and supplements** — certain foods and supplements have been linked to thrombocytopenia. Definite associations include quinine-containing beverages (tonic water, bitter lemon), cow's milk, cranberry juice, and walnuts.
- **Chronic liver disease risk factors** — particularly alcohol intake.
- **Risk factors for HIV, hepatitis B, and hepatitis C.**
- **Autoimmune symptoms** — features of systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or other connective tissue disease.
- **Recent viral illness**, night sweats, weight loss, arthralgia, or rashes.
- **Family history** of bruising, bleeding, or low platelets.
- **Nutritional and alcohol history** — poor nutrition and excess alcohol can cause B12 or folate deficiency, leading to thrombocytopenia.

### Examination

Your doctor will examine for:

- Fundoscopy — CNS bleeding is the most common cause of death in severe thrombocytopenia.
- Skin for bruising and petechiae.
- Skin necrosis — may suggest heparin-induced thrombocytopenia (HIT).
- Lymphadenopathy and hepatosplenomegaly — may indicate underlying systemic disease.
- Signs of chronic liver disease: jaundice, palmar erythema, ascites, gynaecomastia.

### Investigations

**Full blood count (FBC) and blood film** to confirm the platelet count and assess bleeding risk.

**Risk of bleeding by platelet count:**

| Platelet count (x 10⁹/L) | Bleeding risk |
|---|---|
| 50–150 | Limited risk |
| 30–50 | Rarely causes significant bleeding even with trauma |
| 10–30 | May bleed with trauma; severe bleeding unusual at rest; many asymptomatic |
| < 10 | Risk of spontaneous bruising and bleeding; many still asymptomatic |

Additional tests your doctor may arrange:
- Liver function tests (LFTs) if liver disease suspected, plus a coagulation screen (PT, INR, APTT, fibrinogen, thrombin time, D-dimer).
- Von Willebrand factor or platelet function analysis if severe bleeding or family history of bleeding disorder.

---

## Management

Management depends on platelet count, symptoms, and the underlying cause. Your doctor will guide this.

### Urgent situations

- **Significant bleeding** — your doctor will seek haematology advice urgently and may arrange hospital assessment.
- **Platelets < 10 x 10⁹/L** — your doctor will seek haematology advice and consider hospital admission.

### Initial monitoring

- If platelets **> 80 x 10⁹/L and asymptomatic** — repeat FBC and blood film within a week to confirm.
- If platelets **< 80 x 10⁹/L or symptomatic** — repeat FBC, blood film, and coagulation screen the next day.

### On retesting

- **If platelet count returns to normal** — repeat testing after 1 month.
- **If platelet count remains abnormal** — your doctor will arrange further investigations based on clinical suspicion, which may include:
  - DIC screen (APTT, PT, thrombin time, fibrinogen, D-dimer)
  - LFTs including GGT
  - Serum B12 and folate
  - HIV serology
  - Connective tissue disease screen (ANA, ANCA, rheumatoid factor, CCP, anticardiolipin antibodies, complement C3 and C4)

### Further management by platelet level

**Platelets < 80 x 10⁹/L or symptomatic:**
- Your doctor will refer for specialist haematology assessment.
- If platelets are > 30 x 10⁹/L, your doctor will also consider whether another cause of bleeding is more likely (e.g., gastrointestinal pathology, vasculitis).

**Platelets > 80 x 10⁹/L and asymptomatic:**
- Stop any medications started in the last 3 months that could be implicated.
- Treat any nutritional deficiencies (B12, folate) and follow up other abnormalities.
- In patients aged **≤ 50 years**, ITP is the most likely diagnosis:
  - Treatment is usually not necessary.
  - Your doctor will monitor FBC monthly for 2 months, then yearly if stable.
  - You should report any new petechial rash, bruising, or bleeding promptly.
- In patients aged **> 50 years** with otherwise normal blood tests, myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS) is the likely diagnosis and specialist review will follow.

**Thrombocytopenia in pregnancy** always requires obstetric specialist input.

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor will refer you to a haematologist if:

- Significant active bleeding (urgent assessment)
- Platelets < 10 x 10⁹/L (consider admission)
- Platelets < 80 x 10⁹/L or symptomatic (non-urgent haematology assessment)

Obstetric advice is required for any thrombocytopenia in pregnancy.
