# Recurrent Miscarriage

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of recurrent miscarriage — three or more consecutive pregnancy losses in the first trimester.

---

## Background

**Recurrent miscarriage** is defined as 3 or more consecutive pregnancy losses in the first trimester (at 6 weeks gestation or more).

A clinical pregnancy is confirmed by:
- Human chorionic gonadotrophin (hCG) or ultrasound findings, or
- History of a missed period and heavy bleeding with passing clots, or
- Hospital admission or history from a lead maternity carer (LMC)

This definition does **not** include:
- Ectopic pregnancy, molar pregnancy, or termination of pregnancy
- Second trimester losses
- Biochemical pregnancies (pregnancy loss at less than 6 weeks gestation, where the test is positive but does not progress to a clinical pregnancy — these are very common and do not usually indicate underlying pathology)

Recurrent miscarriage remains unexplained in 50–75% of cases. Increasing maternal age is the most important risk factor.

---

## Risk Factors

- **Advanced maternal age** — most likely due to ageing eggs and resulting chromosomal abnormalities (aneuploidy)
- **Maternal obesity** or being significantly underweight
- **Smoking** (either parent)
- **Alcohol** (either parent)
- **Genetic** — parental chromosomal rearrangements (e.g. balanced translocation), embryonic chromosomal abnormalities
- **Anatomical** — congenital uterine abnormalities (e.g. uterine septum, cervical weakness)
- **Endocrine** — polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), poorly controlled diabetes, thyroid dysfunction, hyperprolactinaemia
- **Thrombophilias** — antiphospholipid syndrome (acquired), hereditary thrombophilia (family history)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will sensitively assess and counsel you, as pregnancy loss can be a devastating experience. Assessment typically includes:

### History

Your doctor will ask about:
- Menstrual cycles
- Previous uterine procedures (which may have caused adhesions)
- Galactorrhoea (nipple discharge) — which may suggest endocrine dysfunction
- Family history of congenital abnormalities
- Potential environmental exposure to toxins (both parents' employment)
- Lifestyle factors — smoking, alcohol, illicit drugs, obesity
- History of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE) — which may suggest antiphospholipid syndrome
- Previous successful pregnancies and previous miscarriages
- All previous investigations, especially abnormal results

### Mental Health Screening

Your doctor may screen for depression using the PHQ-2 questions, as recurrent miscarriage significantly increases the risk of depression:

1. During the past month, have you often felt down, depressed, or hopeless?
2. During the past month, have you had little interest or pleasure in doing things?
3. Would you like help with this?

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will typically:
- Measure height, weight, BMI, blood pressure, and pulse
- Look for signs of endocrine conditions (e.g. hirsutism, galactorrhoea)
- Consider an abdominal or speculum examination to check for obvious uterine or cervical abnormalities

---

## Investigations

If you have had two or more consecutive first-trimester pregnancy losses (at 6 weeks gestation or more), your doctor may arrange investigations. These are typically arranged when the urine hCG is negative, or it has been more than 4 weeks since the last miscarriage.

Your doctor may consider:

**For both you and your partner:**
- Parental karyotype testing — to exclude a balanced chromosomal translocation

**For you:**
- Anticardiolipin antibodies (if elevated, repeated at or after 12 weeks)
- Lupus anticoagulant (if positive, repeated at or after 12 weeks)
- B2 glycoprotein 1 (if positive, repeated at or after 12 weeks)
- Thyroid stimulating hormone (TSH)
- Day 2 or 3 follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and oestradiol (E2)
- Full blood count, blood group, and antibody screen
- Hepatitis B immunity
- Syphilis and rubella serology
- HIV
- Ferritin
- Hepatitis C antibody
- HbA1c

**Additionally, your doctor may consider:**
- Autoimmune screening for coeliac disease (tTG antibodies, DGP)
- Vaginal swab for chlamydia and gonorrhoea (NAAT/PCR)
- Ensuring cervical screening is up to date

Note: Imaging (pelvic ultrasound) is not routinely arranged in general practice at this stage — a specialist clinic will arrange a 3D uterine ultrasound if needed.

---

## If Pregnant Again (with 3+ Previous Losses)

If you conceive again after 3 or more first-trimester losses, your doctor will typically arrange:
- Serial serum bHCG measurements (once or twice a week for 7 weeks) to confirm the pregnancy is progressing as expected — in a healthy pregnancy, bHCG levels should double approximately every two days, then plateau and begin to decrease at 8–10 weeks
- An ultrasound if there is any concern about a suboptimal bHCG rise, to confirm the location and viability of the pregnancy

---

## Management

### Reassurance

If recurrent miscarriage remains unexplained despite detailed investigations, the prognosis for a successful future pregnancy is approximately **75%**. Your doctor will provide this reassurance.

### What your doctor may consider

- **If antiphospholipid antibodies are positive (confirmed on two tests 12 weeks apart):** Specialist assessment is warranted; treatment options may be discussed at that point
- **If chromosomal abnormality is found on parental karyotype:** Referral for genetic health assessment in addition to specialist fertility assessment
- **If a second trimester miscarriage:** Referral to Maternal Fetal Medicine
- **If pregnant while awaiting assessment:** Start or continue folic acid and iodine supplementation; keep specialist appointments — you will still be seen by secondary care
