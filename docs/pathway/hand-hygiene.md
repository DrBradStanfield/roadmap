# Hand Hygiene

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Effective hand hygiene is one of the most important ways to prevent the spread of infection — both in healthcare settings and everyday life.

---

## When to Clean Your Hands

The WHO identifies five key moments for hand hygiene in healthcare, but the principles apply more broadly:

1. **Before touching a patient or vulnerable person** — to protect them from germs you may be carrying
2. **Before a procedure** — to prevent introducing germs into the body
3. **After a procedure or contact with body fluids** — to protect yourself and others
4. **After touching a patient or their surroundings** — to prevent spreading germs
5. **After touching objects in a patient's environment** — even without direct patient contact

In everyday life, clean your hands:
- Before preparing or eating food
- After using the toilet
- After blowing your nose, coughing, or sneezing
- After contact with animals or their environments
- After touching surfaces in public spaces

---

## How to Wash Your Hands

Washing with liquid soap is effective at mechanically removing germs when done correctly:

1. Wet hands under running water
2. Keep hands lower than elbows; apply soap
3. Use friction to clean between fingers, palms, back of hands, wrists, and around nails — for a minimum of 20 seconds
4. Rinse thoroughly under running water
5. Dry with paper towels
6. Use the paper towel (or your elbow) to turn off taps

### When to wash with soap and water (rather than hand rub)

- When hands are visibly soiled or feel sticky
- After removing gloves
- When managing known or suspected *C. difficile*, norovirus, or gastroenteritis — alcohol may not be effective against these pathogens

---

## Alcohol-Based Hand Rub

Where hands are not visibly soiled, an alcohol-based hand rub is preferred in clean situations:

- Use a product with 60–80% alcohol (evidence suggests 75% may be optimal)
- Rub into all surfaces of hands until dry
- Not effective against *C. difficile* or norovirus — use soap and water instead

---

## Standard Precautions

- Cover any cuts, abrasions, or broken skin (including eczema) with a waterproof dressing — broken skin increases both the risk of passing germs to others and the risk of infection to yourself
- Remove jewellery (other than a plain wedding ring) before clinical contact — jewellery harbours bacteria and reduces the effectiveness of hand cleaning
- Avoid artificial nails or chipped nail varnish in healthcare settings

---

## Gloves

Your doctor or healthcare team may use gloves whenever there is potential contact with blood or body fluids:

- A new pair of gloves is used for each patient
- Hands must still be washed after removing gloves — organisms can multiply inside the glove
- Both latex and vinyl gloves provide barrier protection, but wearing gloves does **not** replace hand washing

Reusable gloves (for housekeeping or equipment cleaning) should be washed, dried, and checked for cracks or tears before storage.

---

## Skin Care

Frequent hand washing can cause skin breakdown. To protect skin:

- Use a non-glycerol (silicone-based) hand cream regularly
- Rinse and dry hands thoroughly — dabbing rather than rubbing
- Use individual hand cream tubes to avoid contamination of shared dispensers

### If you have dermatitis or a skin condition on your hands

People with dermatitic skin carry higher numbers of bacteria on their skin, and hand washing is less effective at reducing this load. If you have a skin condition on your hands and work in a healthcare setting, speak with your doctor or occupational health team about appropriate precautions.
