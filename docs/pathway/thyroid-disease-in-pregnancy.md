# Thyroid Disease in Pregnancy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of thyroid conditions in pregnancy — including hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, subclinical thyroid dysfunction, and postpartum thyroiditis.

---

## Red Flags — Tell Your Doctor Promptly

Seek urgent medical review if you have a known thyroid condition and develop any of:

- History of Graves' disease (risk of fetal complications if uncontrolled)
- A new or enlarging lump in the neck (thyroid nodule)
- History of thyroid cancer
- Severe vomiting in early pregnancy with possible hyperthyroid symptoms

---

## Background

Thyroid disease is the second most common endocrine disorder in pregnancy (after diabetes), with significant implications for mother and baby.

- Pregnancy increases demand on the thyroid, especially with low iodine intake or thyroid autoimmunity.
- A transient, mild drop in TSH is normal in 10–20% of healthy pregnancies during the first trimester (due to hCG having a weak thyroid-stimulating effect, peaking at 10–12 weeks).
- Significantly abnormal thyroid function can cause miscarriage, stillbirth, and impaired fetal brain development.
- Thyroxine (T4) and thyroid-receptor antibodies cross the placenta. TSH and T3 do not. Anti-TPO antibodies can cross but do not cause fetal thyroid dysfunction.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will generally:

- **Not screen routinely** — thyroid testing in pregnancy is targeted to those with symptoms or risk factors, not all pregnant women.
- Take a history asking about:
  - Family or personal history of thyroid disease
  - Insulin-dependent diabetes or other autoimmune conditions
  - Recurrent miscarriage, preterm birth, or intrauterine growth restriction
  - Previous fertility treatment (ART)
  - Prior thyroid surgery, radio-iodine treatment, or head/neck radiotherapy
  - Symptoms of underactive or overactive thyroid

**Symptoms suggesting an underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism):**
- Feeling cold, weight gain, fatigue, headaches
- Pins and needles (carpal tunnel), poor memory or concentration
- Low mood, reduced exercise tolerance, muscle or joint pain
- Constipation, dry skin, irregular periods or subfertility

**Symptoms suggesting an overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism):**
- Heat intolerance, sweating, weight loss
- Anxiety or irritability, diarrhoea
- Palpitations, shortness of breath on exertion
- Tremor, muscle weakness, menstrual disturbances

- **Examination:** Your doctor will feel your neck for thyroid enlargement (goitre) or nodules.
- **Blood tests:** If primary thyroid disease is suspected, your doctor will usually order TSH first. Abnormal results trigger reflex testing (free T4 and T3). If secondary (pituitary) thyroid disease is suspected, TSH, free T3, and free T4 are ordered together.

### Interpreting Results in Pregnancy

Normal TSH and free T4 ranges are different in pregnancy. Your doctor uses gestational age-specific reference intervals:

| Gestational age (weeks) | TSH (mIU/L) | Free T4 (pmol/L) |
|---|---|---|
| Less than 7 (as non-pregnant) | 0.3 – 4.2 | 12 – 22 |
| 7–13 weeks | 0.1 – 3.5 | 12 – 21 |
| 14–28 weeks | 0.2 – 3.8 | 10 – 18 |
| 28–40 weeks | 0.3 – 3.8 | 9 – 16 |

These ranges apply to the Roche-Cobas assay used by Awanui Labs (Labtests) and Middlemore Labs. Other labs may use different ranges.

If thyroid results are normal, they are not repeated unless new symptoms develop (this does not apply if you have a known thyroid condition — ongoing monitoring is needed).

## Management

### Before Pregnancy — Known Thyroid Conditions

**If you have an overactive thyroid (hyperthyroidism):**
- Untreated hyperthyroidism in pregnancy increases the risk of miscarriage, heart arrhythmias, and fetal tachycardia — risks that outweigh the small risk from medication.
- Your doctor will discuss antithyroid medication with you. If you are planning pregnancy, your doctor may recommend switching to propylthiouracil and will seek specialist advice.
- Both carbimazole and propylthiouracil may have small risks for the fetus. Your doctor will choose the safest option at the lowest effective dose.

**If you have an underactive thyroid (hypothyroidism):**
- It is important to achieve normal thyroid levels before conception.
- The target TSH is within the normal reference range (a target of below 2.5 mU/L is no longer recommended).
- As soon as pregnancy is confirmed, your thyroxine (levothyroxine) dose should be increased by 30%.
- Your doctor will monitor TSH every 6–8 weeks after dose changes.

### During Pregnancy — Hypothyroidism

**If you have existing hypothyroidism:**
- Increase thyroxine dose by 30% immediately when pregnancy is confirmed. A further increase (up to 50% above pre-pregnancy dose) is often needed by mid-pregnancy.
- Dose requirements stabilise around 20 weeks gestation, then return to pre-pregnancy levels quickly after delivery.
- Iodine supplementation is prescribed if appropriate.
- Reduce back to pre-pregnancy dose after delivery, and have thyroid levels checked 4–6 weeks postpartum.

**If hypothyroidism is newly diagnosed in pregnancy:**
- Treatment with thyroxine is started promptly (usually 50–100 micrograms per day depending on TSH level).
- The goal is to restore normal trimester-appropriate thyroid levels as quickly as possible.
- Monitoring: every 3–4 weeks when starting or changing dose, then every 4–6 weeks until stable, then once each trimester.

**If TSH is mildly elevated but below 10 mU/L (subclinical hypothyroidism):**
- Treatment is recommended if you have positive TPO antibodies (anti-thyroid antibodies).
- If TPO antibodies are negative, treatment is recommended if TSH exceeds 10 mU/L.
- The benefit of treating milder TSH elevation (above the normal range but below 10 mU/L) is uncertain based on current evidence — your doctor will discuss this with you.

### During Pregnancy — Hyperthyroidism

**If you have confirmed hyperthyroidism:**
- Your doctor will refer you to the Maternal and Fetal Medicine Unit (obstetric assessment).
- Iodine supplements should be stopped.
- Radionuclide (nuclear medicine) scans must not be done during pregnancy.
- TSH receptor antibody (TRAb) levels will be checked — these can cross the placenta and cause fetal goitre or fetal hyperthyroidism. If positive, the baby will be monitored on ultrasound.
- Antithyroid medication will be discussed with specialist input. Thyroid function is checked every 3–4 weeks.

**If you have Graves' disease:**
- TRAb levels are checked in early pregnancy (may be falsely low later in pregnancy).
- If TRAb is positive, monitoring for fetal tachycardia or goitre on anatomy scan is arranged.
- After delivery, monthly TSH monitoring for 3 months is recommended due to increased relapse risk.
- If breastfeeding, carbimazole is the preferred antithyroid drug (doses up to 30 mg/day do not significantly affect the baby).

**If TSH is low but free T4 is normal (subclinical hyperthyroidism):**
- Transient suppression of TSH in the first trimester is normal and does not require treatment.
- Monitoring includes thyroid function tests every 4–6 weeks and observation for symptoms.
- Specialist advice is sought if TSH remains suppressed beyond the first trimester, or if symptoms of hyperthyroidism develop.

### During Pregnancy — Other Thyroid Issues

**Isolated hypothyroxinaemia** (low FT4 with normal TSH):
- Can reflect relative iodine deficiency or other causes.
- Your doctor will seek specialist advice about whether thyroxine treatment is needed.
- Adequate iodine supplementation is ensured.

**Goitre in pregnancy:**
- Mild thyroid enlargement can occur in pregnancy due to relative iodine depletion.
- Iodine supplementation is given if appropriate.
- Significant thyroid growth is abnormal and will be investigated.

### After Pregnancy — Postpartum Thyroiditis

Postpartum thyroiditis is a temporary autoimmune inflammation of the thyroid that occurs within one year of delivery, affecting 5–10% of women (up to 25% in women with Type 1 diabetes). Women with positive anti-TPO antibodies are at higher risk.

**What to expect:**
- Symptoms can look similar to postnatal depression.
- It may cause a short period of overactive thyroid (usually 3–6 months after delivery), followed by underactive thyroid (usually 6–12 months after delivery), or either alone.
- Most women have a mildly enlarged, non-tender thyroid gland.

**Management:**
- Thyroid function tests every 4–6 weeks.
- If overactive phase: antithyroid drugs don't help; beta-blockers (e.g., propranolol if breastfeeding) may be used for symptom control.
- If underactive phase: thyroxine is started if symptomatic or if trying to conceive. An attempt to taper off thyroxine is made 6–12 months postpartum.
- Annual thyroid function testing is recommended long-term.

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will seek specialist input (endocrinology or obstetric medicine) in the following situations:

**Before pregnancy:**
- Unsure about iodine supplementation
- Patient is hyperthyroid and planning pregnancy

**During pregnancy:**
- New or existing hyperthyroidism → referral to Maternal and Fetal Medicine Unit
- Thyroid function cannot be controlled, or antithyroid medication cannot be used
- Uncertain about antithyroid or thyroxine dosing
- Suspected subclinical hyperthyroidism with uncertain diagnosis or persistent TSH suppression beyond the first trimester
- Abnormal FT4 unexplained by TSH → chemical pathology advice
- Considering thyroxine for isolated hypothyroxinaemia → endocrinology advice

**After pregnancy:**
- Suspected postpartum thyroiditis with uncertain management
- Breastfeeding patient on antithyroid medication
