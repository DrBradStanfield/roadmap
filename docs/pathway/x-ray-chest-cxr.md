# Chest X-ray (CXR)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers when a chest X-ray is clinically indicated — including emergency situations, accepted indications in adults and children, and conditions where a CXR is generally not needed. Chest X-rays involve a small amount of radiation; your doctor will weigh this against the clinical benefit.

---

## Emergency Situations — Seek Immediate Care

Your doctor will arrange urgent emergency assessment (rather than a routine CXR) if any of the following are present:

- **Severe haemoptysis** — coughing up significant amounts of blood (e.g. more than 20 mL in a single episode, or more than 100 mL per hour), or haemoptysis with hoarseness, stridor, haemodynamic instability, or acute chest pain or breathlessness
- **Severe respiratory distress**
- **Suspected large pneumothorax** with significant pain, breathlessness, or fast heart rate
- **Suspected active tuberculosis** in a patient who is acutely unwell

In these situations, emergency assessment takes priority — imaging should not delay treatment.

---

## When a Chest X-ray Is Indicated — Adults

Your doctor may recommend a chest X-ray for any of the following reasons:

### Respiratory Infections

- **Suspected community-acquired pneumonia** with an unexpectedly severe or atypical presentation, or where there is concern about an underlying lung condition (e.g. lung cancer) — typically arranged urgently (within 24 hours)
- **Respiratory tract infection with possible heart failure** — typically arranged urgently (within 24 hours)
- **Lower respiratory tract infection not responding as expected after 48 hours of antibiotics** — typically within 48 hours
- **Follow-up CXR 6 weeks after a chest infection** — if symptoms persist, or if recommended by a specialist or radiologist

Note: in most patients with an acute lower respiratory tract infection, a CXR will not change management.

### Shortness of Breath (Dyspnoea)

- **Subacute dyspnoea of unknown cause** (e.g. suspected pleural effusion or left ventricular failure) — typically within 48 hours
- **Chronic dyspnoea** — your doctor may recommend a CXR as part of the workup

### Cough

- **Chronic cough** persisting for at least 8 weeks

### Suspected Lung Cancer

Your healthcare provider may recommend a CXR within 48 hours if you have one or more of the following symptoms, especially if unexplained and persisting for more than 3 weeks:

- Abnormal chest signs (e.g. suspected pleural effusion)
- Cervical lymphadenopathy (new, persisting, nodes >1 cm, with other lung cancer risk factors or symptoms)
- Chest or shoulder pain
- Repeated or unresolved chest infections
- Chronic respiratory problems with unexplained change in existing symptoms
- Cough that is unexplained, persisting, and worsening
- New or worsening unexplained shortness of breath
- Features of paraneoplastic syndromes (e.g. hypercalcaemia, finger clubbing with painful swollen joints)
- Unexplained persistent haemoptysis (after excluding upper respiratory tract causes and chest infection)
- Unexplained hoarseness persisting more than 3 weeks
- New supraclavicular lymphadenopathy
- Unexplained weight loss and loss of appetite persisting more than 3 weeks

### Lung Nodules

- Follow-up of nodules previously detected on CXR or CT — only if recommended by a specialist or radiologist, at the specified time frame

### Asthma (Adults)

- CXR may be considered to investigate alternative diagnoses or co-morbidities in patients with non-acute asthma who have an unclear diagnosis, poor control, or frequent exacerbations

### Suspected Pneumothorax (Small)

- Suspected small pneumothorax — your doctor will typically arrange a CXR within 48 hours

### Tuberculosis

- Suspected tuberculosis — your doctor will follow the TB pathway and arrange a CXR if indicated, typically within 2 weeks

### Polymyalgia Rheumatica (PMR)

- A CXR is recommended for all patients before starting steroids, to exclude an alternative cause (e.g. pulmonary pathology, chronic infection, or malignancy)

### Cardiac and Respiratory Disease

- Known cardiac or respiratory disease (e.g. COPD, interstitial lung disease, bronchiectasis, sarcoidosis) that is unresponsive to treatment

### Drug-Induced Lung Disease

- Suspected drug-induced lung disease — if you develop new respiratory symptoms while taking a medication known to affect the lungs (e.g. amiodarone, nitrofurantoin, methotrexate), your doctor may arrange an urgent CXR within 2 weeks

### DVT or Pulmonary Embolism

- Idiopathic (unprovoked) DVT or PE where an underlying malignancy needs to be excluded — typically within 2 weeks

### Asbestos Exposure

- History of asbestos exposure with no previous CXR — discuss with your doctor

## When a Chest X-ray Is Generally Not Indicated — Adults

The following situations are generally not funded through the public health system, as a CXR is unlikely to change management:

- Routine lateral X-ray view alongside a standard PA chest X-ray
- Rib views (unless clinically indicated)
- Immigration purposes
- Heart murmur without signs of complications (e.g. heart failure)
- Routine investigation of high blood pressure (hypertension)
- Monitoring of medications (e.g. amiodarone, methotrexate, nitrofurantoin) in patients who have no symptoms

---

## After a Negative CXR

If your symptoms remain unexplained after a normal CXR — particularly if there is clinical concern about a lung condition — your doctor may:

- Seek specialist respiratory advice
- Arrange further imaging (e.g. CT scan of the chest, abdomen, and pelvis)
- Manage according to a lung cancer pathway

## When a Chest X-ray Is Indicated — Children (14 years and younger)

### Emergency Situations in Children

Your doctor will arrange acute paediatric assessment rather than a CXR if any of the following are present:

- Respiratory distress — fast heart rate, fast breathing, stridor
- Breathing difficulties with oxygen levels of 92% or lower
- Acute severe asthma
- Suspected inhaled foreign body (a CXR may not identify the foreign body; bronchoscopy is usually required)
- Swallowed foreign body with any of the following red flags:
  - Suspected blockage in the oesophagus (e.g. drooling or reluctance to swallow)
  - Known or suspected disc or button battery ingestion
  - Multiple magnets, or both magnets and metal swallowed
  - Sharp or large objects (more than 5 cm long and/or 2 cm wide)
  - Lead-based objects

### Asthma in Children

A CXR may be considered to help assess for alternative diagnoses or co-morbidities if there is an unclear diagnosis and specialist referral is being considered. For example, if the child has:

- Unexpected clinical findings such as crackles, clubbing, or cyanosis
- Persistent, non-variable breathlessness
- Monophonic wheeze or stridor
- Systemic features such as weight loss, muscle aches, and fever
- Poor response to asthma treatment despite reviewing inhaler technique and adherence

Asthma is a clinical diagnosis; a CXR is not typically indicated as a baseline examination unless there are atypical features.

### Bronchiolitis in Children

Bronchiolitis is typically a clinical diagnosis. A CXR is only indicated if complications are suspected (e.g. pleural effusion) or there is diagnostic uncertainty.

### Chest Infection / Pneumonia in Children

CXR is rarely required in suspected community-acquired pneumonia in children. Your doctor may consider it if:

- There is diagnostic uncertainty or an atypical presentation
- The child is not making expected clinical progress and complications are suspected
- There is recurrent pneumonia (e.g. 3 separate infections within 6 months) — typically within 2 weeks
- Follow-up CXR after complicated or atypical pneumonia, 6 weeks after the initial CXR or as recommended by a specialist or radiologist

### Persistent Cough in Children

- A persistent moist or loose cough lasting more than 4 weeks, despite a 2-week trial of antibiotics — typically within 2 weeks

### Swallowed Foreign Body in Children

If a child has swallowed an unknown foreign body and is asymptomatic with no red flags (see above), and a specialist has recommended imaging, your doctor may arrange X-rays of the neck, chest, and abdomen.
