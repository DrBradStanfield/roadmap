# Head Injuries in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of head injuries and concussion in adults (aged 15 and over) — from the acute injury through to follow-up and graduated return to activity.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Call an ambulance or go to an emergency department if any of the following are present after a head injury:

**Predictors of risk** (history of head injury plus any one of):
- Glasgow Coma Scale less than 14 at any time
- Glasgow Coma Scale less than 15 at two hours after injury
- Clinical suspicion of skull fracture (haemotympanum, bruising behind the ear, rhinorrhoea, or depressed skull)
- Focal neurological deficit
- Vomiting more than once
- Coagulopathy or anticoagulant use (e.g., warfarin, dabigatran, clopidogrel) or significant alcohol use
- Concerning mechanism: MVA ejection or roll-over; pedestrian or cyclist hit by vehicle; falls from more than own height or 5 stairs; falls from horses or bicycles; focal blunt trauma (e.g., bat, ball, club)
- Prolonged loss of consciousness
- Post-traumatic amnesia (more than 30 minutes before injury, or more than 5 minutes after)
- Severe headache
- Seizure
- Age 65 or older with significant co-morbidities

Any deterioration in condition after initial assessment also requires emergency assessment.

## Background

Head injuries are common, but the majority of people do not sustain a clinically significant traumatic brain injury (TBI) and do not require hospital admission.

**Traumatic brain injury (TBI)** is injury to the brain from externally inflicted trauma — most often from falls, collisions, being struck by a person, or vehicle accidents. TBI can also result from acts causing hypoxia (e.g., strangulation, suffocation).

**Concussion** is the term used to describe findings after mild TBI. Symptoms include headache, nausea, dizziness, blurred vision, confusion, fatigue, poor concentration, memory problems, sleep difficulties, irritability, and noise intolerance. Concussion is thought to result from functional disturbances rather than macrostructural brain damage.

- While all concussions are mild TBIs, not all mild TBIs result in concussion.
- The term "post-concussion syndrome" is no longer used internationally.
- Many people recover within days to weeks; some have symptoms for 3 to 12 months.
- More significant injury and longer post-traumatic amnesia increase the likelihood of persistent symptoms.

**TBI severity classification:**

| Severity | Duration of unconsciousness | Glasgow Coma Scale | Post-traumatic amnesia |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mild | Less than 30 minutes | 13–15 | Less than 24 hours |
| Moderate | 30 minutes to 24 hours | 9–12 | 1–7 days |
| Severe | Longer than 24 hours | 3–8 | Longer than 7 days |

## Assessment

Your doctor will diagnose concussion based on clinical symptoms and signs. Imaging is not required for diagnosis.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:
- The event: date, time, mechanism of injury, concerning factors (see red flags above)
- Symptoms at the time — including loss of consciousness (occurs in fewer than 10% of concussions), amnesia, confusion, disorientation, or focal neurological symptoms
- Ongoing physical, cognitive, behavioural, or emotional changes and their impact on daily functioning
- General health, including other medical conditions and any blood-thinning medications

### Examination

Your doctor will examine:
- The head for signs of skull fracture (see red flags above)
- Other injuries (spinal, fractures, dental, abdominal)
- Full neurological examination including the Glasgow Coma Scale

**Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS)** — scored 3 to 15:
- Eye response (1–4): spontaneous / to verbal command / to pain / none
- Verbal response (1–5): orientated / confused / inappropriate words / incomprehensible sounds / none
- Motor response (1–6): obeys commands / localises pain / withdraws from pain / flexion / extension / none

### Concussion Screening Tools

Your doctor may use one of these tools to assess severity and track recovery:
- **Brain Injury Screening Tool (BIST)** — preferred tool; 6-minute assessment including 10 prognostic questions and a 16-item symptom severity checklist
- **SCAT5** — for sport-related head injuries; validated for physician use; most useful when a pre-season baseline test exists

## Management

### Acute Care

For unstable patients or those meeting red flags, emergency management and ambulance transfer are required. Your doctor will:
- Manage airway, breathing, and circulation
- Consider cervical spine protection
- Check blood pressure, oxygen saturation, and blood glucose

If GCS is 14 or 15 with contributing factors (brief amnesia, witnessed loss of consciousness, intoxication, nausea, one episode of vomiting, significant headache, or unsuitable home circumstances) but without meeting the full red-flag criteria, your doctor may monitor you in primary care for up to 4 hours.

During monitoring, neurological observations are recorded (GCS, pupils, limb movements, vital signs):
- Every 30 minutes until GCS = 15
- Then every 30 minutes for 2 more hours
- Then every hour until discharge

You can go home if GCS has been 15 for at least 2 hours and it is more than 4 hours since the injury.

### Immediate Advice

Your doctor will give you advice on what to expect. Key points:

**Rest:**
- Physical and mental rest for 48 to 72 hours — avoid computer use, television, and strenuous activity.
- Gradually resume normal activities as symptoms allow.

**Pain relief:**
- Paracetamol for the first 48 hours; NSAIDs (such as ibuprofen) can be added after 48 hours.
- Avoid codeine, tramadol, and other opioids.
- Avoid aspirin and anti-platelet medications.

**Avoid:** alcohol, sleeping tablets, and recreational drugs.

**Driving:**
- Commercial drivers: do not drive for 48 hours; reassessment required before resuming.
- All other drivers: do not drive for a minimum of 24 hours, or until you feel ready.

**Graduated return to activity:**
- Rest for 24 to 48 hours, then gradual return.
- Progress only when symptom-free at each stage. If symptoms return, go back to the previous level for 24 hours.
- Full return to activity should not occur before day 14 at earliest.
- Full return to contact sport: minimum 21 days post injury, after reaching stage 5 of recovery and being symptom-free; medical clearance is strongly recommended.

### Follow-Up

Your doctor will review you one week after injury (or sooner if symptoms worsen or new symptoms appear).

At follow-up your doctor will:
- Reassess symptoms and recovery progress
- If you are on anticoagulants that were stopped after the injury, discuss restarting with a specialist
- Provide reassurance and information if minor problems persist within a month
- Consider whether other conditions may be contributing to ongoing symptoms

**If symptoms persist beyond 14 days**, your doctor may refer you to a concussion clinic for early intervention. Concussion clinic services are tailored to your specific symptom profile and may include physiotherapy, vision/oculomotor rehabilitation, cognitive rehabilitation, and behavioural support.

To be eligible for concussion clinic referral, you typically need:
- An injury in the last 12 months, an accepted ACC claim, and a diagnosis of mild or moderate TBI
- Continued symptoms significantly impacting daily functioning (e.g., mood changes, concentration difficulties, visual disturbances, memory problems, balance issues, fatigue, headaches)
- At least one additional risk factor (e.g., inability to work or attend school for more than a week; repeat concussion within 6 months; post-traumatic amnesia >12 hours; high-functioning occupation; secondary or tertiary education)

## Return to Sport

Many sports bodies have specific protocols. The general principle:
- Minimum 21 days post injury before full return to contact sport
- Must be symptom-free and have completed all stages of the graduated return to play protocol
- Medical clearance is strongly recommended for return to contact sport

Your doctor or sports medicine clinician will guide the return to sport process and complete any required documentation.

---

## Important Note

This information is a general summary of clinical guidance. Assessment of head injuries involves many individual factors. Always see your doctor for a full assessment — especially if symptoms are worsening, persistent, or you have any concerns about your recovery. If in doubt, seek emergency care.
