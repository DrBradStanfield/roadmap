# Ankylosing Spondylitis (AS)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of ankylosing spondylitis (AS) — an inflammatory arthritis of the spine — in adults.

---

## Background

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is an inflammatory arthritis affecting the spine that presents as chronic back pain, typically with progressive stiffness, before the age of 40 years. It may also involve the hips, shoulders, and peripheral joints, and may include extra-articular features.

Key facts:
- AS typically develops in young adults, with peak onset between age 20 and 30 years. Diagnosis can take years to confirm.
- Laboratory findings are generally nonspecific. ESR and CRP do not correlate well with axial disease activity; a normal ESR and CRP do not exclude AS.
- HLA‑B27 is present in 95% of those with AS, but in only 8% of the general population. The prevalence of AS in the HLA‑B27-positive population is 5% — so HLA‑B27 is **not** diagnostic and should not be used as a screening test for nonspecific back pain.
- There is a familial increased risk for AS, further increased if the affected first-degree relative is HLA‑B27-positive.
- During early disease, plain X‑rays may be normal or show only minimal changes.

---

## Assessment

AS is most likely in patients who have had back pain or stiffness for more than 3 months, especially those younger than 40 years without history of trauma.

Your doctor will score 1 point for each positive answer from the following:

**Symptoms** (based on Calin and Berlin inflammatory back pain criteria):
- Insidious onset
- Improvement with exercise, or deterioration with immobility
- No improvement with rest
- Awakened by pain at night with improvement upon getting up
- Early morning stiffness > 30 minutes

**Other factors:**
- Elevated CRP
- Family history of spondyloarthropathy
- Good response to NSAIDs within 48 hours
- Alternating buttock pain
- Peripheral manifestations e.g., arthritis, dactylitis, or enthesitis (inflammation at tendon/ligament attachment to bone), especially of the Achilles tendon or plantar fascia
- Extra-articular features e.g., psoriasis, inflammatory bowel disease, uveitis

**Physical examination** checks for:
- Pain on stressing the sacroiliac joints
- Dactylitis (sausage fingers and toes)
- Plantar fasciitis or Achilles tendinitis
- Signs of oligoarthritis in the knees or ankles

**Interpreting the score:**
- 0 or 1 symptoms → consider another cause (e.g., mechanical back pain)
- 2 symptoms but no other factors → consider another cause
- 2 symptoms + 1 other factor → test for HLA‑B27
  - If negative → consider another cause
  - If positive → consider AS
- **Suspect AS if:** 3 or 4 symptoms; OR 2 symptoms + 2 or more other factors; OR 2 symptoms + 1 other factor + positive HLA‑B27

## Management

Confirmation of the diagnosis of AS always requires rheumatology assessment. Once diagnosis is confirmed, patients can usually be managed conservatively in primary care.

Your doctor may:

1. **Request rheumatology assessment** if AS is suspected, for confirmation of diagnosis.

2. **Arrange imaging** — if symptoms have been present for more than 6 months, an X‑ray of the lumbar spine and sacroiliac joints. Note: images are often normal early in AS, as radiographic changes can take 2 to 5 years to appear.

3. **Manage extra-articular features** appropriately (e.g., psoriasis, inflammatory bowel disease, uveitis).

4. **Prescribe analgesia** for symptom control:
   - NSAIDs are first-line treatment where appropriate. Early inflammatory arthritis usually responds well to NSAIDs.
   - If NSAIDs are not suitable, paracetamol with or without codeine may be considered.
   - NSAIDs should be used with caution in patients on anticoagulants, dual antiplatelet therapy, or those with renal or cardiac disease.

5. **Lifestyle interventions:**
   - Regular physical activity has proven efficacy in AS, aiming to improve or preserve spinal mobility and aerobic fitness. Most aerobic exercise is suitable; high-impact sports or high-resistance training may not be, especially in advanced disease.
   - Encourage a stretching and strengthening programme for the spine.
   - Smoking cessation has a significant positive impact on treatment response and symptom control.
   - Address alcohol intake and weight management if relevant.

6. **Monitor disease activity** at least once per year using the Bath Ankylosing Spondylitis Disease Activity Index (BASDAI).

7. **Refer back to rheumatology** if:
   - BASDAI > 4
   - Conservative management is insufficient
   - Any new peripheral joint pain, stiffness, or synovial swelling or deformity
