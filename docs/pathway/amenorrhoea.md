# Amenorrhoea (Absent Periods)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and initial management of amenorrhoea — the absence of menstrual periods.

---

## What is Amenorrhoea?

Amenorrhoea is the absence of menstruation resulting from dysfunction at any level of the reproductive axis (hypothalamus, pituitary, ovary, or uterine/vaginal abnormalities). There are two types:

- **Primary amenorrhoea** — no period by age 16, or by age 15 if there are no signs of puberty (breast development, pubic hair)
- **Secondary amenorrhoea** — no period for 6 months in a woman who previously had periods

Other than pregnancy, the two most common causes of secondary amenorrhoea are PCOS and functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea.

---

## When to Seek Prompt Medical Attention

See your doctor promptly if you have:

- No period by age 16 (or 15 with no signs of puberty)
- No period for 6 months after previously regular cycles
- Headache, visual changes, or increased thirst alongside missed periods (possible pituitary cause)
- Hot flushes and vaginal dryness at a young age (possible early menopause)
- Milk discharge from the breast (galactorrhoea) without recent pregnancy

---

## Possible Causes

### Primary Amenorrhoea

- Constitutional (late puberty) — often runs in families
- Chromosomal/genetic (most common organic cause): Turner syndrome (45XO), gonadal dysgenesis
- Hypogonadotropic hypogonadism: functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea (excessive exercise, weight loss, stress), Kallmann's syndrome (absent sense of smell + delayed puberty), pituitary disease
- Absent uterus or vaginal outflow obstruction (imperforate hymen, Müllerian agenesis, androgen insensitivity syndrome)
- Other: congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), PCOS

### Secondary Amenorrhoea

- **Physiological:** pregnancy, breastfeeding, menopause
- **Ovarian (40%):** PCOS, primary ovarian insufficiency (early menopause)
- **Hypothalamic/pituitary (35%):** functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea (excessive exercise, weight loss, stress), hyperprolactinaemia (prolactinoma), pituitary lesion
- **Other (5%):** thyroid disease, Cushing's syndrome, Asherman's syndrome (uterine scarring from prior surgery), cervical stenosis, medications (antipsychotics, antidepressants, certain contraception)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Timing of puberty and menstrual history before periods stopped
- Possibility of pregnancy
- Weight changes, exercise levels, stress, and chronic illness (suggests hypothalamic cause)
- Acne and unwanted hair growth (suggests PCOS)
- Headache, visual changes, increased thirst (suggests pituitary disease)
- Milk discharge or breast tenderness (suggests high prolactin)
- Hot flushes and vaginal dryness (suggests primary ovarian insufficiency)
- Prior uterine surgery or procedures (Asherman's syndrome risk)
- Family history of late puberty, genetic conditions, or early menopause
- Current medications

Physical examination typically includes:

- Height, weight, and BMI
- Assessment of puberty staging (Tanner stages: breast and pubic hair development)
- Signs of hyperandrogenism (acne, hirsutism), insulin resistance (skin changes), Turner's syndrome features (short stature, widely spaced nipples, webbed neck), or thyroid abnormality
- Visual field check and smell assessment (to screen for pituitary or Kallmann's syndrome)
- Genital examination with consent — to assess vaginal anatomy and presence of a uterus

## Blood Tests

Your doctor will arrange blood tests. Results are interpreted as follows:

| Likely Cause | FSH | LH | Prolactin | Testosterone | Estradiol |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| PCOS | Normal | Normal/slightly raised | Normal/slightly raised | Normal/moderately raised | Normal (>150 pmol/L) |
| Hypothalamic/pituitary | Low/normal | Low/normal | Normal | Normal | Low |
| Hyperprolactinaemia | Normal/low | Normal/low | High | Normal | Low |
| Primary ovarian insufficiency | High | High | Normal | Normal | Low |

Standard tests for all patients include: pregnancy test (bHCG), FSH, LH, TSH, estradiol. Prolactin is measured if there are specific indications (galactorrhoea, prolonged oligomenorrhoea not explained by lifestyle, etc.).

Additional tests may include testosterone (if signs of hyperandrogenism), pelvic ultrasound (selected cases), and karyotype (if primary amenorrhoea with uterus absent on ultrasound, or suspected Turner's syndrome).

---

## Management

Management depends on the identified cause. Most causes require specialist assessment — your doctor will guide you to the appropriate service.

### Functional Hypothalamic Amenorrhoea

Caused by low energy availability from excessive exercise, calorie restriction, stress, or a combination. Your doctor may recommend:

- Reducing exercise intensity
- Increasing calorie intake (aiming for BMI >21)
- Reducing psychological stress
- Considering cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)
- Ensuring adequate calcium and vitamin D intake (for bone health)
- Estrogen replacement may be considered, though it does not treat the underlying cause and may mask the problem
- If periods do not return within 12 months despite lifestyle changes, specialist referral is arranged

### PCOS

Managed along the PCOS clinical pathway — your doctor will discuss treatment options for your specific goals (cycle regulation, fertility, managing androgens, metabolic health).

### Hyperprolactinaemia

If prolactin is persistently elevated (confirmed on two separate tests) with no clear medication or other cause, your doctor will check visual fields and refer for specialist assessment including MRI of the pituitary gland.

### Primary Ovarian Insufficiency (POI)

If you are under 40 with high FSH and LH (confirmed on repeat testing 4–6 weeks apart), your doctor will investigate further (karyotype, Fragile-X screening, autoimmune antibodies). Menopause hormone therapy (MHT) is typically recommended up to age 50 to protect bone density, cardiovascular health, and cognitive health. Specialist referral is arranged.

### Absent Uterus

Depending on age, referral to paediatrics (under 15) or endocrinology (over 15) is arranged for age-appropriate assessment.

### Outflow Tract Obstruction

If ultrasound shows an imperforate hymen, transverse vaginal septum, or vaginal agenesis, gynaecology assessment is arranged.

### High Androgens

If testosterone is significantly elevated (≥5.0 nmol/L), further hormonal tests are arranged to distinguish PCOS from rarer causes such as congenital adrenal hyperplasia, Cushing's syndrome, or an androgen-secreting tumour. Specialist referral follows if tests are abnormal.

### Thyroid Disease

If TSH is abnormal, your doctor will manage thyroid function according to the relevant pathway.

### Trying to Conceive

If you are trying to conceive, your doctor will follow the sub-fertility pathway in addition to managing the underlying cause.

---

## Specialist Referrals

Your doctor will arrange specialist referral in many of these situations. Referral is typically to:

- **Endocrinology:** absent uterus (age >15), absent secondary sexual characteristics with abnormal hormones, persistent functional hypothalamic amenorrhoea, confirmed hyperprolactinaemia, primary ovarian insufficiency under 42, suspected Cushing's syndrome or androgen-secreting tumour
- **Gynaecology:** outflow tract obstruction on ultrasound, suspected Asherman's syndrome with prior uterine surgery
- **Paediatrics:** absent uterus in those under 15
