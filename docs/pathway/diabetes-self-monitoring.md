# Diabetes Self-monitoring

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways (January 2026)*

This pathway covers how people with diabetes monitor their blood glucose levels — including traditional finger-prick meters and continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) systems — and how to use this information to manage diabetes safely.

---

## Two Main Types of Monitoring

### Finger-prick Blood Glucose Monitoring (SMBG)

Self-monitoring blood glucose (SMBG) involves using a small lancet to prick your finger and placing a drop of blood on a test strip inserted into a glucose meter. This gives an immediate reading of your blood glucose level at that moment.

### Continuous Glucose Monitoring (CGM)

CGM is a sensor worn on the body (usually the arm or abdomen) that continuously measures glucose levels in the fluid under your skin. It sends readings wirelessly to a smartphone app or a receiver device throughout the day and night.

CGM is more convenient than finger-prick testing, reduces hypoglycaemia and diabetes distress, and gives you and your doctor more information to guide management.

**Important:** CGM measures glucose in interstitial fluid (just under the skin), not directly in your blood. There can be a 2 to 5 minute lag between CGM readings and actual blood glucose — this matters most when glucose is rising or falling rapidly.

---

## Glucose Targets

Your doctor will set individual targets for you. General targets for most non-pregnant adults are:

| Measure | Target |
|---|---|
| Fasting blood glucose | Less than 7 mmol/L |
| Blood glucose 2–3 hours after meals | Less than 10 mmol/L |
| Time in target range (CGM: 3.9–10 mmol/L) | Greater than 70% |
| Hypoglycaemia (below 4 mmol/L) | Avoid — especially if on insulin or sulfonylurea |

For glucose targets during pregnancy, your doctor will give you more specific guidance as these differ from the above.

## Funded Glucose Meters (New Zealand)

Blood glucose meters are funded by PHARMAC for people who:
- Are receiving insulin or sulfonylurea therapy
- Are pregnant with diabetes
- Are on home total parenteral nutrition (TPN) at risk of hypoglycaemia or hyperglycaemia
- Have a genetic or acquired disorder of glucose homeostasis

Funded meters include the CareSens N, CareSens N POP, and CareSens N Premier.

A **dual blood glucose and ketone meter** (CareSens Dual) is funded for people with type 1 diabetes, permanent neonatal diabetes, those who have had a pancreatectomy, cystic-fibrosis-related diabetes, or certain metabolic conditions.

If you are not eligible for a funded meter, you may be able to get one free of charge directly from the supplier Pharmaco, or purchase one from a pharmacy or online.

## When to Check Your Blood Glucose

Your doctor will create a schedule tailored to you. General principles:

**Type 1 diabetes (if using CGM):**
- CGM is recommended for all people with type 1 diabetes
- Finger-prick testing is still needed as backup when:
  - Symptoms don't match your CGM reading
  - CGM shows hypoglycaemia but you have no symptoms (or vice versa)
  - Before high-risk activities such as driving or diving
  - For calibration if readings seem inaccurate

**Type 1 diabetes — when to check ketones:**
- When you are acutely unwell
- If you use an insulin pump and suspect it has failed

**Type 2 diabetes — when your doctor may recommend SMBG:**
- Before starting sulfonylurea or insulin therapy
- If there are concerns about hypoglycaemia
- When planning pregnancy or during pregnancy
- When HbA1c is unreliable (certain blood conditions)
- To assess the effect of lifestyle changes or medication adjustments

**Type 2 diabetes — ketone testing** is recommended when unwell only if you:
- Have diabetes due to loss of pancreatic function
- Take an SGLT2 inhibitor
- Have previously had diabetic ketoacidosis

## Hypoglycaemia Awareness

Hypoglycaemia means blood glucose below 4 mmol/L. Signs include shakiness, sweating, confusion, and dizziness.

**Hypoglycaemic unawareness** is a condition where you no longer get warning symptoms before going low. It is more common in people with long-standing type 1 diabetes and is caused by repeated exposure to hypoglycaemia. If you think you may have lost awareness of your lows, discuss this with your doctor — CGM can help detect lows you would otherwise miss.

**Note:** Beta-blocker medications can mask the physical symptoms of hypoglycaemia.

Always carry fast-acting glucose and know how to treat a low. Your doctor can provide written guidance.

## Funded CGM Systems (New Zealand, 2026)

CGM is fully funded for people with type 1 diabetes and some other conditions. Any person with diabetes can also self-fund a CGM device without a prescription.

**Who is eligible for funded CGM:**
- All people with type 1 diabetes
- Permanent neonatal diabetes
- Some types of monogenic diabetes with insulin deficiency
- Type 3c diabetes (diabetes from pancreatectomy, cystic fibrosis, or pancreatitis)
- Some atypical inherited forms of diabetes

A Special Authority number is required. Your GP or other prescriber can apply for standalone CGM. Specialist application is needed for pump-integrated systems.

### Standalone CGM Devices (Funded)

**Freestyle Libre 2 Plus** (replaced Freestyle Libre 2 from May 2026):
- Worn on back of upper arm for up to 15 days
- Up to 28 devices funded per year
- Real-time Bluetooth transmission to compatible smartphones or a funded receiver
- Configurable high/low glucose alerts
- Compatible with insulin pumps for AID systems

**Dexcom ONE+:**
- Worn on arm or abdomen (or upper buttocks in young children) for up to 10 days (plus 12-hour grace period)
- Up to 40 devices funded per year
- Real-time Bluetooth transmission to compatible smartphones or a funded receiver
- Configurable high/low glucose alerts
- Adhesive over-patches supplied with all devices

### Pump-integrated CGM Devices (Funded for AID)

These are for people planning to use automated insulin delivery (AID) systems. They are typically started by a diabetes specialist alongside an insulin pump:

**Dexcom G6 and G7** (used with Tandem t:slim X2 or mylife YpsoPump):
- More customisable alerts than standalone devices, including an "urgent low soon" predictive alert
- G7 is smaller, thinner, and has a 30-minute warm-up (vs. 2 hours for G6)
- Both have 10-day wear; G7 has a 12-hour grace period
- Patients can swap between G6 and G7 without updating their Special Authority

**Freestyle Libre 3 Plus** (used with mylife YpsoPump/CamAPS FX; Tandem t:slim compatibility expected):
- One of the smallest CGM sensors available
- Worn for 15 days; up to 28 devices funded per year

### Automated Insulin Delivery (AID / "Closed Loop")

AID systems combine an insulin pump, a compatible CGM device, and a control algorithm that automatically adjusts insulin delivery based on real-time glucose readings.

**Funded AID pump options:**
- **Tandem t:slim X2** with Dexcom G6 or G7 (runs Basal IQ or Control-IQ)
- **mylife YpsoPump** with Dexcom G6 or Freestyle Libre 3 Plus (runs CamAPS FX — Android only; iOS expected soon)

Note: The Medtronic 770G/780G pump was delisted from January 2025 (consumables funded until October 2026).

AID systems reduce or stop insulin when glucose is predicted to go low, and increase insulin when glucose is predicted to go high. Your doctor will still advise you to take a manual bolus before meals, particularly large ones, rather than waiting for the AID to respond.

## Self-funded CGM

If you have type 2 diabetes or are not eligible for funded CGM, you can buy a CGM device directly from the supplier without a prescription. Evidence shows CGM is effective in type 2 diabetes, particularly at diagnosis and for those on insulin or sulfonylureas. It can be used continuously or just for specific periods (e.g. to understand how different foods affect your glucose).

- Freestyle Libre 2: available from Mediray
- Dexcom ONE+, G6, G7: available from NZMS Diabetes

Financial assistance may be available through Work and Income (e.g. disability allowance).

## Understanding Your CGM Data

CGM devices can generate reports. Useful measures include:

- **Time in range (TIR):** Percentage of readings between 3.9 and 10 mmol/L. Target is over 70% for most non-pregnant adults.
- **Time below range (TBR):** Percentage of readings below 3.9 mmol/L. Relevant if you take insulin or a sulfonylurea. Target: less than 4%.
- **Glucose management indicator (GMI):** An estimate of your HbA1c based on average glucose. A large difference between GMI and your actual HbA1c test may indicate a blood condition your doctor should investigate.
- **Ambulatory glucose profile:** A chart of average glucose by time of day — helps identify patterns for medication adjustments.

**Postmeal glucose:** It is normal for glucose to rise significantly after meals, even with the right insulin dose. If it returns to target within 2 to 3 hours, this does not necessarily mean you need more insulin. Taking insulin 15 to 30 minutes before eating ("pre-bolusing") can reduce this spike.

**CGM and hypoglycaemia:** CGM may detect low glucose levels you would not have otherwise noticed, particularly overnight. If you are not on insulin or a sulfonylurea, a low CGM reading is unlikely to be a true hypoglycaemia unless you also have symptoms — always confirm with a finger-prick test.

## Troubleshooting Your CGM

- If your device seems faulty, contact the supplier — replacements are provided free of charge and do not count toward your annual limit.
- Always keep a traditional finger-prick glucose meter as backup.
- If you use an insulin pump, keep an emergency kit (spare cartridges, infusion sets, batteries, finger-prick meter and strips, ketone meter, glucagon kit, basal insulin, and insulin pens/needles).

**Supplier contacts:**
- Dexcom (ONE+, G6, G7): 0508-634-103 (24 hours, 7 days)
- Freestyle Libre (2, 2 Plus, 3 Plus): 0800-106-100 (9am–7pm, 7 days)

## When to See Your Doctor

Discuss with your doctor if:
- You are newly diagnosed with diabetes and unsure what monitoring is right for you
- You think you may have hypoglycaemic unawareness
- Your CGM readings frequently don't match how you feel
- You are considering switching to or starting CGM
- You are interested in an automated insulin delivery system
- You are pregnant or planning pregnancy — glucose targets change significantly

Your doctor will decide if referral to a diabetes specialist is needed. Children with newly diagnosed type 1 diabetes should have specialist input for CGM setup and education.
