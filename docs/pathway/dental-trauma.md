# Dental Trauma

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial assessment and management of dental injuries in adults and children, including knocked-out, fractured, and displaced teeth.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department immediately if:

- Compromised airway, with or without stridor (noisy breathing)
- Severe facial swelling
- Severe trismus (difficulty opening the mouth/lockjaw)

---

## Background

- Management depends on the age of the patient. Treatment and timing differ significantly between primary (baby) teeth and permanent (adult) teeth.
- Tooth avulsions (knocked-out teeth) in primary deciduous teeth are rarely replanted. Permanent teeth, however, need to be replanted as soon as possible.
- Treatment for traumatised teeth is usually part-funded by ACC in New Zealand.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. Clean the face and oral cavity with water or saline and examine:
   - Face, lips, and oral muscles for soft tissue trauma
   - The area of trauma for fractures, loose, cracked, displaced, discoloured, missing, or abnormally positioned teeth, tooth mobility, and disturbance of bite

   > A disturbance of bite may reflect a luxation injury, alveolar jaw fracture, or fracture of the mandibular condyle.

2. If there is a substantial force causing the dental trauma, assess for facial fractures and head, neck, or other injuries.
3. In children, assess for any signs of non-accidental injury (though most injuries are caused by falls or accidents).
4. Document findings accurately — dental injuries can become the subject of litigation or insurance claims. The timing of the injury is particularly important, as it determines treatment options and the likelihood of success.

**Note on dentition:** Primary (baby) teeth are present from around 6 months. Permanent (adult) teeth begin appearing from age 6, and by age 12 most children no longer have primary teeth.

---

## Management

Management is guided by the type of injury:

### Avulsed (Knocked-Out) Permanent Tooth

**Act quickly — time is critical.**

- Teeth replanted within 30 minutes have a greater than 90% survival rate; this drops to 5% when replantation is delayed by more than 2 hours.
- Your doctor may consider replanting the tooth on the spot:
  1. Keep the patient calm.
  2. Pick up the tooth by the crown (the white part) — avoid touching the root.
  3. If dirty, rinse briefly (10 seconds) under cold running water.
  4. Reposition the tooth in the socket and have the patient bite firmly on gauze to hold it in place.
  5. If replantation is not immediately possible, store the tooth in milk (preferred) or keep it in the patient's mouth between the molars and cheek. Avoid storage in water.
- If the avulsed tooth cannot be located, consider whether it may have been swallowed or aspirated, and investigate appropriately.
- Seek emergency dental care immediately after replantation for splinting and follow-up.

### Avulsed (Knocked-Out) Primary (Baby) Tooth

- **Do not replant primary teeth** — there is a risk of forcing a blood clot or tooth fragment into the developing permanent tooth bud beneath.
- If a primary tooth has already been replanted by a parent or first responder and appears stable, do not remove it.
- If teeth are missing, consider whether they may have been swallowed or aspirated and investigate appropriately (e.g. chest X-ray).

### Fractured Tooth

- Keep the tooth fragment — it may be bonded back onto the tooth (store in milk or Hank's Balanced Salt solution).
- Account for all missing fragments; consider that fragments may have been swallowed or aspirated.
- Your doctor will refer you for emergency dental care with a dentist.
- Patients on low incomes may be eligible for subsidised urgent dental care.

### Mobile or Displaced Tooth (Luxation)

- Your doctor will refer you for emergency dental care with a dentist.

### General Steps

- Your doctor may arrange tetanus prophylaxis if appropriate for the wound.
- An ACC form will typically be completed — your dentist will also complete a separate dental ACC form.
