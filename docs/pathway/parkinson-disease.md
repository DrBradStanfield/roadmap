# Parkinson Disease

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of Parkinson disease, including drug treatment options and non-motor symptoms. Diagnosis is confirmed and treatment initiated by a neurologist or geriatrician — always discuss your individual situation with your doctor.

---

## Background

- Parkinson disease is one of the most common neurodegenerative diseases. Peak age of onset is 55–65 years.
- Diagnosis is made by careful history and examination by a neurologist or geriatrician.
- Management is complex due to medication challenges, slow deterioration, and association with dementia, anxiety, and depression.
- Progression is usually slow but varies by age at onset and co-morbidities. Some patients progress gradually; others (often younger at onset) may develop late-stage symptoms more quickly.
- Support and education of patients and their families is very important.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider a diagnosis of Parkinson disease if 2 of the following features are present, one of which must be bradykinesia.

### Bradykinesia (must be present for diagnosis)

Slowness of voluntary movement and reduced automatic movement (e.g. reduced arm swinging when walking). Difficulty with fine, rapid movements — particularly of the fingers (e.g. fastening buttons, shaving, writing).

### Rest Tremor

- Initially unilateral
- Present at rest and when distracted
- May involve hands, arms, legs, feet, jaw, or tongue — but not the head
- Absent during sleep

### Rigidity

- Stiff, often flexed posture
- Lead-pipe type rigidity, mostly in the limbs

### Early Symptoms

- Fixed facial expression with infrequent blinking
- Constipation
- Sleep disorders, especially rapid eye movement (REM) sleep behaviour disorder (RBD) — talking and acting out dreams
- Tiredness
- Stiffness, aching limbs, general slowing
- Mental slowness
- Depression and especially anxiety
- Loss of sense of smell
- Small handwriting (micrographia)
- Speech that is slower, quieter, and more monotonous

### Other Symptoms That May Affect Management

**Autonomic dysfunction:**
- Orthostatic hypotension
- Bladder and bowel dysfunction
- Sexual dysfunction
- Heat and cold intolerance
- Hyperhidrosis (uncommon)

**Cognitive and psychiatric symptoms:**
- Depression and especially anxiety
- Hallucinations and delusions
- Impulse control disorders (e.g. gambling, hypersexuality, excessive buying)

**Sleep disorders:**
- Excessive daytime sleepiness
- Insomnia and sleep fragmentation
- REM sleep behaviour disorder
- Restless leg syndrome

### Gait Changes

Gait changes often begin as unilateral loss of arm swing, progressing to arms held flexed without swing. Early features may include dragging one leg or a stooped posture. A shuffling, short-stepped gait is common. The patient may have difficulty lifting their feet (festination — fast, small steps) and is at increased risk of falls.

### Drug-Induced Parkinsonism

Some medications may unmask or cause Parkinsonism. Your doctor will ask about:
- Antiemetics such as prochlorperazine and metoclopramide
- Dopamine receptor blocking agents (antipsychotics)
- Dopamine depleting agents (tetrabenazine)
- Sodium valproate

Stopping an offending medication (if possible) may improve symptoms, though improvement may take weeks or months.

### Postural Blood Pressure

Your doctor will measure blood pressure after lying and then standing for at least 1 minute. Treatment may be considered if standing blood pressure is below 90/60 and the patient is symptomatic.

## Warning Signs — Parkinson-Plus Syndrome

The following features may indicate a more complex condition (e.g. multiple system atrophy, progressive supranuclear palsy, corticobasal syndrome, or dementia with Lewy bodies) rather than typical Parkinson disease. Your doctor will consider specialist referral if these are present.

- No tremor at time of diagnosis
- Bilateral and symmetrical signs at onset
- Dementia or hallucinations early in the disease course
- Reduced range of eye movements at diagnosis
- Falls or drop attacks early in the history
- Up-going plantar reflex
- No response to dopaminergic medications

## Management

Your doctor will explain the importance of exercise — including Parkinson-specific programmes focused on general fitness, core strength, and balance — to help maintain motor function and improve gait.

Neurology assessment is typically arranged to confirm the diagnosis (ideally before starting medication), commence engagement with a Parkinson disease nurse, and manage difficult symptoms.

### Drug Treatment

Drug treatment is usually initiated by the neurologist, and may be delayed until there are functional problems. The benefit from medication reduces with time.

**Levodopa** (e.g. Sinemet [with carbidopa], Madopar [with benserazide])

Levodopa is the main treatment for Parkinson disease and a precursor of dopamine. Your doctor may consider it if:
- Motor symptoms are causing functional problems
- Other treatments are insufficient

It is usually started at a low dose and titrated upward. It can take 2–3 months for dose changes to be fully effective. Common side effects include nausea, vomiting, hallucinations, somnolence, sleep attacks (patients should be counselled about driving), and postural hypotension. Sudden withdrawal should be avoided as it can rarely cause neuroleptic malignant syndrome.

**Dopamine agonists** (e.g. pramipexole, ropinirole)

Your doctor may consider dopamine agonists as an add-on treatment if patients develop wearing-off of levodopa effect between doses. They have similar but generally more severe side effects to levodopa. They can cause sleep attacks or impulse control disorders — patients are counselled about this.

**Rasagiline** (MAO-B inhibitor)

Your doctor may consider rasagiline for:
- Mild symptomatic improvement in early-stage disease
- Adjuvant therapy for wearing-off in late stage

Note: can be associated with sudden sleep onset and may interact with SSRIs.

**Anticholinergic agents** (e.g. benztropine, procyclidine)

Your doctor may consider these for disabling tremor, particularly in younger patients with preserved cognitive function. They are generally avoided in patients over 70 due to side effects including memory impairment, postural hypotension, urinary retention, constipation, and hallucinations.

**COMT Inhibitor** (Entacapone; combination tablet: Stalevo)

Your doctor may consider entacapone to:
- Reduce breakdown and increase the half-life of levodopa
- Reduce end-of-dose wearing-off and duration of "off time"

It must be taken at the same time as levodopa, and the levodopa dose may need to be reduced. Stalevo (a funded combination tablet of levodopa, carbidopa, and entacapone) is available in four strengths (funded from May 2025).

## Managing Non-Motor Symptoms

### Communication Problems

Common issues include hypophonia, monotone voice, dysarthria, and micrographia. Your doctor may refer for speech language therapy assessment.

### Constipation

Constipation is common due to effects on the gastrointestinal system, reduced mobility, diet, and medication side effects.

### Dementia

Dementia risk increases with disease duration. Parkinson disease can cause difficulties with planning, sequencing, decision making, and visuospatial awareness, as well as visual hallucinations. Medication side effects can also cause confusion and hallucinations.

### Orthostatic Hypotension

Orthostatic hypotension is common and may be a disease feature or medication side effect. Your doctor will assess with lying and standing blood pressure. Non-pharmacological approaches include increasing fluid intake, compression stockings, salt supplementation, or raising the head of the bed by 30 cm.

### Mental Health

Anxiety and depression are very common. Your doctor may consider:
- SSRIs (e.g. escitalopram, paroxetine, venlafaxine) — usually well tolerated; start low and increase slowly
- Mirtazapine — noradrenergic and serotonergic activity; start 15 mg at night
- Tricyclic antidepressants — used with caution due to anticholinergic side effects
- Benzodiazepines — limited daytime use as they increase falls risk
- Quetiapine or clozapine for hallucinations or psychosis

### Mobility

In moderate to late stage, the focus shifts to compensatory strategies, safety, and equipment provision. Your doctor may refer for occupational therapy if there are concerns about safe functioning at home.

### Oral and Dental Health

Motor difficulties can affect oral hygiene, and conditions such as teeth grinding, dyskinesia, drooling, and xerostomia are common. Your doctor may recommend regular dental visits and speech language therapy assessment if swallowing is affected.

### Pain

Pain occurs in up to 50% of people with Parkinson disease. Your doctor may consider nortriptyline, carbamazepine, or gabapentin for chronic neuropathic pain.

### Secretions and Drooling

Your doctor may consider (with caution in cognitive impairment):
- Nortriptyline 10 mg at night (anticholinergic effect)
- Atropine sulfate eye drops 1.0%, 1–3 drops sublingually every four hours as required
- Hyoscine transdermal patch 1 mg/72 hours (changed every 72 hours)
- Hyoscine butylbromide 20 mg every 6–8 hours subcutaneously (preferred over hyoscine hydrobromide — fewer central side effects)

If medication is unsuccessful, your doctor may seek specialist assessment for Botox or other options.

### Sleep Disorders

Sleep disturbances can include REM sleep behaviour disorder, restless leg syndrome, insomnia, sleep fragmentation, and vivid dreams. Your doctor may consider clonazepam or melatonin for REM sleep behaviour disorder.

## Late Stage Management

In patients who have developed motor complications from long-term levodopa:

- Your doctor will look for wearing-off and dyskinesias, which occur in about half of patients after 5–10 years of treatment and are difficult to manage.
- Medication adjustments may include smaller, more frequent levodopa doses; adding rasagiline, a dopamine agonist, or entacapone.
- Only small changes to one drug at a time are made — sudden major changes can cause severe immobility.
- End-of-life planning and palliative care assessment may be discussed.

---

## Driving

Your doctor will advise on driving, which may include a temporary stand-down while adjusting medications. In late-stage disease, driving may need to cease if the patient cannot respond rapidly enough.

---

## Future Planning

Your doctor may recommend early future planning before cognitive changes make it more difficult:
- A will
- Enduring Power of Attorney (personal care and welfare; property)
- An advance care plan
