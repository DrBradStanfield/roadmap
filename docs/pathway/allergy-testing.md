# Allergy Testing

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers testing for specific IgE-mediated allergy — it does not cover other types of allergy or intolerance testing.

---

## What Allergy Testing Is (and Is Not) For

Allergy testing looks for IgE antibodies — the immune proteins involved in classic allergic reactions. Your doctor will only test for allergens where there is a clear clinical reason to suspect IgE involvement.

**Conditions NOT typically caused by IgE (testing is not appropriate):**

- Chronic urticaria (hives lasting more than 6 weeks)
- Eczema flares
- Delayed food reactions (symptoms more than 2 hours after eating)
- Food intolerances — especially gut symptoms such as bloating, abdominal pain, or diarrhoea
- Migraines
- Behavioural disturbances
- Reactions to respiratory irritants
- Non-specific rashes

---

## Background: Two Types of Allergy Testing

### Skin Prick Testing

Skin prick testing is the gold standard for detecting allergen-specific IgE. It is more sensitive, cheaper, and faster than blood testing, with results available within 20 minutes.

How it works: a small drop of allergen extract is placed on the skin and a sterile lancet is used to prick through the drop. A raised wheal of 3 mm or more in diameter appearing within 10–15 minutes indicates a positive result. Positive (histamine) and negative (saline) controls are used in all patients.

Safety:
- The most common reaction is itch at the testing site, or a vasovagal (fainting) response
- There is a small risk of a systemic allergic reaction (0.008–0.03%) — higher in children under 6 months with active eczema, and when testing fresh foods
- Skin prick testing is not performed during pregnancy

### Blood Testing (Serum Specific IgE / sIgE)

Blood testing measures serum specific IgE (sIgE) for individual allergens. Results are reported as a number in kU/L (less than 0.35 kU/L is considered negative) plus a grade.

Important points:
- Levels do not predict severity of allergic reaction
- Cut-offs are arbitrary — a result below the threshold may still be clinically significant
- A negative sIgE result does not exclude reaction to food, drugs, or venom
- Patients do not need to stop antihistamines before blood testing
- Note: "RAST" is an outdated term for this test and is no longer used

## Assessment

Your doctor will determine whether IgE allergy testing is appropriate based on your symptoms and history. Testing is only requested for specific allergens where there is a genuine clinical reason.

### When skin prick testing is used

Skin prick testing is the first-line option for:
- Acute allergic reactions: urticaria, angioedema, anaphylaxis
  - Note: if anaphylaxis occurred within the last 4 weeks, testing may need to be postponed (mast cells take time to regranulate, which can cause false negative results)
- Atopy: allergic rhinoconjunctivitis, asthma
- Atopic dermatitis (eczema) — with caution:
  - Allergy testing is not first-line for eczema and can be unhelpful
  - Eczema is most commonly due to a defect in the skin barrier, not allergy
  - Patients often have high total IgE and sensitisation to many allergens, which may not be clinically relevant
  - Food avoidance can be harmful — your doctor will seek specialist advice before recommending any food exclusion, especially in children

### When blood testing (sIgE) is preferred

Blood testing is preferred when skin prick testing cannot be done, for example:
- Cannot stop antihistamines or medications with antihistamine effects (e.g., tricyclic antidepressants)
- Dermatographism (skin that reacts to light pressure)
- Pregnancy
- Age under 6 months
- In children aged 6–24 months: only limited testing when specifically recommended by a paediatrician, immunologist, or immunopathologist
- Insufficient normal skin available for testing
- The specific allergen is not available for skin testing

### When specialist advice is needed

Your doctor may consider immunology advice if:
- You need testing to determine a threshold dose for food reactions
- You have eczema and food allergy testing is being considered
- You have had food allergy testing in the context of eczema and food avoidance is being considered
- There is concern about a false positive or negative result
- You need skin prick testing for fresh foods, venoms, or drugs (this requires hospital-based testing)

## Management

### Interpreting results

Your doctor will consider test results in the full clinical context. Both false positive and false negative results occur with allergy testing.

### After testing

Management depends on what the test results show and your overall clinical picture. Your doctor may consider:

- **Confirmed IgE-mediated allergy:** advice on allergen avoidance, action plans (including anaphylaxis management if relevant), and referral to an allergist or immunologist if needed
- **Negative or unclear results with ongoing symptoms:** further investigation or specialist review
- **Eczema with positive allergy tests:** skin care remains the mainstay of treatment. Your doctor will seek specialist advice before recommending any food exclusions
