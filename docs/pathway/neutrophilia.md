# Neutrophilia

> **Note:** This is a clinical reference summary. Always defer to your treating doctor for diagnosis and management decisions. If you are unwell or have concerns, seek medical attention promptly.

---

## What is neutrophilia?

Neutrophilia is an elevated neutrophil count on a full blood count (FBC). The most common cause is a bacterial infection. In most cases the cause is not haematological.

**Common reasons neutrophils can be elevated:**
- Infection or inflammation (most common)
- Necrosis (e.g., after a heart attack or other infarct)
- Physical or psychological stress, heavy exercise
- Drugs: steroids, lithium, clozapine, adrenalin
- Pregnancy (slight increase is normal)
- Smoking — smokers have approximately 25% higher white cell counts; heavy smokers (40 cigarettes/day) may have double the normal absolute neutrophil count. This usually normalises within 1–2 years of quitting, but may remain elevated for up to 5 years.

---

## When is neutrophilia more concerning?

A doctor will assess for the following features that suggest a more serious cause:

- The patient is particularly unwell
- The neutrophilia is severe
- The count is rising rapidly over time
- There is a **left shift** on the blood film — immature neutrophils being released from the bone marrow, typically due to serious infection
- Abnormalities on the blood film
- Splenomegaly (enlarged spleen)

**Chronic myeloid leukaemia (CML)** is a rare but important cause to consider when neutrophilia is persistent and unexplained. Features include:
- Neutrophil count usually > 50 × 10⁹/L
- Left shift with a prominence of myelocytes
- Basophilia and/or eosinophilia may also be present
- Can occur at any age but most common in middle-aged and elderly people

---

## How is neutrophilia managed?

Management is directed at treating the underlying cause.

**For essentially well people with incidental neutrophilia:**
- Repeat FBC in 1–2 months
- Follow up every 3 months for 12 months, then occasionally thereafter
- Stop monitoring once the count returns to normal

**When specialist input is needed** (your doctor will arrange this):
- If you are unwell with significant signs or symptoms → urgent medical assessment
- If there is suspicion of a myeloproliferative neoplasm (e.g., progressive rise in multiple blood cell lines, abnormal blood film, splenomegaly) → non-acute haematology referral
- If significant signs and symptoms are present → haematology advice sought

---

## What should I do?

- If you are feeling unwell, have fever, or are deteriorating → see a doctor urgently or go to an emergency department.
- If your neutrophilia was found incidentally and you feel well → your GP will arrange repeat blood tests to monitor the trend.
- Do not attempt to interpret blood results in isolation. The trend over time and the full clinical picture matter.
