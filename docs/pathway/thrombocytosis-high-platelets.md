# Thrombocytosis (High Platelets)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of thrombocytosis — a persistently elevated platelet count — in adults. Thrombocytosis is also known as thrombocythaemia and is most commonly found incidentally on a routine blood test.

---

## Red Flag — Seek Urgent Haematology Advice

Contact haematology urgently if:

- Platelets are unexpectedly > 1000 x 10⁹/L, or
- Platelets > 600 x 10⁹/L **with thrombotic symptoms**

---

## Background

### Two Broad Types of Thrombocytosis

**Primary (haematological disease):**
- Essential thrombocythaemia (also called primary thrombocytosis)
- Polycythaemia vera
- Primary myelofibrosis
- Chronic myeloid leukaemia
- Myelodysplastic syndromes

Most patients with essential thrombocythaemia can remain well for decades.

**Secondary (reactive):** caused by an exaggerated physiological response to a primary problem — infection, inflammation, or neoplasm.

Common secondary causes include:
- Infectious diseases
- Inflammatory diseases
- Neoplasms
- Non-malignant haematological conditions (e.g., acute blood loss, iron deficiency anaemia)
- Functional or surgical hyposplenism
- Tissue damage (e.g., recent trauma or surgery)
- Exercise
- Medications (e.g., steroids, adrenaline, low molecular weight heparins)
- Pregnancy
- Allergic reactions

### Cancer Risk

A 2017 prospective study found that 11.6% of men and 6.2% of women aged > 40 years were diagnosed with cancer in the 12 months following a result showing thrombocytosis. Risk was highest for lung, colorectal, prostate, breast, and ovarian cancers. When a second raised platelet count was recorded within 6 months, the risk rose to 18.1% in males and 10.1% in females.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. Ask about history of arterial or venous thromboembolism or bleeding (features that raise concern for primary thrombocytosis)
2. Look for secondary causes (see list above)
3. Examine for splenomegaly
4. Consider initial investigations — ferritin and CRP

---

## Management

Management follows a stepwise approach depending on platelet count and associated findings:

### Step 1 — Urgent: very high platelets or thrombotic symptoms

If platelets > 1000 x 10⁹/L, or > 600 x 10⁹/L with thrombotic symptoms: your doctor will seek haematology advice immediately.

### Step 2 — Elevated haematocrit or splenomegaly

If haematocrit is elevated or splenomegaly is present, your doctor will consider polycythaemia vera.

### Step 3 — Normal haematocrit, no splenomegaly

Repeat FBC, blood film, ferritin, and CRP in 2–3 weeks, then:

- **Low ferritin** → consider iron deficiency as the cause
- **Platelets normal, CRP elevated but falling** → consistent with resolving secondary/reactive thrombocytosis; monitor
- **Platelets and CRP remain elevated** → ongoing secondary cause; investigate and treat underlying condition
- **Platelets > 600 x 10⁹/L, CRP and ferritin normal** → likely essential thrombocythaemia
  - Your doctor will refer for non-acute haematology assessment
  - If platelets < 1500 x 10⁹/L: low dose aspirin is typically started
  - If platelets > 1500 x 10⁹/L: aspirin is avoided due to bleeding risk

### Persistent thrombocytosis > 400 x 10⁹/L for > 3 months — cancer surveillance

Because thrombocytosis is a recognised cancer risk marker, your doctor will:

- Take a detailed history and perform a clinical examination
- If symptoms or signs are present: investigate as appropriate
- If no symptoms or signs:
  - Encourage participation in national cancer screening programmes (bowel and breast for relevant age groups)
  - Request a chest X-ray
  - Request full iron studies (not just ferritin) to detect iron deficiency in the presence of inflammation
- If all investigations are normal:
  - Monitor platelets at each visit or 6-monthly (whichever is sooner)
  - Encourage early reporting of new symptoms
  - Refer for non-acute haematology assessment if platelet count continues to rise and approaches 600 x 10⁹/L

---

## When Your Doctor Will Refer

- **Urgent haematology advice:** platelets > 1000 x 10⁹/L, or > 600 x 10⁹/L with thrombotic symptoms
- **Non-acute haematology assessment:** platelets persistently > 600 x 10⁹/L with normal CRP and ferritin (likely essential thrombocythaemia)
