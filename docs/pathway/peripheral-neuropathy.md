# Peripheral Neuropathy

> **Doctor's note:** This guide covers assessment and initial management of peripheral neuropathy in primary care. Any concerns about acute or rapidly progressive symptoms, uncertain diagnosis, or failure to improve should be discussed with your doctor promptly.

---

## Overview

Peripheral neuropathy is damage to the peripheral nerves, causing altered sensation, pain, weakness, or autonomic symptoms. Most cases are chronic and symmetrical. The most common causes are diabetes, vitamin B12 deficiency, and alcohol excess — but often no cause is found.

**Acute onset or rapid progression is uncommon and a medical emergency** — it may indicate Guillain-Barré syndrome, vasculitis, or toxin exposure, and requires urgent medical assessment.

---

## Symptoms

Patients typically present with one or more of:

- **Sensory:** paraesthesiae (pins and needles), numbness, burning pain, loss of vibration/position sense
- **Distribution:** "glove and stocking" pattern; feet usually affected first
- **Motor:** weakness of the ankles, loss of ankle reflexes, muscle wasting, ataxia (unsteady gait from loss of position sense)
- **Autonomic:** constipation, loss of bowel or bladder control, orthostatic hypotension (dizziness on standing), dry skin, reduced sweating

If **only motor symptoms** with no sensory component, consider other diagnoses (muscular dystrophy, myasthenia, myopathy) — discuss with your doctor.

If **asymmetrical**, this is less typical and may suggest mononeuritis multiplex or a vasculitic process — warrants medical review.

---

## Common Causes

Often no cause is found. The three most common causes are:

1. **Diabetes** — painful neuropathy; blood glucose and blood pressure control can slow progression
2. **Vitamin B12 deficiency** — treatable; needs prompt identification
3. **Alcohol excess** — painful neuropathy; alcohol reduction is key

Other causes include:
- Metabolic conditions
- Inflammatory conditions (e.g., collagen vascular diseases, sarcoidosis)
- Toxic causes (medications, chemicals, heavy metals, vitamin B6 excess)
- Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP)
- Gammopathy-related demyelinating neuropathies
- Hereditary (e.g., Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease)

---

## Investigations

Your doctor will typically arrange:

- **Urine:** protein
- **Blood tests:** FBC, CRP, vitamin B12, folate, HbA1c, creatinine, sodium, potassium, LFTs, TFTs, serum protein electrophoresis (SPE)

**Nerve conduction studies** are specialist-arranged when needed — these are not organised in primary care. They help distinguish axonal degeneration from demyelinating neuropathy, which guides cause and treatment.

---

## Management

### Treating the underlying cause

This is the most important step where a cause is found:

- **Diabetes:** optimise blood glucose and blood pressure control to slow progression
- **B12 deficiency:** treat with B12 supplementation/injections
- **Alcohol:** alcohol reduction/cessation is essential

### Medications for painful neuropathy

Neuropathic pain is difficult to treat. Your doctor may consider:

**First line:**
- Tricyclic antidepressants (TCA) — **amitriptyline** or **nortriptyline**
  - Start 10 mg at night; increase slowly in weekly increments up to 50 mg at night

**Second line — anticonvulsants:**
- **Gabapentin**, **carbamazepine**, or **sodium valproate**
  - All have similar efficacy
  - Titrate dose with 7-day intervals between increments
  - **Important:** sodium valproate must not be used in women of childbearing age unless no suitable alternative exists, due to serious risks of birth defects and developmental delay — requires specialist recommendation and effective contraception

**Combination therapy:**
- A TCA combined with an anticonvulsant is more effective than either alone

**Topical treatments:**
- **Capsaicin 0.075%** cream — funded for diabetic peripheral neuropathy (endorsed prescription needed)
  - Works by depleting substance P in nerve endings
  - Takes 1 week for initial response; 4–6 weeks for full effect
  - May cause initial burning sensation — this reduces with continued use
  - Apply 3–4 times daily; do not massage in; wash hands after
  - Do not use on broken skin
- **Lidocaine (lignocaine)** topical — may help but not funded for this indication

### Injury prevention and foot care

Loss of sensation means injuries can go unnoticed. Key advice:

- Wear sensible, well-fitting footwear
- Inspect feet visually every day
- Seek early treatment for any foot problems
- Regular professional foot care (podiatry)
- Weight reduction if overweight
- Walking aids may be needed with severe leg weakness

### Monitoring and support

- Watch for **depression** and mental health impacts from loss of independence or chronic pain
- Driving: significant neuropathy may affect fitness to drive — discuss with your doctor

## When to See a Specialist

### Urgent — see a doctor immediately

- **Acute or rapidly progressive neuropathy** (e.g., suspected Guillain-Barré syndrome) — requires emergency assessment

### Specialist referral is appropriate when

Moderate or severe symptoms or impairment, and any of:

- Subacute neuropathy with motor findings (muscle wasting or weakness)
- Distinct asymmetry or involvement of proximal nerves
- Rapidly progressive neuropathy
- Focal neuropathy where surgery may be considered
- Severe sensory loss without identified cause after standard workup

### Specialist referral is generally NOT needed for

- Mild symptoms or minor impairment only
- Chronic sensory symptoms without disability
- Chronic neuropathy with a clear identified cause (e.g., diabetes, B12 deficiency, alcohol) being managed in primary care
- Small fibre neuropathy features (pain/temperature only, no other sensory or motor loss)

> **Note:** Most chronic sensory neuropathies have no specific treatment beyond identifying and addressing contributing medical factors, then providing symptomatic and supportive management. Your GP can manage these in the community.

### Other specialist referrals

- **Age > 65 with comorbidities:** may benefit from Older Person's Health Service assessment
- **Poorly controlled diabetic neuropathy with management complexity:** diabetes specialist referral
- **Family history of Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease:** genetic health service referral

---

## Key Points for Patients

1. Peripheral neuropathy is common — it affects about 2–8% of people, more with age
2. Finding and treating the cause (especially diabetes, B12 deficiency, or alcohol) is the most important step
3. Pain management takes time and patience — medications need gradual titration
4. Protecting your feet from injury is essential when sensation is reduced
5. Most cases are manageable in general practice — specialist referral is needed for specific complex presentations
