# Peripheral Neuropathy

> **Doctor's note:** This guide covers assessment and initial management of peripheral neuropathy in primary care. Any concerns about acute or rapidly progressive symptoms, uncertain diagnosis, or failure to improve should be discussed with your doctor promptly.

---

## Overview

Peripheral neuropathy is damage to the peripheral nerves, causing altered sensation, pain, weakness, or autonomic symptoms. Most cases are chronic and symmetrical. The most common causes are diabetes, vitamin B12 deficiency, and alcohol excess, but often no cause is found.

**Acute onset or rapid progression is uncommon.** It suggests Guillain-Barré syndrome, vasculitis, or toxins, and usually needs hospital admission. If symptoms come on suddenly or get worse quickly, call 111 or go to the emergency department.

---

## Symptoms

Patients typically present with one or more of:

- **Sensory:** paraesthesiae (pins and needles), numbness, burning pain, loss of vibration/position sense
- **Distribution:** "glove and stocking" pattern; feet usually affected first
- **Motor:** may include weakness of the ankles, loss of ankle reflexes, muscle wasting, and ataxia (unsteady gait from loss of position sense)
- **Autonomic:** constipation, loss of bowel or bladder control, orthostatic hypotension (dizziness on standing), dry skin, reduced sweating

If there are **only motor symptoms** with no sensory component, your doctor may consider other diagnoses (muscular dystrophy, myasthenia, myopathy). Discuss this with your doctor.

If symptoms are **asymmetrical**, this is less typical. It may suggest mononeuritis multiplex or a vasculitic process, so discuss it with your doctor.

---

## Common Causes

Often no cause is found. The three most common causes are:

1. **Diabetes.** Good blood glucose and blood pressure control may slow progression.
2. **Vitamin B12 deficiency.** Your doctor will treat any deficiency found.
3. **Alcohol excess.** Your doctor may offer support to cut down.

Painful neuropathy is usually associated with alcohol dependency, diabetes, porphyria, vitamin B1 deficiency, or vitamin B12 deficiency.

Other causes include:
- Metabolic conditions
- Inflammatory conditions (e.g., collagen vascular diseases, sarcoidosis)
- Toxic causes, especially alcohol and drugs
- Chronic inflammatory demyelinating polyneuropathy (CIDP)
- Gammopathy-related demyelinating neuropathies
- Rare causes (e.g., chemicals, metals, vitamin B6 excess)

---

## Investigations

Your doctor will take a full history, including how symptoms started and progressed, medications, alcohol, and any chronic conditions. They will examine your limbs and check your peripheral pulses to rule out peripheral vascular disease as a cause.

Your doctor will typically arrange:

- **Urine:** protein
- **Blood tests:** FBC, CRP, vitamin B12, folate, HbA1c, creatinine, sodium, potassium, LFTs, TFTs, serum protein electrophoresis (SPE)

The diagnosis is usually made from the history, examination, and blood tests.

**Nerve conduction studies** are arranged by a specialist when needed, not by your GP. They help distinguish axonal degeneration from demyelinating neuropathy, which guides cause and treatment.

---

## Management

### Treating the underlying cause

This is the most important step where a cause is found:

- **Diabetes:** good blood glucose and blood pressure control may slow progression
- **B12 deficiency:** treat any vitamin B12 deficiency
- **Alcohol:** support to reduce alcohol (alcohol intervention)

### Medications for painful neuropathy

Neuropathic pain is difficult to treat. Your doctor may consider:

**First line:**
- Tricyclic antidepressants (TCA): **amitriptyline** or **nortriptyline**
  - Start 10 mg at night; increase slowly in weekly increments up to 50 mg at night

**Second line (anticonvulsants):**
- **Gabapentin**, **pregabalin**, or **carbamazepine**
  - All have similar efficacy
  - The dose is adjusted according to the effect, with 7-day intervals between increments

**Combination therapy:**
- A TCA combined with an anticonvulsant is more effective than either alone

**Topical treatments:**
- **Capsaicin 0.075%** cream (funded only for diabetic neuropathy)
  - Works by depleting substance P; it acts on the nerves, not directly on inflammation
  - Takes 1 week for initial response; 4–6 weeks for full effect
  - Build up to full use. There may be a burning sensation at first, which goes away with continued regular use
  - Use regularly 3–4 times daily; do not massage in; wash hands after, unless your hands are being treated
  - Do not use on broken skin or on areas you do not mean to treat
- **Lidocaine (lignocaine)** topical: may be useful, although it is not funded for this indication

### TENS machine

Your doctor may suggest trying a transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation (TENS) machine. This hand-held device passes a small electric current through the skin to nerves.

- It may reduce pain by blocking pain signals or by stimulating production of endorphins. It may also reduce the sensation of muscle tension and spasm.
- The effects may not last very long, and evidence for long-term benefit is weak. A recent review found not enough evidence to definitely support TENS for pain, but it is worth considering as an option.
- It should not be used with a pacemaker.

### Injury prevention and foot care

Loss of sensation means injuries can go unnoticed. Key advice:

- Wear sensible footwear to protect your feet
- Inspect your feet visually
- Seek treatment early for any foot problems
- Regular foot care
- Weight reduction
- Walking aids may be needed with severe leg weakness
- If disability is increasing, your doctor may consider occupational therapy, physiotherapy, or podiatry for foot care

### Monitoring and support

- Watch for issues related to loss of independence and disability, such as **depression**
- Driving: your doctor may consider whether neuropathy affects your fitness to drive

## When to See a Specialist

### Urgent — see a doctor immediately

- **Acute or rapidly progressive neuropathy** (e.g., suspected Guillain-Barré syndrome): call 111 or go to the emergency department. This usually needs hospital admission.

### Specialist referral is appropriate when

Symptoms are not improving with care from your GP, there are moderate or severe symptoms or impairment, and any of:

- Subacute neuropathy with motor findings (muscle wasting or weakness)
- Distinct asymmetry or involvement of proximal nerves
- Rapidly progressive neuropathy
- Focal neuropathy where surgery may be considered
- Severe sensory loss without identified cause after standard workup

Specialist input can change the outcome in inflammatory demyelinating or vasculitic neuropathies, and in focal neuropathies where surgery might be considered.

### Specialist referral is generally NOT needed for

> **Note:** Most chronic sensory neuropathies have no specific treatment beyond identifying and addressing contributing medical factors, then providing symptomatic and supportive management. Your GP can manage these in the community.

### Other specialist referrals

- **Age > 65 with comorbidities:** may benefit from Older Person's Health Service assessment
- **Diabetic neuropathy with longstanding, poorly controlled diabetes and management issues:** diabetes specialist referral
- **Family history of Charcot-Marie-Tooth disease:** genetic health service referral

---

## Key Points for Patients

1. Finding and treating the cause (especially diabetes, B12 deficiency, or alcohol) is the most important step
2. Pain management takes time and patience. Medications need gradual titration
3. Protecting your feet from injury is essential when sensation is reduced
4. Most cases are managed in general practice. Specialist referral is for specific complex presentations
