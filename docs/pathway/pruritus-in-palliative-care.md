# Pruritus in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of pruritus (itch) in adults with advanced illness. Pruritus due to medical conditions may require specific therapy. Management should always be guided by your doctor, as pruritus is often difficult to manage.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will try to identify any underlying cause, which may include:

- **Drug allergy** — a reaction to a medication you are taking
- **Hepatic disease** — obstructive jaundice (bile duct blockage causing bile to back up)
- **Uraemia** — waste product build-up from kidney failure
- **Active lymphoma** — particularly non-Hodgkin's lymphoma
- **Opioid medications** — itch is a recognised side-effect
- **Vasodilator drugs**
- **Scabies** — a skin mite infestation

Where no clear cause is identified, dry skin is the most common explanation — especially in older adults.

---

## Management

### Treat underlying causes

Your doctor will first address any treatable cause:

- If bile duct obstruction is present, biliary stenting may be considered.
- If kidney-related itch (uraemic pruritus), specific management applies — your doctor will guide this.

### Condition-specific medications

Your doctor will choose medications based on the underlying cause:

| Cause | Treatment options |
|-------|-----------------|
| Cholestasis (bile duct obstruction) | Cholestyramine (often poorly tolerated); rifampicin; sertraline; naltrexone (very low doses, as it is an opioid antagonist — requires palliative care guidance) |
| Uraemia (kidney failure) | Capsaicin cream for localised itch; gabapentin; UVB phototherapy |
| Opioid-induced itch | Trial of a different opioid (usually first choice); antihistamine (sedating or non-sedating) |
| Hodgkin's lymphoma | Prednisone; cimetidine combined with an antihistamine such as promethazine |
| Paraneoplastic itch (cancer-related) | Paroxetine or sertraline; mirtazapine |

*Most of the medications listed above are used outside their standard approved indications in this context. Your doctor will determine the most appropriate option.*

### Topical treatments

Your doctor or nurse will recommend:

- **Emollients** (moisturisers) — apply liberally and as often as needed. Options include oil and water fatty emulsion cream, cetomacrogol cream, or sorbolene cream. Use as a soap substitute as well as a moisturiser. A large quantity (e.g., 3 × 500 g) is usually prescribed.
- **Menthol in cetomacrogol cream** (0.25–1%) — a surface cooling agent that can help relieve itch.
- **Pinetarsol bath** — soaking in a pinetarsol bath may provide relief.
- **Mild to moderate topical corticosteroids** (e.g., hydrocortisone cream) — may help with localised inflammation-related itch.

### Practical measures

- **Breaking the itch-scratch cycle:** Keeping fingernails cut short can reduce skin damage from scratching. Cotton gloves or bandaging may help prevent scratching at night.
- **Night sedation or anxiolysis:** Your doctor may consider temazepam for night sedation, or lorazepam or midazolam for anxiety-related itch.

---

## When to Contact Your Doctor or Care Team

Contact your doctor or palliative care team if:

- Itch is severe or significantly affecting your sleep or quality of life
- Topical treatments are not providing enough relief
- You develop a rash, new skin lesions, or signs of skin infection from scratching
- You are unsure about the cause of the itch

Pruritus can be difficult to manage — your doctor will seek adult palliative care support and advice if needed.
