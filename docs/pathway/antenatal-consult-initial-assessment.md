# Antenatal Consult - Initial Assessment

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: August 2022.*

This pathway covers the first visit with a doctor or other clinician when a woman seeks care for a new pregnancy. It covers the questions, examination, and tests at that visit, the supplements and vaccines to start, and finding a midwife.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Prompt Medical Attention

Your doctor will ask about problems that need attention early in pregnancy, such as:
- **Bleeding or pain.** These may need assessment for miscarriage or ectopic pregnancy.
- **An intrauterine contraceptive device (IUD) still in place.**
- **Severe nausea and vomiting.**

If you are not immune to chickenpox and you are exposed to it during pregnancy, **seek immediate medical attention**. Chickenpox carries a risk of severe complications in pregnancy, and your doctor may give zoster immunoglobulin to help prevent infection.

**Pregnancy test recall:** in September 2026 some point-of-care pregnancy tests were recalled because of a small risk of false-positive results. If a positive result is unexpected, your doctor may confirm it with a clinical and laboratory check.

---

## Background

- The aim is for every pregnant woman to be registered with a midwife or lead maternity carer (LMC) as early as possible, and by 12 weeks of pregnancy at the latest.
- Routine early pregnancy scans to check dates are not recommended.
- The first visit is a chance to update your medical, social, mental health, and pregnancy history, start supplements, arrange blood tests, and plan vaccines.

---

## Assessment

### How you feel about the pregnancy

Your doctor will ask how you feel about the pregnancy. If you are unsure about continuing, they can talk with you about abortion or placing a child for adoption.

### Working out your due date

- Dates are calculated from the first day of your last menstrual period (LMP).
- A 12-week scan as part of combined screening is usually enough to check dates, unless your LMP is unknown or you have a high-risk pregnancy.
- Your LMP is usually treated as:
  - **Certain:** you know the start date (within a week either side), and your periods are regular, or only recently irregular but your LMP was less than 8 weeks ago.
  - **Uncertain:** your periods are irregular (cycle lengths vary by more than 20 days in the past year) and your LMP was more than 8 weeks ago.
  - **Unknown:** the pregnancy began while you were using hormonal contraception, you have had no period since a previous pregnancy, or you cannot recall your last period.
- Examination helps estimate how far along you are. The baby's heartbeat can usually be heard by 12 weeks. By 20 weeks the uterus should sit at the level of the belly button.

### Pregnancy history

Your doctor will ask about previous pregnancies and births, including:
- Pre-eclampsia, or HELLP (haemolysis, elevated liver enzymes, low platelets)
- Gestational diabetes, or a baby large for gestational age
- Growth restriction, or a baby small for gestational age (SGA)
- Caesarean or assisted delivery
- Pre-term delivery before 37 weeks
- Stillbirth, or more than 3 miscarriages
- Heavy bleeding after birth (post-partum haemorrhage)

A previous history of pre-eclampsia, growth restriction, or SGA matters because aspirin may be recommended (see Management).

### Medical history and medicines

- Your doctor will check for medical conditions or medicines that may affect, or be affected by, the pregnancy. Some conditions may need a specialist opinion or a transfer of care.
- Medicines that may harm the developing baby are discussed, particularly in the first trimester.
- Conditions that may mean aspirin is recommended include high blood pressure, chronic kidney disease, type 1 or type 2 diabetes, and autoimmune disease such as lupus (SLE), scleroderma, or antiphospholipid syndrome.

### Mental health

Your doctor will ask about your own and your family's history of mental illness, particularly around pregnancy and birth.

Risk factors for mental distress or illness include:
- Personal or family history of mental illness, particularly around pregnancy
- History of abuse or trauma
- Recent loss, including a previous loss of a baby
- An unplanned pregnancy that has been difficult to accept
- Past fertility issues, pregnancy complications, traumatic birth, or premature birth
- Relationship difficulties, poor social support, or isolation
- Money, work, or housing worries
- Addiction issues
- High expectations of yourself, the birth, or parenting
- Pacific women, particularly those aged younger than 25 years

Protective factors include a supportive partner and family, and good contact with your general practice or other health providers.

### Genetic conditions

Your doctor will ask about:
- A previous pregnancy or child with a chromosomal anomaly, significant disability, syndrome, or learning disability
- A family history in either parent of an inherited condition, such as thalassaemia, sickle cell anaemia, cystic fibrosis, spina bifida or other neural tube defect, cleft palate, significant visual or hearing impairment, or congenital dislocation of the hip
- A family history of pre-eclampsia

### Social history

Your doctor may ask about:
- Social and financial support
- Your work, including risks such as radiation
- Your home: smoking, drug, and alcohol use in the household, family violence, harmful gambling, money worries, and housing (overcrowded, cold, damp, or unsafe)

Questions about family violence are a routine part of antenatal care. They should be asked only when you can talk privately, without a partner present.

Your doctor will also ask about any previous sudden unexpected death in infancy (SUDI), and assess your risk of blood clots (venous thromboembolism) in pregnancy.

Your doctor may ask about female genital mutilation (FGM, also called traditional cutting). It can be important to know about this early in pregnancy.

### Risk factors for pre-eclampsia

**Major risk factors:**
- Pre-existing high blood pressure, especially if not well controlled
- Antiphospholipid syndrome (not just antibodies)
- Lupus (SLE) or lupus nephritis
- Previous pre-eclampsia (especially if it required delivery before 37 weeks)
- Previous HELLP
- Pre-existing type 1 or 2 diabetes
- Kidney disease
- Oocyte donation or frozen embryo transfer

**Moderate risk factors:**
- Multiple pregnancy (twins or more)
- A first-degree relative with pre-eclampsia
- A new partner, especially if the partner's previous pregnancies were complicated by pre-eclampsia
- BMI 35 or greater (early or pre-pregnancy)
- First pregnancy at younger than 18 years, or at 40 years or older
- Pregnancy interval of 10 years or more
- Assisted reproductive technology (ART) or sperm donation
- Systolic blood pressure 130 mmHg or greater, or diastolic blood pressure 80 mmHg or greater, at booking (or less than 14 weeks)
- African or Indian ethnicity

### Smoking, alcohol, and drugs

- Asking about smoking, alcohol, and drug use is routine, because it affects both mother and baby.
- If there are concerns, you will be supported to get help.
- Your information is kept private within the team treating you, unless there are safety concerns or you agree to share it. Parents or caregivers do not have access to your health information.
- If you use a patient portal, your doctor will check who has access to it.

### Examination

- **Blood pressure:** a baseline reading is recorded. High blood pressure increases risk in pregnancy and is a major risk factor for pre-eclampsia. Blood pressure is considered high in pregnancy if the systolic pressure is 140 or higher, or the diastolic pressure is 90 or higher, on 2 or more readings in a row, at least 4 hours apart.
- **Heart murmurs:** these need careful assessment in pregnancy, and early referral if there are concerns.
- **Height, weight, and BMI:** these are essential at the first visit. A healthy weight gain chart can help you track your weight during pregnancy.
- **Size of the uterus:** checked by pelvic and abdominal examination, especially if dates are uncertain. This can help detect twins early. By 12 weeks the uterus can usually be felt above the pubic bone.
- **External genitals:** checked for warts, ulcers, or other abnormalities.

### Investigations

**Routine antenatal blood tests:**
- Full blood count (FBC), blood group, and antibody screen
- Hepatitis B immunity
- Rubella immunity
- Syphilis
- HbA1c
- Ferritin (iron stores)
- HIV

Since January 2026 the standard antenatal blood test request includes all of these, including HIV. If you want to decline any of them, tell your doctor, who will then request the others one by one.

**Ferritin and anaemia:**
- Testing ferritin early gives a baseline for later in pregnancy, and allows early treatment of iron deficiency before it progresses to anaemia.
- Anaemia in women (including in pregnancy) is a haemoglobin lower than 115 g/L.
- Iron deficiency is a ferritin less than 20 micrograms/L, or less than 100 micrograms/L in people with chronic inflammatory conditions.
- Early treatment with oral iron is preferable, and less costly than IV iron infusions later in pregnancy.

**HIV:** screening is offered to all pregnant women. Without treatment, the risk of passing HIV to the baby is 30%. Treatment with antiretroviral therapy during pregnancy reduces this risk to under 1%.

**Other tests your doctor may consider:**
- **Chickenpox (varicella) antibody** if you have no history of chickenpox. If you are not immune, avoid contact with chickenpox, and plan to be immunised before any future pregnancy.
- **Thyroid tests** only if there is a reason, such as thyroid symptoms, a personal or family history of thyroid disease, insulin-dependent diabetes or other autoimmune disease, recurrent miscarriage, previous pre-term birth or growth restriction, or previous head or neck radiotherapy or radio-iodine treatment.
- **Urine test (MSU)** to screen for bacteria in the urine without symptoms (asymptomatic bacteriuria).
- **Cervical screening** if it is due.
- **Chlamydia test**, recommended regardless of risk factors. A vaginal swab, collected by you or by your clinician, can reduce complications for you and your newborn.

**Ultrasound:** routine early scans for dating are not recommended. Your doctor may arrange an early scan if:
- Your dates are significantly uncertain
- You are being referred for an abortion
- You have bleeding or pain, or concern about pregnancy loss
- You have severe vomiting in pregnancy (hyperemesis gravidarum)
- You have had trauma
- You are pregnant with an IUD in place
- You have had a previous ectopic pregnancy
- You have a complex medical condition where a change in medicine may be needed, such as warfarin

## Management

### Finding a midwife (lead maternity carer)

- Your doctor will recommend finding a midwife or LMC straight away. The aim is to be registered by 12 weeks of pregnancy at the latest.
- If you have trouble finding one, your doctor can refer you to the local maternity service.
- If you are not eligible for publicly funded maternity care, ask midwives about their fees.
- Think about where you would like to give birth: at home, in a birthing centre, or in hospital. All of these options are safe, unless complications during pregnancy need hospital care. Your choice may influence which midwife you choose.

### Folic acid

Folic acid is recommended for all pregnant women from at least 4 weeks before conception until week 12 of pregnancy, to reduce the risk of neural tube defects.

If you are not already taking it, your doctor may advise:
- **0.8 mg once daily** if you are at low risk.
- **5 mg once daily** if you have:
  - a personal or family history (in either parent) of neural tube defect
  - a BMI greater than 30
  - diabetes
  - coeliac disease or another malabsorption disorder
  - sickle cell anaemia
  - epilepsy, or take anti-seizure medicine
  - an autoimmune or rheumatic condition treated with a folate antagonist such as sulfasalazine
  - tuberculosis treated with a folate antagonist
- With sickle cell anaemia, 5 mg once daily is advised from 4 weeks before conception and throughout the whole pregnancy.

### Iodine

- Iodine supplements are recommended if you are pregnant, considering pregnancy, or breastfeeding. Iodine is essential for both mother and child.
- Iodine deficiency in pregnancy is associated with goitre and thyroid problems in the mother, and with reduced head circumference at birth, ADHD, and impaired cognitive outcomes in the child. Supplements have shown significant benefits in severe deficiency, with fewer benefits seen in mild to moderate deficiency.
- The recommended dose, if your thyroid works normally, is **potassium iodate 150 micrograms daily**. This is funded.
- Start at conception and continue until you finish breastfeeding. If you are vegetarian or vegan and do not use iodised salt or eat seaweed, start when you begin planning a pregnancy.
- Seaweed and kelp tablets are not recommended. Their iodine levels vary a lot and can be toxic.
- Not all over-the-counter antenatal vitamins contain the recommended dose of iodine (it is often too low).
- If you have a thyroid condition, your doctor will advise. People who are thyrotoxic, hyperthyroid, or already on thyroxine may not need iodine.

### Aspirin and calcium to lower the risk of pre-eclampsia

If you have a **major risk factor** for pre-eclampsia (see Assessment), your doctor may recommend:
- **Low-dose enteric-coated aspirin 100 mg at night** from 12 weeks of pregnancy, and
- **Calcium carbonate, 2 × 500 mg tablets once daily**, from booking until birth.

Your doctor will discuss the risks and benefits of aspirin in pregnancy, and check for reasons not to take it: a previous peptic ulcer, asthma triggered by anti-inflammatory medicines (NSAIDs), or an aspirin allergy.

If you have **moderate risk factors**, or it is unclear whether to start aspirin and calcium, your doctor may seek specialist advice.

### Aspirin for risk of poor fetal growth

If you are at high risk of the baby's growth being restricted, your doctor may consider low-dose enteric-coated aspirin (100 to 150 mg enteric coated, daily) started before 16 weeks. Risk factors include:
- A previous pregnancy with growth restriction, a small baby, or pre-eclampsia
- Age older than 35 years
- BMI over 30

Anyone whose baby in a previous pregnancy was small for gestational age should be on low-dose aspirin unless there is a reason not to take it.

### Vitamin D

- There is some evidence to suggest that vitamin D deficiency (less than 25 nmol/L) and insufficiency (less than 50 nmol/L) are associated with gestational diabetes and pre-eclampsia, and with low birth weight, dental caries, and acute respiratory infections in infants.
- **Common risk factors** for low vitamin D:
  - darker skin, including many women from Africa, the Indian Subcontinent, and the Middle East, and Māori and Pacific women with minimal sun exposure
  - avoiding the sun for religious, personal, or medical reasons (for example, covering with veils or clothing, a history of melanoma, or medicines that make the skin sensitive to light)
  - living south of Nelson-Marlborough in winter or spring
- **Medical risk factors:** gut malabsorption, chronic liver or kidney disease, or medicines that affect vitamin D levels (such as some anticonvulsants).
- With 1 to 2 common risk factors, your doctor may prescribe **colecalciferol 400 to 800 international units a day** (1 to 2 drops of oral liquid).
- With any medical risk factor, or all 3 common risk factors, you will need an individual treatment plan. Your doctor may test your vitamin D level to guide it.
- Your doctor will also give advice on safe sun exposure.

### Thiamine

- People from refugee backgrounds may be at higher risk of thiamine deficiency. It occurs in areas with rice-based diets lacking variety, such as parts of Myanmar, Thailand, Nepal, and Bhutan.
- Pregnant and breastfeeding women are at higher risk because they need more thiamine. There is a risk of beriberi, and of death in breastfed babies of thiamine-deficient mothers.
- Signs include nerve problems (peripheral neuritis), heart problems (cardiac insufficiency), and swelling (oedema).
- If deficiency is suspected in a woman at risk, your doctor may prescribe one tablet of vitamin B complex daily. Testing may not be helpful.

### Existing medical conditions

- Conditions such as asthma, diabetes, epilepsy, factor V Leiden, heart conditions, high blood pressure, and thyroid disease each have specific pregnancy care.
- Mental health conditions are managed with perinatal mental health care.
- Some pregnancy-related conditions may need a specialist opinion or a transfer of care.

### Screening for Down syndrome and other conditions

- Your doctor will discuss antenatal screening for Down syndrome and other conditions.
- Book with your midwife in plenty of time to have this screening done.
- If you may not have a midwife by 12 weeks, your doctor may give you the forms for the blood test (maternal serum screening) and the nuchal scan.
- This matters most if your baby is due in December or January, when finding a midwife over the holidays can be hard.

### Lifestyle

- **Food safety:** healthy eating, and avoiding listeria and toxoplasma.
- **Exercise and healthy weight gain:** a weight gain chart can guide how much weight to gain. If you are overweight or obese, your doctor may offer a Green Prescription (support with activity and healthy eating).
- **Smoking:** continuing to smoke during pregnancy is one of the largest risk factors for sudden unexpected death in infancy (SUDI). Stopping can significantly reduce this risk.
  - All pregnant women who smoke are strongly encouraged to use a stop-smoking service. Support is also offered to anyone in the household who smokes.
  - Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) can be used in pregnancy or breastfeeding if you would otherwise keep smoking. NRT is safer than smoking. If you use a patch, remove it overnight.
  - Going back to smoking after birth is common. Support around the time of birth may help.
- **Alcohol:** stopping alcohol is recommended. Community alcohol and drug services can help if you need extra support.
- **Other drugs:** your doctor can give advice on substance use in pregnancy.
- **Antenatal classes.**
- **Vaginal birth after caesarean**, if this applies to you.

### Support services

Your doctor may discuss:
- Māori and Pacific health services, including mental health, stop-smoking, and addiction support
- Family Start, a home visiting programme from the second trimester for families facing challenges that may make it harder to care for a baby
- Community support for young mothers, including help with family violence concerns or food
- The Healthy Homes Initiative, a free service to help families have warmer, drier, healthier homes

### Vaccines in pregnancy

- **Influenza (flu):** safe at any time in pregnancy. It can be given straight away. If it is not yet available, it is given when the flu vaccine season starts (for example, early March). Flu in pregnancy is linked to higher rates of premature birth, stillbirth, and small babies. New Zealand research shows healthy pregnant women are nearly 5 times more likely to be admitted to hospital with flu complications than women who are not pregnant.
- **Whooping cough (pertussis):** recommended from 16 weeks of pregnancy, preferably in the second trimester for the most protection. There is still some effect up until 2 weeks before birth. The mother's antibodies pass to the baby and give some protection until the baby is old enough to be immunised.
- **COVID-19:**
  - If you are unvaccinated, a single dose of Comirnaty 30 micrograms is recommended at any stage of pregnancy.
  - If you were vaccinated before and your pregnancy is healthy, an extra dose is a personal choice, given from 12 months after your last COVID-19 vaccine.
  - An extra dose is recommended from 6 months after your last dose if you have a high-risk pregnancy (such as gestational diabetes, high blood pressure, blood clots, BMI greater than 40, or past pregnancy complications), are severely immunocompromised, have conditions that raise the risk of severe COVID-19, or are aged 35 years and older.
  - Antibodies pass to the baby through the placenta and breast milk, and give protection against hospitalisation for up to 6 months.
- Comirnaty, Boostrix, and flu vaccines can be given at the same time. Studies show no increase in side effects.

### Handover to your midwife

Your doctor will send your midwife or LMC a referral letter with your assessment, medicines, vaccines, and any services already requested.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you or seek advice if:
- You are at increased risk of a fetal chromosome abnormality: maternal fetal medicine assessment, and genetic health assessment if you are not already known to the genetic service.
- You have moderate risk factors for pre-eclampsia, or it is unclear whether to start aspirin and calcium: gynaecology advice.
- The right vitamin D dose is unclear: gynaecology advice.
- A heart murmur raises concerns: early referral.
- You have a medical or pregnancy-related condition that may need a specialist opinion or transfer of care.
- You need help to stop smoking, drinking alcohol, or using drugs: a stop-smoking service or community alcohol and drug service.

## Key Concepts for Patients

- Find a midwife as early as you can, and register by 12 weeks at the latest.
- Folic acid and iodine are recommended in pregnancy. Your doctor will tell you whether you need the higher folic acid dose.
- Routine early scans to check dates are not recommended. A 12-week scan is usually enough.
- If you have risk factors for pre-eclampsia, ask your doctor whether aspirin and calcium are right for you.
- Flu, whooping cough, and COVID-19 vaccines are recommended in pregnancy.
- Always discuss any new symptoms or medication questions with your doctor before making changes.
