# Allergy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of allergies to food, stinging insects, and medications.

---

## When to Seek Emergency Care

If there are features of **anaphylaxis**, seek emergency care immediately:

- Dizziness or collapse
- Generalised skin rash spreading rapidly
- Face, lip, or tongue swelling
- Difficulty breathing or wheeze
- Low blood pressure

See the [Anaphylaxis pathway](anaphylaxis.md) for full management.

---

## Background

### What Is an IgE-Mediated Allergy?

An IgE‑mediated allergy (type 1 hypersensitivity) is the immune system's response to a normally harmless substance (allergen) — such as pollens or foods. Reactions can range from mild (urticaria, allergic rhinitis) to severe (anaphylaxis).

For a true IgE-mediated allergy, two factors are usually present:

1. Symptoms occur **within 1 hour** of allergen exposure.
2. **Sensitisation** confirmed by skin prick test (SPT) or serum specific IgE (ssIgE) blood test.

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about and check for:

- Type, severity, and duration of symptoms
- Timing of symptoms relative to allergen exposure
- Systemic features suggesting anaphylaxis (collapse, generalised rash, lip/tongue swelling, breathing difficulty)
- Consistent triggers

### Food Allergy

Your doctor will take a history to distinguish **food allergy (immune-mediated)** from **food intolerance (non-immune)**.

**Symptoms of IgE-mediated food allergy** typically occur within minutes to 2 hours of eating:
- Skin: urticaria (hives), angio-oedema, flushing, itching
- Gut: vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea
- Systemic: pallor, dizziness, collapse
- Respiratory: sneezing, tongue swelling, hoarseness, wheeze, stridor

**Most common food allergens:**
Milk, eggs, peanuts, tree nuts, fish, shellfish, soy, and wheat account for up to 90% of reactions. In New Zealand, kiwifruit and sesame are also common.

**Key distinctions:**
- **Angio-oedema** (swelling of deeper skin layers, e.g. lips, eyes, groin) alone does not mean anaphylaxis.
- **Urticaria** that varies in location hour to hour is not anaphylaxis. Urticaria lasting days to weeks is unlikely to be food allergy unless there is repeated exposure.

**Risk of severe reaction is higher with:**
- Older children and adolescents
- Concurrent asthma
- Peanut or nut allergy

**Food intolerance** (not immune-mediated) can be caused by:
- Enzyme deficiency e.g., **lactose intolerance** (diarrhoea, bloating, abdominal pain — uncomfortable but not dangerous, does not cause rash or anaphylaxis)
- Food poisoning
- Pharmacological reactions e.g., caffeine, MSG, salicylates, amines
- Psychological food avoidance

IgE-mediated food allergy usually starts in childhood. Adult-onset IgE-mediated food allergy is rare.

### Stinging Insect Allergy

Your doctor will identify the insect (honeybee, yellow jacket wasp, or paper wasp) and classify the reaction:

- **Localised** — redness or pain at the sting site only
- **Systemic without life-threatening features** — e.g., urticaria without breathing difficulty or low blood pressure
- **Anaphylaxis** — see emergency section above

### Medication Allergy

Your doctor will ask about:
- The medication name, dose, and route
- Timing between the dose and onset of symptoms
- Nature of the reaction (rash, breathing difficulty, collapse)
- Any treatment given and response
- Prior exposure to the same or similar medications

**PEN-FAST score for penicillin allergy:**

If penicillin allergy is reported or suspected, your doctor will calculate a PEN-FAST score:

| PEN-FAST item | Points |
|---|---|
| ≤ 5 years since reaction, or unknown | 2 |
| Anaphylaxis, angioedema, or severe skin reaction | 2 |
| Treatment required for reaction, or unknown | 1 |

| Total score | Risk of true penicillin allergy |
|---|---|
| 0 | Very low (<1%) |
| 1–2 | Low (5%) |
| 3 | Moderate (20%) |
| 4–5 | High (50%) |

Severe skin reactions include Stevens-Johnson syndrome, toxic epidermal necrolysis, drug reaction with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms (DRESS), and acute generalised exanthematous pustulosis.

Your doctor will also determine whether the reaction was:
- **IgE-mediated** (immediate, within 30–60 minutes)
- **Non-IgE-mediated** (delayed hypersensitivity or non-allergic)

## Management

Allergy management involves reducing allergen exposure and treating symptoms. Your doctor may also recommend a medic alert for any significant allergy.

### Food Allergy

- Avoidance of the trigger food is the main strategy
- Your doctor will seek specialist immunology advice if adult-onset IgE-mediated food allergy is suspected

### Stinging Insect Allergy

If you have had a systemic reaction (not just localised) to a bee or wasp sting:

- Your doctor will arrange baseline blood tests (serum tryptase and insect-specific IgE)
- A referral to immunology may be made to consider **venom immunotherapy (desensitisation)**

**About venom immunotherapy:**
- An effective treatment for systemic reactions to bee or wasp stings
- Reduces the risk of systemic reaction from around 60% per sting to less than 10%
- Therapy typically takes 3–5 years
- Initiated in hospital; maintenance injections can be given in GP clinics every 4 weeks

**Sting avoidance advice:**
- Avoid strong fragrances outdoors
- Cover skin with clothing, hat, shoes, and gloves when gardening
- Avoid picking fruit from the ground or trees
- Keep food covered outdoors; wash hands after eating sweet foods
- Do not drink from open bottles or cans outdoors
- Have nearby bee or wasp nests removed by professionals

### Medication Allergy

- Your doctor will consider alternative medications
- For suspected penicillin allergy, alternative antibiotics may be recommended
- If there is diagnostic uncertainty, a trial of medication challenge under specialist supervision may be arranged
- If you are allergic to a long-term medication that is still indicated (e.g., aspirin, NSAIDs, cotrimoxazole), specialist immunology assessment will be sought

---

## When Your Doctor May Refer

Your doctor may refer you to immunology (allergy specialist) if:

- You have had systemic symptoms following a bee or wasp sting (for venom immunotherapy consideration)
- There is diagnostic uncertainty and a medication challenge is needed
- You are allergic to a long-term medication that is still clinically needed
- Adult-onset IgE-mediated food allergy is suspected
