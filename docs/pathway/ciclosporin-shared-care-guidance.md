# Ciclosporin Shared Care Guidance

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This guidance supports patients who have been started on ciclosporin by a dermatology or rheumatology specialist. It covers what to expect, what your doctor will monitor, and when to seek urgent help.

---

## Background

Ciclosporin is an immunosuppressant — a medication that dampens the immune system. It is used for conditions such as psoriasis and inflammatory arthritis when other treatments have not been sufficient.

Your specialist will have done a safety check before starting treatment, including blood tests, blood pressure, and urine testing. Ciclosporin is generally started at a low dose (2–3 mg/kg/day in two divided doses) and may be gradually increased if needed, up to a maximum of 5 mg/kg/day. A response is usually seen within 1–3 months but can take up to 6 months.

---

## Warning Signs — Contact Your Doctor Urgently

Tell your doctor straight away — they may need to stop the medication and contact your specialist — if you notice:

- Tremor (shaking) or tingling / pins and needles (paraesthesia)
- Signs of reduced kidney function (your doctor will detect this on blood tests — creatinine rising more than 30% above your baseline on more than one reading)
- Significantly elevated liver enzymes (more than twice the upper limit of normal on blood tests)

---

## Monitoring

Your doctor will arrange regular blood tests and blood pressure checks while you are on ciclosporin:

- **Blood tests** (FBC, creatinine, potassium, AST, ALT): every month for the first 3 months, then every 3 months — or as directed by your specialist
- **Blood pressure**: every 3 months
- **Cervical screening**: if you have a cervix and are aged 25–70, your doctor will recall you for three-yearly cervical screening. People on long-term immunosuppression have an increased risk of HPV infection and should attend regularly.

---

## Possible Side Effects

Your doctor will discuss any side effects with you. Common ones and what may be done:

| Side effect | What your doctor may consider |
|---|---|
| Nausea, stomach discomfort, gum changes, increased hair growth | Monitor |
| Tremor or tingling | Stop medication and seek urgent specialist advice |
| Elevated potassium, urate, or cholesterol | Monitor and treat as appropriate |
| Raised liver enzymes (2–3 times upper limit of normal) | Reduce dose |
| Kidney impairment (creatinine rises >30% above baseline) | Reduce dose and seek specialist advice; if persists beyond 1 month, stop medication |
| High blood pressure | Treat with a calcium channel blocker; stop medication if blood pressure cannot be controlled |
| Increased skin cancer risk | Avoid excess sun exposure, use sunscreen, and monitor |

For a full list of side effects, your doctor can refer to the NZ Formulary or Medsafe.

---

## Drug Interactions

Ciclosporin has multiple potential interactions with other medications. Always tell your doctor and pharmacist about all medicines you are taking (including over-the-counter and herbal products). Your doctor or pharmacist can check for interactions before any new medicine is started.

---

## Vaccinations

Being on an immunosuppressant affects which vaccines are safe and recommended:

- **Annual flu vaccine** — recommended each year
- **Pneumococcal vaccine** — recommended every 5 years (funding depends on your diagnoses)
- **Shingles vaccine (Shingrix)** — your doctor may consider this. Note: the older Zostavax vaccine is a live vaccine and must **not** be given while on ciclosporin
- **Hepatitis B vaccine** — your doctor may consider this if you are not immune and are in a higher-risk group
- **Live vaccines must be avoided** — these include MMR (measles/mumps/rubella), oral polio, oral typhoid, BCG, chickenpox (Zostavax), and yellow fever

---

## Surgery

If you need surgery, ciclosporin may need to be paused. Tell your surgeon and anaesthetist that you are taking this medication so they can liaise with your prescribing team.

---

## Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

Ciclosporin is considered safe during pregnancy and breastfeeding. Discuss your individual situation with your doctor or specialist.

---

## Specialist Follow-Up

Once stable, your specialist will usually review you annually. Your GP manages the ongoing monitoring between specialist visits.

---

*Always discuss any concerns about your medication with your doctor or specialist before making any changes.*
