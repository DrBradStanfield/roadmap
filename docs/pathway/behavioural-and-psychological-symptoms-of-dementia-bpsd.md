# Behavioural and Psychological Symptoms of Dementia (BPSD)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of behavioural and psychological symptoms that arise in people living with dementia — for both the person with dementia and those who care for them.

---

## Background

Behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD) are:

- **Very common** — affecting practically all people with dementia at some stage
- **Distressing** — for both the person with dementia and their carers
- **Associated with higher healthcare use** and increased risk of moving into residential care

**Psychological symptoms include:**
- Anxiety
- Depression
- Psychosis (e.g. hallucinations, persecutory delusions, delusions of infidelity)

**Behavioural symptoms include:**
- Aggression and agitation
- Apathy
- Inappropriate eating, dressing, or toileting behaviour
- Sexually inappropriate behaviour
- Sleep disruption
- Vocally disruptive behaviour
- Wandering

---

## Core Principle

**Always try non-pharmacological approaches first.**

Pharmacological approaches carry real risks of harm and should not be the first option. Any treatment trial — pharmacological or not — should aim to improve the quality of life and safety of everyone affected.

A reasonable treatment goal is **reduction**, rather than complete cessation, of the challenging behaviour.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Ask about the specific behavioural and psychological symptoms and how much they are affecting the person with dementia and their carer
- Explore what need the person with dementia may be trying to communicate through their behaviour — needs may be biological (thirst, hunger, pain, continence) or emotional (feeling respected, safe, valued, loved)
- Ask: for whom is this behaviour a problem? If it is not the person with dementia themselves, management may not be best targeted towards them

---

## Management

### Step 1 — Person-Centred Approach

Your doctor may discuss with the carer:

- Most behaviours that carers find challenging arise from the person with dementia's unmet needs
- Identifying the purpose behind the behaviour — for example, afternoon agitation may be frustration and anxiety because the person feels they need to "go home" to prepare dinner
- How the carer can help meet those needs through trial and error
- Avoiding restraining the person with dementia

### Step 2 — Behavioural Approach (ABC Chart)

Your doctor may ask the carer to keep an **ABC chart** to record episodes of the behaviour. This captures:

- **A**ntecedents — what happened before
- **B**ehaviour — what occurred
- **C**onsequences — what happened after

Reviewing the chart helps identify:
- Environmental factors contributing to the behaviour
- Patterns of triggers that make the behaviour more likely
- Patterns of responses that may be reinforcing the behaviour
- Situations when the behaviour does not occur, and what is different

### Step 3 — Psychotropic Medication (if other approaches are ineffective)

Psychotropic medication is considered **only as a last resort** (except in exceptional circumstances). It is not very effective for behaviours that challenge carers, carries significant side-effect risks, and does not address the underlying needs of the person with dementia.

**Earlier use of psychotropics may be appropriate in exceptional circumstances:**
- True emergency situations (typically very short-term or one-off use)
- Ongoing high risk of serious harm to the person or others
- Carer is too distressed to participate in a non-pharmacological approach
- Reasonable non-pharmacological strategies have been tried and proved ineffective
- Recurrence of a situation where the person previously had clear benefit and minimal side effects from a particular psychotropic

If the person with dementia lacks capacity to consent to treatment, your doctor will discuss with formal proxy decision-makers or next of kin.

---

## Medications Your Doctor May Consider

All psychotropic medication in dementia requires:
- Starting with the **lowest possible dose**
- Increasing very gradually (no less than weekly)
- Planning for dose review and discontinuation — most can be successfully withdrawn after 3 to 6 months
- Regular review (at least every 3 months)

### Antidepressants

Depression in dementia is an important diagnosis to consider. Your doctor may consider antidepressants if non-pharmacological interventions are insufficient or if the depression is severe:

- **Citalopram** 20 mg — may help depressive states, anxious states, and isolated psychotic symptoms without depression
- **Mirtazapine** 15–30 mg — may be particularly helpful for insomnia associated with depression or anxiety

Note: antidepressants with anticholinergic activity (including tricyclics, paroxetine, and venlafaxine) are avoided in dementia.

### Cholinesterase Inhibitors

These have the strongest effect for apathy, some effect for psychotic symptoms, and least effect for anxiety and agitation. They can cause neuropsychiatric side effects, especially low mood.

### Benzodiazepines

Benzodiazepines may be considered for sedation for sleep or specific procedures, and for states of emotional arousal such as significant anxiety or anger:

- **Lorazepam** 0.25–1 mg — can be effective for short-term management of agitation and anxiety as needed

**Important cautions:**
- Benzodiazepines cause sedation, confusion, impaired cognition, incontinence, slurred speech, and increased falls risk
- Clonazepam is avoided due to its long half-life and risk of accumulation
- Regular review is essential as accumulation or heightened sensitivity can develop over time

### Zopiclone

- **Zopiclone** 3.75–15 mg at night — may help with sleep

### Antipsychotics

Antipsychotics are most effective for psychotic or manic symptoms, and less effective for anxiety, sedation, or sundowning agitation. There is limited evidence for short-term use when aggression or agitation is severe.

**Antipsychotic options your doctor may consider:**
- **Risperidone** 0.25–0.5 mg/day (maximum 2 mg/24 hours) — avoid in parkinsonism
- **Quetiapine** 12.5–25 mg once or twice daily — risk of postural hypotension and sedation
- **Haloperidol** 0.25–0.5 mg/day (maximum 2 mg/24 hours) — do not use in parkinsonism

**Contraindications and risks:**
- Contraindicated in parkinsonism (especially Parkinson disease or Lewy body dementia) without specialist advice
- Significant adverse effects include: sedation, postural hypotension, anticholinergic symptoms, extrapyramidal side effects (especially parkinsonism)
- Associated with increased risk of stroke, falls, over-sedation, pneumonia, and all-cause mortality
- Avoid if stroke risk is high

**Monitoring:**
- Watch for extrapyramidal side effects (mainly parkinsonism), sedation, increased confusion, postural hypotension, and falls
- Once symptoms are improved for three months, antipsychotics can usually be safely tapered and stopped over 2–4 weeks

---

## When to Seek Specialist Review

Your doctor will consider referral for specialist cognitive impairment assessment if symptoms are:
- Severe
- Associated with high risk of harm
- Likely to lead to a move into a higher level of care
- Not responding to the interventions above
