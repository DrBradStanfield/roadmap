# Pain Management in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of pain in adults with cancer-related pain or advanced illness managed palliatively. Good pain management is a core part of palliative care. The goal is a manageable level of pain — not always complete pain freedom — to help you maintain the best possible quality of life. All pain management decisions should be made together with your doctor.

---

## Principles of Pain Management

Your doctor will:

1. Assess your pain thoroughly.
2. Determine the cause and whether it is reversible.
3. Agree on goals of management with you.
4. Apply non-pharmacological measures.
5. Use medications if required.
6. Review you regularly between interventions.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed pain history, including:

- Character, location, onset, duration, and severity of pain
- What makes it better or worse
- Associated symptoms
- Impact on daily activities, mood, and relationships
- What the pain means to you
- Treatments tried and how well they worked

A systematic approach (PQRSTU) may be used: Palliative/Provoking factors; Quality; Radiation; Severity; Time pattern; Understanding (what you know and feel about the pain).

Your doctor will also consider:

**Possible causes of pain**, which may include more than one:
- The underlying illness (tumour, ischaemia, nerve damage, or cancer treatment effects)
- Complications of the disease (e.g., constipation, pressure injuries)
- Pre-existing conditions (e.g., arthritis)
- Acute causes such as spinal cord compression, pathological fracture, DVT, raised intracranial pressure, or urinary retention

**Existential distress ("total pain")** — Pain in palliative care often has psychological and spiritual dimensions. Fear, grief, loss, and guilt can magnify physical pain. Addressing these is as important as medication.

**Exacerbating factors** that may worsen pain or reduce medication effectiveness:
- Constipation (can worsen absorption of oral medications)
- Urinary retention
- Infection
- Hypercalcaemia
- Renal failure
- Insomnia, depression, or anxiety

## Management

### Non-pharmacological measures

Your doctor may recommend:

- Avoiding or minimising triggers
- Environmental modifications
- Physiotherapy, TENS (transcutaneous electrical nerve stimulation)
- Occupational therapy (bed aids, mobility equipment, commodes)
- Relaxation, massage, counselling
- Social and community support

### Medications — by pain severity

**Mild pain:**

- **Paracetamol** — Up to 1000 mg four times a day (maximum 4 g per day). Dose reduced with liver problems or low body weight. Regular paracetamol can reduce the amount of opioids needed.
- **NSAIDs** (e.g., ibuprofen, diclofenac) — Useful for bone pain. Rarely started in palliative care due to stomach and kidney side-effects. Always combined with a stomach-protecting medication (proton pump inhibitor).

**Mild to moderate pain:**

In palliative care, patients are usually moved directly to stronger opioids. Codeine and tramadol have significant limitations and are generally not recommended in palliative care.

**Moderate to severe pain:**

Your doctor will usually prescribe a stronger opioid. **Morphine** is the standard first-line choice.

#### Morphine

Morphine is available in:
- Immediate-release (fast-acting): morphine elixir (RA Morph) or Sevredol tablets — used for titration and breakthrough doses.
- Controlled-release (slow-release): m-Eslon capsules — taken twice daily once pain is stable.

**Starting morphine:**
- Your doctor will start at a low dose (typically 2.5–5 mg every 4 hours as needed) and increase gradually until pain is controlled.
- A laxative (e.g., docusate + senna) is always started at the same time — constipation is an almost universal side-effect of opioids.
- An antiemetic may also be prescribed for nausea, which often settles after the first few days.

**Once pain is stable:**
- You will be switched to a controlled-release morphine twice a day, plus an immediate-release dose available every hour for breakthrough pain (approximately one-sixth of the total daily dose).
- Your doctor will regularly review and adjust the dose.

**Subcutaneous opioid:**
If you are unable to take oral medication (e.g., due to vomiting, unconsciousness, or bowel obstruction), morphine can be given under the skin — either as bolus injections or as a continuous infusion via a syringe driver (a small pump). The subcutaneous dose is approximately half the oral dose.

#### Alternative opioids

If your kidney function is impaired (eGFR below 30) or morphine is not tolerated, your doctor may consider:

- **Oxycodone** — Similar to morphine; more potent (5 mg oxycodone ≈ 10 mg morphine). Not used if eGFR < 15. Available as immediate-release and controlled-release tablets and as a subcutaneous injection. Specify the brand when prescribing to avoid confusion.
- **Fentanyl** — Often used as a transdermal patch; dose conversion guided by your doctor.
- **Methadone** — Used only in specific circumstances (kidney failure, morphine allergy, morphine not tolerated). Requires specialist palliative care supervision due to complex dosing.

When changing between opioids, your doctor will use a conversion guide to ensure the correct dose.

#### Starting opioids — what to expect

Your doctor will discuss:

- **Common side-effects:** constipation (does not diminish — requires ongoing laxatives), nausea (usually settles over a few days), drowsiness and sedation (also usually settles).
- **Uncommon side-effects:** urinary retention, itch, rashes.
- **Signs of opioid toxicity** (too much opioid): pinpoint pupils, muscle jerks, excessive sleepiness, confusion, hallucinations, or increasing agitation. If these occur, contact your care team immediately.
- **Driving:** You should not drive until you have been on a stable opioid dose for at least 14 days, and only if your alertness and concentration are not affected.

### Co-analgesics (add-on medications)

These can be added at any stage alongside regular pain medication:

**Corticosteroids** (e.g., dexamethasone, prednisone):
- Useful when pain relates to swelling (e.g., raised intracranial pressure), nerve compression, or soft tissue infiltration.
- Your doctor will start a higher dose for a short trial (3–5 days) and reduce to the lowest effective dose to minimise side-effects.

**For neuropathic (nerve) pain** — first-line options:
- **Nortriptyline or amitriptyline** (tricyclic antidepressants) — Effective for burning or shooting nerve pain; also helps with sleep. Watch for constipation and low blood pressure.
- **Gabapentin** — Start at 100 mg three times a day; increase gradually. Can be combined with nortriptyline/amitriptyline for added effect. Dose reduced in kidney impairment.
- **Pregabalin** — Start at 75 mg twice a day; increase gradually. Do not use at the same time as gabapentin. When stopping, withdraw slowly over at least 7 days.

**For neuropathic pain** — second-line options:
- **Sodium valproate** or **carbamazepine** — Useful for "shooting" or "electric shock-like" nerve pain. Started at low doses and increased gradually.

**For muscle spasm pain:**
- Benzodiazepines (e.g., diazepam) or baclofen.

**For metastatic bone pain:**
- Bisphosphonates (e.g., zoledronic acid) — used for hypercalcaemia and to reduce risk of fracture in bone metastases. A temporary flare of bone pain can occur after the first dose. Requires specialist advice.

## When to Contact Your Doctor or Care Team

Contact your doctor or palliative care team promptly if:

- Pain is not adequately controlled
- You are using more breakthrough medication than usual (more than 3 extra doses in 24 hours suggests the regular dose needs reviewing)
- You experience signs of opioid toxicity (confusion, excessive sleepiness, muscle jerks, pinpoint pupils)
- You experience a sudden new or severe pain that feels different from usual (may indicate an acute complication)
- You have concerns about medications, side-effects, or driving

Your doctor will seek specialist palliative care advice if pain persists despite treatment, if you need methadone, if opioid toxicity or hyperalgesia is suspected, or if a second syringe driver is needed.
