# MASLD (Fatty Liver)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) — formerly known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) or fatty liver. It applies to patients with persistently raised ALT/AST or a finding of fatty liver on abdominal imaging.

---

## Background

MASLD results from abnormal fat deposition in the liver caused by metabolic dysregulation, particularly insulin resistance. It is very common, affecting more than 25% of the population, and can co-exist with other liver diseases.

- Most common presentation age: 40–60 years, but increasing in younger people
- MASLD can occur at normal body weight with normal liver function tests
- It is a diagnosis of exclusion — there is no specific diagnostic test; it is often found incidentally on abdominal imaging

**Why it matters:** 5–15% of patients develop advanced liver fibrosis, which is the main risk factor for liver failure and liver cancer. Fibrosis progresses slowly but is accelerated by obesity, type 2 diabetes, metabolic syndrome, and alcohol use. However, mortality is mostly from cardiovascular disease, so metabolic risk factor management is the key treatment priority.

---

## Who Should Be Assessed for MASLD

Your doctor will consider MASLD assessment if you have:

- Fatty liver reported on an ultrasound scan, **or**
- Abnormal ALT/AST blood tests (on 2 readings 3 months apart, with no improving trend), with other causes excluded, **and** any of:
  - Type 2 diabetes
  - Overweight or obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² for non-Asians, ≥ 23 kg/m² for Asians)
  - One or more cardiometabolic risk factors (see below)

**Cardiometabolic risk factors include:**
- Increased waist circumference (European/Polynesian: > 102 cm men, > 88 cm women; South Asian: > 90 cm men, > 80 cm women)
- Blood pressure ≥ 130/85 mmHg or on blood pressure medication
- Triglycerides ≥ 1.7 mmol/L
- Low HDL cholesterol (< 1.0 mmol/L men, < 1.3 mmol/L women)
- On cholesterol-lowering medication
- Pre-diabetes (HbA1c 41–49 mmol/mol, fasting glucose 6–6.9 mmol/L, or glucose tolerance test 7.8–11.1 mmol/L)

Other causes of abnormal liver tests are excluded first, including viral hepatitis (hepatitis B and C), autoimmune hepatitis, haemochromatosis, and medication/alcohol causes.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. **Arrange blood tests** — ALT, AST, and platelet count are needed to calculate the FIB-4 score. Diabetes and cholesterol screening may also be done if not already completed.

2. **Calculate the FIB-4 score** — this is a formula using your age, ALT, AST, and platelet count to estimate your risk of liver fibrosis. It guides whether you need a fibroscan.

3. **Arrange a fibroscan if indicated** — a painless, non-invasive scan (an alternative to liver biopsy) that measures the stiffness of the liver to assess the degree of fibrosis. Your doctor refers for a fibroscan if:
   - You are aged 60 or under and FIB-4 ≥ 1.3
   - You are aged over 60 and FIB-4 ≥ 2.0

4. **Determine your fibrosis risk level:**
   - **Minimal risk** — FIB-4 below the threshold for your age (no fibroscan needed initially)
   - **Mild risk** — Fibroscan score < 8 kPa
   - **Moderate to high risk** — Fibroscan score ≥ 8.0 kPa

> Note: Abdominal ultrasound is not routinely used for MASLD diagnosis. Your doctor uses it only in specific circumstances where no other cause for abnormal liver tests has been found.

---

## Management

### Fibrosis risk monitoring

Your doctor will manage ongoing monitoring based on your risk level:

- **Moderate to high risk** (fibroscan ≥ 8.0 kPa) — Referral to a gastroenterology specialist
- **Mild risk** (fibroscan < 8 kPa) — Repeat fibroscan every 5 years; referral to gastroenterology if score rises to ≥ 8 kPa
- **Minimal risk** — FIB-4 recalculated every 2 years; fibroscan arranged if score becomes elevated

### Metabolic risk factor management (all patients)

The most important treatment for MASLD is reducing insulin resistance and addressing metabolic risk factors. This improves cardiovascular risk and reduces fat deposition in the liver, which can normalise liver function and reduce the risk of cirrhosis.

Your doctor will discuss:

- **Diabetes management** — optimising blood glucose control if you have diabetes
- **Medications** — for patients without diabetes, metformin or a glitazone may be considered
- **Weight loss** — if you are overweight, a > 10% reduction in body weight is the goal. This involves a calorie-reduced diet (500–1000 kcal deficit per day) and regular exercise (> 250 minutes per week). Bariatric surgery may be discussed if relevant criteria are met.
- **Exercise** — regular physical activity is beneficial independent of weight loss
- **Smoking cessation**
- **Blood pressure** — target below 130/85 mmHg
- **Cholesterol** — statin therapy if you have high cholesterol

### Avoiding substances that harm the liver

Your doctor will advise:

- **Alcohol cessation** — alcohol worsens MASLD progression for all patients with fatty liver, regardless of how much they drink
- **Reviewing all medications** — including non-prescription medicines, herbal remedies, supplements, and illicit drugs, to identify anything hepatotoxic (harmful to the liver). Your doctor will weigh the benefit versus the liver risk for each one.
- **If you have cirrhosis** — you should check all new medications for hepatotoxicity before starting them

### Ongoing screening

Your doctor will continue to screen for diabetes and other cardiometabolic risk factors at regular appointments.

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will refer you to a gastroenterology specialist if:

- Your liver ultrasound shows serious pathology
- Your fibroscan score is ≥ 8.0 kPa (moderate to high fibrosis risk)
- Your ALT/AST remain abnormal after 6 months of monitoring with no identifiable cause — specialist liver advice will be sought
