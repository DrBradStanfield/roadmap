# Joint Aspiration

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Joint aspiration (also called arthrocentesis) is a procedure where fluid is removed from a joint using a needle and syringe. It is used to diagnose or treat conditions affecting the joint.

---

## Why Joint Aspiration May Be Performed

Your doctor may consider joint aspiration for:

- **Following trauma** — to provide pain relief or to help diagnose a fracture (fat droplets in the fluid can indicate a fracture)
- **Inflammatory conditions and arthritis** — to establish a diagnosis (e.g., gout, pseudogout, or infection), provide pain relief, and rule out joint infection (septic arthritis)

---

## Contraindications — When It Will Not Be Performed

Your doctor will check whether aspiration is safe. The procedure will not be performed if:

- Septic arthritis (joint infection) is strongly suspected — this requires urgent specialist assessment instead
- You have a prosthetic (artificial) joint — orthopaedic assessment is arranged to manage this
- There is a skin abnormality over the joint (e.g., cellulitis, active infection, or psoriasis at the entry site)
- The patient is unable to cooperate with the procedure

Note: Being on blood thinners (anticoagulants) is **not** a contraindication to aspiration.

---

## What to Expect During the Procedure

### Before the Procedure

Your doctor will:

- Explain the procedure and potential risks, and obtain your consent
- Position you so the joint is relaxed and accessible
- Clean the skin thoroughly with an antiseptic solution (such as 70% isopropyl alcohol, povidone-iodine, or chlorhexidine)

### Local Anaesthetic

Your doctor may use a local anaesthetic to numb the area before inserting the aspiration needle. Options include:

- **Lidocaine 1%** injected under the skin at the needle entry point
- **EMLA cream** (topical numbing cream) applied beforehand
- **Ethyl chloride spray** as a topical alternative

You may feel some discomfort when the needle passes through the joint lining (synovium) or touches cartilage.

### During the Procedure

- A needle is inserted into the joint and fluid is withdrawn using a syringe
- "Milking" the joint (gentle pressure around the joint) may help move fluid toward the needle
- If a large volume of fluid is present, the syringe may be changed during the procedure while the needle remains in place

### After the Procedure

- A dressing is applied and you will rest for at least 15 minutes
- A compression bandage (e.g., Tubigrip) may be applied
- Your doctor will document the procedure and arrange follow-up for results

---

## Risks of the Procedure

Risks are rare but include:

- Infection introduced into the joint
- Bleeding into the joint (more likely if you have a bleeding disorder or are taking anticoagulants)
- Damage to surrounding structures

---

## How Results Are Interpreted

Your doctor will review the appearance and laboratory analysis of the fluid:

### Fluid Appearance

| Finding | Suggests |
|---|---|
| Clear or pale yellow, non-turbid | Normal or non-inflammatory |
| Yellow, turbid | Inflammatory (e.g., gout, rheumatoid arthritis) |
| Yellow, cloudy or purulent | Possible infection (septic arthritis) |
| Blood with fat droplets on top | Fracture |

### Cell Count

| White cell count (per mm³) | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Less than 2,000 | Normal or non-inflammatory |
| 2,000 – 10,000 | Inflammatory |
| 10,000 – 50,000 | Strongly inflammatory |
| More than 50,000 | Suspicious for infection |

A negative gram stain or culture does **not** rule out septic arthritis.

### Crystal Analysis

Synovial fluid can be examined under polarised light microscopy for crystals:

- **Gout** — strongly negative birefringent, needle-shaped crystals (monosodium urate)
- **Pseudogout** — weakly positive birefringent, rectangular or rhomboid-shaped crystals (calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate)
