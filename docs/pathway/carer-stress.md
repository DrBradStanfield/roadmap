# Carer Stress

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers recognising and managing stress in people caring for an older person, and the role of general practice in preventing carer burnout and elder abuse.

---

## Background

Caring for an older person is demanding and complex:

- Caring is often in addition to other roles and responsibilities
- The carer may take on responsibility out of a positive relationship or sense of obligation, yet may feel uncertain, resentful, angry, or afraid
- Negative feelings are intensified when the carer feels trapped, or when the relationship is difficult or distant
- If there is a history of family violence, negative feelings may be especially strong
- Caring over an extended period can be physically and emotionally exhausting
- Caring tasks are often unscheduled, irregular, and unpredictable — making it difficult for carers to manage their own lives and meet their own needs
- A belief that families must cope alone can make it hard to acknowledge problems or accept help
- Carer stress is often denied, or if acknowledged, may feel like personal failure

Caring for the carer is one of the primary steps in preventing elder abuse.

---

## Assessment

### Emotional Stress Indicators

- Loss of interest and motivation
- Anxiety and sudden mood changes
- Anger at self as well as the older person being cared for
- Feelings of isolation
- Feelings of guilt
- Low self-esteem

### Physical Stress Indicators

- Chronic exhaustion and lethargy
- Weight loss or gain
- Frequent colds or infections
- Backache or headache

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following steps:

1. **Acknowledge stress as a factor** — consider carer stress when making clinical assessments, and monitor stress levels over time where appropriate

2. **Discuss the realities of caring** — including dependency, practical demands, and personal adjustments the carer is managing

3. **Identify and manage stress** — support the carer to recognise their stress and connect with education and support resources

4. **Provide information on managing difficult behaviour** — particularly relevant where the older person has dementia or other conditions affecting behaviour

5. **Address the possibility of role reversal** — the older person may also be placing stress on the carer. Contributing factors include difficulty accepting reliance on the carer, psychiatric illness or dementia, or a disorder resulting in aggression or poor judgement

6. **Offer assistance** — options include day care centres, respite care, sitter services, and information about residential care alternatives

7. **Re-offer services if refused** — the carer may not recognise their own stress; the older person's refusal of help may itself add to carer stress. Always offer again in a few weeks

8. **Arrange respite care** — your doctor will arrange this based on urgency:
   - If there is an acute need, urgent respite care can be arranged through your doctor
   - If non-acute, a formal older person's health assessment can be requested
