# Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the use of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs) — specifically dabigatran and rivaroxaban — as alternatives to warfarin for anticoagulation. Prescribing decisions are made by your doctor based on your individual clinical situation.

---

## When to Seek Emergency Care

If you experience serious or life-threatening bleeding while on a DOAC:

- **Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately.**
- Dabigatran has a specific reversal agent (idarucizumab/Praxbind) available in hospital.
- Rivaroxaban can be partially reversed with prothrombin complex concentrate in hospital — seek haematology advice.

**Signs of abnormal bleeding to report to your doctor:**
- Prolonged nosebleed
- Red or dark urine, or red/black bowel motions
- Coughing up blood
- Excessive or unusually prolonged bleeding from cuts
- Heavier than usual menstrual periods
- Unexplained bruising, or bruising that enlarges without cause
- Severe headache or dizziness (possible intracranial bleed)

---

## About DOACs

DOACs are oral anticoagulants ("blood thinners") used to prevent or treat blood clots. There are two types available in New Zealand:

- **Direct thrombin inhibitors:** dabigatran (Pradaxa)
- **Factor Xa inhibitors:** rivaroxaban (Xarelto)

They are used as alternatives to warfarin (an indirect anticoagulant that inhibits vitamin K-dependent clotting factors) in most conditions.

---

## Indications (When DOACs Are Used)

Your doctor may consider a DOAC for:

- **Atrial fibrillation (AF)** — to prevent stroke, systemic embolism, and vascular mortality
- **Treatment of DVT or pulmonary embolism (PE)** — note: dabigatran requires initial treatment with enoxaparin injections for the first 5 days; rivaroxaban does not
- **Prevention of recurrent DVT or PE**
- **Short-term VTE prevention** after major orthopaedic surgery (e.g. hip or knee replacement)

---

## Who Should Not Take DOACs

Your doctor will not prescribe dabigatran or rivaroxaban if you have:

- **Mechanical heart valves** — DOACs are not safe; warfarin is required
- **Significant liver disease** with coagulopathy (e.g. moderate or severe hepatic impairment, cirrhosis with oesophageal varices)
- **Severe kidney impairment** — creatinine clearance below 30 mL/min for dabigatran, or below 15 mL/min for rivaroxaban
- **High risk of serious bleeding** that outweighs the benefit of anticoagulation
- **Pregnancy or breastfeeding** — oral anticoagulants are contraindicated; long-term injections (LMWH) are used instead

**Use with caution** if moderate kidney impairment (creatinine clearance 15–50 mL/min).

---

## Dabigatran

### Dosing

Your doctor will choose a dose based on your indication, age, kidney function, and bleeding risk:

**For acute DVT or PE:**
- Requires 5 days of enoxaparin injections first
- After 5 days: usually 150 mg twice a day
- Reduced dose (110 mg twice a day) if aged ≥80, aged 75–80 with renal impairment and high bleeding risk, or other specific risk factors

**For atrial fibrillation or prevention of recurrent DVT/PE:**
- Usually 150 mg twice a day
- Reduced to 110 mg twice a day if aged ≥80, or specific renal/bleeding risk factors apply

### Key Points for Dabigatran

- Must be taken **twice daily** — consistent compliance is essential
- More gastrointestinal side-effects than warfarin, particularly **dyspepsia** (indigestion)
- **Fewer intracranial bleeds** but **more gastrointestinal bleeds** than warfarin overall
- Has a specific antidote: idarucizumab (Praxbind), available in hospital for serious bleeding or urgent surgery
- Do not take with: rifampicin, carbamazepine, St John's wort (reduce levels); discuss amiodarone, clarithromycin, verapamil, ketoconazole with your doctor (increase levels)
- Aspirin is usually stopped when starting dabigatran — discuss with your doctor if you have ischaemic heart disease or a stent

### Kidney Function Monitoring on Dabigatran

Your doctor will check your kidney function (creatinine clearance using the Cockcroft-Gault equation) before starting, then every 6–12 months. Dabigatran is stopped if kidney function deteriorates significantly or falls below 30 mL/min.

---

## Rivaroxaban

### Dosing

**For acute VTE treatment (DVT or PE):**
- 15 mg twice a day for the first 3 weeks
- Then 20 mg once a day
- **Take with food** — absorption is significantly reduced on an empty stomach

**For atrial fibrillation or prevention of recurrent DVT/PE:**
- Usually 20 mg once a day (with food)

No bridging with enoxaparin is needed — rivaroxaban can be started immediately.

### Key Points for Rivaroxaban

- Once-daily dosing (after initial 3-week phase for acute VTE) is convenient, but the short half-life (7 hours) means **missed doses matter** — inadequate anticoagulation can result
- **Fewer intracranial bleeds** but **more gastrointestinal bleeds** than warfarin
- Can cause **menorrhagia** (heavier periods) — more so than dabigatran
- **No direct antidote** — if life-threatening bleeding occurs, hospital treatment with prothrombin complex concentrate may be used; haematology advice is needed
- Cannot be used if creatinine clearance <15 mL/min
- Do not take with HIV protease inhibitors or ketoconazole

### Kidney Function Monitoring on Rivaroxaban

Your doctor will check creatinine clearance before starting, then every 6 months. Rivaroxaban is stopped if kidney function falls significantly or drops below 30 mL/min.

---

## Comparison: Warfarin vs Dabigatran vs Rivaroxaban

| Feature | Warfarin | Dabigatran | Rivaroxaban |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dosing frequency | Once daily | Twice daily | Twice daily (3 weeks for acute VTE), then once daily |
| Food restrictions | Consistent vitamin K intake | None | Take with food |
| Monitoring | INR (regular blood tests) | Creatinine/LFTs every 3–12 months | Creatinine/LFTs every 6 months |
| Drug interactions | Many | Fewer | Fewer |
| Intracranial bleeding | Higher than DOACs | Lower than warfarin | Lower than warfarin |
| GI bleeding | Lower than DOACs | Higher than warfarin | Higher than warfarin |
| Antidote | Vitamin K, FFP, Prothrombinex | Idarucizumab (specific) | No direct antidote |
| Mechanical valves | Yes | No | No |

## Drug Interactions to Be Aware Of

Both DOACs interact with drugs that affect bleeding — tell your doctor and pharmacist about all medications including:
- Aspirin, clopidogrel (usually stopped when starting a DOAC unless specifically required)
- NSAIDs (ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac) — increase bleeding risk
- SSRIs and SNRIs (antidepressants)

Rivaroxaban specifically interacts with drugs affecting CYP3A enzyme and P-glycoprotein — your doctor will review your full medication list.

---

## Switching Between Anticoagulants

Your doctor manages all transitions carefully to avoid periods of either under-anticoagulation (clot risk) or over-anticoagulation (bleeding risk):

- **Warfarin → DOAC:** warfarin is stopped and the DOAC is started once INR falls below 2.0. Starting a DOAC when INR >2.0 carries high bleeding risk.
- **Enoxaparin → dabigatran:** dabigatran is started 0–2 hours before the next enoxaparin dose would have been due; they are never given simultaneously.
- **Enoxaparin → rivaroxaban:** same timing principle.

---

## Medic Alert

Your doctor may recommend wearing a medic alert bracelet while on anticoagulants, so emergency staff are aware in the event of an accident.
