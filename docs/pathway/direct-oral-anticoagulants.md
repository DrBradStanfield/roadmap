# Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: November 2022.*

This pathway covers the use of direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs), dabigatran and rivaroxaban, as alternatives to warfarin for anticoagulation.

> Prescribing decisions are made by your doctor based on your individual clinical situation. Always discuss your treatment with your doctor.

---

## When to Seek Emergency Care

If you have serious or life-threatening bleeding while on a DOAC:

- **Call 111 or go to the emergency department immediately.**
- Signs of serious bleeding include coughing up blood, a bad headache, or dizziness.
- Dabigatran has a specific reversal agent, idarucizumab (Praxbind). It is given into a vein in the emergency department.
- Rivaroxaban has no direct antidote. The hospital may give prothrombin complex concentrate, although data on its use for this are limited.

**Signs of abnormal bleeding to report to your doctor:**
- Prolonged nosebleed
- Red or dark urine, or red or black bowel motions
- Excessive bleeding, or bleeding that lasts longer than usual
- Heavier than usual menstrual periods
- Unexplained bruising, or bruising that gets bigger without cause

---

## About DOACs

DOACs are oral anticoagulants ("blood thinners"). DOACs include two types:

- **Direct thrombin inhibitors:** for example, dabigatran
- **Factor Xa inhibitors:** for example, rivaroxaban

In most conditions they are used as an alternative to warfarin. Warfarin is an indirect anticoagulant: it blocks the making of vitamin K-dependent clotting factors.

---

## Indications (When DOACs Are Used)

Your doctor may consider a DOAC for:

- **Atrial fibrillation (AF)**, to prevent stroke, systemic embolism, and vascular mortality
- **Treatment of DVT or pulmonary embolism (PE).** Dabigatran needs initial treatment with enoxaparin injections for 5 days. Rivaroxaban does not.
- **Prevention of recurrent DVT or PE**
- **Short-term VTE prevention** after major orthopaedic surgery

---

## Who Should Not Take DOACs

Dabigatran and rivaroxaban are not used if you have:

- **Mechanical heart valves.** DOACs are not recommended. Warfarin is suitable for all indications.
- **Significant liver disease** with coagulopathy (for example, moderate or severe hepatic impairment) or a higher bleeding risk (for example, cirrhosis with oesophageal varices)
- **Marked kidney impairment:** creatinine clearance less than 30 mL/min for dabigatran, or less than 15 mL/min for rivaroxaban (or dialysis)
- **Active bleeding, or a risk of serious bleeding** that outweighs the benefit of anticoagulation. Examples include a recent stomach or gut ulcer, a cancer with high bleeding risk, oesophageal varices, recent brain, spine or eye surgery, a recent bleed in the brain, a vascular aneurysm, or a low platelet count.
- **Pregnancy or breastfeeding.** In pregnancy, oral anticoagulants are not used. Long-term treatment with injections (LMWH) is needed instead.

**Use with caution** if you have moderate kidney impairment (creatinine clearance 15 to 50 mL/min).

Before starting, your doctor may arrange blood tests if they have not been done: full blood count, clotting tests (INR and APTT), creatinine, sodium, potassium, and liver function. A pregnancy test may be done if you could become pregnant.

## Dabigatran

### Dosing

Your doctor will choose a dose based on your indication, age, kidney function, and bleeding risk.

**For acute DVT or PE:**
- Enoxaparin injections for 5 days first. Enoxaparin and dabigatran are not started at the same time.
- After 5 days: usually 150 mg twice a day, or 110 mg twice a day if you are aged 80 years or older
- Your doctor may consider 110 mg twice a day if you are aged between 75 and 80 years, or have moderate kidney impairment (creatinine clearance 30 to 50 mL/min), and your bleeding risk is high and your clot risk is low. They may also consider it if you have other bleeding risk factors, or a very low body weight (for example, under 50 kg; data are limited).

**For atrial fibrillation or prevention of recurrent DVT/PE:**
- Usually 150 mg twice a day
- Reduced to 110 mg twice a day if you are aged 80 years or older, or if any of the other reasons above apply

### Key Points for Dabigatran

- Must be taken **twice daily**. Taking every dose helps avoid inadequate anticoagulation.
- More gastrointestinal side-effects, particularly **dyspepsia** (indigestion). Warfarin may be preferable if dyspepsia is severe.
- Similar overall bleeding risk to warfarin, but **fewer intracranial bleeds** and **more gastrointestinal bleeds**
- Has a specific antidote, idarucizumab (Praxbind), for an acute bleed or urgent surgery
- Some medicines lower dabigatran levels, for example rifampicin, carbamazepine, and St John's wort. Others raise them, for example amiodarone, clarithromycin, verapamil, ketoconazole, and ciclosporin. Tell your doctor about all your medicines.
- Aspirin is usually stopped when dabigatran is started, because the combination increases bleeding risk. If you have ischaemic heart disease, especially after coronary surgery or a stent, both may be needed. Your doctor may seek specialist advice.

### Kidney Function Monitoring on Dabigatran

Your doctor will check your kidney function (creatinine clearance using the Cockcroft-Gault equation) before starting, then every 6 to 12 months during long-term treatment. Some people need more frequent checks. Dabigatran is stopped if kidney function falls significantly or creatinine clearance is less than 30 mL/min. Your doctor may then consider another anticoagulant, such as warfarin or enoxaparin.

## Rivaroxaban

### Dosing

**For acute VTE treatment (DVT or PE):**
- 15 mg twice a day for the first 3 weeks
- Then 20 mg once a day
- **Take with food.** On an empty stomach, only about 66% of the dose is absorbed, compared with over 99% with a meal.

For other uses, rivaroxaban is taken once a day.

No bridging with enoxaparin is needed. Rivaroxaban can be started on its own at the time of diagnosis.

### Key Points for Rivaroxaban

- Once-daily dosing (after the first 3 weeks for acute VTE) is convenient. But the half-life is short (7 hours, compared with 36 hours for warfarin), so **missed doses matter**. They can leave you inadequately anticoagulated.
- Similar overall bleeding risk to warfarin, but **fewer intracranial bleeds** and **more gastrointestinal bleeds**
- Can cause **menorrhagia** (heavier periods). The risk is lower with dabigatran.
- **No direct antidote.** For life-threatening bleeding, the hospital may use prothrombin complex concentrate, with haematology advice.
- Cannot be used if creatinine clearance is less than 15 mL/min, or on dialysis
- Not used with HIV protease inhibitors or ketoconazole

### Kidney Function Monitoring on Rivaroxaban

Your doctor will check creatinine clearance before starting, then every 6 to 12 months during long-term treatment. Some people need more frequent checks. Rivaroxaban is stopped if kidney function falls significantly or creatinine clearance is less than 30 mL/min. Your doctor may then consider another anticoagulant, such as warfarin or enoxaparin.

## Comparison: Warfarin vs Dabigatran vs Rivaroxaban

| Feature | Warfarin | Dabigatran | Rivaroxaban |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dosing frequency | Once daily | Twice daily | Twice daily (3 weeks for acute VTE), then once daily |
| Dietary restrictions | Consistent vitamin K intake | None | None (take with food) |
| Monitoring | INR (regular blood tests) | Creatinine and liver tests every 3 to 12 months | Creatinine and liver tests every 6 months |
| Drug interactions | Many | Fewer | Fewer |
| Intracranial bleeding | Higher than DOACs | Lower than warfarin | Lower than warfarin |
| GI bleeding | Lower than DOACs | Higher than warfarin | Higher than warfarin |
| Antidote | Vitamin K, fresh frozen plasma, Prothrombinex-VF | Idarucizumab (specific) | No direct antidote |
| Mechanical valves | Yes | No | No |

## Drug Interactions to Be Aware Of

Take care combining either DOAC with drugs that affect bleeding. Tell your doctor and pharmacist about all your medicines, including:
- Aspirin and clopidogrel. Aspirin is usually stopped when starting anticoagulation, unless your doctor advises otherwise.
- NSAIDs (anti-inflammatory painkillers), which may increase the risk or severity of a bleed. If you use them, watch for signs of abnormal bleeding.
- SSRIs (a group of antidepressants)

Your doctor will also check your medicines for interactions through the CYP3A enzyme and P-glycoprotein. With rivaroxaban, they may seek haematology advice on dosing if you take drugs that affect these.

---

## Switching Between Anticoagulants

Your doctor will time any switch carefully:

- **Warfarin to a DOAC:** warfarin is stopped, and the DOAC is started once INR is less than 2.0. Starting a DOAC when INR is over 2.0 carries a high risk of bleeding.
- **Enoxaparin to dabigatran:** dabigatran is started 0 to 2 hours before the next enoxaparin dose would have been due. They are never given at the same time.
- **Enoxaparin to rivaroxaban:** the same timing applies.

---

## Medic Alert

Your doctor may recommend wearing a medic alert bracelet while on anticoagulants.
