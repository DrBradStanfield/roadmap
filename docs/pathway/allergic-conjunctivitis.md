# Allergic Conjunctivitis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of allergic conjunctivitis — a common condition characterised by bilateral itchy, red, watery eyes, often linked to allergies or hay fever.

---

## Red Flags — See Your Doctor Urgently

See your doctor promptly if you have:

- Contact lenses (these patients need different management — do not self-treat)
- Decreased or blurred vision
- Corneal changes or eye pain

Children and adolescents with chronic symptoms also need careful monitoring.

---

## Background

Approximately 15–20% of the world population is affected by some form of allergy, and eye symptoms occur in 40–60% of allergic patients.

Allergic conjunctivitis usually affects both eyes and develops suddenly. The hallmark symptom is **itch**. It can be:

- **Seasonal** — triggered by outdoor allergens such as grass and pollens
- **Perennial** (year-round) — triggered by indoor allergens including dust mites, moulds, and animal fur

The itch is usually bilateral but can be asymmetric, and is typically most intense at the inner corner of the eye near the nose.

---

## What Your Doctor Will Assess

Your doctor will take a history asking about:

- Bilateral redness and itching
- Eye rubbing, watery discharge, and tearing
- Association with atopic conditions (eczema, asthma) or hay fever
- Whether symptoms are intermittent or seasonal
- Any reduction in vision (more common with chronic symptoms and mucous discharge)

Your doctor will examine:

- Conjunctival injection (redness), more obvious on the inside of the eyelids
- Visual acuity (measured with an eye chart)
- Signs of chronic or severe disease: large red bumps (papillae) visible when the upper lid is everted, eczema around the lids, or corneal changes

They will classify the condition as:

- **Acute allergic response** — red, swollen conjunctiva; lids may be puffy; vision and cornea normal
- **Chronic severe allergic disease** — papillae under the upper lid; possible eczema around lids; vision may be reduced if there is a corneal epithelial defect (shield ulcer)

---

## Management

Treatment is stepped according to severity:

### Important: Avoid Topical Steroids

Topical steroid eye drops — although effective for quick symptom relief — are associated with cataract formation, glaucoma, and corneal infections. Your doctor will advise against their use for allergic conjunctivitis.

### Step 1 — Address the Cause

Your doctor will recommend removing or minimising exposure to likely triggers (e.g., removing pets from the bedroom, using allergen-proof bedding to reduce dust mite exposure).

### Step 2 — Hay Fever Control

If you have associated nasal symptoms, nasal steroid sprays and oral antihistamines can help. Note that oral antihistamines alone are not very effective for eye symptoms, and can cause dry eyes, dry mouth, and visual blurring. They should be used with caution if you have narrow-angle glaucoma risk.

### Step 3 — Mild or Intermittent Symptoms

- **Cold compresses** — a cold flannel applied to closed eyes often provides effective relief
- **Lubricant eye drops** — can flush allergens from the tear film. Avoid drops containing decongestants or preservatives, as these can worsen symptoms with prolonged use

### Step 4 — Moderate to Severe Symptoms

Your doctor may prescribe a **mast cell stabiliser** eye drop:

- Lodoxamide 0.1% or cromoglicate sodium (eye drops)
- Must be used **4 times daily for at least 2 weeks** before a reliable response is seen
- Can be combined intermittently with an oral antihistamine if needed

### Step 5 — Significant or Unresponsive Symptoms

A **combined antihistamine and mast cell stabiliser** drop may be more effective:

- **Olopatadine (Patanol)** — used twice daily; takes approximately 2 weeks to reach full effect

### Contact Lens Wearers

Your doctor will **not** prescribe eye drops for contact lens wearers with allergic conjunctivitis — these patients need management by their optometrist. If you wear contact lenses and develop worsening redness, pain, or irritation, stop wearing the lenses immediately and see your optometrist or doctor, as bacterial keratitis (corneal infection) must be excluded.

### Children and Adolescents

Your doctor will monitor for any reduction in visual acuity. For children with chronic irritation, blinking, normal vision, and mild redness, olopatadine (Patanol) twice daily is typically the first-line treatment. Children who do not respond, or who have papillae visible under the upper lid, will be referred for ophthalmology assessment.

---

## When Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will refer you if:

- **Decreased vision or abnormal cornea** — urgent ophthalmology assessment
- **Child with normal vision who has not responded to olopatadine**, or who has papillae under the upper lid — non-urgent ophthalmology assessment
- **Contact lens wearers with persistent redness or irritation** — referral to the optometrist who prescribed their lenses
- **Any ongoing concerns** — private optometry assessment (note: this involves out-of-pocket cost, and further referral to an ophthalmologist may be needed)
