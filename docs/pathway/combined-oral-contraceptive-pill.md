# Combined Oral Contraceptive Pill

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment, prescribing, and ongoing management of the combined oral contraceptive pill (COC). Decisions about which pill is right for you — and whether you're suitable for it — should be made with your doctor.

---

## Background

The COC works by preventing the ovaries from releasing eggs, thinning the endometrium, and thickening cervical mucus to block sperm. With typical use (accounting for missed pills), the pregnancy rate is approximately 8% per year.

The estrogen component is associated with rare but serious risks — particularly venous thromboembolism (VTE) and cardiovascular events. These risks vary depending on the type of progestogen used. In absolute terms, VTE risk on the COC (7–12 cases per 10,000 per year) is higher than in non-pregnant, non-contraceptive users (2 per 10,000), but lower than in pregnancy (29 per 10,000) or immediately postpartum (300–400 per 10,000).

Non-contraceptive benefits of the COC include:

- Reduced risk of endometrial, ovarian, and colorectal cancer (protective effect persists for years after stopping)
- Improved acne, hirsutism, and PCOS symptoms
- Management of dysmenorrhoea, heavy menstrual bleeding, premenstrual syndrome, and endometriosis pain
- Cycle control and relief of perimenopausal symptoms

**Note:** Long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs — intrauterine devices and the contraceptive implant) are recommended as first-line contraception in New Zealand due to their superior efficacy and safety. Your doctor will discuss all options with you.

## Contraindications

Before prescribing the COC, your doctor will screen for conditions that make it unsafe.

### Absolute Contraindications (UKMEC 4 — COC should not be used)

- Age 35 or older and smoking 15 or more cigarettes per day
- Less than 6 weeks postpartum if breastfeeding, or less than 3 weeks postpartum if not breastfeeding but with other VTE risk factors
- Blood pressure 160/100 mmHg or higher (clinic) or 150/95 mmHg or higher (home)
- Migraine with aura at any time in the last 5 years
- History of venous thromboembolism (DVT or PE), including current VTE on anticoagulants
- Major surgery, or multiple VTE risk factors (e.g., cancer, high BMI, thrombotic or inflammatory disorders)
- Cardiovascular disease: TIA, stroke (ischaemic or haemorrhagic), ischaemic heart disease (including angina), intermittent claudication, hypertensive retinopathy
- Atrial fibrillation
- Current kidney disease (nephrotic syndrome, dialysis)
- Known thrombogenic mutation
- Complicated valvular or congenital heart disease
- Cardiomyopathy with impaired cardiac function
- Severe decompensated liver cirrhosis or liver tumour (hepatocellular adenoma or carcinoma)
- Positive antiphospholipid antibodies
- Currently being treated for breast cancer

### Relative Contraindications (UKMEC 3 — risks usually outweigh benefits; specialist judgement required)

- 0–3 weeks postpartum, not breastfeeding, no other VTE risk factors (UKMEC 4 if breastfeeding or other VTE risk factors present)
- 3–6 weeks postpartum with other VTE risk factors
- Age 35 or older and smoking fewer than 15 cigarettes per day, or stopped smoking less than 1 year ago
- BMI 35 or more
- Migraine with aura 5 or more years ago, or migraine without aura that develops while on the COC
- Multiple cardiovascular risk factors (e.g., smoking, diabetes, hypertension, obesity, high cholesterol)
- Diabetes with microvascular complications or vascular disease
- Known hypertension (even if well controlled), or clinic BP 140–159/90–99 mmHg, or home BP 135–149/85–94 mmHg
- Family history of VTE in a first-degree relative
- Completed treatment for breast cancer, undiagnosed breast mass, or carrier of known breast cancer gene mutations
- Immobility (e.g., wheelchair use, multiple sclerosis with prolonged immobility)
- Medically treated or symptomatic gallbladder disease, or past cholestasis related to the COC
- Acute or flare of viral hepatitis
- Complicated organ transplant (graft failure, rejection, cardiac allograft vasculopathy)

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a targeted history to check for contraindications, relevant medical conditions (such as acne, PCOS, endometriosis, menstrual disorders, or PMS that the COC might help), and any factors that could affect absorption or effectiveness.

**Conditions that may reduce COC effectiveness:**

- Vomiting or severe diarrhoea
- Bariatric surgery or small bowel resection
- Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)

**Medications your doctor will review:**

- **Liver enzyme-inducing drugs** reduce COC effectiveness. These include certain anti-seizure medications (carbamazepine, phenytoin, oxcarbazepine, phenobarbital, primidone, rufinamide, eslicarbazepine, topiramate), antibiotics (rifampicin, rifabutin), antiretrovirals (nevirapine, nelfinavir, tipranavir/ritonavir, efavirenz, etravirine), St John's wort, bosentan, modafinil, aprepitant, and sugammadex. The enzyme-inducing effect persists for 28 days after stopping.
- **Lamotrigine:** The COC increases lamotrigine metabolism, raising seizure risk during active pill days and causing a rise in lamotrigine concentration during the pill-free interval. Your doctor may advise alternative contraception.
- **Griseofulvin:** May interact — your doctor will advise on pill dose or alternative contraception.
- **Other medications** that the COC may affect include antihypertensives, anti-diabetics, thyroxine, tacrolimus, and some anxiolytics and hypnotics.
- Non-liver-enzyme-inducing antibiotics (e.g., penicillins, cephalosporins, tetracyclines) do **not** reduce COC effectiveness.

**Physical examination** will include blood pressure measurement and BMI calculation.

- BP below 140/90 mmHg (clinic) or 135/85 mmHg (home) is acceptable if no known hypertension.
- COC is generally not recommended if the patient has known hypertension (even if controlled) or clinic BP 140–159/90–99 mmHg.
- COC is contraindicated at BP 160/100 mmHg or higher (clinic) or 150/95 mmHg or higher (home).

Cervical screening should be up to date. STI screening may be offered if at increased risk. Pelvic examination is not routinely required before starting the COC.

## Management

Management decisions — including which pill to start and how — rest with your doctor. The information below explains what your doctor may consider.

### Pill Selection

Your doctor may consider:

- Pills containing 35 micrograms or less of ethinylestradiol (EE) with **levonorgestrel** or **norethisterone** as a reasonable first-line choice — these have the lowest VTE risk and are fully funded in New Zealand.
- Using the lowest EE dose that provides good cycle control. Pills with 20 micrograms EE may have fewer side-effects but carry a higher risk of breakthrough bleeding.
- For acne: the COC can improve acne; some formulations (e.g., containing **drospirenone**) may be preferred.
- For PCOS: the COC manages acne, hirsutism, and menstrual irregularities.
- For heavy menstrual bleeding: a levonorgestrel intrauterine system (LNG-IUS) may be more appropriate.
- For lamotrigine users: continuous COC-taking with no pill-free interval, with dose adjustment guided by the prescribing specialist.

### Starting the COC

Your doctor will advise on the right starting method:

**Traditional start:** Begin with an active pill on day 1–5 of the menstrual cycle. Contraception is effective immediately.

**Quick start:** Can be used at any point in the cycle (e.g., irregular cycles, infrequent periods, or when prompt cover is needed). Pregnancy must be reasonably excluded first. Requires use of condoms for the first 7 days, and a follow-up pregnancy test at least 21 days after last unprotected intercourse.

**After pregnancy:**
- After miscarriage, abortion, or ectopic pregnancy: if starting within 5 days, the COC can be started at any time for immediate effect.
- After childbirth: if breastfeeding or with other VTE risk factors, wait at least 6 weeks. If no VTE risk factors and not breastfeeding, wait at least 21 days.

### Guidance for Ongoing Use

Your doctor will advise on:

- **Late pills** (less than 24 hours late): take immediately; no extra precautions needed.
- **Missed pills** (more than 24 hours late): avoid intercourse or use condoms; see NZ Formulary missed pill guidance. Consider emergency contraception if unprotected sex has occurred.
- **Vomiting within 2 hours of taking a pill:** take another pill as soon as possible.
- **Vomiting or diarrhoea lasting 24 hours or more:** treat each affected dose as missed.
- **Continuous/tricycling use:** taking active pills continuously (e.g., for 3 months, or up to 12 months) is considered safe. This reduces the risk of unintended pregnancy and hormone-free interval side-effects. Unpredictable breakthrough bleeding can occur — if bleeding occurs on 4 consecutive days, a 4-day pill-free break may be taken.
- **Temporary VTE risk increases:** stop the COC at least 4 weeks before major surgery or extended immobility. Seek advice before travelling to high altitude (above 4,500 m) for more than 1 week.
- **Condoms:** the COC does not protect against sexually transmitted infections (STIs). Condoms are recommended for STI prevention.
- **Grapefruit, limes, and pomelos** can worsen COC side-effects.

### Side-Effect Management

Your doctor may consider the following if you experience side-effects. Most side-effects (other than melasma) improve within the first 3–4 months.

| Side-effect | Your doctor may consider |
|---|---|
| Acne | Changing to a different pill formulation |
| Breakthrough bleeding | Increasing EE dose; changing progestogen to norethisterone or drospirenone; tricycling or continuous use |
| Breast tenderness | Reducing EE or progestogen dose; changing progestogen type; switching to progestogen-only contraception |
| Headaches | Reducing EE dose; switching to progestogen-only; continuous use; low-dose estradiol patch during pill-free week |
| Melasma | Switching to progestogen-only or non-hormonal method; sun protection |
| Libido or mood changes | Changing to levonorgestrel or drospirenone; switching to progestogen-only or copper IUD |
| Nausea | Reducing EE dose; taking pills at night; switching to progestogen-only |
| Bloating or fluid retention | Reducing EE dose; switching progestogen to drospirenone |
| Dysmenorrhoea | Using COC continuously to stop periods; adjusting hormone doses |
| Weight concerns | Evidence does not support weight gain on the COC. Drospirenone-containing pills may be associated with small weight loss |

Seek medical advice promptly if you experience:
- New or worsening headaches or migraine at any time while on the pill
- Persistent or new irregular bleeding after 3 months on the pill

### When to Stop the COC

Your doctor may advise stopping the COC if:

- You wish to become pregnant (fertility returns immediately after stopping)
- You become pregnant
- You are aged 35 or older and smoke
- You are older than 50 years
- You develop a new contraindication or caution

If stopping for reasons other than pregnancy or menopause, your doctor will offer alternative contraception if needed.

### Follow-up

Your doctor will usually review you after 3 months (earlier for some patients), then annually. Follow-up will include blood pressure, BMI, review of any new medications or medical conditions, side-effects, and satisfaction with the method.
