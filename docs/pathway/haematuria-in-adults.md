# Haematuria in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and initial management of blood in the urine (haematuria) in adults.

---

## Background

Haematuria means blood in the urine. It can be:

- **Macroscopic (visible)** — blood is visible to the naked eye
- **Microscopic (non-visible)** — blood detected only on dipstick urinalysis or urine microscopy (defined as > 10 × 10⁶/L red blood cells)

Key points to understand:

- Haematuria with large amounts of protein in the urine, especially in younger patients, is more likely to have a kidney (glomerular) origin
- The risk of an underlying malignancy increases with age, smoking history, and with macroscopic haematuria
- Persistent or macroscopic haematuria can be associated with significant urological disease (such as renal tract malignancy or kidney stones) or significant nephrological disease (such as acute glomerular disease)
- The most common cause of microscopic haematuria is idiopathic (no cause found), though renal parenchymal disease is an important cause

---

## When to Seek Urgent Care

Your doctor will assess urgency. Seek prompt medical review if you notice blood in your urine — particularly if:

- The blood is visible (macroscopic haematuria), especially if painless
- You have flank pain or fever
- You have urinary retention (unable to pass urine)
- You have a history of significant weight loss or other cancer symptoms

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Urinary symptoms such as frequency, burning, or pain on urination (which suggest infection)
- Flank pain or fever (suggesting kidney stones or kidney infection)
- Whether haematuria is associated with pain — painless haematuria raises more concern for malignancy
- Association with activities such as menstruation or vigorous exercise
- In men: blood at the start of urination only is often prostatic in origin; your doctor will ask about urinary flow, waking at night to urinate, and impact on daily life
- Smoking history
- Previous urological investigations or operations
- Family history of kidney disease
- Occupational exposure to chemicals such as benzene derivatives, aromatic amines, textile dyes, rubber manufacturing, paints, solvents, or diesel exhaust fumes — these are risk factors for urothelial (bladder/urinary tract) cancer

Your doctor will examine you, including:

- Blood pressure, weight, checking for anaemia, swelling, and fever
- Abdominal examination: flank tenderness, bladder enlargement, rectal examination in men to assess the prostate
- Pelvic examination in women if indicated (urethral or vaginal prolapse)

---

## Tests Your Doctor May Arrange

- **Midstream urine (MSU):** for dipstick urinalysis, microscopy, and culture
- **Urine microscopy (cell count):** to confirm and quantify red blood cells
- **Urine culture:** if there are symptoms of urinary tract infection (UTI), or if you are male, pregnant, have a urinary tract abnormality, recurrent UTIs, or other risk factors
- **Urine albumin:creatinine ratio (uACR) and serum creatinine:** if a kidney (nephrological) cause is suspected
- **Urine casts:** if a glomerular cause is suspected
- **Repeat MSU:** microscopic haematuria is confirmed when two samples taken at least 2 weeks apart both show elevated red blood cells without evidence of infection
- **Ultrasound of the urinary tract:** to look for structural abnormalities, stones, or masses
- **Urology assessment (cystoscopy):** a flexible camera examination of the bladder — your doctor may refer you for this, particularly if you have macroscopic haematuria or are aged over 40 with confirmed microscopic haematuria

---

## Management

### If a UTI Is Found

Your doctor will treat this with antibiotics. After treatment:

- If you had microscopic haematuria: a repeat urine test is arranged 1 month later to check the haematuria has resolved
- If you had macroscopic haematuria with complex infection: antibiotics for a longer course (following pyelonephritis guidance), with a repeat urine test 2 weeks after treatment

### If No UTI Is Present

Management depends on whether haematuria is macroscopic or microscopic.

#### Macroscopic Haematuria

Your doctor will typically arrange:

- Ultrasound of the urinary tract
- Referral to urology for cystoscopy (camera examination of the bladder)
- In some cases, urine cytology (cells in urine examined under a microscope)

If imaging does not suggest malignancy, you may be referred for Cxbladder triage testing — a molecular urine test that uses genomic markers to assess the likelihood of urothelial (bladder) cancer. Results are typically available 1–2 weeks after your urine sample.

#### Microscopic Haematuria Without Significant Proteinuria

Your doctor will arrange an ultrasound and likely refer you to urology. Management depends on your age and risk factors:

**Aged under 40:**

- Ultrasound of the urinary tract
- Regular monitoring of blood pressure, kidney function (eGFR), and urine protein (ACR/PCR) — initially at 1 month, then every 6 months
- Nephrology advice if blood pressure is difficult to control, kidney function is declining, or there is significant proteinuria
- Annual review while haematuria persists
- Referral to urology if haematuria becomes macroscopic, or if microscopic haematuria persists beyond 5 years with a history of smoking

**Aged 40 and over:**

- Ultrasound of the urinary tract
- Referral to urology for cystoscopy if ultrasound is normal
- Urgent urology referral if imaging suggests renal or bladder malignancy

**Smokers aged 30 and over** with confirmed microscopic haematuria: referral to urology even if asymptomatic.

#### Microscopic Haematuria With Significant Proteinuria (ACR ≥ 30)

Your doctor will monitor kidney function closely. If there is also a rapid fall in kidney function (eGFR) or red cell casts on urine microscopy, urgent nephrology assessment is needed.

Otherwise, your doctor will review the following tests at 3 months:

- Urine microscopy and urine casts
- Albumin:creatinine ratio (ACR)
- eGFR (kidney filtration rate)
- Blood pressure

If persistent microscopic haematuria with proteinuria and reduced kidney function continues beyond 3 months, your doctor will follow a chronic kidney disease (CKD) pathway.

If you have diabetes with CKD stage G3 (eGFR 30–59 mL/min/1.73m²) and ACR ≥ 3, nephrology review is recommended.

---

## When Your Doctor May Refer You

Your doctor may consider referral to:

- **Urology** — for cystoscopy and further investigation of macroscopic haematuria, or confirmed microscopic haematuria (particularly in smokers or those over 40)
- **Nephrology** — if there is significant or worsening proteinuria (ACR > 30 or PCR > 50), eGFR below 30, deteriorating kidney function, or difficult-to-control high blood pressure despite two antihypertensive medications including an ACE inhibitor (ACEi) or angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB)

All management and referral decisions are made by your doctor based on your full clinical picture.
