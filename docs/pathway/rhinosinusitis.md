# Rhinosinusitis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers both acute and chronic rhinosinusitis in adults and children — including assessment, when antibiotics are needed, and when to see a specialist.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance immediately if there are signs of spread beyond the sinuses:

**Intracranial spread** (e.g., meningitis, brain abscess, cavernous sinus thrombosis):
- Severe frontal headache
- Frontal swelling
- Neck stiffness
- Photophobia (sensitivity to light)
- Focal neurological deficit
- Decreased level of consciousness

**Orbital spread** (e.g., orbital cellulitis, abscess):
- Significant eye pain
- Eyelid swelling and redness
- Pain with eye movements
- Double vision
- Decreased visual acuity
- Proptosis (eye bulging forward)

**Other:** Suspected nasal foreign body in a child (unilateral offensive discharge)

---

## Background

Rhinosinusitis is classified by how long symptoms last:

- **Acute rhinosinusitis (ARS):** symptoms lasting less than 12 weeks. Usually triggered by an upper respiratory tract infection. Can be viral, post-viral, or bacterial. The most common bacteria are *Streptococcus pneumoniae*, *Haemophilus influenzae*, and *Moraxella catarrhalis*.
- **Chronic rhinosinusitis (CRS):** symptoms lasting 12 weeks or more. An inflammatory condition of the paranasal sinuses and nasal cavity lining. Associated with adult-onset asthma. May occur with or without nasal polyps — this distinction matters for management.

Up to 80% of patients with acute rhinosinusitis resolve within 14 days without antibiotics.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

**Take a history**, asking about:

- Symptom duration to classify the condition:
  - **Acute viral rhinosinusitis:** nasal blockage or discharge lasting less than 10 days, plus facial pressure/pain or reduced smell (adults) or cough (children)
  - **Acute post-viral rhinosinusitis:** symptoms that follow a viral episode and either increase after 5 days or persist for less than 12 weeks
  - **Acute bacterial rhinosinusitis:** purulent (pus-like) nasal secretion, fever above 38°C, severe predominantly one-sided facial pain, or symptoms that worsen after an initial mild illness
  - **Chronic rhinosinusitis:** nasal blockage, congestion, or discharge lasting 12 weeks or more, plus facial pain/pressure or loss of smell (adults) or cough (children)
- Signs of complicated spread (intracranial or orbital — see red flags above)
- Symptoms of allergic rhinitis: sneezing, watery discharge, nasal itching, itchy watery eyes

**Examine**, checking:
- General appearance and vital signs including temperature
- Nasal cavity: discharge, polyps, inflammation, septal deviation
- Oral cavity: postnasal discharge, dental disease
- Ears for associated pathology
- Signs of intracranial or orbital spread

**Consider other diagnoses** that can mimic rhinosinusitis:
- Mid-segmental facial pain (also called facial pain syndrome): facial soft tissue pain with minimal nasal symptoms — a diagnosis of exclusion
- Migraine or other headache variants (headache as the primary symptom of rhinosinusitis is uncommon)
- Temporomandibular disorder
- Dental pain

**Investigations:** Diagnosis is clinical — initial investigations are generally not needed. Plain sinus X-rays are no longer used. CT sinus scans are useful in chronic rhinosinusitis when medical management has failed and specific criteria are met.

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following based on your diagnosis:

### For Acute Rhinosinusitis (ARS)

**Symptomatic treatment (all cases):**
- Simple analgesics such as paracetamol or NSAIDs (unless contraindicated)
- Nasal saline irrigation — reduces postnasal drainage and removes secretions. Can be done with commercial rinse kits or a home-made solution (½ teaspoon salt + ¼ teaspoon baking soda dissolved in 250 mL warm boiled water)
- Warm facial packs applied for 5–10 minutes around three times a day
- Maintain adequate hydration
- Topical nasal decongestants (e.g., oxymetazoline, xylometazoline) for short-term use only — no more than 5 days to avoid rebound congestion. Not recommended for young children

Note: oral decongestants, antihistamines, and steam inhalation are not recommended — there is no evidence of benefit in acute rhinosinusitis.

**If post-viral ARS** (symptoms persist beyond 10 days or worsen after 5 days):
- Add topical nasal steroids (e.g., budesonide, fluticasone propionate) — start twice daily for 2 weeks, then once at night. A full response may take 3–4 weeks. The most common side effect is a minor nosebleed.

**If suspected acute bacterial rhinosinusitis:**
- Continue symptomatic treatment and add nasal steroids
- Your doctor may prescribe antibiotics if there are significant co-morbidities, or if symptoms are worsening or severe and lasting more than 5 days (in patients aged over 12 years)
- First-line antibiotic is amoxicillin. If you have a penicillin allergy, alternatives include doxycycline (not in children, pregnancy, or breastfeeding) or roxithromycin
- For severe symptoms, your doctor may also consider a short course of oral prednisone (20 mg daily for 1 week, then 10 mg daily for 1 week)
- If no improvement on antibiotics, your doctor will review the diagnosis and may switch to amoxicillin + clavulanic acid or cefalexin
- Antibiotics are generally not recommended in children aged under 12 years

**If recurrent acute bacterial rhinosinusitis** (4 or more episodes per year): your doctor will manage this as chronic rhinosinusitis.

**Specialist referral for ARS** may be considered if symptoms have a major impact on quality of life, or if there are more than 4 episodes in a year (or 2–3 per year over 3 years) with failure of medical management.

---

### For Chronic Rhinosinusitis (CRS)

CRS is an inflammatory disorder — not simply an infection. The main treatment focus is anti-inflammatory, primarily with topical steroids. Recovery of normal function may take months.

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Treating underlying causes (allergic rhinitis, asthma, smoking, dental infections, immune deficiency)
- Regular long-term nasal saline irrigation
- Long-term topical nasal steroids (budesonide or fluticasone propionate)
- Re-evaluation after 4 weeks: if no improvement, a 2-week course of antibiotics may be added (doxycycline 100 mg daily or roxithromycin 300 mg daily — for both antibacterial and anti-inflammatory effect, in patients aged 12 or older)
- A course of oral prednisone if symptoms are not improving
- Referral for CT sinus scan if medical management has failed
- Specialist ENT referral if CT is abnormal or after at least 3 months of medical management without improvement. Surgery (endoscopic sinus surgery) may be considered — but must be followed by ongoing medical management to prevent recurrence

**For acute flare-ups of CRS:**
- A 7–14 day course of antibiotics, as with acute rhinosinusitis
- A short course of oral corticosteroid may also be needed

**Samter's triad** (nasal polyps + asthma + aspirin/NSAID sensitivity) has a poorer prognosis and is difficult to treat. Your doctor may refer for simultaneous respiratory, ENT, and immunology assessment.
