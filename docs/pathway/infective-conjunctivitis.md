# Infective Conjunctivitis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers conjunctivitis in adults and children, but not neonates.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

Tell your doctor immediately or seek emergency care if you have:

- **Orbital cellulitis** (eyelid swelling with deep eye pain, pain on moving the eye, or double vision)
- **Reduced vision** when tested

---

## Background

Conjunctivitis is inflammation of the conjunctiva (the clear membrane covering the white of the eye and inside of the eyelids).

- Most conjunctivitis is viral, caused by adenovirus
- Bacterial and viral types have different features that help with diagnosis
- Most cases are self-limiting

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history asking about:

- **Discharge** — whether it is thick and purulent (more bacterial) or watery/stringy (more viral)
- Eyes being stuck together or crusted on waking
- Marked irritation or gritty feeling
- Eyelid swelling
- Any change to vision
- Contact lens use

Physical examination typically includes:

- Conjunctival redness (erythema)
- **Visual acuity** — usually unaffected in straightforward conjunctivitis
- Cornea, iris, and pupil examination — usually normal
- If symptoms are unilateral or severe, your doctor may use fluorescein staining (a dye with a special light) to check for any corneal surface damage

### Differentiating Viral vs Bacterial Conjunctivitis

**Viral conjunctivitis:**
- Usually starts in one eye, spreading to the other within 1–2 days
- Watery or faint stringy discharge; eye may be crusted in the morning but discharge during the day is serous
- Burning or gritty feeling is the main complaint
- Often associated with cold or flu symptoms
- Other household contacts may be similarly affected
- Severe adenoviral infections can cause epidemic keratoconjunctivitis — marked irritation worsening over 2–3 days and potentially lasting weeks

**Bacterial conjunctivitis:**
- Thick purulent (pus-like) discharge continuing throughout the day
- Eye often "stuck shut" in the morning
- Discharge is usually the main complaint
- Usually starts in one eye but can spread to the other
- In children under 5, *Haemophilus influenzae* is common and can cause conjunctivitis-otitis (ear and eye infection together)
- Consider chlamydia or *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* if there is a history of sexually transmitted infection, profuse discharge, or an unusual/prolonged course

### Differential Diagnosis

Your doctor will also consider:

- **Non-infective conjunctivitis** — dry eyes, blepharitis, or allergic conjunctivitis
- **Preseptal (periorbital) cellulitis** — infection of the eyelid tissue, not the orbit; requires oral antibiotics
- **Orbital cellulitis** — deeper infection involving the eye socket; always requires hospital admission
- **Herpetic causes** — herpes zoster ophthalmicus (shingles affecting the eye), herpes simplex, or herpes simplex keratitis
- Other causes of red eye

### Investigations

Routine bacterial or viral swabs are **not recommended** for typical conjunctivitis.

A PCR/NAAT swab for chlamydia and *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* may be taken if:
- There is a history of sexually transmitted infection
- Discharge is profuse
- The course is unusual or prolonged

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following approaches:

### Viral Conjunctivitis

- **No antibiotics** — topical antibiotics are not indicated and can worsen irritation or prolong symptoms
- Symptoms may worsen for 3–5 days and can last 2–3 weeks
- Symptomatic relief: cool flannel over the lids, paracetamol, and lubricant eye drops
- Infection control: avoid public places, work, or school while discharge is present; good hand hygiene to reduce spread

### Bacterial Conjunctivitis

- Treatment is recommended if you wear contact lenses; a watchful waiting approach may be considered for others, as the condition is often self-limiting
- Your doctor may consider **chloramphenicol eye drops 0.5%** four to six times daily for 5–7 days (available from community pharmacy)
- Symptoms typically take 2–3 days to improve even when drops are effective
- Avoid using multiple types of eye drops together (anti-allergy, decongestant, and antibiotic drops)
- Symptomatic relief: cool flannel, paracetamol, and lubricant drops

### Suspected Chlamydia or Gonococcal Conjunctivitis

Your doctor will follow specific treatment pathways for sexually transmitted infection-related conjunctivitis.

### Contact Lens Advice

If you wear contact lenses:
- Stop wearing lenses for the duration of the infection and for 48 hours after it has cleared
- Discard any disposable lenses and lens cases; use new ones once clear
- If using non-disposable lenses, ensure thorough cleaning and storage before reusing

### Follow-Up

For uncomplicated infective conjunctivitis, a follow-up review is not routinely needed. Return to your doctor if you develop:
- Decline in vision
- Fever
- Double vision (diplopia)
- Severe pain
- Symptoms lasting longer than 2 weeks

If symptoms return, your doctor will reassess the diagnosis and may seek ophthalmology advice.

---

## When Your Doctor Will Seek Specialist Input

Your doctor will seek urgent ophthalmology assessment or advice if:
- Orbital cellulitis is suspected
- Vision declines (e.g., 6/12 or worse)
- There is a corneal surface defect on fluorescein staining
- The diagnosis is uncertain
- Recovery is delayed (symptoms for more than 2 weeks)
- There are any other concerns
