# Neutropenia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of neutropenia in adults — low neutrophil count on a full blood count (FBC), which is often an incidental finding but can indicate serious underlying causes.

> **Important:** This information is for general educational purposes. All decisions about investigation and management should be made with your doctor.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Attention

Contact your doctor or go to the emergency department urgently if you have neutropenia and:

- Neutrophil count is < 0.5 × 10⁹/L
- You feel unwell or have a fever (temperature ≥ 38°C)

---

## Background

Neutropenia means the neutrophil count in the blood is lower than normal. It is often found incidentally on a routine blood test.

**Normal ranges vary by ethnicity:**
- The widely accepted lower limit is 2 × 10⁹/L for Caucasians
- Levels of 1.5 × 10⁹/L or even lower may be normal for African Americans and some Jewish groups

### Common Causes

- **Infections** — particularly viral (including HIV), also malaria, typhoid, TB. Changes are often noted within 1–2 days of infection and may persist for several weeks
- **Drugs** — antipsychotics, anticonvulsants, and antithyroid drugs are the most important
- **Autoimmune diseases** — SLE, rheumatoid arthritis
- **Nutritional** — B12 deficiency, folate deficiency, alcohol dependency, anorexia nervosa
- **Splenomegaly**
- **Bone marrow pathology** — leukaemia, myelodysplasia, aplastic anaemia

### Drugs Known to Cause Neutropenia

- Anti-inflammatory drugs: aminopyrine, phenylbutazone, sulfasalazine
- Antibiotics: chloramphenicol, co-trimoxazole, sulfasalazine, penicillins
- Anticonvulsants: phenytoin, carbamazepine
- Antithyroid drugs: carbimazole
- Cytotoxic agents, radiation
- Phenothiazines: chlorpromazine, thioridazine
- Psychotropics/antidepressants: clozapine, mianserin, imipramine
- Others: gold, penicillamine, ticlopidine, tolbutamide, some herbal remedies

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess neutropenia by taking a history, examining you, and arranging blood tests.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:
- Frequency and severity of infections
- Mouth ulcers
- Recent viral illness
- Exposure to drugs and toxins (including all current medications)
- Symptoms of malabsorption
- Symptoms suggesting reduced immunity

### Examination

Your doctor will look for:
- Mouth ulcers
- Fever
- Signs of infection
- Jaundice
- Lymphadenopathy (swollen lymph nodes)
- Hepatomegaly or splenomegaly (enlarged liver or spleen)
- Signs of autoimmune or connective tissue disease

### Blood Tests

Initial tests include:
- FBC with blood film — checking haemoglobin, platelets, and cell morphology

**Severity classification (used to guide management):**
- **Mild:** 1.0 to 2.0 × 10⁹/L
- **Moderate:** 0.5 to 1.0 × 10⁹/L
- **Severe:** < 0.5 × 10⁹/L

If there is persistent moderate neutropenia without an obvious cause, further tests may include:
- Antinuclear antibodies (ANA)
- Vitamin B12 and folate
- Hepatitis B serology
- HIV test
- Liver enzymes
- Rheumatoid factor
- Serum protein electrophoresis (SPE)

## Management

Management depends on severity and whether there are symptoms.

### Moderate to Severe Neutropenia (neutrophils < 1.0 × 10⁹/L)

The risk of significant bacterial infection rises when neutrophils fall below 1.0 × 10⁹/L and becomes more serious below 0.5 × 10⁹/L.

**If unwell or febrile:**
- Your doctor will arrange same-day hospital assessment (haematology if already known to that service, otherwise general medicine)

**If well and afebrile:**
- You should seek medical attention immediately if you develop fever or feel unwell
- Your doctor will repeat the FBC in 48 hours
- If neutropenia remains < 1.0 × 10⁹/L, your doctor will seek haematology advice

### Mild Neutropenia (neutrophils 1.0 to 2.0 × 10⁹/L)

- Your doctor will repeat the FBC in 1–2 weeks
- If neutropenia persists for more than 6 weeks, further investigations will be arranged (see above)
- A referral to haematology is considered only if neutrophils remain persistently below 1.0 × 10⁹/L, or if other significant blood count abnormalities develop
- If blood counts remain stable over a few months with no other abnormalities, your doctor will check annually for 2 years — no further follow-up is needed if counts stay normal and you remain well

### Drug-Induced Neutropenia

- If a drug is suspected as the cause and WBC < 3 × 10⁹/L or neutrophils < 1.5 × 10⁹/L, your doctor will stop the suspected drug
- FBC will be rechecked in one week
- You should seek medical attention immediately if you become unwell or develop a fever
- **Clozapine-induced neutropenia is a special case** — if you are taking clozapine and develop neutropenia, urgent protocols apply as severe life-threatening neutropenia can occur. Your prescribing team must be notified immediately.

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor may refer you to a haematologist in the following situations:

**Same-day (acute) assessment:**
- Severe neutropenia (< 0.5 × 10⁹/L) with fever or feeling unwell
- Known haematology patient → acute haematology referral
- Not known to haematology → acute general medicine referral

**Non-urgent (planned) haematology assessment:**
- Severe neutropenia (even if currently well)
- Persistent moderate neutropenia
- All relevant blood results (FBC, blood film, and any additional tests) should be included with the referral
