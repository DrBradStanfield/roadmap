# Pain in Spinal Cord Impairment (SCI)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of pain in adults with spinal cord impairment (SCI). Pain in SCI can be complex and difficult to localise. This information is a guide only — always discuss your symptoms with your doctor.

---

## Background

People with SCI may experience musculoskeletal, neuropathic, or spasticity-related pain. Chronic pain is common.

**Important:** Lack of normal sensation can mask the true location of pain and alter typical symptom patterns. For example, appendicitis or cholecystitis may present as vague spasms of diffuse abdominal pain rather than the typical focal patterns seen in people without SCI. Gallbladder conditions are also more common in SCI. Your doctor will consider these atypical presentations.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a full history and physical assessment (which can take considerable time) including:

- What causes the pain and what makes it better or worse
- Non-classic presentations of common conditions (e.g., abdominal pain patterns)
- Past medical history for treatable causes (e.g., surgical metalware needing removal, bowel obstruction from adhesions)
- Features that might suggest a **post-SCI syrinx**

**Post-SCI syrinx:** A syrinx is an accumulation of cerebrospinal fluid that disrupts and expands spinal cord tracks. Signs include gradual or progressive decline in neurological function, increasing spasticity, or increasing neuropathic pain. An MRI may be needed — your doctor will advise.

Your doctor will also perform a neurological examination looking for causes of spasticity or muscle spasm — such as pressure injuries, ingrown toenails, or unrecognised fractures in areas without sensation.

---

## Types of Pain in SCI

Your doctor will determine whether pain is due to:

- **Musculoskeletal** — bone and joint causes
- **Neuropathic** — nerve-related pain from the SCI itself
- **Spasticity-related** — muscle spasm pain
- **Medical/visceral** — internal organ causes (which may present atypically)

---

## Management

### Local or Spasticity-Related Pain

If pain is compounded by a local nociceptive cause (e.g., a pressure wound), your doctor will manage the underlying cause with appropriate local treatment and analgesic.

Spasticity management is covered in the Spasticity in SCI pathway.

### Musculoskeletal Pain

Your doctor will manage bone and musculoskeletal causes according to the Bone and Musculoskeletal Health in SCI pathway.

### Neuropathic Pain

Your doctor may trial the following medications. Do not start or stop these without medical supervision.

**Gabapentinoids (gabapentin or pregabalin):**

These are first-line medicines for neuropathic pain in SCI. They can enhance the effects of opioids, alcohol, and other drugs — increasing risk of accidental overdose. Your doctor will discuss risks, especially if you take opioids or have a history of substance use.

- **Gabapentin:** Started at a low dose and increased slowly over weeks. Usually taken in divided doses 3–4 times daily. Dose is adjusted for kidney function.
- **Pregabalin:** Similar action to gabapentin but more easily titrated. Started at a low dose and increased slowly. Do not stop suddenly — abrupt cessation can cause severe withdrawal (anxiety, nausea, tremors, return of pain). Your doctor will taper the dose gradually if stopping.

**Tricyclic antidepressants (e.g., nortriptyline or amitriptyline):**

These are adjuvant (add-on) medicines for neuropathic pain, taken as a single evening dose. Your doctor will start at a low dose and increase slowly. A trial of at least 3 weeks is needed to assess effectiveness. Amitriptyline is more sedating and may help if sleep is also a problem.

**Other contributing factors:** Your doctor will also address other causes of neuropathic pain — such as diabetes, alcohol use, or vitamin B deficiencies.

### Increasing Care Support

If a painful area needs to be rested, an increase in care support may be required. Your doctor will advise on contacting your ACC case manager or care support agency.

### Chronic Pain

If pain is chronic and difficult to treat, your doctor may refer to a specialised pain service.

---

## When to Seek Help

Contact your doctor if:

- Your pain is worsening or changing in character
- You develop progressive decline in neurological function, increasing spasticity, or increasing neuropathic pain (possible syrinx)
- Pain is interfering significantly with function, sleep, or quality of life
- You have complex chronic pain not responding to current treatment

**Your doctor will discuss** specialist referral for: neuropathic pain related to SCI, possible syrinx requiring MRI, or complex chronic pain.
