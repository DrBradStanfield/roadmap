# Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of BPH — enlargement of the prostate gland that causes urinary symptoms in men, typically aged over 50.

---

## Urgent — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department if you have:

- Complete inability to urinate (acute urinary retention) and no catheter has been placed
- Signs of kidney failure (confusion, markedly reduced urine output, very elevated creatinine)

---

## Background

BPH is a common condition that becomes more frequent with age in men over 50. Most men do not develop serious complications. Only a small percentage develop complications such as acute urinary retention, recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs), hydronephrosis, or kidney failure. Many men can be managed with watchful waiting alone.

---

## Symptoms

BPH causes two main types of urinary symptoms:

**Voiding (obstructive) symptoms:**
- Poor urine flow
- Hesitancy (difficulty starting)
- Intermittent stream
- Dribbling at the end of urination
- Overflow incontinence

**Storage (irritative) symptoms:**
- Increased frequency
- Nocturia (waking at night to urinate)
- Urgency

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Assess your symptoms and calculate an **International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS)** — a standardised questionnaire scoring urinary symptoms from 0 to 35
- Consider alternative causes of your symptoms (see below)
- Perform a **digital rectal examination (DRE)** to assess prostate size, consistency, and shape
- Examine the abdomen for a distended bladder
- Examine the external genitalia

### Investigations your doctor may arrange

- Urinalysis and urine culture
- PSA (prostate-specific antigen) blood test — discussed and decided jointly with you
- Serum creatinine (kidney function)
- Ultrasound — not routine, but arranged if there is suspected chronic urinary retention, an abdominal mass, falling kidney function, haematuria (blood in urine), or recurrent UTIs

### Possible alternative diagnoses

Your symptoms may be caused by conditions other than BPH, including:

- Urinary tract infection (UTI)
- Urethral stricture or phimosis
- Constipation
- Poor bladder contraction (e.g., from diabetes or neurological conditions such as stroke or spinal injury)
- Anxiety or diabetes
- Medications — including diuretics, sympathomimetics (e.g., pseudoephedrine), anticholinergics (antihistamines, tricyclic antidepressants, SSRIs, oxybutynin, phenothiazines), opiates, benzodiazepines, or muscle relaxants

## Management

Management depends on the severity of your symptoms as measured by the IPSS score.

### Mild BPH (IPSS score less than 8)

Watchful waiting is a good option for most men with mild, non-bothersome symptoms. Around 85% remain stable at 12 months.

Lifestyle measures your doctor may recommend:
- Reduce potential triggers: caffeine, carbonated drinks, alcohol, and artificial sweeteners
- Manage fluid timing — reduce intake in the evenings
- Bladder training exercises

### Moderate BPH (IPSS score 8 to 19)

With watchful waiting, up to a third of men will need surgery within five years; the majority do well without it.

- If your symptoms are not particularly bothersome, watchful waiting with lifestyle changes is a reasonable option
- If symptoms are significantly bothersome, your doctor may consider the medical treatments used for severe BPH (see below)
- Lifestyle measures are the same as for mild BPH

### Severe BPH (IPSS score 20 or above)

Your doctor will consider whether complications are present. If so, specialist urology assessment is likely needed.

**Complications that may require urology referral:**
- Suspected chronic urinary retention (abdominal mass, distended bladder)
- Significantly elevated creatinine (impaired kidney function)
- Haematuria (blood in urine)
- Recurrent UTIs
- Incontinence not responding to medical treatment
- Hydronephrosis
- Bladder stones

Your doctor may consider the following:

**Step 1 — Review medications:** Discontinue any medications that may be contributing to urinary symptoms.

**Step 2 — Medical therapy:**

- **Alpha blockers** (e.g., doxazosin, titrated up to 4 mg once daily): relax the muscles around the prostate and bladder neck to improve urine flow. If not tolerated, the selective alpha blocker tamsulosin may be used (special authority required in New Zealand).

- **If primarily outflow obstruction and prostate is enlarged on DRE:**
  - Your doctor may trial finasteride 5 mg once daily for 3 months (special authority required). Finasteride shrinks the prostate over time.
  - Note: finasteride reduces PSA levels by around 50% after 6–12 months — this needs to be factored into any prostate cancer screening interpretation.

- **If primarily overactive bladder symptoms (urgency, frequency) with acceptable flow:**
  - Your doctor may consider solifenacin succinate, starting at the lowest dose. If symptoms persist beyond 3 months, urology assessment is likely needed.

- **Pelvic floor physiotherapy** may be considered for stress or urge incontinence — your doctor will rule out overflow incontinence first.

### Raised PSA

If your PSA result is elevated, your doctor will follow the prostate cancer diagnosis pathway.

### Acute urinary retention

If you are unable to urinate, a urethral catheter will usually be placed. Management depends on your kidney function and age. Your doctor will arrange appropriate urgent or non-urgent urology assessment based on these factors.

---

## Watchful Waiting — Key Points

- Many men with BPH do not need surgery or medication
- Reducing caffeine, alcohol, and evening fluids often helps
- Symptoms can remain stable for years
- Regular review with your doctor is recommended to monitor for progression or complications
