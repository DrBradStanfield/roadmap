# Methamphetamine (P)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of methamphetamine (P) use — including acute intoxication, withdrawal, and longer-term harm reduction and support.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance (111) or go to the emergency department immediately if:

- Marked agitation, psychosis, or aggressive/violent behaviour
- Paranoid delusions or hallucinations
- Hyperthermia (high body temperature)
- Symptoms suggesting a heart attack or stroke (e.g., chest pain, sudden weakness or speech problems)
- Significant cardiovascular instability
- Seizures
- Loss of or altered consciousness

---

## Background

Methamphetamine (known as P in New Zealand, or ice/crystal meth in Australia) is a highly purified psychostimulant. It is a powerful dopamine agonist with a long half-life of approximately 12 hours.

It is available in crystal, pill, powder, and liquid forms — the crystal form is most common in New Zealand.

Key points:
- Two-thirds of people who use methamphetamine do so less than once a month. Even once- or twice-weekly use can cause withdrawal symptoms due to the long half-life.
- Polysubstance use is very common (e.g., combined with GHB, benzodiazepines, alcohol, or cannabis).
- People who use methamphetamine often experience stigma and discrimination, including from health professionals. Fear of judgement is a significant barrier to accessing care — a non-judgemental approach matters.

---

## Assessment

If a patient is displaying marked agitation, psychosis, or aggressive behaviour, further assessment may not be possible — arrange urgent transfer to the emergency department or call 111.

For less acute presentations, your doctor will:

1. **Take a full history** — remaining calm and non-judgemental, with simple questions.

2. **Assess mental health**, including:
   - Orientation
   - Hallucinations and delusions
   - Mood and any thoughts of self-harm or suicide
   - Insight and judgement
   - Prescribed medications
   - Recent sleep patterns
   - Social circumstances
   - Readiness to change

3. **Assess extent and pattern of methamphetamine use**, including:
   - Amount used (a typical recreational dose is a "point" — 100 mg; higher doses may reach 1 g/day)
   - Recent changes in use, particularly any escalation
   - Route of administration (smoking is most common; others include swallowing, snorting, rectal use, or injecting)
   - Frequency and pattern: daily, weekly, monthly, or binge use (several days of heavy use followed by abrupt crash)
   - When they last used
   - Duration of use and age of first use
   - Motivation for use (e.g., energy for shift work, sex work, caregiving)

4. **Assess associated risks**, including:
   - Sexual risk-taking
   - Financial risk-taking (e.g., gambling, dealing)
   - Legal and relationship risks
   - Injecting: ask about safe injecting practices

5. **Assess physical health**, including:
   - Temperature, pulse, blood pressure, blood sugar, oxygen levels
   - Chest pain or fast heart rate
   - Injection sites or track marks
   - Past history of seizures, arrhythmias, high blood pressure, heart failure, or psychosis

6. **Screen for other substance use** (alcohol, tobacco, cannabis, opioids).

7. **Consider investigations**:
   - Liver function tests if also using alcohol
   - Blood-borne virus testing (hepatitis B, C, HIV) if history of injecting
   - STI screening (chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis) if at risk
   - ECG if chest pain or fast heart rate

## Management

### Acute Intoxication

Your doctor may consider the following based on severity:

- **If acutely intoxicated, disturbed, or refusing oral medication:** Emergency department assessment or call 111. If the patient is an adult, floridly psychotic, and no emergency department is nearby, olanzapine intramuscularly 5–10 mg may be given while waiting for emergency services.
- **If moderately aroused and agitated:** Olanzapine 5–20 mg wafer and/or diazepam 5–10 mg or lorazepam 1–2 mg.
- **If mildly agitated:** Olanzapine 5–10 mg wafer or quetiapine 25–50 mg and/or diazepam 5 mg or lorazepam 0.5–1 mg.

In the acute setting, your doctor will distinguish between:

- **Methamphetamine toxicity** — severe agitation, arrhythmia, fluctuating blood pressure, hyperthermia, metabolic and electrolyte abnormalities, seizures.
- **Serotonin syndrome** — agitation, hyperreflexia, sweating, high blood pressure, fast heart rate, hyperthermia, possibly delirium or seizures.
- **Withdrawal symptoms** — restlessness, irritability, lethargy, low mood, sleep problems, diarrhoea; in severe cases: depression, hallucinations, paranoia, psychosis.

### Acute Psychosis

If there is an acute psychotic illness, your doctor will seek specialist mental health advice and arrange an urgent mental health assessment.

### Harm Reduction

Your doctor may discuss the following, regardless of whether you wish to stop using:

- Reducing risk from injecting — e.g., information about needle exchange services
- Drug checking services (a free, legal service to identify what substances are in a drug supply)
- Hepatitis B vaccination; hepatitis C, HIV, and STI testing
- Diet and dental hygiene (methamphetamine is associated with significant dental damage)
- Driving — it is illegal to drive while impaired, or with methamphetamine in the bloodstream above legal limits. If stopped by police and failing a compulsory impairment test, a blood test may be required.

### Associated Medical Conditions

Your doctor will manage any associated conditions including:
- High blood pressure
- Cardiovascular risk (methamphetamine increases risk of heart attack, stroke, and heart failure)
- Hepatitis B or C
- Sexually transmitted infections

For STI results that are positive, management follows the relevant treatment pathway. Even if results are negative, your doctor may discuss condom use, PrEP (for men who have sex with men), and regular STI screening.

### Withdrawal in the Community

Most people can undertake methamphetamine withdrawal in the community or outpatient setting. Your doctor will assess whether this is suitable.

**Withdrawal typically has three phases:**
- **Crash** (2–3 days)
- **Withdrawal** (peaks day 3–5): restlessness, irritability, agitation, lethargy, low mood, sleep problems, diarrhoea; in severe cases, depression, hallucinations, paranoia, psychosis
- **Extinction** (protracted — can last weeks to months): ongoing cravings, mood fluctuations, irritability, sleep disturbance

**Your doctor may consider medications for withdrawal:**
- **Benzodiazepines** — e.g., diazepam in the first 24–48 hours, up to 5–10 mg up to four times a day for 3 days, then reduced by 5–10 mg a day. Not prescribed for longer than 5 days.
- **Antipsychotics** — if paranoia or delusions are prominent: e.g., olanzapine 2.5–5 mg as required every 6–8 hours (max 20 mg/24 hours), or quetiapine 25–50 mg as required every 6–8 hours (max 300 mg/day). Quetiapine 25–50 mg may also help with agitation and sleep.

Note: While use of benzodiazepines and antipsychotics in this context is common practice, the evidence of efficacy is limited.

**Important:** Your doctor will wait 2–4 weeks after stopping methamphetamine before starting any antidepressant treatment, to allow mood to stabilise. A diagnosis of depression should not be made during the withdrawal period.

### Cravings

Cravings can persist for many weeks or months after stopping. Your doctor may discuss a cravings coping plan and strategies for managing triggers.

### Specialist Support

Your doctor may refer you to specialist addiction services (such as CADS — Community Alcohol and Drug Services) for ongoing counselling, withdrawal support, or culturally appropriate care. This referral is appropriate even if you continue to use, as harm reduction support remains relevant.

Inpatient withdrawal may be needed if:
- You have a home environment unsuitable for safe withdrawal
- You have had repeated unsuccessful attempts to withdraw
- You have significant mental or physical health problems, or significant use of other substances (especially alcohol, benzodiazepines, or GHB)
