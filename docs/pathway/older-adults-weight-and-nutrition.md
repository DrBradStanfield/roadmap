# Older Adults Weight and Nutrition

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: January 2023.*

Unintentional weight change is a marker of poor nutrition in older adults. Nutrition screening helps identify who is at risk and why.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any changes to your diet or medications with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to See Your Doctor

- All older adults with unintentional weight loss need a medical assessment, an examination, and appropriate tests.

---

## Background

- BMI (body mass index) may not accurately reflect health or nutrition risk in older adults.
- Nutrition screening identifies who is at risk and gives information about why.
- The risk of poor nutrition is higher with eating alone, how a person sees their own weight, low intake of milk and milk products, and difficulty cooking.
- In a survey of older adults living in the community in Christchurch, 31% were at high risk of poor nutrition.

---

## Assessment

### Measuring BMI

Your doctor may measure your BMI (weight in kilograms divided by height in metres squared), noting that BMI may not accurately reflect health risk in older people.

- Less than 18.5: underweight.
- Between 18.5 and 24.9: healthy weight.
- Between 25 and 29.9: overweight.
- Greater than 30: obese (moderate 35 or more, extreme 40 or more).

### Nutrition screening

Your doctor may consider a nutrition screening questionnaire called SCREEN-14 if:
- you are aged more than 65 years and there is a clinical concern, such as:
  - unintentional weight change
  - long-lasting, ongoing, or repeated illness
  - depression
  - fragile skin
  - poor wound healing
  - living alone
- you are aged more than 75 years (screening once a year).

A SCREEN-14 score less than 50 is considered a high nutrition risk.

### Possible causes

If there are weight or nutrition concerns, your doctor may consider:
- chronic conditions, such as depression, COPD, alcohol dependency, dementia, cancer, or end-stage disease
- medications
- dental problems. Gum disease and failing teeth are a cause of poor nutrition in older people.

### Tests

If indicated, your doctor may consider blood tests such as a full blood count (FBC), ferritin, CRP, thyroid function (TSH), kidney function, and liver function.

---

## Management

Your doctor will manage based on the results of the examination and screening, and treat any underlying cause.

### If you may benefit from weight gain

Your doctor may suggest a **Food First** approach:
- Eat more often, such as snacks between meals.
- Use high-energy foods, such as milkshakes, full-fat milk, butter, cream, cheese, and sugar.
- Healthy fat sources include margarine, avocado, nuts, peanut butter, oil, and cheese.
- Try over-the-counter nutrition supplements.

If there is no improvement after 4 weeks of Food First, your doctor may consider oral nutrition supplements and reassess your history, examination, and tests. Your doctor will check how well you tolerate the supplements. The aim is to stop them once their goals are met, to avoid taste fatigue.

### If weight loss may help

Most older people are advised to maintain their weight. If you are obese and at high risk of cardiovascular disease, you may benefit from weight loss. Large amounts of weight loss are not necessary. Even a sustained, modest loss of 3 to 4 kg has been linked with improvement. Your doctor may give you information or refer you to a dietitian.

### Exercise

Your doctor may advise physical activity that includes the three core components (strength, balance, and flexibility) plus aerobic activity. Training for strength, balance, and flexibility:
- is very effective for people who are at the limit of their stability when doing daily tasks
- aims to prevent or delay reaching that limit or, if it is reached, to help you be safer
- helps you cope more safely with daily activities, even if progress is limited
- is a useful preventive activity as people age and become more at risk of falling.

Your doctor may suggest a Green Prescription or another exercise programme for older adults, or falls prevention if appropriate.

---

## When to Refer

Your doctor may refer you to a dietitian if:
- your BMI is less than 18.5, or you have lost more than 10% of your weight unintentionally in the past 3 to 6 months
- your BMI is less than 20 and you have lost more than 5% of your weight in the past 3 to 6 months
- SCREEN-14 shows nutrition risk and you have lost more than 5 kg in the past 6 months
- you are obese and meet the referral criteria.

Your doctor may also refer you to a healthy eating service for older people if you may benefit from weight gain.

## Key Concepts for Patients

- BMI may not accurately reflect nutrition risk in older adults. Screening helps show who is at risk.
- Unintentional weight loss in an older adult needs a medical assessment.
- Gum disease and failing teeth can cause poor nutrition.
- Most older people are advised to maintain their weight.
- Always discuss any new symptoms or changes to your diet with your doctor.
