# Anxiety, Distress, and Agitation in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway applies to adults in palliative care. Management of anxiety, distress, and agitation at the end of life is a specialist area. Always discuss with your doctor or palliative care team.

---

## Overview

Anxiety, distress, and agitation are common in palliative care. They may have physical, psychological, spiritual, or medication-related causes — and often several at once. Identifying and addressing causes is the first step, alongside symptom relief.

---

## Assessment — Identifying Causes

Your doctor will look for possible causes, which may include:

### Psychological and emotional causes
- Depression
- Fear (fear of death, pain, losing control, being a burden)
- Spiritual or existential distress — as life shortens, some patients question the meaning of their life or their beliefs; this can intensify physical symptoms and make them harder to manage

### Physical symptoms
- Unrelieved pain
- Constipation, urinary retention
- Breathlessness
- Alcohol or nicotine withdrawal

### Medication-related causes
- Corticosteroids can cause agitation or mood changes
- **Opioid toxicity:** hallucinations, delirium, myoclonic jerks — requires dose reduction or opioid rotation
- **Opioid excess:** sedation, pinpoint pupils, respiratory depression — can be reversed with naloxone
- **Serotonin toxicity:** can occur when combining certain medications — stimulants, tramadol, pethidine, SSRIs, MAOIs, venlafaxine, clomipramine, ondansetron, St John's Wort, lithium. Symptoms usually appear within 24 hours of a dose change.

### Delirium
- Acute delirium is a common cause of agitation in palliative care — your doctor may check calcium, renal function, and liver function

### Terminal agitation
In the last days of life, your doctor will consider terminal agitation, which may present with:
- Agitation and restlessness
- Impaired consciousness
- Distressed vocalising
- Muscle twitching, myoclonus, or convulsions

---

## Management

### 1. Listening and emotional support
Your doctor or care team will take time to listen to your fears and concerns. This is often the most important first step.

### 2. Treating underlying causes
Where possible, your doctor will:
- Provide explanations and reassurance
- Consider whether having a family member present would help
- Treat physical symptoms (pain, constipation, urinary retention)
- Address spiritual, social, and religious concerns through a multi-disciplinary team including chaplaincy, social work, and counselling
- Reduce the dose of an opioid or switch to a different opioid if toxicity is present
- Treat associated delirium with haloperidol if appropriate

### 3. Medications for acute anxiety

Your doctor may prescribe benzodiazepines for acute anxiety episodes:

**Lorazepam**
- 0.5 mg to 1 mg orally, every 4 to 6 hours as needed
- In palliative care, some doses and routes may be used in ways not officially approved by the medicines regulator

**Midazolam** (for crisis or severe distress)
- Given buccally (inside the cheek) or sublingually: 2.5 mg
- In a crisis: 2.5 to 5 mg by subcutaneous injection, up to every 15 minutes, then every 2 hours once settled
- Maximum 15 mg per day without specialist palliative care advice

**Clonazepam**
- Potent and long-acting — sedation can be significant and sustained; accumulates with repeated dosing
- Oral/sublingual/buccal drops: 0.2 to 0.5 mg (2 to 5 drops) every 6 hours as needed — drop onto a spoon, not directly into the mouth
- Subcutaneous: 0.5 to 1 mg, repeated after 6 hours if needed
- Note: clonazepam sticks to PVC tubing — non-PVC tubing is recommended for subcutaneous infusion

For longer-term anxiety, antidepressants may be more effective than ongoing benzodiazepines. Your doctor may follow the general Anxiety pathway for this.

### 4. Last days of life — terminal agitation

If a patient in the last days of life has unrelieved agitation or extreme fear, your doctor may recommend sedation with a continuous subcutaneous midazolam infusion:

- Typical starting dose: 10 to 20 mg midazolam over 24 hours via syringe driver
- For acute crisis: 2.5 to 5 mg subcutaneously up to every 15 minutes
- Higher doses may be needed but should be prescribed with palliative care specialist input

Terminal agitation can be very difficult to manage. Early involvement of the palliative care team is recommended.

---

## Involvement of the Palliative Care Team

Your doctor will seek palliative care support and advice when:
- The patient has terminal-phase agitation
- The patient is in the last days of life with unrelieved agitation or extreme fear
- Anticipatory planning or acute management is needed
- A joint visit from a palliative care specialist would be helpful
- Admission to hospice is being considered

---

## Working with Your Doctor

Anxiety and agitation in palliative care often have multiple overlapping causes. Your doctor and care team will work with you and your family to identify what is driving the distress and to find the most effective combination of support, symptom relief, and, where needed, medication. Open conversations about fears and wishes are an important part of this care.
