# Chest Pain

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to chest pain in adults - assessing the cause and determining urgency.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department if:

- Significantly abnormal vital signs
- Collapse or respiratory compromise
- Suspected aortic dissection (sudden onset tearing chest pain, pulse deficit, blood pressure differences between arms)
- Suspected pulmonary embolism with haemodynamic instability

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Pain site, onset, duration, character, severity, radiation, and what makes it better or worse
- Cardiovascular risk factors (smoking, family history of premature coronary artery disease, diabetes, high cholesterol, high blood pressure)
- In Māori or Pacific patients, cardiovascular disease is more common and presents at a younger age

Physical examination typically includes:

- General appearance (sweating, pallor)
- Blood pressure, oxygen saturation, temperature, respiratory rate, pulse rate and rhythm
- Peripheral pulses
- Checking for signs of deep vein thrombosis
- Chest palpation to see if pressure reproduces the pain
- Heart and lung examination

An **ECG** is performed for all but the most trivial chest pain. A normal ECG does not exclude acute coronary syndrome.

---

## Possible Causes

### Acute Coronary Syndrome (ACS)

More likely if:
- Pain predominantly in the chest lasting longer than 15 minutes
- Pain radiates to the arm or shoulder
- Associated sweating
- Previously stable angina with abrupt worsening

Less likely if:
- Pain occurs or worsens with breathing in (pleuritic)
- Pain can be reproduced by pressing on the chest

### Oesophageal Rupture

- Can result from medical instrumentation or repeated retching/vomiting (Boerhaave syndrome)
- Typically sudden onset of severe chest pain after repeated vomiting
- Pain may radiate to the back or left shoulder

### Pneumothorax

- Symptoms range from mild shortness of breath with or without pleuritic chest pain to cardiovascular compromise
- Signs may include reduced chest movement and diminished breath sounds on the affected side

### Pulmonary Embolism (PE)

Suspect if unexplained chest pain, shortness of breath, low oxygen levels, collapse, raised jugular venous pressure, fast heart rate, or abnormal heart rhythm.

### Thoracic Aortic Dissection

High risk features include:
- Known Marfan syndrome, aortic valve disease, or thoracic aortic aneurysm
- Chest or abdominal pain of abrupt onset, severe intensity, ripping or tearing character
- Pulse deficit or blood pressure difference between arms

### Pericarditis

Diagnosis requires at least 2 of:
- Sharp, pleuritic chest pain improved by sitting up and leaning forward
- Pericardial friction rub
- ECG changes (widespread ST-segment elevation or PR depression)
- New or worsening pericardial effusion

### Musculoskeletal

- Aggravated by physical activity, movement, or coughing
- Associated with tenderness on pressing
- Consider rib fracture, costochondritis, or Tietze syndrome

### Gastrointestinal

- Related to food and gastrointestinal symptoms
- Can be difficult to distinguish from cardiac chest pain
- Consider gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD), oesophageal spasm, biliary colic, pancreatitis

### Other Causes

- Respiratory (e.g., pneumonia)
- Anxiety or panic attack
- Viral (e.g., herpes zoster/shingles)
- Breast pain
- Non-specific chest pain (common — often no identifiable cause)

---

## Management

Management depends on the identified or suspected cause:

- **ACS or suspected heart attack:** Emergency ambulance, aspirin, and immediate hospital assessment
- **Suspected aortic dissection:** Emergency department assessment
- **Suspected oesophageal rupture:** Urgent surgical assessment
- **Pulmonary embolism:** Urgent medical assessment
- **Pneumothorax:** If respiratory compromise, emergency assessment. If stable, your doctor may manage with monitoring and serial chest X-rays
- **Pericarditis:** Your doctor will seek cardiology advice
- **Musculoskeletal:** Symptomatic relief
- **GORD/gastrointestinal:** Treat the underlying cause
- **Anxiety/panic:** Address the underlying condition

Once a serious cause has been ruled out, your doctor will provide reassurance and symptom relief.
