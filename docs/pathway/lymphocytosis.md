# Lymphocytosis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Lymphocytosis means the lymphocyte count in your blood is higher than normal. It is a common finding. Your doctor will assess whether this is a short-lived (reactive) change or something that needs further investigation.

---

## Background

Peripheral blood lymphocytosis is a common finding.

- **Acute (transient) lymphocytosis** is usually due to infections or acute bone marrow stress — it typically settles within weeks.
- **Persistent lymphocytosis** may be caused by autoimmune conditions, smoking, post-splenectomy state, or — less commonly — blood cancers such as leukaemia or lymphoma.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you with the following approach:

### History

Your doctor will ask about symptoms that might indicate an underlying cause:

- Lethargy
- Fevers
- Night sweats
- Weight loss

### Examination

Your doctor will examine you for signs of:

- Infection (low neutrophil count / neutropenia)
- Inflammation
- Pallor (suggesting anaemia)
- Bruising or petechiae (suggesting low platelets / thrombocytopenia)
- Enlarged lymph nodes (lymphadenopathy) or an enlarged spleen or liver (hepatosplenomegaly), if a lymphoproliferative disorder is suspected

### Blood Tests and Investigation

Your doctor will:

- Review the rest of your full blood count (haemoglobin, platelets, neutrophil count) and the blood film comment
- Arrange a blood film examination if not already done automatically and there is concern about an underlying lymphoproliferative disorder (a blood film is automatically examined by the lab if lymphocytes are 7 x 10⁹/L or more)
- Consider and investigate possible causes based on the clinical picture:

**Causes of acute lymphocytosis:**

Infections:
- Epstein-Barr virus (EBV) / Infectious Mononucleosis
- Cytomegalovirus (CMV)
- Viral hepatitis
- HIV/AIDS
- Pertussis (whooping cough)
- Toxoplasmosis
- Tuberculosis
- Brucellosis

Acute bone marrow stressors:
- Heart attack or cardiac arrest
- Trauma
- Status epilepticus
- Vigorous exercise
- Adrenaline administration

**Causes of persistent lymphocytosis:**
- Autoimmune conditions (e.g., rheumatoid arthritis, autoimmune thyroiditis)
- Smoking
- Post-splenectomy state
- Monoclonal B-cell lymphocytosis (a clonal lymphocytosis similar to B-CLL, but with fewer than 5 x 10⁹/L clonal lymphocytes and without other features of B-CLL)
- B-Cell Chronic Lymphocytic Leukaemia (B-CLL)
- Other T-cell or B-cell lymphoproliferative disorders (e.g., mantle cell lymphoma, splenic marginal zone lymphoma)

If lymphocytosis of 7 x 10⁹/L or more persists for more than 3 months with no underlying cause found, your doctor will arrange **immunophenotyping** — a specialised blood test to check for an abnormal (clonal) cell population.

---

## Management

Your doctor will manage lymphocytosis as follows:

### Immediate referral to haematology (non-acute)

Your doctor will refer you to a haematologist if you have lymphocytosis with any of the following:

- Persistent unexplained enlarged lymph nodes or enlarged spleen/liver
- Unexplained anaemia, low neutrophil count, or low platelets
- Severe constitutional symptoms: weight loss of more than 10% within 3 months, drenching night sweats, or unexplained fevers (after infection is excluded)
- Blood film or immunophenotyping results that suggest referral

### Monitoring if well or mildly symptomatic

If you are well or have only mild symptoms, your doctor will recheck your white cell count in **2 months**.

- Reactive lymphocytosis usually resolves within 2 months.
- If it persists, your blood count will be rechecked at 3 months.

### If lymphocytosis ≥7 x 10⁹/L persists beyond 3 months

Your doctor will:

1. Reconsider possible causes (autoimmune conditions, smoking, post-splenectomy, CLL, other lymphoproliferative disorders)
2. Arrange **immunophenotyping** to check for a clonal (abnormal) cell population

**After immunophenotyping results:**

- If a **clonal B-cell population** is confirmed: your doctor will follow the B-Cell Chronic Lymphocytic Leukaemia (B-CLL) pathway.
- If a **clonal T-cell population** is confirmed: your doctor will refer you to haematology.
- If **no clonal population** is found and you remain well with a high but stable lymphocyte count:
  - Monitoring every 6 months for 2 years, then annually if stable
  - Treatment decisions are based on disease progression, not the absolute lymphocyte number

Your doctor will seek specialist haematology advice if there is any uncertainty about the right management.

---

## Key Thresholds

| Lymphocyte count | Action |
|---|---|
| Any elevation | Assess with history, examination, and full blood count review |
| ≥7 x 10⁹/L | Blood film automatically examined by lab |
| ≥7 x 10⁹/L for >3 months | Arrange immunophenotyping |
| Any level with red flag features | Non-acute haematology referral |
