# Urinary Incontinence in Men

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of urinary incontinence in men, including identifying the type of incontinence and appropriate treatment.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Review

Contact your doctor urgently or go to the emergency department if you experience:

- Urinary retention with overflow (unable to pass urine, with leakage)
- Sudden (acute) onset of incontinence

---

## Background

Urinary incontinence in men occurs at an older age and about half as often as in women. However, men with urinary incontinence are half as likely as women to seek assessment from a healthcare provider.

Common causes include urinary tract infection (UTI), overactive bladder, chronic urinary retention with overflow, and complications from prostate surgery (radical prostatectomy or, rarely, TURP).

New urinary incontinence in men is nearly always due to prostate or urethral disease, or occasionally a more serious underlying condition.

---

## Types of Urinary Incontinence

| Type | Typical Features | Common Causes |
|------|-----------------|---------------|
| **Stress incontinence** | Leaking with coughing, sneezing, or lifting | Rare in men; usually follows prostate surgery or radiation therapy |
| **Urge incontinence (overactive bladder)** | Sudden strong urge to urinate; worse with caffeine and alcohol | Medications, enlarged prostate, diabetes, neurological disease (e.g. stroke) |
| **Overflow incontinence (chronic retention)** | Poor urinary stream; no warning before leakage; continuous or intermittent leakage including night-time | Atonic or neurogenic bladder; obstruction from enlarged prostate, pelvic mass, or faecal impaction |

## Assessment

Your healthcare provider will typically:

**Take a history asking about:**
- Onset, frequency, and severity of leakage (dribbling or flooding)
- Pattern of incontinence (sudden urge, with coughing, at random)
- Night-time bed wetting (enuresis)
- Blood in urine (haematuria)
- Pain or burning when urinating (dysuria)
- Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), which may suggest retention with overflow:
  - Obstructive: poor flow, hesitancy, terminal dribbling, overflow leakage
  - Irritative: frequency, dysuria, nocturia, urgency
- Bowel symptoms
- Medical history (diabetes, spinal cord disease, disc prolapse, stroke, multiple sclerosis)
- Previous prostate conditions or surgery
- Medications — commonly implicated include:
  - Diuretics
  - Antipsychotics
  - Acetylcholinesterase inhibitors (e.g. donepezil)
  - Sedatives (e.g. diazepam)
  - Tricyclic antidepressants (can cause retention, leading to overflow incontinence)

**Perform an examination:**
- BMI
- Abdomen for bladder size
- Digital rectal examination for prostate size
- External genitalia examination
- Neurological examination if indicated

**Arrange investigations:**
- Mid-stream urine (MSU) — dipstick and culture if infection is suspected
- Blood tests: creatinine, HbA1c
- PSA (prostate-specific antigen) if indicated
- Renal ultrasound if indicated

**Bladder diary:** Your doctor may ask you to keep a bladder diary for at least 3 days, covering both working and leisure days, to track your fluid intake and urination pattern.

## Management

### General Measures — For All Patients

Your healthcare provider will likely recommend:

- **Fluid intake:** Aim for 6–8 cups (1.5–2.0 L) per day including all drinks except alcohol
  - Limit caffeine to 2–3 cups per day; try decaffeinated alternatives
  - Aim for at least 4 glasses of water daily, spaced throughout the day
  - For nocturia, have your last drink 2–3 hours before bed and empty your bladder before sleeping
- **Weight loss** if BMI is above 30
- **Smoking cessation**

---

### Urge Incontinence (Overactive Bladder)

Your healthcare provider may recommend:

- Minimising caffeine intake
- **Bladder retraining** — a 6-week programme to gradually extend the time between toilet visits
- **Medication**, if lifestyle measures are insufficient:
  - *Oxybutynin* — typically started at 5 mg twice daily, increased to three times daily if needed (lower starting dose in the elderly)
  - *Solifenacin* — started at 5 mg once daily, increased to 10 mg if needed; may be better tolerated than oxybutynin in some patients
  - Note: Your doctor will consider the total anticholinergic burden when recommending these medications. The risk of urinary retention from these drugs is now considered uncommon in men.

---

### Overflow Incontinence / Chronic Retention

If you have chronic retention with symptoms of prostatic obstruction and a large residual bladder volume, your healthcare provider may recommend:

- **Alpha-1 blockers** (e.g. doxazosin) — relax smooth muscle in the prostate and bladder neck to improve urine flow. Postural hypotension is a possible side effect, particularly if already taking blood pressure medication.
- **Finasteride** — reduces prostate size over 6–12 months; also reduces PSA (usually by ~50%). Main side effect is sexual dysfunction. Less effective than alpha-1 blockers for short-term relief but reduces the need for surgery (TURP) long term. Often combined with an alpha-1 blocker for larger prostates.
- If residual bladder volume is very high (greater than 400 mL), a urethral catheter may be inserted while awaiting specialist assessment.

---

### Post-Surgical Incontinence (After Prostatectomy or TURP)

If incontinence follows prostate surgery, your healthcare provider may recommend:

- **Pelvic floor exercises** — see [Continence NZ: Pelvic Floor Training for Men](https://www.continence.org.nz/adults-information/pelvic-floor-training-for-men)
- Referral to urology for specialist assessment

---

### Recurrent UTI

If you have recurrent urinary tract infections alongside incontinence, your healthcare provider may advise:

- Increasing fluid intake (around 1.5 L per day) and urinating more frequently
- Double voiding (urinating, waiting a moment, then trying again)
- Note: D-mannose, probiotics, and cranberry products are not well supported by evidence in men
- **Prophylactic antibiotics** in some cases:
  - If a trigger can be identified (e.g. intercourse): take an antibiotic within 2 hours of the trigger
  - If no trigger is identified: a 3–6 month course of low-dose prophylactic antibiotic (e.g. nitrofurantoin 50 mg or trimethoprim 150 mg daily); quinolones are avoided

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your healthcare provider will refer you to urology or neurology when:

- Renal ultrasound shows hydronephrosis (urgent urology review)
- Residual bladder volume is very high (greater than 400 mL) with suspected overflow incontinence
- Incontinence is significant or persistent despite initial management
- Recurrent UTI is confirmed
- Acute neurological cause is suspected (e.g. spinal cord compression) — urgent neurology review
- Non-acute neurological cause is suspected (e.g. multiple sclerosis)
- PSA is raised for age or prostate feels abnormal on examination — discuss prostate cancer pathway
