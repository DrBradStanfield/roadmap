# Ultrasound Neck and Thyroid

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers when ultrasound of the neck or thyroid is recommended, what red flags to watch for, and what alternative assessments may be needed.

---

## Red Flags for Thyroid Cancer — Seek Urgent Assessment

Your doctor will arrange urgent assessment (within 2 weeks) if thyroid swelling is present **and** any of the following apply:

- Unexplained voice change or stridor (noisy breathing)
- Thyroid nodule in a child or young person
- Cervical lymphadenopathy (swollen lymph nodes in the neck)
- Painless thyroid mass that has been rapidly enlarging over 2–3 months
- Family history of multiple endocrine neoplasia
- Previous biopsy (cytology) result indicating high risk of cancer (Bethesda 5 or 6)

Note: A Bethesda 4 result does not automatically mean high suspicion of cancer, but review by an endocrinologist or surgeon is still required — the risk of malignancy is up to 30%.

---

## Compressive Symptoms — Seek Urgent Assessment

If a goitre or thyroid swelling is causing severe compressive symptoms, emergency assessment is needed. These symptoms include:

- **Hoarseness** — may indicate compression or infiltration of the recurrent laryngeal nerve
- **Dysphagia** (difficulty swallowing)
- **Airway obstruction** — dyspnoea (often worsening with exertion or when leaning forward), stridor or wheezing at rest when compression is severe, cough or choking sensation
- **Superior vena cava obstruction** (from a large retrosternal thyroid): facial oedema, distended neck and chest veins, periorbital oedema, facial plethora, arm oedema, cyanosis, or increased respiratory rate

Mild to moderate compressive symptoms are managed differently — your doctor will assess the severity and refer accordingly.

## When Ultrasound Is Recommended

Your doctor may consider referring you for a neck or thyroid ultrasound in the following situations:

### Neck Masses (Adults)

An urgent ultrasound (within 2 weeks) is arranged if a neck mass meets **all** of the following:

- Present for more than 3 weeks, **and**
- Larger than 1 cm in diameter, **and**
- No obvious cause identified
- The mass is solitary and non-pulsatile

### Palpable Thyroid Nodules

Your doctor may consider an urgent ultrasound (within 2 weeks) if:

- You have a palpable thyroid nodule with red flags for thyroid cancer (see above), or
- A secondary care specialist has recommended urgent ultrasound

Your doctor may consider a routine ultrasound if:

- You have a palpable thyroid nodule and are euthyroid (normal thyroid function), and
- The nodule has not previously been imaged

### Incidental Thyroid Nodules (Found on CT or MRI)

Your doctor may consider arranging ultrasound if a thyroid nodule has been found incidentally on a CT or MRI scan:

- **FDG-avid nodule on PET-CT** — urgent ultrasound within 2 weeks regardless of nodule size. (An FDG-avid nodule shows increased uptake of the PET tracer, which can reflect increased metabolic activity from either benign inflammation or malignancy.)
- **Solitary nodule on CT or MRI with normal or elevated TSH** — routine ultrasound may be arranged if:
  - You are younger than 35 and the nodule is larger than 10 mm, or
  - You are aged 35 or older and the nodule is larger than 15 mm, or
  - The CT or MRI report describes the nodule as abnormal
- **Multiple thyroid nodules on CT or MRI with normal or elevated TSH** — routine ultrasound may be arranged if one nodule is 20 mm or larger

### Follow-up of Known Thyroid Nodules

If a previous scan or specialist has recommended follow-up imaging for a high-risk thyroid nodule, your doctor will arrange an ultrasound at the recommended timeframe.

---

## When Ultrasound Is Not Recommended

In some situations, alternative investigations are more appropriate:

- **Multiple neck masses or suspected metastatic lymphadenopathy** — managed according to the neck lumps pathway rather than thyroid ultrasound
- **Incidental thyroid nodule on CT or MRI with low TSH (suppressed)** — a radionuclide thyroid scan is more appropriate than ultrasound; your doctor may seek endocrinology advice first
- **Goitre or thyroid swelling with severe compressive symptoms** — requires emergency surgical (ORL/head and neck) assessment rather than an outpatient ultrasound
- **Hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism** — managed according to dedicated thyroid pathways

### Situations Where Ultrasound May Not Be Publicly Funded

Your doctor may discuss whether imaging is appropriate if:

- You have significant co-morbidities and limited life expectancy — the clinical benefit of investigating an incidental nodule may be limited
- A solitary incidental nodule on CT or MRI is smaller than 10 mm and there are no red flags for thyroid cancer on imaging or examination
- Multiple incidental thyroid nodules are all smaller than 20 mm, appear likely benign on CT or MRI, and there are no red flags for thyroid cancer

In these situations, your doctor will discuss the options with you, including whether privately funded imaging is appropriate.
