# Renal Ultrasound

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

A renal ultrasound is an imaging test that uses sound waves to look at the kidneys, ureters, and bladder. Your doctor may recommend one when there are signs that the kidneys need closer investigation.

---

## When Your Doctor May Consider a Renal Ultrasound

### In Adults

Your doctor may consider ordering a renal ultrasound if you have:

**Protein in the urine (proteinuria or albuminuria)**
- Significant protein detected on testing (protein:creatinine ratio > 50 mg/mmol, or albumin:creatinine ratio > 30 mg/mmol)
- Note: Repeat ultrasound in diabetic patients with known diabetic kidney complications is generally not needed — your doctor will follow a separate diabetes kidney disease pathway

**Reduced kidney function (low eGFR)**
- eGFR below 30
- eGFR below 45 if you also have diabetes
- Progressive chronic kidney disease — a sustained decline in eGFR of 5 or more within the previous 12 months, particularly if eGFR is below 45 or there is severe proteinuria (ACR > 250). These patients have a higher risk of developing end-stage kidney disease requiring dialysis or transplant

**Blood in the urine (haematuria)**
- Persistent microscopic blood in the urine found on two or more urine microscopy tests (not dipstick) more than 2 weeks apart
  - Men: more than 15 × 10⁶/L red blood cells
  - Women: more than 35 × 10⁶/L red blood cells (with low epithelial and white cell counts)
- Visible blood in the urine (macroscopic haematuria)

**Polycystic kidney disease (family history)**
- If one or more first-degree relatives have polycystic kidneys, your doctor may arrange an ultrasound when you turn 30
- Repeat ultrasounds are not routinely needed unless the radiology report recommends it, or new symptoms develop

**Urinary tract infections (UTIs) — women**
- Recurrent UTIs (more than 3 in 12 months, or more than 2 in 6 months) despite preventive antibiotics
- An ongoing UTI with significant symptoms that has not resolved with antibiotics
- UTI caused by *Proteus*, *Klebsiella*, or *Pseudomonas* bacteria — these organisms are associated with a higher risk of kidney stones

**Urinary tract infections — men**
- Recurrent or persistent infections that have not previously been investigated with imaging
- Enlarged prostate or elevated PSA alongside infection

**Pyelonephritis (kidney infection)**
- If no kidney tract imaging has been done in the last 6 months and:
  - A man has had acute pyelonephritis
  - A woman has had 2 or more episodes of acute pyelonephritis within the last 12 months

**Other indications**
- Loin pain suggesting a blockage at the pelvi-ureteric junction (PUJ obstruction)
- Chronic urinary retention with a palpably enlarged bladder
- Suspected kidney stones in a pregnant woman
- Lower urinary tract symptoms in men, if there is also an enlarged bladder, a palpable abdominal or pelvic mass, or reduced kidney function thought to be caused by urinary retention

---

### In Children

Your doctor may consider ordering a renal ultrasound in children if they have:

**Urinary tract infections**
- First UTI in a child under 6 months of age
- Recurrent UTIs (2 or more confirmed episodes, or one confirmed plus a strongly suspected earlier episode)
- Atypical infection features (e.g., seriously unwell, poor urine stream, abdominal mass, sepsis, high blood pressure, poor response to treatment at 48 hours, or immunosuppression) — your doctor will also arrange urgent paediatric assessment
- Note: The ultrasound is usually requested at least 6 weeks after the acute infection has resolved

**Antenatal kidney findings**
- If renal tract widening was found on your baby's antenatal scans, your doctor or midwife will arrange follow-up ultrasound imaging after birth

**Blood in the urine (haematuria)**
- Visible, painless blood in the urine with no other obvious cause
- Persistent microscopic blood on urine microscopy for more than 1 year, without protein in the urine, and with elevated urinary calcium

**Protein in the urine (proteinuria)**
- Confirmed on an early morning protein:creatinine ratio when the child is well

**Loin mass or loin pain** with a suspected kidney cause

## When a Renal Ultrasound Is Not the Right Test

Your doctor will consider alternative investigations or pathways if you have:

- **Suspected renal colic (kidney stone pain) and you are not pregnant** — a different scan (CT KUB) is usually more appropriate
- **Acute kidney injury** — managed through a separate pathway
- **Enlarged prostate or elevated PSA** — assessed through a prostate cancer diagnosis pathway
- **Children under 3 years with atypical infection features** — urgent paediatric assessment is arranged first
- **Microscopic blood and protein together (in children)** — paediatric medical assessment is needed
- **Visible blood with pain (in children)** — paediatric surgical assessment is needed
- **Asymptomatic bacteriuria (bacteria in urine without symptoms, in children)** — ultrasound is not indicated

If your situation does not clearly fit the standard criteria but your doctor still suspects an ultrasound may be needed, they will seek advice from a relevant hospital specialist before proceeding.
