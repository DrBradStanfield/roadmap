# Impetigo (School Sores)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of impetigo (commonly called school sores) — a highly contagious bacterial skin infection most often seen in children.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

See a doctor urgently or call for emergency help if any of the following apply:

- Suspected systemic infection (with or without fever or fast heart rate)
- Child is younger than 3 months
- The child is immunocompromised (e.g., on chemotherapy, has HIV, or a condition affecting the immune system)

---

## Background

- Impetigo presents as discrete sores or lesions with clear blisters, pus, or crusts — often described as "school sores."
- It is **highly contagious** and can spread from person to person or appear seemingly out of nowhere.
- It often begins at a site of minor skin injury — a graze, insect bite, or scratched eczema.
- The most common causes are _Staphylococcus aureus_ and/or _Streptococcus pyogenes_.
- **Non-bullous impetigo** (90% of cases): usually caused by _S. aureus_. Systemic signs are uncommon unless infection is extensive.
- **Bullous impetigo**: caused by _S. aureus_, most often in infants, and more likely to be associated with fever or swollen lymph nodes.
- In rare cases, impetigo caused by _Streptococcus_ can increase the risk of rheumatic fever or post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.
- New Zealand has some of the highest rates of serious skin infections — children, Māori, and Pacific peoples are disproportionately affected, often due to overcrowding and reduced access to healthcare.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider impetigo if the child has:

- Pustules and round, oozing patches that grow larger day by day
- Clear blisters (bullous impetigo) or golden-yellow crusts

**History your doctor will take:**

- Duration and progression of lesions
- Symptoms — pain, itch, discharge, crusting
- Systemic symptoms — fever, vomiting, chills, lethargy, inability to drink
- Recent skin injuries — insect bites, trauma, scratching
- Any current skin treatments being applied
- Previous episodes of impetigo
- History of eczema in the child or family
- Whether other household members have similar symptoms
- Allergies
- Whether the child is immunocompromised

**Examination:**

- Temperature and heart rate
- Location, number, and size of lesions

**Investigations:**

- Your doctor may take a wound swab if considering oral antibiotics — this helps guide antibiotic choice.
- If there are risk factors for MRSA (methicillin-resistant _Staphylococcus aureus_), a specific MRSA swab may be taken. Risk factors include:
  - Frequent courses of antibiotics
  - Infection not responding to normal antibiotic treatment
  - Family history of MRSA
  - Recent stay in a high-risk hospital (e.g., overseas hospital)
  - Crowded living conditions
  - Open or broken skin

## Management

### Urgent referral

Your doctor will seek urgent paediatric assessment if any red flags are present (see above).

If there are signs of spreading infection such as cellulitis, this will be managed separately.

### Wound care

Your doctor may advise the following wound care measures:

- **Soak off crusted areas:** For one or two sores, use a moist clean cloth or running tap water. For many or large sores, daily baths with a small amount of diluted bleach (a quarter cup in a full bath) for up to 7 days.
- **Topical antiseptic** for small, localised lesions: povidone-iodine ointment (Betadine) or hydrogen peroxide cream (Crystaderm) applied 2–3 times daily after soaking, for 5–7 days.
- Keep affected areas covered with a breathable dressing — preferably a fabric plaster or gauze.
- Apply emollient (moisturiser) to dry or itchy areas of skin.

### Oral antibiotics

Your doctor may consider oral antibiotics if:

- There are multiple lesions, or lesions larger than a 10-cent coin (moderate to severe impetigo), **or**
- The sores are not responding to wound care and topical antiseptics.

**First-line options your doctor may consider:**

- **Cefalexin** — palatable suspension, well tolerated, funded. Typically 12.5–25 mg/kg twice daily (max 1 g per dose) for 5–7 days.
- **Flucloxacillin** — very effective against _S. aureus_. Typically 12.5 mg/kg four times daily (max 500 mg per dose) for 7–10 days. The suspension is unpalatable, so usually used as capsules only.

**If penicillin allergy:**

- **Erythromycin** ethylsuccinate — 10–12.5 mg/kg four times daily (max 400 mg per dose).
- **Roxithromycin** — for children over 12 years who can swallow tablets. 2.5–4 mg/kg twice daily (max 150 mg per dose).

**If confirmed MRSA:**

- **Co-trimoxazole** — 24 mg/kg twice daily (max 960 mg per dose).
- **Mupirocin** (Bactroban) may be considered for small, localised MRSA impetigo only.

**Note:** Topical antibiotics such as fusidic acid are generally not recommended routinely due to increasing antibiotic resistance.

### School and childcare exclusion

If the child has open sores that cannot be covered, they should stay away from school until the sores have dried up, or until at least 24 hours after starting oral antibiotics.

### Follow-up

Your doctor will typically recommend a review:

- 3 days after starting antibiotics if not improving
- 48 hours if the child is an infant
- At any time if the condition worsens

**Watch for:** If the child develops any new or unusual symptoms in the 2–4 weeks after impetigo (e.g., swollen joints, dark urine, swelling of the face or body), seek review — these may indicate rheumatic fever or glomerulonephritis.

## Hygiene Measures to Prevent Spread

Your doctor may advise the following to prevent the infection spreading to others:

- Keep discharging wounds covered with clean, dry plasters or bandages
- Wash hands with soap and water (or alcohol hand gel) regularly, especially after touching infected skin
- Avoid sharing baths, washing water, or swimming with others
- Avoid sharing personal items — towels, sheets, razors
- Check and treat all household members who have symptoms
- Clean "high-touch" surfaces frequently — door knobs, benches, basins, toilets, bath tubs
- Hot-wash towels, bedding, and clothing of those infected
- Use a spoon or spatula when removing cream from shared containers — do not contaminate the jar
- Complete the full antibiotic course

---

## Recurrent Impetigo

If impetigo keeps coming back, your doctor may:

1. Check that any underlying eczema is well controlled — this is a significant contributor to recurrent skin infections.
2. Reinforce household hygiene strategies (see above).
3. Consider **decolonisation** if _Staphylococcus aureus_ has been cultured:
   - **Bleach baths** 2–3 times per week — diluted sodium hypochlorite solution. Not suitable if there are extensive areas of broken skin or chlorine allergy.
   - **Chlorhexidine washes** may be used if no bath is available (not recommended in eczema).
   - Assess and treat all household contacts.
   - **Nasal swabbing** may be performed if there are recurrent episodes — if _S. aureus_ is found in the nose, intranasal antibiotics may be considered:
     - **Fusidic acid cream or ointment** (Foban) — first-line, applied three times daily for 7 days.
     - **Mupirocin ointment** (Bactroban) — reserved for confirmed MRSA.
