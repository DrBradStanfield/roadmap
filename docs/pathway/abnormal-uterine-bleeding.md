# Abnormal Uterine Bleeding

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB) in non-pregnant women of reproductive age. See also related pathways for postcoital bleeding, postmenopausal bleeding, and dysmenorrhoea.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if:

- Heavy bleeding causing haemodynamic instability (faintness, collapse, very low blood pressure, rapid heart rate)

Seek urgent medical assessment (same day or within 24 hours) if:

- Soaking through pads very rapidly with symptoms of anaemia (dizziness, weakness, pallor)

---

## Background

Abnormal uterine bleeding (AUB) affects up to 1 in 3 women of reproductive age at some stage and can significantly impact quality of life.

**Definitions:**

- **Normal period:** lasts 3–8 days, cycle length 24–38 days
- **AUB:** bleeding that is abnormal in regularity, volume, frequency, or duration, in the absence of pregnancy
  - *Acute AUB* — heavy bleeding requiring immediate intervention
  - *Chronic AUB* — present for most of the previous 6 months
- **Heavy menstrual bleeding (HMB):** excessive blood loss interfering with daily life
- **Intermenstrual bleeding (IMB):** vaginal bleeding between periods or after intercourse
- **Prolonged bleeding:** lasting longer than 8 days

**Regularity:**
- Cycles are regular if variation between shortest and longest is 9 days or less (ages 18–25 and 42–45) or 7 days or less (ages 26–41)
- Irregular cycles have variation of 10 days or more
- Secondary amenorrhoea: absence of periods for 6 or more months in a woman who was previously menstruating

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Regularity, frequency, volume, and duration of bleeding
- Bleeding between periods or after intercourse
- Pelvic pain or pressure
- Impact on daily life and quality of life (a menstrual diary may be helpful)
- Symptoms of anaemia (dizziness, fatigue, pallor)
- Sexual and reproductive history
- Symptoms of thyroid disease, PCOS, or other systemic conditions

**Risk factors for endometrial cancer or hyperplasia your doctor will consider:**

- BMI 30 or higher (risk increases proportionally with BMI)
- Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
- Chronic anovulation (fewer than 4 menstrual cycles per year)
- Nulliparity in women over 35
- Early menarche or late menopause (after age 55)
- Long-standing unopposed oestrogen menopause hormone therapy (MHT)
- Tamoxifen therapy
- Diabetes mellitus
- Iron deficiency anaemia (Hb less than 100)
- Family history of endometrial, ovarian, breast, or bowel cancer
- Increasing age (highest premenopausal incidence in women aged 45–50)

**Medications and devices that can cause AUB:**

- Anticoagulants
- Antidepressants (SSRIs, tricyclics)
- Antipsychotics (e.g. olanzapine, risperidone)
- Corticosteroids
- Copper IUD
- Hormonal contraceptives (progesterone-only pill, Jadelle, Mirena in first 6 months, Depo-Provera)
- Menopause hormone therapy (breakthrough bleeding common in first 3–6 months)
- Tamoxifen

Physical examination typically includes:

- General assessment: pulse, blood pressure, BMI, signs of androgen excess (hirsutism, acne)
- Abdominal examination
- Gynaecological examination (with informed consent): bimanual and speculum examination; cervical co-test (HPV and cytology) if not done in the last 6 months

## Possible Causes

### Structural causes (PALM)
- **Polyps** — endometrial or cervical polyps
- **Adenomyosis** — endometrial tissue within the uterine muscle; often associated with endometriosis; may cause heavy periods and dysmenorrhoea
- **Leiomyoma (fibroids)** — common benign uterine growths; symptoms depend on size and location
- **Malignancy and hyperplasia** — endometrial cancer or precancerous changes

### Non-structural causes (COEIN)
- **Coagulopathy** — bleeding disorders (e.g. von Willebrand disease)
- **Ovulatory dysfunction** — PCOS, hypothyroidism, hyperprolactinaemia, adrenal or hypothalamic disorders
- **Endometrial** — primary dysfunction of endometrial haemostasis
- **Iatrogenic** — hormonal contraception or medications
- **Not otherwise classified** — e.g. Caesarean scar niche (a pouch-like defect at the site of a previous Caesarean section)

### Non-uterine (lower genital tract) causes
- Vulvovaginal atrophy or atrophic vaginitis (vaginal dryness, irritation, mild bleeding)
- Vulval dermatoses
- Sexually transmitted infections (e.g. chlamydia)
- Malignancy of cervix, vagina, or vulva
- Cervical ectropion
- Urethral causes (e.g. urethral caruncle)

## Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- **Pregnancy test** if clinically indicated
- **STI swabs** if signs, symptoms, or risk factors for sexually transmitted infection
- **Full blood count (FBC) and ferritin** to check for anaemia
- **HbA1c** if risk factors for diabetes are present
- **TSH** if thyroid disease is suspected
- **Coagulation screening** (INR, APTT, fibrinogen) if heavy periods since menarche and a personal or family history of a clotting disorder
- **Investigations for PCOS** if indicated

**Pelvic ultrasound** may be arranged if:

- A pelvic mass is suspected on examination
- Two or more risk factors for endometrial cancer or hyperplasia are present (urgent, within 2 weeks)
- One or more risk factors for endometrial cancer in a woman under 45
- No response to at least 3 months of first-line treatment (under 45)
- Aged 45 or older (routine indication)

Pelvic ultrasound is useful for identifying structural causes such as fibroids and polyps. Endometrial thickness alone should not be used to determine cancer risk.

**Pipelle biopsy** (endometrial sampling) may be performed if:

- Aged 20–30 with multiple risk factors or excess oestrogen exposure (case by case)
- Aged 30–44 with any risk factors for endometrial cancer
- All women aged 45 or older with AUB
- Pelvic ultrasound suggests endometrial cancer

## Management

Your doctor may consider:

### Acute (active) bleeding

- **Norethisterone** (Primolut N) 5 mg three times a day for 10 days, or **medroxyprogesterone acetate** (Provera) to stop prolonged bleeding — a withdrawal bleed will occur after stopping
- **Tranexamic acid** 1 g three times a day for 3–5 days to reduce heavy bleeding

### Ongoing management — non-hormonal options

More effective when bleeding is predictable or cyclic:

- **Tranexamic acid** 1 g three to four times a day for 3–5 days (started at or just before onset of menses)
- **NSAIDs** (e.g. ibuprofen 400 mg three times a day) started at menses onset and continued regularly for the first 3–4 days

### Ongoing management — hormonal options

- **Levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system (Mirena IUS)** — first-line for long-term management when structural abnormality of the uterus is not suspected; reduces bleeding significantly
- **Combined oral contraceptive pill (COC)** — tri-cyclical or continuous use recommended; continuous use is considered safe and does not cause blood to accumulate in the womb
- **Norethisterone** (starting 5 mg twice daily) or **medroxyprogesterone acetate / Provera** (starting 10 mg once daily), used from day 5 to day 25 of the cycle — manages bleeding but does not provide contraception
- **Depo-Provera** (injected depot medroxyprogesterone acetate) — may take several injections to achieve reduced or absent bleeding; associated with reduced bone density with long-term use

### Condition-specific management

**Fibroids:** Your doctor will individualise treatment based on size, location, and symptoms. Medical management with non-hormonal and/or hormonal options is tried first. Referral to gynaecology is considered if fibroids are large (uterus palpable on examination, or fibroid 8 cm or more on ultrasound) or symptoms persist after 3–6 months of treatment.

**Adenomyosis:** Managed with non-hormonal and/or hormonal options. Mirena is often effective.

**Caesarean scar niche:** If not planning pregnancy, non-hormonal or hormonal treatment for symptoms. If fertility is desired, gynaecology referral is recommended.

**Chronic endometritis** (diagnosed on pipelle biopsy): Your doctor may consider **doxycycline** 100 mg twice a day for 10–14 days.

**Endometrial hyperplasia without atypia:** First-line treatment is **Mirena** for a minimum of 12 months. If Mirena is unsuitable, **medroxyprogesterone (Provera)** 30 mg per day or **norethisterone** (up to 15 mg per day) on a continuous regimen may be used. Follow-up includes repeat pipelle biopsy at 6 and 12 months to confirm resolution.

**Genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM):** If vaginal atrophy is contributing to bleeding, your doctor may prescribe **vaginal oestrogen** (e.g. estriol cream). Vaginal moisturisers and lubricants can also help with symptoms.

### Co-existing conditions

Your doctor may also address:

- **Iron deficiency anaemia** — oral iron or IV iron infusion
- **Dysmenorrhoea** — painful periods
- **Depression and anxiety** — commonly associated with AUB

### Modifiable risk factors

Your doctor may discuss actively managing factors that significantly increase the risk of endometrial hyperplasia or cancer:

- **Obesity** (BMI 30+ carries a relative risk of 2.18–7.82 depending on degree)
- **Diabetes or prediabetes** (relative risk ~1.97)
- **PCOS / chronic anovulation** (increases risk of endometrial hyperplasia threefold; 9% lifetime risk of endometrial cancer)

## When Specialist Referral May Be Needed

Your doctor may refer to gynaecology if:

- Haemodynamic instability due to blood loss
- Cervical appearance or HPV co-test is abnormal (urgent colposcopy)
- Pelvic ultrasound suggests structural pathology (endometrial polyps, endometrial thickness 16 mm or more, cystic spaces, hypervascular endometrium, focal lesions on tamoxifen, symptomatic submucosal fibroids)
- Pipelle biopsy shows inadequate specimen, atypical hyperplasia, or endometrial cancer
- Patient has Lynch or Cowden syndrome
- AUB persists despite 3–6 months of medical management
- Large fibroids (uterus palpable, or fibroid 8 cm or more on imaging)
- Endometrial hyperplasia without atypia not responding to treatment

If malignancy is suspected, referral is marked urgent with "high suspicion of cancer."
