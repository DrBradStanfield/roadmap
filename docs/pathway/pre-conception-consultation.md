# Pre-conception Consultation

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers what to discuss and assess when planning a pregnancy — before conception occurs. More than half of all pregnancies are unplanned, so these topics are often woven into routine consultations opportunistically.

Always discuss your individual situation with your doctor or midwife.

---

## History Your Doctor Will Take

### Medical and Surgical History

Your doctor will ask about existing medical conditions. Some conditions need to be stabilised before pregnancy and may require specialist input. High-risk conditions include:

- Pre-existing heart disease (rheumatic heart disease, mechanical heart valves, cardiomyopathy, coronary artery disease)
- Kidney disease (congenital or pre-existing), chronic proteinuria
- Hypertension
- Autoimmune disease (e.g. Hashimoto thyroiditis, Graves' disease, lupus, Sjögren's syndrome, scleroderma, rheumatoid arthritis, ITP, primary biliary cirrhosis)
- Inflammatory bowel disease on current treatment
- Diabetes (type 1, type 2, or previous gestational diabetes)
- History of venous thromboembolism or clotting disorders (e.g. Factor V Leiden)
- Liver disease (e.g. hepatitis B or C, portal hypertension)
- Cystic fibrosis
- Personal or family history of perinatal mental health issues

Moderate-risk conditions include:
- Weight > 100 kg or BMI ≥ 30
- Family history of genetic abnormality
- Moderate to severe asthma
- Epilepsy
- Previous bariatric surgery or splenectomy
- Haematological conditions (low haemoglobin, small red cells, low ferritin, or low platelets)

### Obstetric History

- Previous pregnancies, miscarriages, or terminations
- How long you have been trying to conceive
- Menstrual and gynaecological history (e.g. PCOS, endometriosis)
- High-risk obstetric history: stillbirth, recurrent miscarriages, preterm birth before 30 weeks, pre-eclampsia or HELLP syndrome, previous baby with growth restriction (IUGR), placenta accreta, blood group antibodies, malignancy history, cervical incompetence, previous cervical surgery
- Moderate-risk obstetric history: age under 18 or 35 and over, previous caesarean section, severe hyperemesis, recurrent postpartum haemorrhage, PCOS

### Medications and Substances

- Current medications (some are unsafe in pregnancy and need to be changed before conception)
- Smoking and nicotine-containing vapes
- Alcohol use
- Cannabis, methamphetamine, and other drugs
- Chemicals, solvents, or radiation at work

### Genetic History

Your doctor will ask about personal or family genetic disorders in either parent, including:
- Previous pregnancy or child with a chromosomal anomaly or significant disability
- Family history of: inherited genetic conditions, thalassaemia, sickle cell anaemia, significant visual or hearing impairment, cleft palate, spina bifida or neural tube defects, cystic fibrosis, congenital hip dislocation
- Family history of pre-eclampsia
- If couples are first cousins or closer, genetic advice is recommended

---

## Examination

Your doctor will:
- Record baseline blood pressure, weight, and BMI
- Check for signs of undiagnosed conditions (e.g. heart murmur, thyroid abnormality, acanthosis nigricans)

---

## Investigations

### Routine Blood Tests

These are typically done at or before the first antenatal visit. Some will be repeated in pregnancy:
- Full blood count (FBC), blood group, and antibody screen
- Hepatitis B immunity
- Rubella immunity
- Syphilis serology
- HbA1c
- Ferritin — checked early so iron deficiency can be treated before anaemia develops. Early oral iron is preferable to IV iron infusions later in pregnancy.
- HIV screening — the risk of passing HIV to a baby is around 30% without treatment, but antiretroviral therapy in pregnancy reduces this to under 1%. HIV testing is now included in standard antenatal bloods and cannot be opted off; individual tests must be selected separately if a patient declines.

**Additional tests depending on your situation:**
- If vegetarian or vegan: vitamin B12 and urine culture
- If hypertension or kidney disease history: electrolytes, urea, creatinine, and possibly a urine creatinine ratio
- If thyroid disease history: TSH and free T4. Free T4 at the higher end of normal is preferred — fetal brain development depends on it until week 20.
- HIV testing for both partners if at risk
- STI swabs and cervical screening if due

### Contraception

Your doctor will discuss stopping current contraception, including removing IUCDs, implants (Jadelle), and discontinuing hormonal methods. You may be advised to wait for a regular menstrual cycle to return before trying to conceive.

## Management Advice

### Pre-existing Medical Conditions

If you have an existing medical condition, your doctor will aim to stabilise it at least 8 weeks before conception. All current medications should be reviewed for safety in pregnancy before you start trying.

### Genetic Risk

If there is a personal or family history of a genetic condition, your doctor may refer to Genetic Health Services. This includes checking whether genetic counselling, thalassaemia management, or antenatal screening (e.g. for Down syndrome) is appropriate.

### Fertility Timing

Your doctor can advise on the most fertile times in your cycle. If you have been trying to conceive without success, a sub-fertility assessment may be recommended.

### Weight and Nutrition

BMI affects fertility and pregnancy outcomes:
- **Underweight (BMI < 18.5):** increased risk of premature birth, small-for-gestational-age babies, nutritional deficiencies, and breastfeeding difficulties
- **Overweight (BMI > 25):** increased risk of stillbirth, neural tube defects, premature birth, gestational hypertension, pre-eclampsia, gestational diabetes, caesarean birth, postpartum haemorrhage, and depression

A healthy BMI improves the chance of conception and a healthy pregnancy. Your doctor may discuss diet and consider a referral to a dietitian.

### Exercise

- If already active: aim for 30 minutes of moderate exercise most days
- If not currently active: start with gentle exercise such as walking or swimming

### Supplementation

**Folic acid** — start at least 4 weeks before pregnancy and continue until week 12:
- **0.8 mg daily** for most people
- **5 mg daily** if you have any of the following:
  - Personal or family history of neural tube defect (in either parent)
  - BMI over 30
  - Diabetes
  - Coeliac disease or other malabsorption disorder
  - Sickle cell anaemia (take 5 mg throughout the entire pregnancy)
  - Epilepsy or taking anti-seizure medication
  - Autoimmune/rheumatic condition and taking a folate antagonist (e.g. sulfasalazine)
  - Tuberculosis and on folate antagonist treatment

**Iodine** — essential for fetal brain development:
- Recommended dose: potassium iodate 150 micrograms daily (fully subsidised in New Zealand)
- Start at conception and continue through breastfeeding
- Vegetarians and vegans who don't use iodised salt should ideally start iodine when planning pregnancy
- **Seaweed and kelp tablets are not recommended** — iodine content is extremely variable and can be toxic
- Not all antenatal vitamins contain the recommended iodine dose — check the label
- If you have a thyroid condition, discuss iodine with your doctor first — those who are hyperthyroid or on thyroxine may not need it

### Smoking

It is best to be tobacco and nicotine free before and during pregnancy. Your doctor can refer you to a stop smoking service. Options include:
- Nicotine replacement therapy (NRT) — if using patches, remove overnight
- Quitline support
- Vaping as a last resort if all else has failed — it is not harmless, and dual use of vaping and smoking is likely more harmful than either alone. Aim to stop smoking entirely, then work toward stopping vaping.

### Vaping

Your doctor will advise stopping vaping before or during pregnancy.

### Alcohol

There is no safe time, amount, or type of alcohol during pregnancy. Stop drinking if you could be pregnant, are pregnant, or are trying to conceive.

### Other Substances

Cannabis, methamphetamine, and other drugs can harm pregnancy outcomes. Your doctor can connect you with addiction support services.

### Men's Health and Sperm Quality

These lifestyle factors can reduce sperm quantity or quality:
- BMI over 29
- More than 2 standard alcoholic drinks per day
- Cigarette smoking
- Illicit drugs and some medications (e.g. sulfasalazine, calcium channel blockers)
- Exposure to chemicals, pesticides, or organic solvents

### Dental Care

It is recommended to ensure dental care is up to date before pregnancy.

## Immunisations

### Rubella and Measles

Your doctor will check rubella antibody levels. If levels are low (< 15):
- 2 previous MMR doses (documented): no action needed
- 1 previous MMR dose: one booster
- 0 previous MMR doses: 1–2 doses as appropriate
- No documentation: one dose given

MMR is a live vaccine and **cannot be given during pregnancy** — it must be given before conception.

### Varicella (Chickenpox)

If you have no history of chickenpox, varicella serology will be checked. If non-immune, vaccination is offered (not government-subsidised). **Avoid pregnancy for 3 months after vaccination.**

### Other Vaccines

Recommended for both the patient and their partner:
- Pertussis (whooping cough)
- Influenza
- COVID-19

---

## Referrals Your Doctor May Make

- **Genetic Health Services** — if personal or family history of genetic disorder, or if partners are first cousins or closer
- **Obstetric advice (written)** — if concerns about workplace chemical, solvent, or radiation exposure
- **Specialist addiction services** or drug and alcohol support — if concerns about alcohol or substance use
- **Dietitian** — if weight concerns or nutritional needs
- **Sub-fertility assessment** — if difficulty conceiving
