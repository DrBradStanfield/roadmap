# Constipation in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of constipation in children, from infants through to teenagers.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Attention

Contact your doctor urgently or go to the emergency department if your child has:

- Constipation in a newborn, or delayed passage of meconium (first stool) beyond 24 hours of birth
- Abdominal distension (swollen belly) with vomiting, especially if the vomit is green (bilious)
- New or worsening weakness or deformity of the legs or lower limbs
- Faecal impaction that has not responded to treatment at home

---

## Background

### What is constipation?

Constipation is the difficult passage of infrequent, hard stools. It is a common childhood problem that can cause distress in the child and anxiety for parents. Constipation is different from normal variation in stool frequency — it is constipation when it causes the child discomfort or makes them unwell.

Constipation is a risk factor for urinary tract infections (UTIs), bedwetting, faecal incontinence, and recurrent abdominal pain.

### What is faecal soiling?

"Overflow incontinence" or soiling is most commonly caused by faecal impaction — a large, hard mass of stool in the bowel. Soft stool leaks around the hard mass, causing soiling. This is not the child being naughty or lazy. Disimpaction treatment is needed.

### Normal stool patterns in children

- Breastfed babies may pass a stool after every feed, or as infrequently as every 7–10 days — both can be normal
- Bottle-fed babies and children over 1 year usually pass a stool at least every 2–3 days
- Straining or crying before passing a soft stool can be normal in young infants and is not necessarily constipation

### Why does constipation happen?

Constipation in childhood is almost always **functional** — meaning there is no underlying disease causing it. It is uncommon for there to be an organic (medical) cause.

Common contributors include:
- Passing a hard stool causes pain, so the child holds on to avoid the pain — this makes the stool harder, creating a cycle
- Poor diet (low fibre, excessive milk, low fluid intake)
- Toilet training, fear of the toilet, or poor access to toilets at school
- Stressful events (starting school, changes in family routine)
- Certain medications (see below)

Rare causes include Hirschsprung's disease, coeliac disease, hypothyroidism, inflammatory bowel disease, and spinal cord problems.

### Medications that can cause constipation

- Antacids containing aluminium or calcium
- Anticholinergics
- Antidepressants (tricyclics, SSRIs)
- Sedating antihistamines
- Antipsychotics (phenothiazines, clozapine, olanzapine, quetiapine)
- Atomoxetine
- Calcium supplements
- 5HT3 antagonists (e.g., ondansetron)
- Iron supplements
- Opioids, including codeine
- Proton pump inhibitors

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your child by:

**Taking a history**, including:
- How long the problem has been present, and any triggering events (change in diet, illness, dehydration, starting school, stressful events)
- Stool frequency, consistency, and size — fewer than 3 stools per week (except in breastfed babies) suggests constipation
- Whether passing stools is painful or distressing
- Any soiling, withholding behaviours (e.g., squeezing buttocks, refusing to sit on toilet), or loss of the urge to defecate
- Rectal bleeding, urinary incontinence, or recurrent UTIs
- Medications, growth concerns, family history (coeliac disease, Hirschsprung's disease)
- Diet and fluid intake, access to toilets at home and school

**Physical examination**, including:
- Height, weight, and growth (poor growth may suggest an underlying cause)
- Abdominal examination for distension, tenderness, or palpable stool
- Perianal area — checking for fissures, skin tags, or infection (digital rectal examination is not recommended)
- Neurological examination of the lower limbs and spine — looking for midline skin defects (dimples, hair tufts) that may suggest a spinal abnormality

**Investigations** are not usually needed — the diagnosis is made from the history and examination. If an organic cause is suspected, your doctor may consider blood tests (calcium, thyroid function, coeliac serology, full blood count) or other targeted tests. Abdominal X-ray is not recommended to diagnose idiopathic constipation.

## Management

Successful treatment requires patience. The most common reasons treatment fails are: not giving enough laxative, not getting the stool soft enough, and stopping treatment too soon.

### 1. Reassurance and education

Your doctor will explain that functional constipation has no underlying disease. The cycle of pain → holding on → harder stool → more pain is very common and very treatable.

### 2. Dietary advice

Your doctor may suggest:
- Fruit juice containing sorbitol (prune, pear, or apple juice) or kiwifruit to help soften stools
- At least 3 servings of fruit and 3 servings of vegetables daily (best with the peel on)
- Increased plain water intake — a drink at every meal and extra in hot weather
- Reduced milk intake if excessive — no more than 2 cups per day for children over 1 year
- Limiting sweets, chips, and junk food

### 3. Regular toileting routine

Your doctor may recommend:
- Sitting on the toilet once or twice a day, preferably after a meal (to use the natural gastro-colic reflex)
- Sitting time: roughly 1 minute per year of age (e.g., 5 minutes for a 5-year-old) — use a timer
- Using a footstool so the child's knees are higher than their hips (squatting position)
- Leaning forward with elbows on knees
- Avoiding electronic devices on the toilet — the child needs to focus on the task
- Using a star or reward chart to reward sitting on the toilet (not just for successful bowel movements, as the child may not always be able to control that)

### 4. Laxatives

Your doctor may start laxatives early in the course of constipation to break the cycle and prevent it becoming chronic. Laxatives are safe for long-term use in children and do not cause dependence.

**Important:** Laxatives usually need to be given every day for a prolonged period — at least 3 months, often longer. Do not stop suddenly. Wean gradually once your child has had a normal bowel habit for at least 3 months. The dose should be adjusted to achieve one soft, formed stool per day (at minimum, 3 soft stools per week without pain or soiling). Ensure adequate fluid intake for laxatives to work effectively.

**First-line — Lactulose** (osmotic laxative):
- Not suitable for children with lactose intolerance or galactosaemia
- Takes up to 48 hours to work
- Side effects may include abdominal bloating and flatulence
- Advise children to clean their teeth after use
- Fully subsidised in New Zealand
- Doses (your doctor will advise the appropriate dose for your child):
  - 1 month to 1 year: 2.5 mL twice daily
  - 1 to 5 years: 2.5–10 mL twice daily (max 75 mL/day)
  - Over 5 years: 5–20 mL twice daily (max 75 mL/day)

**If lactulose is ineffective, your doctor may consider:**

- **Macrogol** — suitable for children over 6 months; each sachet dissolved in 125 mL water
- **Poloxamer** — oral liquid; suitable for children aged up to 3 years
- **Docusate sodium** — suitable for children over 2 years
- **Picosulfate sodium** (Dulcolax SP elixir) — available under special authority; do not confuse with Dulcolax tablets (bisacodyl), which are a different drug
- **Magnesium hydroxide** — suitable for children aged 3 years and over

### 5. Faecal impaction — disimpaction treatment

If your doctor determines your child has faecal impaction, a short course of higher-dose laxatives is needed to clear the bowel before starting maintenance treatment.

**Signs of faecal impaction include:** soiling, a palpable abdominal mass, long-standing constipation, no sensation of needing to defecate, or poor response to maintenance laxatives.

Your doctor may consider oral disimpaction at home using macrogol at higher doses over several days. The child will likely need to stay home from school during this period. Disimpaction must be followed immediately by long-term daily maintenance laxative treatment.

If oral disimpaction is unsuccessful, or if rectal therapies are being considered, your doctor will seek specialist paediatric advice.

### 6. Anal fissures

If your child has an anal fissure (a small tear near the anus, causing pain or bleeding with stools), your doctor may recommend:
- A stool softener such as lactulose or liquid paraffin to make stools easier to pass
- A barrier cream (e.g., castor oil and zinc oxide ointment) or a topical anaesthetic preparation for 2–3 days only (longer use is not recommended)

### 7. Follow-up

Your doctor will arrange regular review to monitor progress and adjust medication. At each review:
- Ensure the laxative dose is adequate — aim for "wet cement" or "porridge" consistency stools
- Address any barriers to treatment (difficulty taking medicine, toilet access, behaviour)
- Once your child has been passing soft, formed stools regularly for at least 3 months, the laxative can be weaned gradually

**Referral to a paediatrician** is recommended if:
- There is a suspected organic cause (e.g., faltering growth, "ribbon" or "toothpaste" stools, abnormal neurological findings)
- Constipation persists despite adequate laxative treatment: 4 weeks in children under 1 year, 3 months in children over 1 year
