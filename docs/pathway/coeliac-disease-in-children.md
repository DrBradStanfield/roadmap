# Coeliac Disease in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of coeliac disease in children aged under 15 years.

---

## Background

Coeliac disease is an immune-mediated, permanent intolerance to gluten — a protein found in wheat, rye, barley, and some oats.

It affects around 1% of the New Zealand population and is more common in European and Indian populations. It appears to be increasing in Māori, Pacific, and Chinese populations.

Untreated coeliac disease carries significant risks including osteoporosis, lymphoma, infertility, nutritional deficiencies, and anaemia.

The only proven effective treatment is a **lifelong strictly gluten-free diet.**

Coeliac disease can now be diagnosed by blood tests alone (without biopsy) in children, if specific criteria are met.

---

## Who Is at Higher Risk

Certain groups have a higher chance of developing coeliac disease:

- First-degree relative with coeliac disease (10% risk)
- Immunoglobulin A (IgA) deficiency
- Type 1 diabetes
- Down syndrome
- Turner's syndrome
- Williams syndrome
- Autoimmune thyroid disease
- Autoimmune liver disease
- Dental enamel defects
- Dermatitis herpetiformis (a chronic, blistering skin rash)

If a child with one of these conditions is already under the care of a paediatrician, your doctor will seek advice from that paediatrician before proceeding with assessment.

---

## Symptoms That May Suggest Coeliac Disease

- Persistent abdominal pain
- Persistent diarrhoea (with negative stool culture)
- Constipation
- Abdominal distension
- Large, foul-smelling stools
- Persistent vomiting
- Persistent anaemia or iron deficiency despite treatment
- Short stature or faltering growth
- Poor weight gain or weight loss
- Delayed puberty
- Chronic fatigue
- Recurrent mouth ulcers (aphthous ulcers)
- Dermatitis herpetiformis skin rash
- Fracture with inadequate trauma, osteopenia, or osteoporosis
- Abnormal liver blood tests

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider coeliac disease when a child presents with the symptoms above, especially if a high-risk factor is present.

**Important before testing:** For accurate results, the child must have been eating a gluten-containing diet (at least 2–3 slices of wheat-based bread per day, or equivalent) for at least **4 weeks** before blood tests are taken. If this is not possible, your doctor will manage this differently.

### Growth Assessment

Your doctor will measure and record height, weight, and BMI.

### Blood Tests Your Doctor May Arrange

- **FBC and ferritin** — to check for anaemia and iron deficiency
- **Tissue transglutaminase antibodies (tTG-IgA) and total IgA** — the main coeliac screening tests
  - If tTG-IgA is abnormal, the laboratory will automatically add endomysial antibodies (EMA)
  - If total IgA is low, the laboratory will automatically run an IgG-based test (tTG-IgG or deamidated gliadin peptide [DGP] IgG)
- If significant malabsorption is suspected: albumin, vitamin D, and clotting profile
- If another diagnosis is also being considered: stool cultures (e.g., for Giardia), inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP, faecal calprotectin)

### Screening for High-Risk Children Without Symptoms

Your doctor may screen children who have a first-degree relative with coeliac disease, even without symptoms:

- Children under 3 years are generally not tested unless they have symptoms
- If initial serology is negative and the child is under 5 years, your doctor may repeat testing at ages 7 and 12 years, as the condition can develop over time

---

## Interpreting Test Results

Your doctor will manage next steps based on the pattern of blood test results:

### Normal tTG-IgA and Normal Total IgA

The child does not have coeliac disease. Serology-negative coeliac disease does not occur in children. Your doctor will consider other diagnoses if symptoms continue.

### Normal tTG-IgA and Low Total IgA

The laboratory will run a DGP-IgG assay instead. Your doctor will interpret this and may seek specialist advice depending on the result.

### High tTG-IgA (≥ 10 × upper limit of normal)

Your doctor will arrange a **repeat test** to confirm the result — this is important because it is a new, lifelong diagnosis. The repeat can be done immediately. The child must continue eating gluten until the repeat test is completed.

- If the repeat shows elevated tTG-IgA (any level) **and** a positive EMA: diagnosis of coeliac disease can be made without a biopsy. Your doctor will refer to a paediatric gastroenterologist and manage as per the diagnosed coeliac disease section below.
- If the repeat tTG-IgA is normal (with or without negative EMA): further investigation is needed. The child must continue eating gluten until this is sorted.

### tTG-IgA < 10 × Upper Limit of Normal and EMA Positive

Your doctor will refer to a paediatric gastroenterologist for further investigation, which may include a small bowel biopsy. The child must continue eating gluten until after any biopsy.

### tTG-IgA < 10 × Upper Limit of Normal and EMA Negative

Your doctor will seek written gastroenterology advice.

---

## If Testing Was Not Possible (Child Could Not Eat Gluten)

If the child was already on a strict gluten-free diet and could not complete a gluten challenge before testing, your doctor will assess based on whether symptoms have improved:

- **Symptoms resolved on gluten-free diet:** It is likely the child has coeliac disease, but a formal diagnosis cannot be confirmed without a gluten challenge and repeat testing. Your doctor will discuss options with the family.
- **Symptoms not resolved on gluten-free diet:** Coeliac disease is unlikely. Your doctor will consider other diagnoses.

---

## Management

### Diagnosed Coeliac Disease

Your doctor will:

1. **Educate the patient and family** that management requires a lifelong strictly gluten-free diet.
2. **Apply for a gluten-free diet subsidy** (Pharmac Special Authority) — this partly funds gluten-free staples such as bread, pasta, and flour. Note: if the diagnosis was made without a biopsy (biopsy-free), only a paediatric gastroenterologist can apply for the Special Authority.
3. **Refer to a community children's dietitian** for dietary guidance.
4. **Refer to Coeliac New Zealand** ([coeliac.org.nz](https://coeliac.org.nz/)) — for support, information, food subsidy guidance, and access to recipes and community forums.

### Follow-Up Reviews

Your doctor will review your child **3 months after diagnosis**, then annually. At each review:

- Ask about any ongoing or new symptoms (persistent symptoms are almost always due to accidental gluten exposure)
- Measure height, weight, and BMI
- Check coeliac serology — especially 12 months after starting the gluten-free diet, to confirm dietary adherence
  - If tTG-IgA remains elevated, gluten is still being consumed (whether knowingly or accidentally)
  - It can take 6–24 months for tTG-IgA to fully normalise

Your doctor may also refer to a dietitian:
- Before the child starts school
- If serology remains elevated 12 months after starting the gluten-free diet
- When entering adolescence, to check adherence and dietary adequacy

If there are any concerns about growth, ongoing symptoms, or dietary adequacy, your doctor will refer to a paediatric gastroenterologist.
