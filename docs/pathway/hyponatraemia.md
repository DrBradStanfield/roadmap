# Hyponatraemia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of hyponatraemia (low serum sodium) in adults.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Your doctor will arrange urgent hospital assessment if any of the following are present:

- Serum sodium < 125 mmol/L in a patient with previously normal sodium levels
- Rapidly decreasing sodium levels
- Systemically unwell
- Neurological symptoms — tremor, altered conscious state, seizures, or new onset falls
- Persistent vomiting or diarrhoea

---

## What Is Hyponatraemia?

- Hyponatraemia means a serum sodium level below 135 mmol/L.
- **Severity:**
  - Mild — sodium 130–135 mmol/L
  - Moderate — sodium 125–129 mmol/L
  - Severe — sodium < 125 mmol/L
- More common in older people due to age-related factors and other health conditions.
- Often found incidentally on routine blood tests — some patients have no symptoms, especially if it has developed slowly (chronic hyponatraemia).
- Antidiuretic hormone (ADH) from the pituitary gland controls water balance. When ADH is released inappropriately, the kidneys retain too much water, diluting the sodium — this is called SIADH.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Check for previous low sodium results and arrange repeat blood tests including glucose.
- Assess your symptoms:
  - Mild or chronic hyponatraemia — often no symptoms, or nonspecific symptoms such as headache, nausea, tiredness, muscle cramps, gait disturbance, or new onset falls.
  - Severe or rapid-onset hyponatraemia — feeling systemically unwell, vomiting, diarrhoea, neurological symptoms (tremor, confusion, seizures, new falls).
- Examine your volume status (how hydrated you are) — this is key to finding the cause:
  - Heart rate, blood pressure lying and standing (a drop > 20/10 mmHg on standing suggests dehydration), jugular venous pressure, weight
  - Mucous membranes and skin turgor, peripheral oedema, ascites
- Assess neurological status.

**Investigations your doctor may order:**

- Blood tests: creatinine, potassium, liver function, TSH, glucose
- Pro-BNP if fluid overload is suspected
- Triglycerides, protein, and albumin if pseudohyponatraemia (lab artefact) is possible
- Paired urine and plasma sodium, and urine osmolality if SIADH is suspected
- Pre-9 am cortisol to check for adrenal insufficiency (a level > 300 nmol/L excludes it; ≤ 300 nmol/L may require a Synacthen test arranged via endocrinology)

## Causes

### Dehydration (Hypovolaemic Hyponatraemia)

The most common cause — the body loses more sodium than water.

- Signs: low blood pressure, postural drop, fast heart rate, low urine sodium (< 20 mmol/L)
- Common causes: diarrhoea and vomiting, poor fluid intake, diuretic medications, burns, pancreatitis, Addison's disease

### Fluid Overload (Hypervolaemic Hyponatraemia)

Too much water relative to sodium.

- Common causes: heart failure, liver cirrhosis, kidney failure, nephrotic syndrome

### Normal Fluid Balance (Euvolaemic Hyponatraemia)

Total body water is increased but sodium is normal. Urine sodium is typically > 20 mmol/L.

- Common causes: SIADH, hypothyroidism, adrenal insufficiency, water intoxication

### SIADH (Syndrome of Inappropriate ADH)

ADH is released when it shouldn't be, causing the kidneys to retain water and dilute the sodium.

Causes of SIADH include:
- **Medications:** carbamazepine, sodium valproate, SSRIs, TCAs, haloperidol
- **Lung conditions:** tumours, infections (including TB), asthma
- **Brain/nervous system:** infection, bleed, mass, alcohol withdrawal (delirium tremens)
- **Cancer:** lung, mesothelioma, bowel, lymphoma, urinary tract
- **Other:** post-surgery pain, idiopathic (more common in the elderly)

### Drugs Causing Hyponatraemia

Many medications can cause or contribute to low sodium:

- **Common:** thiazide diuretics (especially in older patients also on ACE inhibitors or ARBs)
- **Others:** SSRIs, antipsychotics, NSAIDs, carbamazepine, TCAs, PPIs, sulfonylureas, some chemotherapy agents, opioids, amiodarone

Your doctor will consider whether any recently started medications coincide with the onset of hyponatraemia.

### Adrenal Insufficiency

- Suspect if cortisol < 170 nmol/L before 9 am, or at any time when systemically unwell
- Also suspect if hypoglycaemia (without insulin or oral hypoglycaemics) occurs alongside hyponatraemia
- Risk factors: pituitary disease, previous long-term steroid use, tuberculosis

### Water Intoxication

- Often associated with mental health conditions
- Urine osmolality is typically low (< 100 milliosmoles/kg)

### Hyperglycaemia (HHS or DKA)

Moderate to severe high blood glucose can cause low sodium. This corrects when the blood glucose is treated.

- HHS (hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state): a medical emergency with high mortality; gradual onset polyuria, dehydration, drowsiness, neurological symptoms — managed in hospital.
- DKA: also a medical emergency — managed in hospital.

---

## Management

Management depends on the underlying cause and severity. Your doctor will guide treatment.

- **Red flags present:** urgent hospital assessment arranged.
- **Suspected SIADH:** urgent advice if acute; specialist review if longer-standing.
- **Suspected HHS or DKA:** urgent diabetes or general medicine review.
- **Acute or symptomatic moderate-to-severe hyponatraemia:** urgent general medicine review.
- **Asymptomatic mild-to-moderate or chronic hyponatraemia:** treat according to cause:
  - Stop or switch medications that may be causing it, if possible.
  - Correct dehydration if volume-depleted.
  - Treat the underlying condition if fluid-overloaded.
  - If not correcting with initial treatment, specialist review (endocrinology or general medicine).
- **Suspected adrenal insufficiency:** specialist endocrinology review.

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will decide on the appropriate referral:

- **Urgent hospital (acute general medicine):** any red flag, new suspected SIADH, acute or symptomatic moderate-to-severe hyponatraemia
- **Urgent endocrinology:** suspected adrenal insufficiency with cortisol < 170 nmol/L, hypoglycaemia plus hyponatraemia, or adrenal risk factors
- **Non-acute endocrinology:** suspected cortisol deficiency, Synacthen test needed, cortisol 170–300 nmol/L with uncertainty
- **Non-acute general medicine:** uncertain cause, suspected SIADH not requiring acute review, or chronic hyponatraemia not responding to treatment

*Always discuss your results and treatment plan with your doctor. This information is a guide — your doctor will tailor management to your individual circumstances.*
