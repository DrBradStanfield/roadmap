# Ear Wax

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of ear wax (cerumen) blockage in primary care.

---

## Background

Ear wax (cerumen) is part of the normal environment of the ear canal. It prevents infection and irritation by trapping foreign bodies and pathogens as it is pushed towards the outer ear by hair follicles and migrating skin cells.

Most ear wax does not require removal. Very rarely, ear wax can form a plug and cause symptoms.

---

## Symptoms of Ear Wax Blockage

A wax plug may cause:

- Feeling of fullness in the ear
- Itchiness
- Decreased hearing (rare)
- Tinnitus
- Cough reflex

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Take a history including ear symptoms and any previous ear surgery or perforated eardrum
- Carefully examine the ear canal

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following steps:

### Step 1: Soften the Wax

A trial of softening drops for 4 days may encourage the wax to work its way out naturally:

- **Olive oil** (even cooking grade) or **almond oil** applied using a dropper or teaspoon is a cheap and effective option
- **Proprietary ear drops** are also available
- Drops containing antibiotics and steroids (such as **Sofradex** or **Kenacomb**) may soften wax effectively but are generally reserved for patients prone to otitis externa

### Step 2: Wax Removal (if blockage persists)

If ear wax blockage persists or the eardrum needs to be examined, your doctor will decide on the best removal method:

**Microsuction (preferred method)**
- Performed by a qualified health professional under direct vision with an oto-microscope
- Considered best practice — especially for children, hearing concerns, and patients with a history of ear problems
- Low risk of damage to the eardrum or ear canal
- You may feel some discomfort or dizziness during the procedure

**Ear irrigation**
- An option in carefully selected cases using electrical irrigation equipment for controlled water pressure
- Requires softening drops for at least 5 days beforehand
- Small risks include inadequate wax removal, perforated eardrum, infection, or trauma to the ear canal (major complication risk is approximately 1 in 1000)
- Your doctor will discuss risks and obtain your consent before proceeding

**Ear irrigation is not suitable if you have:**
- A perforated eardrum (past or present)
- Had ear surgery (other than grommets that have been out for at least 18 months)
- Grommets present or removed in the last 18 months
- Had an ear infection in the last 6 weeks
- A cleft palate (repaired or unrepaired)
- Had complications from ear irrigation in the past
- (Children should not have ear irrigation)

### Prevention

Avoid using cotton wool buds to clean your ears — this can push wax deeper and increase the risk of impacted ear wax.
