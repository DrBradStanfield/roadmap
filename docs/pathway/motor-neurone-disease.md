# Motor Neurone Disease

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: April 2024.*

Motor neurone disease (MND) is a group of progressive neurological disorders of unknown cause. It causes progressive weakness and wasting of the muscles that control limb movement, speech, swallowing, or breathing. It is hard to diagnose in the early stages, because it is uncommon and starts slowly.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Contact Your Doctor Promptly

**See your doctor soon if:**
- You take riluzole and develop a fever or feverish illness. Your doctor may check for a low white cell count (neutropenia). If it is low, riluzole is stopped.
- You take riluzole and develop a dry cough, or your breathlessness rapidly worsens. Your doctor may consider a chest X-ray to check for lung inflammation (interstitial lung disorders).
- Your mobility changes. Changes in mobility can happen quickly, and your general practice team can advocate for rapid help from providers.
- Your condition changes a lot. Your doctor may refer you back to the same neurologist.

Choking on food is possible. Ask your doctor or nurse to teach you and your family the correct [first aid techniques for choking](https://www.healthinfo.org.nz/choking.htm).

---

## Background

- MND usually causes death within 5 years, from breathing failure and swallowing problems.
- In 215 people seen in Canterbury, average survival from the first symptoms was 28.5 months.
- More males than females are affected. The average age of onset is 58.
- In New Zealand, over 400 people are living with MND.
- 5 to 10% of cases are genetic.
- MND can affect the upper and lower motor neurones in the brain and spinal cord.
- Treatment is supportive. There is no cure.

## Assessment

### Common presentations

- Weakness in one region that comes on gradually (subacute onset), for example a wasted hand, foot drop, or trouble swallowing.
- A variable mix of arm, leg, or head and neck muscles, for example foot drop, focal weakness, cramps, muscle twitching (fasciculation), slurred speech, a weak or hoarse voice, and swallowing difficulty.
- Other symptoms include cough, pain, drooling, and shortness of breath.
- MND spreads to nearby areas, usually limb to limb.
- The key feature is a combination of upper motor neurone signs and lower motor neurone signs. A wasted, twitching muscle with brisk reflexes and no sensory changes must raise suspicion of MND.

### Types of MND

There are four types:
- **Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS)**, also called Lou Gehrig's disease. The most common type. Affects the limbs early, with weakness, spasm, wasting and twitching. Problems with swallowing, speech, and breathing follow later.
- **Progressive bulbar palsy (PBP).** Affects speech, swallowing, and breathing early. Limb problems similar to ALS come later.
- **Progressive muscular atrophy (PMA).** Mainly lower motor neurones. Wasting, twitching, and weakness begin in either the arms or the legs. It progresses more slowly, with significantly longer survival than ALS and PBP. Diagnosis is difficult.
- **Primary lateral sclerosis (PLS).** Affects upper motor neurones. It is extremely rare and difficult to diagnose.

### History and examination

Your doctor may ask about:
- Your type of MND, if already diagnosed.
- All medicines, including over-the-counter medicines and complementary therapies, and any recent changes.
- Any difficulty taking medicines, for example from excess saliva or swallowing problems.
- Falls.

Your doctor may examine:
- Muscle tone, reflexes, power, co-ordination, and cognitive function.
- Breathing strength. You may be asked to take a deep breath and count as far as you can on one breath. Each number counted roughly equals 100 mL of lung capacity (FVC).
- Voice strength, cough strength, noisy breathing (stridor), and the pattern of belly movement when breathing.

### Differential diagnoses to consider

- **Spinal cord disease.** Always considered.
- In younger people: **Hirayama disease** or **multifocal motor neuropathy with conduction block**.

## Management

### Confirming the diagnosis

- Your doctor may refer you to a neurologist to confirm the diagnosis. This may take a year or more.
- Referral to the MND Association usually follows once a neurologist confirms the diagnosis.
- Anxiety can be considerable. Common worries include unexplained symptoms, a long investigation period, the need for certainty before a diagnosis, and a diagnosis of an illness that has no cure. Your doctor can support you and your family/whānau through this.

### Riluzole

- Riluzole is funded in New Zealand for MND. Specialists may use it in ALS to extend life.
- The usual dose is 50 mg 12 hourly.
- It is not used in liver disease, including baseline liver enzymes (transaminases) greater than 3 times the upper limit of normal.
- Because of the risk of hepatitis, liver tests (ALT) are checked before and during treatment:
  - every month during the first 3 months,
  - every 3 months for the rest of the first year,
  - then periodically.
- See "When to Contact Your Doctor Promptly" for fever, dry cough, or worsening breathlessness while on riluzole.

### Your care team

After diagnosis, an MND clinical nurse specialist usually coordinates care and can arrange referrals. You should be involved in planning your care, and understand who is involved, what they do and why. The team may include:
- your general practitioner
- neurologists and respiratory physicians
- community nursing and community pharmacists
- specialist palliative care services
- an MND Association regional field worker, who checks your needs are met and offers information, support and advice
- allied health: speech and language therapists, dietitians, social workers, occupational therapists and physiotherapists.

Your doctor may also arrange home and community support, for example respite care, carer support, and Meals on Wheels.

**Pharmacist:** tell your pharmacist you have MND. They can advise on the best forms of medicine if swallowing is hard, and suggest ways to keep taking medicines as prescribed.

**Equipment and mobility:** most people with MND will need a range of adaptive and mobility equipment. This helps them stay at home as long as possible and helps prevent pressure injury. Occupational therapists prioritise people with MND.

### Future planning

Once you have adjusted to the diagnosis, your doctor may encourage you to:
- Talk openly with your family about your preferences and wishes.
- Make a will.
- Appoint an [Enduring Power of Attorney (EPA)](https://www.officeforseniors.govt.nz/our-work/promoting-enduring-power-of-attorney). This is a legal process and can be costly, but it is important if you have, or are likely to lose, capacity.
- Complete an [Advance Care Plan (ACP)](https://www.myacp.org.nz/), with help from your MND clinical nurse specialist or general practitioner.
- Think about how you would like end-of-life issues managed. Start these talks early, before changes in communication or thinking make them difficult.

### Work, driving, and money

- Your doctor may give driving advice.
- Work can benefit physical and mental well-being. Problems at work may come from fatigue, limited mobility, cognitive changes, the work environment, or the work role. Seek advice early about your employment and about when and how to tell your employer. Your doctor may arrange a work assessment if your job is at risk.
- Help may be available for [financial](https://mnd.org.nz/support-information/financial-assistance/) and [transport](https://mnd.org.nz/support-information/living-with-mnd/transport-and-getting-about/) needs.

## Symptom Control

In established MND the focus is on symptom control. In advanced disease the emphasis is on palliative care.

### Pain

Pain is common. It can come from constipation, loss of the muscle control that stabilises large joints and the spine, and passive injury to joints, for example shoulder damage during assisted transfers. Your doctor will look for the cause and treat it.

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Paracetamol and NSAIDs first for muscle and joint pain.
- Opioids as the disease advances, for pain and/or breathing symptoms. Doses are adjusted carefully, because needs vary widely.
- A laxative, regular or as needed, because constipation can be very disabling.
- Codeine phosphate 30 mg to 60 mg, four times per day as needed (beware of constipation). Tramadol is not recommended.
- Stronger opioids, such as morphine or oxycodone (by mouth, through a gastrostomy, or under the skin), or fentanyl patches:
  - Immediate release morphine: usual starting dose 2.5 mg to 5 mg four hourly.
  - Immediate release oxycodone: usual starting dose 2 mg to 5 mg six hourly.
  - Fentanyl patches are a funded non-oral option. They are avoided if you have not taken opioids before.

### Constipation

Causes include less exercise and mobility, drinking less to reduce toilet trips, fear of laxatives, medicine side effects (especially opioids), and weak muscles.

**Your doctor may consider:**
- A regular toilet routine.
- More fibre and fruit, for example kiwifruit.
- A softener laxative, a stimulant, or both, increased cautiously:
  - Docusate sodium + sennoside B, one to two tablets at night.
  - Docusate sodium 120 mg, one to two tablets at night (softener).
  - Bisacodyl 5 mg, one to two tablets at night (stimulant).
  - Macrogols, ½ to 3 sachets daily.
- Rectal options (enemas, glycerin or bisacodyl), daily or alternate days as needed, given by a district nurse.

### Bladder problems

Urinary frequency and urgency may come from a neurogenic bladder. Your doctor may consider a trial of solifenacin.

### Cognitive changes

Up to a third of people with MND have cognitive problems without dementia. About 20% develop dementia, usually frontotemporal lobar dementia. This commonly affects decision-making, financial planning, relationships, personality, empathy for loved ones, and responding to changes around them.

### Coughing and choking

Cough is common, from retained saliva and silent aspiration.
- Sit in a good position when eating and drinking.
- Pineapple juice may help ease thick secretions.
- Some head elevation may be needed at night.
- A physiotherapist can advise on cough management, clearing secretions, and suctioning.

### Speech

Early referral to a speech language therapist can help with communication and swallowing strategies and with communication aids. Ask early about [voice banking](https://mnd.org.nz/support-information/living-with-mnd/voice-banking/).

### Swallowing and nutrition

- Good oral hygiene.
- Monitoring of eating and swallowing, with a speech language therapist.
- Check with a pharmacist before crushing medicines. Not all tablets and capsules work properly when crushed.
- All people with MND need a dietitian to monitor weight and advise on suitable foods, liquids, and supplements.
- Early advice about a PEG (percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy, a feeding tube into the stomach), especially if a meal takes more than 30 minutes. A PEG does not mean you cannot eat. It helps keep up nutrition. It is better placed early, while you are still eligible for anaesthesia.
- Physiotherapy or occupational therapy for posture, positioning, head and neck support, and equipment.

### Joint stiffness

**Your doctor may consider:** simple pain relief such as paracetamol; NSAIDs if there is inflammation (a single daily slow-release form may suit); steroid injections into the joint, which help some people.

### Muscle cramps and twitching

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Tonic water first, if cramps are mild.
- Quinine sulphate 300 mg at night for cramps.
- Baclofen 5 mg, two to three times daily, slowly increased to 30 mg to 80 mg daily, for twitching (fasciculations).
- Carbamazepine 200 mg twice daily.
- Verapamil 120 mg twice daily.
- Nifedipine is avoided, as it can make cramp worse.

### Spasticity or jaw spasm

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Baclofen, at the same doses as above.
- Clonazepam. It can be very sedating, which limits its use except near the end of life. The most common dose is 0.5 to 1 mg at night, or twice daily if needed. It is often given as drops (1 drop = 0.1 mg). Do not put the drops straight into the mouth: drop them onto a spoon first. Doses are prescribed as a number of drops, not mL, to avoid errors and over-sedation.
- Dantrolene 25 mg daily, slowly increased to 200 to 400 mg daily.

### Secretions and drooling

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Nortriptyline 10 mg at night, for its anticholinergic (drying) effect.
- Atropine 1.0% eye drops, 1 to 3 drops under the tongue every four hours as needed.
- A hyoscine skin patch (1.5 mg/patch), changed every 72 hours.
- Hyoscine butylbromide, 20 mg every six to eight hours by injection under the skin, or 60 mg per 24 hours by syringe driver. It has fewer side effects on the brain than hyoscine hydrobromide.
- If medicines do not work, referral to a neurologist for Botox injections. The effect wears off over time. Radiotherapy is an alternative, but its effect is permanent.

### Swelling (oedema)

Causes include reduced activity or posture, a blood clot in the leg (DVT), or other illness. Your doctor will treat any specific cause and may consider posture and seating, physiotherapy, compression stockings, or a diuretic. Diuretics are used with caution: they risk dehydration, especially if you drink little, and can make it hard to reach the toilet quickly.

### Pressure care

Good skin and pressure care matters. Regular repositioning is needed, even at night, and equipment does not remove that need. Occupational therapists or physiotherapists can help prevent pressure sores, monitor your skin, and provide special cushions, mattresses, and positioning advice.

### Sleep

If sleep is disrupted, your doctor may arrange a sleep study, because disordered breathing may be the cause.

## Advanced MND and Palliative Care

Your doctor may involve palliative care specialists early. In the final phase of life, the condition can worsen quickly. Extra support may need to be arranged fast, especially if you wish to die at home.

- **Breathing failure** from muscle weakness, aspiration, or infection. Breathlessness is managed. Some people depend on bilevel positive airway pressure (BiPAP) ventilation; a request to stop it needs careful consideration. Palliative care services are contacted, as decline is likely to be rapid.
- **Gut failure.** A PEG tube may be used for medicines until absorption fails. Some medicines are not suitable through a PEG. Stopping tube feeding is reviewed, often prompted by leakage around the tube, more secretions, and aspiration. Without a PEG, medicines are likely to be needed under the skin, for example by syringe driver. Fluids under the skin can cause harm and are usually not recommended.
- **Pain** can be a big issue in the last few days. Opioids give background pain relief, with extra doses for breakthrough pain.
- **Carer fatigue.** Respite can be difficult, as usual care cannot always be kept up in residential care. Support for families to plan regular breaks and self-care is vital.

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you:
- **Neurology:** for diagnosis and ongoing care, and again if your condition changes a lot.
- **Allied health, early:** occupational therapy, speech and language therapy, physiotherapy, dietitian, and social work.
- **Respiratory:** if oxygen or breathing support (ventilation) is being considered.
- **Palliative care:** for difficult symptoms, end-of-life decisions, advance care planning, and advanced disease.
- **Genetic assessment:** if you have a family history of MND.
