# Inflammatory Arthritis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of inflammatory arthritis in people aged 15 years and older.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Care

Your doctor will treat the following as urgent:

- A **single, painful, hot, swollen joint** — your doctor will need to exclude septic arthritis (joint infection) and rheumatic fever urgently, as these can cause serious harm if untreated

If you have any joint that is hot, severely swollen, and very painful — especially with fever — contact your doctor the same day or go to an emergency department.

---

## Background

Inflammatory arthritis is a group of conditions where the immune system causes joint inflammation. The hallmark is:

- Morning stiffness and pain lasting **more than 30 minutes**, which generally improves with activity
- Pain that may be **worse at night**

Key points:

- Many different diseases can cause joint inflammation — it is not always rheumatoid arthritis
- In early disease, diagnosis can be difficult. Blood tests can support a diagnosis but are not on their own diagnostic.
- Many patients cannot be classified in the first 4–6 months (called "undifferentiated inflammatory arthritis")
- Swollen joints (rather than just painful joints) indicate inflammation of the joint lining (synovium) and are an important sign
- Features outside the joints (e.g. rashes, dry eyes, bowel symptoms) are often the key to identifying the correct diagnosis

---

## How Your Doctor Assesses This

Your doctor will take a detailed history and examination. This is a condition where the pattern of symptoms matters enormously — all decisions about investigation and treatment should be made by your doctor.

### What Your Doctor Will Ask About

- Nature and location of joint pain, tenderness, and swelling
- Duration and pattern of morning stiffness (more than 30 minutes suggests inflammatory cause)
- Whether pain improves with activity (inflammatory pattern) or worsens (mechanical pattern)
- Constitutional symptoms: fever, weight loss, fatigue
- Response to anti-inflammatory medications (NSAIDs)
- Back pain, bowel symptoms, rashes, recent infections
- Family history of inflammatory arthritis or psoriasis

### Patterns Your Doctor Will Consider

**Rheumatoid arthritis** is suspected if:
- Joint inflammation has persisted more than 6 weeks
- Small joints of hands (knuckles, wrists) or feet are involved symmetrically

**Seronegative spondyloarthritis** is suspected if there is back pain, bowel symptoms, rashes, or recent infection. This group includes:
- Ankylosing spondylitis (inflammatory back pain)
- Psoriatic arthritis
- Enteropathic arthritis (associated with inflammatory bowel disease)
- Reactive arthritis (following gut or genitourinary infection)
- Typical pattern: asymmetrical, lower limb joints; may have "sausage" fingers/toes (dactylitis) or tendon insertion pain (enthesitis, e.g. Achilles tendon, plantar fascia)

**Other conditions your doctor will consider:**

- **Palindromic rheumatism** — episodes of joint pain lasting hours to days, with symptom-free intervals; approximately 30% eventually develop rheumatoid arthritis
- **Connective tissue disorders** (e.g. lupus/SLE, Sjögren's syndrome) — suggested by dry eyes or mouth, Raynaud's phenomenon, skin thickening, hair loss, mouth ulcers, rashes, sensitivity to sun, or abnormal urine or blood tests
- **Crystal arthropathy** — gout or calcium pyrophosphate disease (CPPD/pseudogout): more common in middle-aged men, older people, and Māori and Pacific peoples; often a single acutely inflamed joint; may occur alongside osteoarthritis
- **Viral arthritis** — associated with infections such as parvovirus or hepatitis B; typically resolves within 12 weeks
- **Rheumatic fever** — particularly in Māori and Pacific peoples, those aged 3–35 years, or those living in crowded housing; requires urgent assessment
- **Polymyalgia rheumatica, osteoarthritis, fibromyalgia** — also considered as part of the differential

### Tests Your Doctor May Arrange

Your doctor will decide which tests are appropriate based on your symptoms. Tests are not a substitute for clinical assessment.

For joint swelling lasting more than 6 weeks (or earlier with strong family history):
- Blood tests: full blood count, CRP (inflammation marker — can be normal), kidney function, urate, liver function, rheumatoid factor (RhF), CCP antibody, urine dipstick
  - CCP antibody is more specific for rheumatoid arthritis than rheumatoid factor; both being positive together has 97% specificity
- ANA (antinuclear antibody) test: only requested if there is a high suspicion of a connective tissue disorder (lupus, Sjögren's) — it is not a useful general screening test
- Specialised tests (e.g. HLA-B27 for ankylosing spondylitis, parvovirus serology): only when there is specific clinical suspicion
- X-rays of hands and feet: if multiple joint swelling has persisted more than 12 weeks

## Treatment

All treatment decisions are made by your doctor in consultation with you. This is a complex area where the right choice depends on the specific diagnosis, your other health conditions, and individual circumstances.

### Initial (Symptom) Treatment

Your doctor may start treatments to reduce pain and inflammation while the diagnosis is being established. These treat symptoms but do not slow the disease:

1. **Anti-inflammatory medications (NSAIDs)** — such as ibuprofen or naproxen — are usually tried first. They work well for early inflammatory arthritis. Your doctor will check for contraindications (e.g. kidney disease, heart disease, stomach problems, blood-thinning medications).

2. **Prednisone (steroid)** — may be used if NSAIDs are not suitable or not working, or if there will be a significant wait to see a rheumatologist. Typical starting dose: 15–20 mg daily, then gradually reduced over weeks.

3. **Additional pain relief** — such as paracetamol, if needed.

### Disease-Modifying Treatment (DMARDs)

Once a diagnosis is made, your doctor will refer you to a **rheumatologist** (specialist) to start a DMARD (disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drug). This is essential:

- In rheumatoid arthritis, starting a DMARD within 3 months of symptom onset is important to preserve joint function and prevent disability
- The most commonly used DMARD is **methotrexate** — other options include sulphasalazine, hydroxychloroquine, and leflunomide
- When your condition is stable, specialist reviews become less frequent (e.g. annual), and if you reach remission you may be referred back to your GP

DMARD treatment requires regular blood test monitoring (usually monthly for the first year, then every 2–3 months). Your GP is responsible for monitoring these results between specialist visits.

### Long-term Management

Your doctor will also:

- Monitor and help manage **cardiovascular risk** — rheumatoid arthritis increases the risk of heart disease, and controlling inflammation and smoking cessation both reduce this risk
- Ensure you are **up to date with vaccinations** — annual flu vaccine, and additional vaccines (e.g. shingles vaccine Shingrix) recommended for people on immunosuppression
- Arrange **cervical screening** recalls if relevant
- Consider **occupational therapy** (for home safety, adaptive equipment, joint protection) and **physiotherapy** (splints, walking aids, exercise advice) as needed — either through the rheumatology team or from your GP

### Stopping a DMARD

If you have been well (no inflammation, no need for NSAIDs or steroids) for 2 years on a DMARD, your doctor may discuss stopping it — but this should only happen in consultation with your rheumatologist and after repeat X-rays of hands and feet.

---

## Specialist Referral

Your doctor will refer you to a rheumatologist:

- Once a diagnosis of inflammatory arthritis is made — to start a DMARD
- Before stopping a DMARD — to confirm this is safe
- If there is diagnostic uncertainty

Your doctor may also refer you to:
- **Occupational therapy** — for home safety assessment, adaptive equipment
- **Physiotherapy** — for splints, walking aids, joint protection education

---

> **Important:** Inflammatory arthritis diagnosis and management is complex. The information above describes what your doctor considers and does — it is not a self-management guide. Always discuss your symptoms, test results, and treatment options with your doctor before making any changes.
