# Inflammatory Arthritis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: February 2023.*

This pathway covers assessment and management of inflammatory arthritis in people aged 15 years and older.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Care

Your doctor will treat the following as urgent:

- A **single, painful, hot, swollen joint**. Your doctor will need to exclude septic arthritis (joint infection) and rheumatic fever.

If you have a joint that is painful, hot, and swollen, contact your doctor the same day or go to an emergency department.

---

## Background

Inflammatory arthritis is a group of conditions where the immune system causes joint inflammation. The hallmark is:

- Morning stiffness and pain lasting **more than 30 minutes**, which generally improves with activity
- Pain that may be **worse at night**

Key points:

- Many different diseases can cause joint inflammation. It is not always rheumatoid arthritis.
- In early disease, diagnosis may be difficult. Positive blood test results support a diagnosis but are not on their own diagnostic.
- Many patients cannot be classified, particularly in the first 4–6 months (called "undifferentiated inflammatory arthritis")
- Swollen joints indicate inflammation of the joint lining (synovium) and suggest inflammatory arthritis
- Features outside the joints (e.g. rashes, dry eyes, bowel symptoms) are often the key to the diagnosis

---

## How Your Doctor Assesses This

Your doctor will take a detailed history and examine your joints. All decisions about investigation and treatment should be made by your doctor.

### What Your Doctor Will Ask About

- Joint pain, tenderness, and soft tissue swelling
- Pain and stiffness in the early morning or after inactivity, lasting more than 30 minutes
- Pain that improves with activity, or is worse at night
- Constitutional symptoms: fever, weight loss, fatigue
- Whether symptoms respond to anti-inflammatory medications (NSAIDs)
- Back pain, bowel symptoms, rashes, recent infections
- Family history of inflammatory arthritis or psoriasis

### Patterns Your Doctor Will Consider

**Rheumatoid arthritis** is suspected if:
- Joint inflammation has persisted more than 6 weeks
- The knuckles (MCP joints), wrists, or the joints at the base of the toes (MTP joints) are involved. A squeeze test may show tenderness over these joints on both sides.

**Seronegative spondyloarthritis** is suspected if there is back pain, bowel symptoms, rashes, or recent infection. This group includes:
- Ankylosing spondylitis (inflammatory back pain)
- Psoriatic arthritis
- Enteropathic arthritis (associated with inflammatory bowel disease)
- Reactive arthritis (following gut or genitourinary infection). Your doctor may ask about recent diarrhoea, conjunctivitis, flu-like illness, or symptoms of a sexually transmitted infection such as chlamydia.
- Typical pattern: a few joints, asymmetrical, mainly in the lower limbs; may have "sausage" fingers/toes (dactylitis) or tendon insertion pain (enthesitis, e.g. Achilles tendon, plantar fascia)

**Other conditions your doctor will consider:**

- **Palindromic rheumatism**: episodes of pain affecting 1 to several joints in turn, for hours to days, with symptom-free periods lasting days to months; approximately 30% develop rheumatoid arthritis
- **Connective tissue disorders** (e.g. lupus/SLE, Sjögren's syndrome): suggested by dry eyes or mouth, Raynaud's phenomenon, thickened skin (especially of the fingers), hair loss, mouth ulcers, rashes, sensitivity to sun, or abnormal urine or blood tests. The arthritis is often symmetrical and involves the small joints.
- **Crystal arthropathy**: gout or calcium pyrophosphate disease (CPPD/pseudogout). More common in middle-aged men, older people, and Māori and Pacific peoples. Often a single acutely inflamed joint, but may affect several joints. May occur alongside osteoarthritis. Testing fluid drawn from the joint is the gold standard if the diagnosis needs confirming.
- **Viral arthritis**: associated with infections such as parvovirus or hepatitis B; may mimic other forms of inflammatory arthritis, but symptoms typically resolve within 12 weeks
- **Rheumatic fever**: considered especially in people at high risk who have any painful, warm, or swollen joints. High risk means a personal, family, or household history of rheumatic fever or rheumatic heart disease, or 2 or more of: Māori or Pacific peoples; aged 3–35 years (especially 4 to 19 years); living in crowded housing or deprivation.
- **Polymyalgia rheumatica, osteoarthritis, fibromyalgia**: also considered as part of the differential

### Tests Your Doctor May Arrange

Your doctor will decide which tests are appropriate based on your symptoms. Tests are not a substitute for clinical assessment.

For joint swelling lasting more than 6 weeks (or earlier with strong family history):
- Blood tests: full blood count, CRP (inflammation marker, which may be normal), creatinine (kidney function), urate, liver function, rheumatoid factor (RhF), CCP antibody, and a urine test
  - CCP antibody is more specific for rheumatoid arthritis than rheumatoid factor; both being positive together has 97% specificity
- ANA (antinuclear antibody) test: only requested if there is a high suspicion of a connective tissue disorder (lupus, Sjögren's). Without features of these conditions, a positive ANA is seldom useful. An ANA of 1 in 160 is found in 5% of the normal population.
- Specialised tests (e.g. HLA-B27 for ankylosing spondylitis, parvovirus): only when there is specific clinical suspicion. HLA-B27 is not diagnostic for ankylosing spondylitis and should not be used as a screening test for nonspecific back pain.
- X-rays of hands and feet: if multiple joint swelling has persisted more than 12 weeks

## Treatment

All treatment decisions are made by your doctor in consultation with you. This is a complex area where the right choice depends on the specific diagnosis, your other health conditions, and individual circumstances.

### Initial (Symptom) Treatment

If septic arthritis or ankylosing spondylitis is suspected, your doctor will follow the pathway for that condition. Otherwise, your doctor may start treatments to reduce pain and inflammation while the diagnosis is being established. These treat symptoms but do not slow the disease:

1. **Anti-inflammatory medications (NSAIDs)** are the first-line treatment. Early inflammatory arthritis usually responds well to them. NSAIDs should be avoided if you take dual antiplatelet therapy or an anticoagulant (blood thinner). If you also take an ACE inhibitor or a diuretic, your doctor may check your kidney function within 10 to 14 days. A stomach-protecting medicine (proton pump inhibitor) may be considered if you are at risk of stomach symptoms. Side effects can occur in older people and those with asthma, or stomach, heart, or kidney disease.

2. **Prednisone (steroid)** may be considered if NSAIDs are not suitable, not tolerated, or not working, especially if there is likely to be a significant wait to see a rheumatologist. Typical starting dose: 15–20 mg daily for 2 to 4 weeks, then reduced by 5 mg every 2 to 4 weeks to the lowest dose you can tolerate.

3. **Additional pain relief**, such as paracetamol or codeine phosphate, if needed.

### Disease-Modifying Treatment (DMARDs)

Once a diagnosis is made, your doctor will refer you to a **rheumatologist** (specialist) to start a DMARD (disease-modifying anti-rheumatic drug):

- Rheumatologists will ideally start a DMARD within 3 months of symptom onset. In rheumatoid arthritis, early treatment with a DMARD is essential to maintain function and prevent disability.
- In other forms of inflammatory arthritis, the evidence for early treatment is less compelling. If there are markers of a poorer outcome (strong CCP positivity, persistent synovitis, functional impairment, developing deformity, or joint erosions), a DMARD may be considered before a formal diagnosis is made.
- **Methotrexate** is the preferred first choice. Other options include sulphasalazine, hydroxychloroquine, and leflunomide.
- When your condition is stable, specialist reviews become less frequent (e.g. annual), and if you reach remission you will be referred back to your GP

DMARD treatment requires regular blood test monitoring (usually monthly for the first 12 months, then every 2–3 months if stable). Your GP may follow shared care guidance (e.g. for methotrexate or azathioprine). Whoever prescribes the DMARD is responsible for checking these results, and for reviewing you, before signing each prescription.

### Long-term Management

Your doctor will also:

- Monitor and help reduce **cardiovascular risk**. Rheumatoid arthritis increases the risk of heart disease. This risk can be reduced by both tight control of inflammation and treating other risk factors, especially smoking. Smoking adds to both the risk and the severity of rheumatoid arthritis, so your doctor can offer support to stop.
- Ensure you are **up to date with vaccinations**: annual flu vaccine, and other recommended and funded vaccines (e.g. shingles vaccine Shingrix) for people on immunosuppressive treatment
- Arrange **cervical screening** recalls if relevant
- Consider at every appointment whether **occupational therapy** (home safety, equipment and home modifications, adaptive equipment to preserve joints) or **physiotherapy** (splints, walking aids, education in managing symptoms, joint care) would help, if not already arranged by the rheumatology team

### Stopping a DMARD

If you have had no symptoms for 2 years on a DMARD, without needing NSAIDs or prednisone, your doctor may discuss stopping it. This should only happen in consultation with your rheumatologist, and after repeat X-rays of hands and feet to look for new or worsening joint erosions.

---

## Specialist Referral

Your doctor will refer you to a rheumatologist:

- Once a diagnosis of inflammatory arthritis is made, to start a DMARD
- Before stopping a DMARD, for rheumatology advice or assessment

Your doctor may also refer you to:
- **Occupational therapy**: for home safety and functioning, and the need for equipment or home modifications
- **Physiotherapy**: for splints, walking aids, education, and joint care and protection

---

> **Important:** Inflammatory arthritis diagnosis and management is complex. The information above describes what your doctor considers and does. It is not a self-management guide. Always discuss your symptoms, test results, and treatment options with your doctor before making any changes.
