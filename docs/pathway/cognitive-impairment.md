# Cognitive Impairment

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: April 2024.*

Cognitive impairment is a broad term for any impairment in a person's ability to think, remember, and learn. It has various causes, including dementia. This pathway is mainly for people aged 65 years and older. It can be used for younger people, though they are atypical and need further assessment.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Red flag:** a subdural haematoma (bleeding between the brain and the skull) can cause cognitive impairment.

**Hospital assessment is needed if:**
- A head CT shows a significant abnormality, such as a bleed, a space-occupying lesion (a mass), or hydrocephalus (fluid build-up in the brain). Your doctor may arrange acute hospital assessment by the neurosurgery team or seek their advice. If you are sent to hospital, call 111 or go to the emergency department.

**Your doctor may arrange an urgent head CT (within 2 weeks) if there is:**
- Rapid cognitive decline over weeks.
- Focal neurological signs.
- A history of falls.
- Anticoagulant (blood-thinning) therapy.
- Unsteady walking (gait ataxia) or incontinence.
- A history of cancers more likely to spread to the brain (e.g. lung, melanoma, breast).

**A sudden change is different.** Delirium usually starts suddenly, often at twilight, lasts days or weeks, and fluctuates. Dementia cannot be diagnosed while delirium is present. Tell your doctor about any sudden change in thinking.

---

## Background

- **Mild cognitive impairment (MCI)** is a problem with thinking and memory that is not severe enough to interfere with complex functioning and daily life. It may be linked with depression and anxiety (as cause or effect). About half of all people with MCI are at risk of eventually progressing to dementia.
- **Dementia** is a gradual, progressive pattern of cognitive impairment affecting at least 2 cognitive domains. It causes social, occupational, or functional impairment. It cannot be diagnosed in the presence of delirium, untreated physical illness, or depression, anxiety, or psychosis.
- **Behavioural and psychological symptoms of dementia (BPSD)** are not uncommon at any stage. They include agitation, aggression, depression, disinhibition, and psychosis.
- The most widely accepted Māori words for dementia are **mate wareware**, though this can vary between iwi. "Mate" means sick or unwell, and "wareware" means to forget or be forgotten.
- Māori and Pacific peoples may have a higher rate of dementia than NZ Europeans of the same age, yet they are less likely to be given anti-dementia medicines such as donepezil or rivastigmine. Medicines should be offered equitably.

## Assessment

The aim is a timely diagnosis so management and long-term planning can start. Assessment may take several visits and may involve the wider general practice team. New symptoms should be explored as soon as they are raised, not dismissed as part of ageing.

### History

Where possible, your doctor will include close family/whānau or carers. For Māori, recommended practice is to engage with the whānau as a whole, beginning with an assessment of wairua (spiritual wellbeing). Family members may see the situation differently, and some people fear what a diagnosis may mean.

Your doctor may ask about:
- When symptoms started, how they have progressed, whether they fluctuate, and how they affect daily life.
- **Daily functioning:** toileting, bathing, dressing, eating, cooking, housework, shopping, socialising, managing money, work, and driving.
- **Thinking symptoms:** memory, language, insight, judgement, concentration, problem solving, forgetting appointments, and trouble finding words (using phrases such as "that thing" or "you know what I mean").
- **Behaviour changes:** apathy, sleep problems, restlessness, agitation, aggression, disinhibition, repetitive behaviour, getting lost when going somewhere, and changes in eating and drinking.
- **Mental health symptoms:** depression, anxiety, mood instability or personality change, and psychosis.
- **Other neurological symptoms:** gait and balance, vision, speech, Parkinsonism, and seizures.
- **Risks to self and others:** home safety, elder abuse, poor self-care, aggression, alcohol and drug use, medication adherence, money problems, dangerous driving, and access to firearms.
- **Carer stress.**
- **Past history and risk factors:**
  - Age older than 65 years.
  - Family history of dementia.
  - Head injury or falls.
  - Congenital intellectual disability, especially Down syndrome (screening for dementia starts from age 35 years).
  - Vascular risk factors or disease.
  - Alcohol or drug use.
  - Depression or other major mental illness.
  - Obesity and obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA).
  - Significant social isolation.

### Medicines that may cause or worsen confusion

Your doctor will review your medicines, including adherence. This matters most for medicines where too much or too little is risky, such as warfarin.
- **Sedatives and sleeping pills:** benzodiazepines, zopiclone.
- **Pain relief:** opioids.
- **Strongly anticholinergic medicines** (problems can occur with dose increases or withdrawal): antihistamines, antimuscarinic Parkinson's medicines, antispasmodics, tricyclic antidepressants, neuroleptics.
- **Mildly anticholinergic medicines** (effects can add up when used together): digoxin, furosemide, bronchodilators such as ipratropium.
- **Others:** anticonvulsants, corticosteroids, dopaminergic Parkinson's medicines, anti-nausea medicines that block dopamine (such as prochlorperazine), lithium, antibiotics (occasionally), and medicines that raise serotonin.

### Examination

Your doctor will check vital signs, do a brief neurological examination, and assess mental state. In early Alzheimer's disease the examination is usually normal. Findings that may point elsewhere:
- **Parkinsonian features** (shuffling gait, reduced arm swing, reduced facial expression, quiet speech, rigidity): suggest dementia with Lewy bodies. Your doctor may ask about hallucinations, marked fluctuation, falls, and autonomic symptoms.
- **Brisk reflexes, increased tone, or motor problems:** suggest diffuse cerebrovascular disease.
- **An unsteady or wide-based gait:** may suggest cerebrovascular disease or normal pressure hydrocephalus (your doctor may ask about incontinence).
- **Asymmetry** (for example in eye movements, the face, reflexes, or strength): may indicate structural brain disease.

### Conditions that can mimic dementia or worsen thinking

More than one cause may be present. Delirium, dementia, and depression commonly coexist.

| Feature | Delirium | Dementia | Depression |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Onset | Usually sudden, often at twilight | Chronic and generally gradual | Often abrupt, coinciding with life events |
| Duration | Days or weeks | Months to years | Variable, months to years |
| Progression | Rapid, fluctuating | Slow but even | Variable and uneven |

Your doctor may also consider:
- Medicines (including over-the-counter medicines).
- Alcohol or drug use.
- Metabolic causes: vitamin B12 or folate deficiency, hypothyroidism, low or high sodium, high calcium, and liver or kidney problems.
- Neurological illness: subdural haematoma, a mass, hydrocephalus, or brain infection, especially HIV or neurosyphilis.
- Hepatic encephalopathy.
- Obstructive sleep apnoea.

### Investigations

**Your doctor may consider:**
- **Blood tests:** FBC, TSH, electrolytes, creatinine, adjusted calcium, B12, folate, HbA1c, and lipids.
- **If indicated:** HIV and syphilis tests, LFTs, CRP, and thiamine. Thiamine deficiency is seen almost only with malabsorption from gut disease, eating disorders, or heavy alcohol use. Testing is rarely needed; a trial of thiamine treatment may be considered instead.
- **Urine culture** only if there are signs of a urinary infection (pain passing urine, urgency, frequency, lower abdominal, back, or flank tenderness) or of body-wide infection (fever, low temperature, chills, rigors, new low blood pressure, or fast heart rate). It is not needed for confusion alone.
- **Hearing and eye tests** if poor hearing or vision is suspected.
- **A baseline ECG** if you have cardiovascular risk factors or cholinesterase inhibitors are being considered.

### Cognitive testing

Screening tests are not diagnostic. They show cognitive function at one point in time. Your doctor watches how you do the test as well as the score. Wear your hearing aids and glasses if you use them.
- **Mini-Addenbrooke's Cognitive Examination (Mini-ACE)** is the recommended tool in most situations. It takes about five minutes and is scored out of 30. A score of 25 or less suggests cognitive impairment is likely. A score of 21 or less suggests significant impairment is likely. Results are read in light of first language, education, other conditions (such as delirium, depression, or anxiety), and neurological problems that affect the test (such as weakness affecting writing).
- **Māori Assessment of Neuropsychological Ability (MANA)** is a diagnostic tool for Māori aged 55 years and older. It has four parts, including an assessment of wairua and wellbeing, and may take up to an hour. The parts can be done over several visits.
- **Rowland Universal Dementia Assessment Scale (RUDAS)** suits people whose first language is not English (where the Mini-ACE is not available in their language) or who have lower literacy. It is scored out of 30. Scores of 23 to 30 are considered normal, read in clinical context.
- A trained interpreter is preferred over family translating.
- **Informant questionnaire:** family/whānau or carers may be asked to complete a questionnaire (such as the IQCODE) without the patient taking part. It can show whether things are stable or getting worse. A whānau version is part of the MANA tool.

### Working out the diagnosis

- **MCI** is more likely if social, work, and daily functions are not impaired.
- **Dementia** is likely if other conditions are excluded and there is progressive decline in at least 2 cognitive domains:
  - **Perceptual motor:** e.g. difficulty using tools, driving, handwriting, or using a fork or spoon.
  - **Executive function:** e.g. difficulty with familiar complex tasks, relying on others to plan or decide, loss of initiative.
  - **Complex attention:** e.g. difficulty with background noise, or remembering phone numbers, addresses, or what was just said.
  - **Social cognition:** e.g. becoming more insensitive, withdrawn, or isolated.
  - **Learning and memory:** e.g. repeating yourself, confusion about time or place.
  - **Language:** e.g. using general terms such as "that thing".
- **Typical dementia:**
  - **Alzheimer's disease:** short-term memory loss and word-finding difficulty, then behaviour changes and reduced function, with reduced insight. It often coexists with cerebrovascular disease.
  - **Vascular (multi-infarct) dementia:** a sudden start, then step-wise decline, with signs of previous stroke. Some cases develop gradually without stroke.
- **Atypical dementia** (including frontotemporal and Lewy body dementia) is more likely with young onset (before age 65 years), rapid or fluctuating progression, an abnormal physical examination, Parkinsonism, function worse than the degree of cognitive impairment, hallucinations or delusions, or significant personality change.
- **Lewy body dementia** covers dementia with Lewy bodies (dementia starts within 1 year of Parkinson's symptoms) and Parkinson's disease dementia (dementia starts more than 1 year after). It can fluctuate like delirium, with hallucinations and Parkinsonism. **Antipsychotic medicines may cause severe and often fatal reactions in people with Lewy body dementia.**
- **Uncertain:** your doctor may request a specialist older person's health assessment.

### Head CT

CT cannot diagnose dementia by itself. It can help exclude other causes (such as a bleed, a mass, or hydrocephalus) and can sometimes show the subtype (such as vascular, Alzheimer's, or frontotemporal), which may change management. Your doctor may consider it when finding the subtype would change management. For suspected frontotemporal dementia (for example younger than 65 years, speech disturbance, or difficulty making purposeful movements), a specialist assessment is recommended and MRI may be more suitable as the first scan.

## Management

### Explaining the diagnosis

Your doctor will explain the diagnosis sensitively to you and your family/whānau or carers, at a pace that suits you. You can choose how much you want to know, and whether more detail is shared with whānau. A follow-up visit with whānau may be offered. The diagnosis is discussed alongside a plan, to give hope.

With consent, and as soon as possible, people with dementia and their family/whānau or carers are referred to a local dementia support service for support, education, and help finding services.

### Planning ahead

Legal and end-of-life planning should start early, before changes in thinking affect your capacity to decide.
- **Enduring Power of Attorney (EPA):** a legal authority you set up while able, so a trusted person can decide for you if you later lose capacity. An EPA can cover care and welfare decisions (EPA Welfare), property and money (EPA Property), or both. An EPA Welfare is held by only one person at a time; an EPA Property may be held jointly by 2 or more people. An EPA is activated only after a health practitioner formally assesses that you can no longer manage that part of your affairs. An ordinary power of attorney is not the same, and stops working when capacity is lost.
- **A will.**
- **Advance care planning:** talk about aged residential care, resuscitation, and limits on future medical treatment. Naming someone in an advance care plan does not give them legal authority; an EPA does.
- If cost is a barrier, consider getting quotes from more than one solicitor.

### Brain health for everyone

Whatever the diagnosis, your doctor may:
- Manage cardiovascular risk factors actively.
- Treat conditions that can mimic or worsen dementia, especially if thinking has worsened since the last check.
- Address vision or hearing problems, which may be disease modifying in MCI.
- Encourage physical activity for strength, mobility, day-night routine, mood, and social contact, and consider falls prevention.
- Assess and manage frailty.
- Strongly encourage cutting down alcohol and other substances. Switching to lower-alcohol or no-alcohol drinks is reasonable, because the alcohol dose is what matters.
- Discuss good sleep habits. People with cognitive impairment are more sensitive to side effects of sleep medicines, so non-drug approaches come first.
- Review medicines and consider stopping or changing those that may worsen confusion.
- Encourage cognitive stimulation. "Use it or lose it" applies. Regular social activity is the key intervention. Puzzles may help people who enjoy them. Web-based brain-training programmes may help but cost money.

### Nutrition

Nutrition can decline for many reasons, some reversible: trouble planning meals, shopping, or cooking, swallowing problems, appetite changes, or forgetting to eat and drink. Undernutrition is common in dementia, and weight loss often starts before dementia is diagnosed.
- For MCI, or to lower risk, a Mediterranean diet is encouraged.
- For everyone: plenty of vegetables, fruit, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and seeds, healthy fats, and a protein-rich food at every meal and snack.
- Regular weighing is advised.
- **There is no reliable evidence that any specific nutrition supplement or alternative medicine slows dementia.**
- Risk factors for poor nutrition include eating alone, skipping meals, poor appetite, missing food groups, trouble chewing, cooking, or swallowing, and difficulty getting groceries.
- Malnourishment is defined as a BMI less than 18.5 kg/m2, or unintentional weight loss of more than 10% within the last 3 to 6 months, or a BMI less than 20 kg/m2 with unintentional weight loss of more than 5% within the last 3 to 6 months. Your doctor may refer you to a dietitian.

### Safety and practical support

- **Getting lost:** a medical alert bracelet or other identification, or a tracking system.
- **Oven or heater hazards:** an electrician can fit a timer switch.
- **Medication risks:** a medication management service can help with adherence.
- **Firearms:** your doctor has legal responsibilities if a licensed firearm user may be unsafe.
- **Falls, frailty, elder abuse, and carer stress** should be watched for. People with dementia whose carers are stressed are more likely to need a higher level of care.
- **Support services** may include a needs assessment, carer support, workplace support, home occupational therapy or physiotherapy, strength and balance programmes, and speech and language assessment if swallowing problems risk choking. Financial help such as a disability allowance or carer support allowance may be available.

### Driving

Your doctor will watch for health changes that affect fitness to drive. With your consent, they may ask a family member or friend about your driving rather than rely on self-report. If there is concern, a specialist occupational therapy driving assessment may be arranged. An on-road safety test may be a useful screen in MCI when there is little concern about safety.

## Management by Diagnosis

### Mild cognitive impairment (MCI)

- Cholinesterase inhibitors are **not** prescribed for MCI.
- Brain health strategies are important.
- Review in 6 months to 1 year, with updated tests, cognitive testing, and an informant questionnaire.
- If a scan was done within the past 12 months, it is not repeated unless a new, acute event is strongly suspected.
- If impairment progresses, your doctor will consider whether it now meets the criteria for dementia.

### Diagnosis uncertain

Some people have subjective memory concerns with no other supporting findings.
- Conditions that can mimic dementia are reconsidered.
- Brain health strategies are important.
- 6-monthly reviews with updated tests, cognitive testing, and an informant questionnaire.
- If still uncertain longer than 1 year after the first assessment, your doctor may request a specialist older person's health assessment.

### Confirmed dementia

- Atypical dementia is referred for a specialist older person's health assessment.
- Medicines that may worsen confusion are carefully stopped, withdrawn, or changed.
- Your doctor may consider whether cholinesterase inhibitors are suitable.
- **BPSD** (aggression, agitation, depression or anxiety, sleep problems, repeated shouting out, sexually inappropriate behaviour, wandering, psychosis) are assessed and managed. Your doctor will look for triggers such as infection, medicine side effects, electrolyte problems, constipation, untreated pain, depression, and psychosocial and environmental factors.
- Formal cognitive stimulation therapy may be offered. It is evidence-based, with benefits comparable to cholinesterase inhibitors. A kaupapa Māori version (haumanu whakaohooho whakāro) may be available.
- Carer stress is asked about and addressed, with education and support for the patient and family/whānau.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer or seek advice if:
- **Hospital neurosurgery assessment:** a CT shows a significant abnormality such as a bleed, a mass, or hydrocephalus.
- **Specialist older person's health assessment:**
  - Atypical dementia, including suspected frontotemporal dementia.
  - Complex co-morbidities.
  - Longer than 1 year since the first presentation and the diagnosis is still uncertain.
- **Older person's health specialist advice:** the diagnosis is uncertain at the first assessment, or after treating conditions that can mimic dementia.
- **Secondary care team:** the link between possible dementia and untreated physical illness, delirium, or a psychiatric disorder is unclear.
- **Dietitian:** concerns about nutrition.
