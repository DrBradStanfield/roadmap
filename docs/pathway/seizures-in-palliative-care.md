# Seizures in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to seizures in adults receiving palliative care — assessing the cause, managing acute episodes, and planning ongoing care.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance (111) if:

- A seizure lasts longer than 5 minutes (prolonged seizure)
- Seizures recur rapidly without recovery between episodes (status epilepticus)
- Medication to stop the seizure is needed but not available

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history of the episode, including:

**From the patient:**
- Circumstances and any specific triggers
- Posture at the time of the event
- Any warning or prodromal symptoms
- How the event unfolded over time
- Loss or alteration of consciousness
- Tongue biting, loss of bowel or bladder control
- Symptoms after regaining consciousness
- Whether the patient remembers the event

**From an eyewitness (if present):**
- Duration and how the episode evolved
- Whether the patient lost awareness or responsiveness
- The patient's colour during the event (e.g. pale, blue)
- Type and frequency of any motor movements
- The patient's state after the seizure

It can help to record any further episodes on video if possible.

**Physical examination** typically includes:
- Mental state, blood pressure, temperature, heart rate, and peripheral pulses
- Listening for heart murmurs
- Neurological assessment: cranial nerve function, limb or facial weakness, speech, cerebellar signs (e.g. balance or coordination problems)

**Other diagnoses your doctor will consider:**
- Syncope (fainting — vasovagal or cardiogenic)
- Stroke or transient ischaemic attack (TIA)
- Movement disorder
- Psychiatric causes (e.g. panic attack, psychogenic non-epileptic seizure)
- Abnormal movements or behaviours related to terminal illness

**Possible causes of seizure your doctor will check for:**
- Pre-existing epilepsy with missed doses or loss of the oral route for medication
- Medications that lower the seizure threshold (e.g. tramadol, SSRIs, antipsychotics such as haloperidol or levomepromazine)
- Medication or alcohol withdrawal (e.g. benzodiazepines)
- Metabolic causes: low blood sugar, low sodium, or other biochemical abnormalities
- Central nervous system causes: primary brain tumour, cerebral metastases, CNS infection, bleeding or stroke, radiotherapy-related swelling, or low oxygen levels

**Investigations** your doctor may arrange (depending on stage of illness and whether results would change management):
- Blood tests: full blood count, kidney function, liver function, calcium, glucose
- CT scan of the head (if likely to affect management)

---

## Goals of Care

An important part of assessment in palliative care is considering where you are in your illness and what your goals are:

- If you cannot communicate your wishes, your doctor will check advance care planning documents or consult your designated enduring power of attorney, family/whānau, or carers
- If you have declined from a highly functioning state acutely, your doctor may investigate and treat reversible causes
- If your short-term prognosis is poor, your doctor may focus on comfort care only — this will be discussed clearly with family and carers

---

## Management

### Acute Seizure

If a seizure is prolonged (over 5 minutes) or recurring rapidly without recovery, your doctor may administer:

- **Subcutaneous midazolam:** 5 mg subcutaneously, repeated every 10 minutes if seizures continue. Midazolam has a short half-life, so repeated doses may be needed. In palliative care, doses, indications, and routes may differ from standard use.
- **Rectal diazepam:** 10–20 mg if midazolam is not available. May also be given buccally (inside the cheek). Can be repeated after 15 minutes.

After medication is given, your doctor will arrange appropriate monitoring and may seek palliative care or oncology advice.

Your doctor may also seek urgent medical assessment if:
- Status epilepticus occurs
- Seizures are intermittent but uncontrolled
- Reversible causes (such as infection, medication issues, or low blood sugar) cannot be addressed

---

### After a Seizure

Your doctor, family, and carers will be given guidance on managing future episodes:

- Most seizures are self-limiting, lasting a few seconds to a couple of minutes
- During a seizure: move harmful objects away, do not restrain the patient or place anything in their mouth
- Once the seizure has passed: place the patient in the recovery position and ensure a clear airway

**Buccal midazolam for family or carers:** Your doctor may prescribe midazolam plastic ampoules (15 mg/3 mL) for carers to use at home during an acute seizure:
- Squirt approximately half an ampoule (about 7.5 mg) inside the cheek at a time, or
- Draw 1.5 mL (about 7.5 mg) into a needleless syringe and administer buccally

If midazolam has been given or the seizure is prolonged, call 111.

---

### Ongoing Management

**If a first-time seizure:** Your doctor will manage based on the cause. If a reversible cause is found and treated, long-term anticonvulsant medication is not usually needed — recurrence risk is low once the cause is addressed. Corticosteroids may be considered if raised intracranial pressure is contributing.

**If seizures recur in previously well-controlled epilepsy:** Your doctor will review antiepileptic drug (AED) use — checking compliance, triggers, and drug interactions. If no clear trigger is found, the dose may be increased. If you are nearing end of life or cannot take oral medication, your doctor will seek palliative care advice.

**Palliative care advice** may be sought:
- If seizures are difficult to manage in the community
- When considering starting anticonvulsant therapy
- If you are nearing end of life or unable to take oral medication
- When prescribing buccal midazolam for family or carer use
