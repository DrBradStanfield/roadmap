> This information is for health professionals. Always use your clinical judgement and defer to your treating clinician for decisions about individual patients.

See also: Acute Exacerbation of COPD.

## Background

Discussion about advance care plans and advance directives should happen as part of usual management at a suitable time in the disease course — this can be at any stage, not just as the patient approaches end of life.

Most patients with life-limiting conditions prefer to identify goals of treatment and discuss end-of-life care preferences early. Patients and their family/whānau value honest conversations with realistic information and appropriate hope.

The intentions of palliative care in COPD are for patients to:
- Have their physical symptoms under control
- Feel safe
- Know what to expect in the future

Palliative care may be combined with curative interventions or become the sole focus of care when curative interventions are no longer working or desired.

## Assessment

### Criteria for advanced or end-stage COPD

Consider advanced or end-stage COPD in patients with any of the following:
- FEV1 < 30% of predicted
- Weight loss with BMI below 18, or more than 10% weight loss in last 6 months
- Two or more hospital admissions for COPD exacerbation in the past year
- Breathlessness on minimal exertion or at rest, or housebound
- Meets criteria for long-term oxygen therapy (resting O2 saturation < 90% on air)

### Risk table: exacerbations, hospitalisations, and mortality

| Spirometry | Exacerbations/year | Hospitalisations/year | 3-year mortality |
|---|---|---|---|
| FEV1 50–80% predicted | 0.7–0.9 | 0.11–0.2 | 11% |
| FEV1 30–50% predicted | 1.1–1.3 | 0.25–0.3 | 15% |
| FEV1 < 30% predicted | 1.2–2.0 | 0.4–0.54 | 24% |

Source: Global Strategy for Diagnosis, Management, and Prevention of COPD.

## Management

### 1. Initiate advance care planning discussions

Begin ACP discussions at an appropriate time in the disease course. Several appointments may be needed. Topics to discuss with the patient and family/whānau:
- Their understanding of the disease and prognosis
- Current stage of disease and treatment options
- Patient's wishes for care (home-based vs hospital-based)
- Who the patient wishes involved in their care
- Who the primary healthcare provider has permission to speak to about their condition
- Specific treatments and CPR (see below)
- What to do in an emergency and out-of-hours
- Whether a current advance care plan (ACP) exists; if not, discuss and provide patient information
- Appointing or activating an enduring power of attorney (EPA) — important if cognitive impairment is present or likely
- Creating a will

### Cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) — how to explain

CPR (chest compressions + rescue breaths) manually pushes blood around the body while the cause of cardiac arrest is treated. It is a bridging procedure for sudden, potentially reversible events (e.g. heart attack, anaesthesia arrest). In advanced disease, cardiac arrest is typically the final event of the dying process — CPR is ineffective in this context.

**Key principles:**
- Use neutral language; avoid scare tactics or graphic descriptions
- Explain that CPR does not "restart" the heart
- Demonstrate physically (hands mimicking chest compressions) to illustrate what CPR does

**Suggested phrases:**
- "CPR is pushing on your chest to move blood around your body, and also helping you breathe."
- "CPR does not restart your heart; it supports your body while we try to treat you."
- "CPR works best when the heart stops suddenly, not when it stops as the last part of the dying process."

### Enduring power of attorney (EPA) — clinical summary

- An EPA allows a trusted person (attorney) to make decisions on behalf of the patient (donor) once the donor loses capacity
- EPA Welfare covers care and health decisions; EPA Property covers finances
- An EPA Welfare is activated after a formal capacity assessment by a relevant health practitioner (GP, nurse specialist, secondary care specialist, or other appropriately experienced clinician)
- A general power of attorney is not the same as an EPA — it lapses when capacity is lost
- Obtain a copy of any EPA document before acting on it
- The attorney for an activated EPA Welfare cannot refuse standard medical treatment required to save life or prevent serious harm to health
- EPA Welfare can be held by only one person at a time (successors may be nominated)
- EPA Property and EPA Welfare may or may not be held by the same person

Encourage patients to research EPA, consider setting one up, and engage with advance care planning alongside the EPA process. Being named in an ACP does not give that person legal authority — only an EPA does.

### 2. Continue COPD management

- Continue optimised pharmacological COPD management
- Consider requesting a non-acute respiratory assessment if the patient has not been seen in a respiratory clinic and further evaluation is appropriate

### 3. Manage breathlessness (dyspnoea)

See also: Dyspnoea in Palliative Care.

#### Morphine for dyspnoea

Morphine reduces dyspnoea by reducing respiratory effort. Lower doses than for pain are usually required.

- **Renal caution:** If eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m², seek palliative care advice for alternatives to morphine
- **Acute crisis:** 2.5 mg subcutaneous morphine as a one-off dose if acutely distressed
- **Regular use:** 2.5–5 mg oral liquid morphine every four hours or as required (consider lower doses in older patients)
- **Dose titration:** Titrate gradually; aim for comfort, not full resolution of dyspnoea
- **Converting to slow-release (if > 2 doses/day regularly effective):**
  - Slow-release morphine capsules 10 mg twice daily, or continuous subcutaneous infusion 10 mg/24 hours
  - Prescribe as-required morphine liquid 2.5 mg up to four times a day
  - Do not incorporate as-required doses into long-acting formulations (unlike in pain management)
- 30 mg/day oral morphine is generally sufficient for dyspnoea alone

#### Benzodiazepines (anxiety component only)

Benzodiazepines have no role for dyspnoea alone. Use only if anxiety is contributing. Be cautious about excessive sedation.

- **Lorazepam:** 0.5 mg orally every 4–6 hours (appropriate starting dose)
- **Midazolam:** Short half-life; two routes:
  - Intranasal midazolam (unsubsidised) — rapidly absorbed, useful for anxiety-associated dyspnoea
  - Subcutaneous midazolam 1.25–2.5 mg — up to two doses for persistent distressing breathlessness; seek palliative care support if limited response after two doses

### 4. Manage common symptoms

- Depression
- Chest wall pain
- Urinary incontinence
- Anorexia and weight loss
- Fatigue

### 5. Home oxygen

If resting oxygen saturation on pulse oximetry is ≤ 90% in a stable state, consider requesting home oxygen.

### 6. Provide supports

- Arrange appropriate equipment
- Consider mobility parking permit and/or subsidised transport if breathlessness limits mobility
- Manage complex psychosocial problems (e.g. guilt, loss of meaning, euthanasia requests, difficult family dynamics) — seek adult palliative care support
- Apply for carer support (see also: Carer Stress)
- Arrange a needs assessment if required
- Explore funding options (PHO palliative care funding may be available)

## Referral and requests

- **Non-acute respiratory assessment:** Consider if not recently seen in a respiratory clinic
- **According to goals of care, consider requesting:**
  - Pulmonary rehabilitation
  - Dietetics
  - Psychological services
  - Occupational therapy
  - Needs assessment and service coordination (NASC)
  - Home oxygen
  - Additional community support
- **Palliative care:** Seek adult palliative care support if symptoms are complex or difficult to manage, or if complex psychosocial problems are present
