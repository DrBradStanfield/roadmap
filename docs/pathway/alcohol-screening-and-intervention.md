# Alcohol Screening and Intervention

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers how doctors identify and manage harmful alcohol use — from routine screening to managing withdrawal and supporting longer-term change.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance immediately if you experience:

- Severe alcohol withdrawal symptoms (seizures, severe tremors, confusion, fever)
- Gastrointestinal bleeding (vomiting blood, black tarry stools)
- Seizures
- Signs of Wernicke's encephalopathy (confusion, eye movement problems, loss of coordination)
- Imminent risk of harm to yourself

---

## Background

Alcohol use disorder (AUD) is a single diagnosis that covers a spectrum from hazardous drinking to severe dependence (previously described separately as alcohol abuse and alcohol dependence under older criteria).

Alcohol contributes to more than 60 health conditions, including hypertension, stroke, liver disease, and seven types of cancer. In New Zealand:

- Around 78% of adults drink alcohol
- Nearly 1 in 5 adults drink in a way that may harm themselves or others (hazardous drinking)
- Young adults aged 18–24 have the highest rates of hazardous drinking (around 40%)
- Māori drinkers are significantly more likely to drink hazardously than non-Māori
- There is no safe level of alcohol consumption during pregnancy

## Assessment

### Screening Tool: AUDIT-C

Your doctor will use the AUDIT-C questionnaire to assess your level of alcohol use. This is a standard 3-question screen:

| Question | 0 | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| How often do you have a drink containing alcohol? | Never | Monthly or less | 2–4 times/month | 2–3 times/week | 4+ times/week |
| How many standard drinks on a typical drinking day? | 1–2 | 3–4 | 5–6 | 7–9 | 10+ |
| How often do you have 6 or more standard drinks on one occasion? | Never | Less than monthly | Monthly | Weekly | Daily or almost daily |

**Interpreting the score:**
- Less than 5: Low-risk drinking
- 5–10: Hazardous or harmful drinking
- 11–12: High-risk or likely severe alcohol use disorder

One standard drink = 10 g of alcohol. Your doctor may use an online standard drinks calculator to help quantify consumption.

*For young people aged 13–18, a different tool (SACS — Substances and Choices Scale) is typically used instead.*

### Further Assessment for High Scores

If your score is 5 or higher, your doctor will also assess for:

**Features of alcohol dependence:**
- Strong compulsion to drink
- Loss of control over how much you drink
- Cravings
- Needing more alcohol to feel the same effect (tolerance)
- Alcohol taking priority over other activities
- Continuing to drink despite harm
- Withdrawal symptoms when you stop

**Withdrawal risk (your doctor may ask):**
- Do you get tremors, sweating, nausea, or anxiety when you stop drinking?
- Have you ever had a seizure related to stopping alcohol?
- Have you ever had delirium tremens (DTs)?
- Do you need a drink in the morning or overnight?
- Have you previously needed a medically supervised detox?

**Physical examination may look for:**
- Signs of liver disease (jaundice, abdominal swelling, spider veins on skin)
- Neurological signs (eye movement problems, poor coordination, peripheral nerve changes)
- Withdrawal signs (tremor, sweating, rapid heart rate, raised blood pressure)
- Injury

**Investigations your doctor may consider:**
- Liver function tests (LFTs) and AST — an AST:ALT ratio greater than 1.5 suggests alcohol-related liver damage
- Full blood count (FBC)
- Coagulation profile
- Electrolytes, urea, and creatinine

### Special Considerations

**Pregnancy:** There is no safe level of alcohol consumption in pregnancy. Fetal alcohol spectrum disorder (FASD) is the leading cause of preventable intellectual disability in New Zealand. Your doctor will always discuss this and may refer to specialist services.

**Older adults:** Even drinking within standard limits may cause harm in older people due to age-related changes, medication interactions, and increased fall risk.

## Management

### Brief Advice for All Drinkers

Regardless of your risk level, your doctor will discuss the benefits of reducing alcohol:

- Better sleep and energy
- Improved mood and memory
- Better sexual performance
- Reduced weight
- Lower risk of high blood pressure, liver damage, brain injury, and certain cancers
- Improved family and work relationships

**Low-risk drinking guidelines (for adults who are not pregnant):**
- Men: No more than 3 standard drinks on any day, no more than 15 per week
- Women: No more than 2 standard drinks on any day, no more than 10 per week
- Have at least 2 alcohol-free days per week

### Hazardous or Harmful Drinking (AUDIT-C score 5–10)

Your doctor may:

- Discuss your screening results and what they mean for your health
- Ask whether you have any concerns, whether you've tried to change before, and whether you'd like to change now
- Provide personalised information about alcohol-related health risks
- Suggest practical tips to reduce drinking:
  - Set a limit before you start and stick to it
  - Alternate alcoholic and non-alcoholic drinks
  - Drink slowly; choose lower-alcohol options
  - Eat before or while drinking
  - Plan safe transport home
  - Avoid situations that trigger heavy drinking
- Encourage you to set goals and record your progress
- Recommend community support services, online programmes, or counselling
- Ask you to return in 4–8 weeks for review

### Severe Alcohol Use Disorder (AUDIT-C score 11–12)

Your doctor will:

- Assess your withdrawal risk and safety for community detox (see below)
- Address nutrition — malnutrition is common in severe AUD
- Prescribe vitamin supplements (see below)
- Consider medications to reduce drinking or support abstinence
- Refer to specialist addiction services for comprehensive support

**Nutrition support:**
Your doctor may ask about your diet and, if needed, refer you to a dietitian.

**Vitamins your doctor may prescribe:**
- **Thiamine** 50 mg four times a day or 100 mg twice a day — to protect against Wernicke encephalopathy. Continued for 6 weeks after stopping alcohol, or indefinitely if drinking continues.
- **Multivitamin** (Mvite) and **vitamin B complex** (Bplex) twice a day
- **Folic acid** 0.8 mg daily if dietary deficiency is suspected or confirmed

## Community Withdrawal (Detox)

Withdrawal management depends on the severity of your alcohol use and your overall health.

| Risk Level | Criteria | Typical Management |
|---|---|---|
| **Mild risk** | Drinking <10 standard drinks/day; no significant health problems; stable mental health; adult support at home | May consider diazepam detox at home |
| **Moderate risk** | Drinking 10–20 standard drinks/day; otherwise well; adult support at home | May consider diazepam detox at home with close review |
| **Severe risk** | Drinking >20 standard drinks/day; significant health or mental health problems; aged over 65 | Refer to specialist detox services; do not prescribe diazepam |

Community detox is not started on a Thursday or Friday (in case weekend support is needed), and is not appropriate for those who are medically unwell, have severe liver disease, COPD, or are aged 65 or older.

### Diazepam for Mild Withdrawal (example regimen)

Your doctor may consider:
- Day 1: Diazepam 5 mg four times a day
- Day 2: 5 mg three times a day
- Day 3: 5 mg twice a day
- Days 4–5: 5 mg at night
- Day 6: Stop

**Plus:** Thiamine, multivitamins, and anti-nausea medication if needed (metoclopramide or ondansetron).

This is a guide — your doctor will review you every 24–48 hours and adjust as needed.

### Diazepam for Moderate Withdrawal (example regimen)

Your doctor may consider:
- Day 1: Diazepam 10 mg four times a day
- Day 2: 10 mg three times a day
- Day 3: 5 mg four times a day
- Day 4: 5 mg twice a day
- Days 5–6: 5 mg at night
- Day 7: Stop

**Plus:** Thiamine, multivitamins, and anti-nausea medication if needed.

Diazepam is not prescribed if you have severe liver impairment, COPD, or are aged over 65.

## Medications to Support Abstinence or Reduce Drinking

These are used alongside counselling and support — not as a standalone treatment.

### Naltrexone

Naltrexone is an opioid antagonist that reduces the pleasure and cravings associated with alcohol.

**How it works:**
- Reduces alcohol-induced euphoria and cravings
- Not sedating, not addictive, no withdrawal on stopping
- Can be started without being completely alcohol-free first
- Reduces the likelihood of a slip becoming a full relapse

**Dose:** 50 mg once daily (usually started at 25 mg for 2 days first)

**Important:** Cannot be used if you are taking opioid medications (prescribed or otherwise). Tell your doctor about all your medications. Do not take naltrexone for at least 72 hours before any planned surgical procedure where opioid pain relief may be needed.

**Common side effects:** Nausea, headache, dizziness, fatigue, anxiety

**Monitoring:** Liver function tests before starting, and 4 weeks after. Ongoing monitoring if you have liver disease.

### Disulfiram

Disulfiram works by making you feel very unwell if you drink alcohol while taking it.

**How it works:**
- Blocks an enzyme needed to break down alcohol
- If alcohol is consumed, causes flushing, sweating, palpitations, nausea, and vomiting (the disulfiram-ethanol reaction) — usually within 5–10 minutes, lasting 2–4 hours
- Severe reactions can include convulsions and loss of consciousness

**Dose:** 100–200 mg daily. No loading dose required.

**Important:** You must be alcohol-free for at least 3 days before starting. The reaction can occur up to 3 weeks after stopping disulfiram. Avoid alcohol-containing products (hand sanitiser, mouthwash, some medications, aftershave).

**Cannot be used if you have:** Severe heart disease, advanced liver or kidney disease, psychosis, allergy to rubber/latex, or are taking metronidazole or paraldehyde.

**Use with caution in:** Epilepsy, diabetes, hypertension, asthma, hypothyroidism, pregnancy (seek specialist advice).

**Interactions:** Phenytoin, warfarin, diazepam.

**Side effects:** Drowsiness, nausea, headache, acne, halitosis, impotence, elevated cholesterol, rarely severe liver injury.

**Monitoring:** Liver function tests monthly for the first 3 months, then every 6 months.

### Antidepressants

SSRIs such as citalopram or sertraline may be used if anxiety or depression persists after 4 weeks of abstinence. Antidepressants are not used routinely alongside alcohol reduction alone.

## If You Are Not Ready to Stop Drinking

Your doctor will not push you to change before you are ready. They may:

- Discuss harm reduction strategies (e.g. safe transport, not driving while under the influence or withdrawing)
- Explore what is keeping you drinking (stress, relationships, housing, employment)
- Use motivational interviewing — exploring your own reasons for change
- Continue to monitor your health and check in regularly
- Note that severe alcohol use disorder is a chronic relapsing condition and that trying again after a relapse is always encouraged

---

## Safe Driving

It is illegal to drive while intoxicated or while withdrawing from alcohol (due to seizure risk). If you have had a withdrawal seizure, you need to be seizure-free for 12 months before you can resume driving. Your doctor can advise on this and may need to notify the transport authority.

---

## Pregnancy and Alcohol

There is no safe level of alcohol in pregnancy. FASD causes permanent brain and body damage to a developing baby and is entirely preventable. If you are pregnant or trying to conceive, your doctor will advise complete abstinence from alcohol and may refer you to specialist support services.
