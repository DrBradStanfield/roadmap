# Immunomodulators for Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the four main groups of medications used for IBD (ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease), either for acute flares or long-term maintenance.

---

## Important Safety Note

Patients on steroids, thiopurines, methotrexate, or biologics are immunosuppressed. Your doctor should add an alert to your notes and recall you for annual flu vaccinations.

---

## Aminosalicylates (ASA)

Examples: **mesalazine** (Pentasa, Asacol), **olsalazine**, **sulfasalazine**

### What they are used for

- First-line drugs for **ulcerative colitis (UC)** — for both inducing and maintaining remission
- Limited effect in active Crohn's disease
- Mesalazine is effective and well-tolerated
- Sulfasalazine and olsalazine are used less commonly due to side effects but useful in certain situations

### How they are taken

Your doctor may consider:

- **Mesalazine (Asacol):** Oral tablets that release in the colon. Up to 3.2 g per day in divided doses. Also available as suppositories.
- **Mesalazine (Pentasa):** Oral tablets releasing throughout the gut. Up to 4 g per day. Also available as sachets, suppositories, and enemas.
- **Sulfasalazine:** 1–2 g four times daily in acute disease; 500 mg four times daily for maintenance. May cause yellow-orange discolouration of skin, urine, and other body fluids — this is harmless but expected.
- **Rectal therapy** (suppositories, enemas): Effective for disease limited to the lower bowel.

### Blood disorder warning

Tell your doctor immediately if you develop unexplained bleeding, bruising, purpura, sore throat, fever, or generally feeling unwell — these may signal a blood disorder. Your doctor will check a blood count and may stop the medication.

### Monitoring

Your doctor will check kidney function (creatinine) and urine every 6 months.

---

## Steroids

Example: **prednisone**

### What they are used for

- Used to achieve remission in moderate-to-severe flares of both UC and Crohn's disease
- Not suitable for long-term maintenance — your doctor will aim to wean you off
- Carry risks including increased chance of serious infection

### How they are taken

Your doctor may consider:

- Starting at a full dose (e.g., 40 mg per day), then reducing by 5 mg per week over about 8 weeks
- If you need more than two courses of steroids in a year, or a prolonged course, your doctor will refer to a gastroenterologist to consider immunomodulators or biologics

### Monitoring

- Your doctor will consider bone protection during steroid use (vitamin D, calcium intake)
- Bone density scans (DXA) may be arranged if you have repeated or prolonged courses

## Immunomodulators

### Thiopurines — azathioprine and mercaptopurine (6MP)

Both are first-line immunomodulators. Mercaptopurine use in UC is unapproved but used in practice.

#### What they are used for

- Maintenance therapy when aminosalicylates are not effective in UC, or when repeated steroid courses are needed
- Inducing remission in severe or complex Crohn's disease
- Only started by a gastroenterologist after a full risk discussion

#### Important considerations

- Slow onset — may take **3 to 6 months** to see benefit
- Before starting, a **TPMT blood test** is done to identify people at higher risk of toxic side effects (about 1 in 250 people have very low TPMT levels and may not be able to take these drugs, or need a much lower dose)
- **Do not take with allopurinol** — this combination greatly increases the risk of serious blood toxicity (unless your gastroenterologist specifically manages this combination)
- **Live vaccines must be avoided** while on thiopurines (see list below)
- Generally considered safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding — discuss with your doctor and specialist

#### Side effects

Nausea and vomiting, flu-like illness (headache, muscle aches), and rarely — bone marrow suppression, liver toxicity. Long-term: slightly increased risk of skin cancer and lymphoma.

#### Monitoring

Your doctor will check **full blood count (FBC) and liver function tests (LFTs)**:
- Every week for the first 4 weeks
- Then every 3 months long-term

---

### Methotrexate (second-line)

#### What it is used for

- Used when thiopurines are not tolerated or the disease does not respond
- Taken weekly (orally or by subcutaneous injection)
- Use is limited by its side effects

#### Important considerations

- **Absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy** — causes serious birth defects
- Both men and women must use **effective contraception** and wait at least **3 to 6 months after stopping** before trying for a pregnancy
- **Folic acid 5 mg** is co-prescribed to reduce side effects — do not take it on the same day as methotrexate

#### Monitoring

Your doctor will check FBC and LFTs every 3 months.

---

## Biologics (TNF Blockers)

Examples: **infliximab** (Remicade), **adalimumab** (Amgevita, Humira)

### What they are used for

- Potent anti-inflammatory drugs for moderate-to-severe IBD that has not responded to other treatments
- Initiated and supervised by gastroenterologists

### Funding (New Zealand)

Your doctor may consider:

- **Adalimumab (Amgevita):** Available for new patients via Pharmac special authority — initial application by a gastroenterologist only; GPs may apply for renewal
- **Adalimumab (Humira):** Available for patients already established on Humira who meet the criteria
- Response is assessed at 6 months

### Important considerations

- Before starting, your doctor will arrange **screening tests** for varicella zoster virus (VZV), hepatitis, and tuberculosis — biologics can reactivate latent infections
- You will receive training on how to self-inject adalimumab
- **Withhold your dose if you have signs of active infection** — contact your doctor
- Tell your surgeon and gastroenterologist if you are having major surgery — your dose may need to be withheld
- Flu and pneumococcal vaccinations may be less effective while on biologics — still recommended
- **Live vaccines must be avoided** (see list below)

### Monitoring

No regular blood tests required, but your gastroenterologist will review you at least every 6 months once stable.

---

## Live Vaccines to Avoid on Immunosuppressants

Patients on thiopurines, methotrexate, or biologics must **not** receive live attenuated vaccines, including:

- Herpes zoster vaccines
- Varicella (chickenpox)
- MMR (measles, mumps, rubella)
- Oral polio
- Intranasal influenza
- BCG, oral typhoid, yellow fever

Discuss your vaccination history with your doctor before starting any immunosuppressant.
