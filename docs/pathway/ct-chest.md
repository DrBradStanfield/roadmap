# CT Chest

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers when a CT chest scan is clinically indicated — including critical situations, accepted indications, and important safety considerations around radiation and contrast.

---

## Critical — Emergency Care First

Do not arrange imaging before seeking emergency care if any of the following apply:

- **Life-threatening or massive haemoptysis** (coughing up large amounts of blood)
- **Stridor** (high-pitched breathing noise) or suspected superior vena cava (SVC) obstruction
- **Severe respiratory distress**
- **Suspected large pneumothorax**

These require emergency assessment. CT should not delay hospital admission.

---

## Background

### Radiation Risk

CT uses ionising radiation, which carries a small risk of cancer later in life, and risks to an unborn child. Your doctor will consider:

- Whether CT is necessary given the clinical question
- Whether alternative imaging (e.g. ultrasound) is appropriate, particularly in patients under 40 years or where repeated follow-up scans may be needed
- CT scanning is avoided in pregnancy unless the scan is critical

### IV Contrast Risk

Some CT scans require an intravenous (IV) iodinated contrast injection (a dye that improves visibility of soft tissues and blood vessels). IV contrast is generally safe but can cause:

- Mild, moderate, or severe (anaphylactic) allergic reactions
- Contrast-induced kidney impairment in susceptible patients
- Worsening or triggering of thyrotoxicosis (overactive thyroid)
- Special precautions are needed if you take metformin

**Potential contraindications to IV contrast** (your doctor will assess):

- Previous allergy to IV contrast
- Significantly reduced kidney function (eGFR less than 30 mL/min)
- Currently taking metformin
- Hyperthyroidism

## When CT Chest May Be Indicated

Your doctor may consider a CT chest in the following situations. This is a clinical decision — not all of these automatically result in a CT being ordered.

### Urgent CT Chest

Your doctor may consider an urgent CT chest if:

- **Unexplained haemoptysis** (coughing up blood) where initial investigations including chest X-ray have not identified a cause, and upper respiratory causes (nose, sinuses, oral cavity) and chest infection have been excluded
- **Smoker aged over 55** with one or more concerning symptoms or signs of lung cancer that have persisted for 6 weeks despite treatment, and remain unexplained after chest X-ray and blood tests
- **Indeterminate chest X-ray finding** suggesting possible lung cancer (e.g. bulky hilum) where CT is recommended in the radiology report
- **Persistent consolidation** on a 6-week follow-up chest X-ray where CT is recommended
- **Suspicious lesion on chest X-ray** where a specialist or radiologist advises urgent CT for staging

### Non-Urgent CT Chest

Your doctor may consider a non-urgent CT chest if:

- An isolated pulmonary nodule on chest X-ray cannot be confirmed as benign from X-ray appearances alone, and a non-urgent CT is recommended in the radiology report
- A non-urgent CT is recommended by a specialist or radiologist

---

## Concerning Symptoms or Signs of Lung Cancer

The following symptoms or signs may prompt your doctor to investigate further, particularly in smokers aged over 55:

- Unexplained abnormal chest signs persisting more than 3 weeks (e.g. suspected pleural effusion)
- New cervical lymph node enlargement persisting more than 3 weeks (nodes over 1 cm) with other lung cancer risk factors
- Unexplained chest or shoulder pain persisting more than 3 weeks
- Repeated or unresolved chest infections
- Unexplained changes in chronic respiratory symptoms persisting more than 3 weeks
- Unexplained, worsening cough persisting more than 3 weeks
- Unexplained new or worsening shortness of breath
- Features suggesting paraneoplastic syndrome (e.g. high calcium, finger clubbing)
- Persistent unexplained haemoptysis (blood in sputum)
- Unexplained hoarseness persisting more than 3 weeks
- New supraclavicular (base of neck) lymph node enlargement
- Unexplained weight loss and loss of appetite persisting more than 3 weeks

---

## When CT Chest Is Not the First Step

Your doctor may recommend a different approach if:

- Symptoms are concerning but the chest X-ray is normal and CT criteria are not met — specialist advice (e.g. respiratory, ENT, haematology) may be sought first
- Non-specific symptoms such as weight loss or fatigue without a localising cause — a CT chest/abdomen/pelvis may be more appropriate
- Initial presentation with symptoms concerning for lung cancer — a lung cancer pathway is typically followed first
- Incidentally discovered lung nodule on CT — respiratory advice is usually sought before further imaging, unless the radiologist confirms it is benign and no follow-up is needed
- Children aged 14 or under — publicly funded direct-access CT is not available
