# Infantile Haemangioma

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of infantile haemangioma — benign vascular birthmarks common in infancy.

---

## Background

Infantile haemangiomas are benign soft tissue vascular tumours of infancy, with a reported incidence of up to 5%. They appear at or shortly after birth as a telangiectatic patch, or pale or bluish area, and then proliferate. This growth phase is fastest in the first 1 to 3 months and tends to last until age 6 to 9 months, although some continue to grow until 18 months or older.

About 50% resolve by school entry, and 95% by age 10 years. A small proportion may persist into adulthood or incompletely regress, leaving a fibrofatty scar or telangiectasia.

**Three morphological types:**

- **Superficial** — Bright red colour in the superficial dermis (the "strawberry birthmark")
- **Deep** — Involve deep dermis, appear as blue or skin-coloured lumps
- **Mixed** — Features of both

**Distribution:**

- **Focal (localised)** — Well-defined borders with no linear or geographic pattern
- **Segmental** — More plaque-like, with a linear or geographic pattern
- **Multiple** — Five or more lesions

Most haemangiomas will not need treatment. However, some can cause complications such as ulceration, visual impairment, airway obstruction, or significant disfigurement.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

Your doctor should arrange urgent paediatric assessment if any of the following are present:

- Active bleeding from the haemangioma
- Ulceration
- Compromise of vision, airway, or feeding

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history of the lesion including:

- Age at which the lesion was first noted (haemangiomas develop at or shortly after birth — lesions present at birth are more likely to be vascular malformations)
- Number and location of lesions
- Subsequent growth pattern
- Presence or history of ulceration, pain, bleeding, or infection
- Interference with feeding or breathing

Physical examination will typically include:

- Location, size, number, and appearance of lesions
- Evidence of bleeding, ulceration, or infection
- Signs of hepatomegaly, high-output cardiac failure, congenital heart disease, or bruising

### Segmental haemangiomas

Larger haemangiomas that are segmental (plaque-like, linear or geographic patterning) carry a higher risk of complications:

- They can grow up to 10 times the size of focal haemangiomas
- They tend to occur at a younger age and be more prominent
- They may be associated with visceral haemangiomas
- In a "beard" distribution (cervicofacial or mandibular), there is a higher risk of progressive airway involvement

### Associated congenital abnormalities

Large segmental haemangiomas (greater than 5 cm) in certain locations may be associated with underlying syndromes:

**PHACE syndrome** (facial or scalp haemangioma):
- Posterior fossa and structural brain malformations
- Arterial anomalies of the great cerebral vessels
- Cardiac defects and coarctation of the aorta
- Eye anomalies
- Sternal cleft and/or supraumbilical raphe

**PELVIS/LUMBAR/SACRAL syndrome** (lumbosacral haemangioma):
- Perineal or lumbosacral haemangioma
- External genital malformations
- Lipomyelomeningocele or spinal abnormalities
- Vesicorenal abnormalities
- Imperforate anus or other anal malformations
- Skin tag or sacral abnormalities

### Differential diagnosis

Late-appearing lesions are unlikely to be infantile haemangioma. Your doctor may consider:

- Congenital haemangiomas — present fully formed at birth and grow with the child
- Capillary vascular malformations (e.g., port wine stain, salmon patch)
- Other vascular malformations (e.g., lymphangioma)
- Other soft tissue tumours (for deep lesions)
- Pyogenic granuloma

If the lesion is deep without characteristic skin changes, an ultrasound scan may be arranged to confirm the diagnosis.

---

## Indications for Treatment

Your doctor will assess whether treatment is needed. Indicators include:

- Rapid growth
- Large size (may cause cardiac failure due to high blood flow through the lesion)
- Ulceration, pain, bleeding, or infection
- Feeding or breathing interference
- Close to the eye or interfering with vision
- Perioral, nasal tip, lip, or facial haemangioma
- Cartilaginous structure involvement
- Nappy area or flexural haemangioma
- Physical developmental interference (e.g., on the palm or fingers)
- Lumbosacral haemangioma (associated with spinal and urogenital anomalies)
- Multiple haemangiomas (5 or more, may be associated with visceral involvement and hypothyroidism)
- Large segmental haemangioma, particularly in the head and neck (associated with airway involvement or PHACE syndrome)

---

## Management

Most haemangiomas will not need treatment. Your doctor will determine the appropriate approach based on severity and location.

**Management goals include:**

- Preventing life- or function-threatening complications
- Preventing or treating ulceration to minimise pain, bleeding, and infection
- Preventing or minimising residual scarring and disfigurement
- Minimising long-term psychosocial distress for the patient and family

### Propranolol

If treatment is indicated, your doctor may consider propranolol, a beta-blocker medication used to slow or reverse haemangioma growth.

**Benefits:**
- Relatively low-risk compared with other treatment options
- Non-surgical approach
- Current evidence does not show any adverse neurodevelopmental effects

**Risks and side effects:**
- Sleep disturbance (common)
- Bronchoconstriction during respiratory illness
- Irritability and fussiness
- Rare: bradycardia, hypotension, cold extremities, constipation or diarrhoea
- Hypoglycaemia — **important:** propranolol must be withheld during periods of reduced oral intake (illness, diarrhoea, vomiting) and restarted only when feeding has resumed

Your doctor will educate your family about the natural history of haemangiomas, and the benefits and risks of propranolol treatment.

### Monitoring while on propranolol

If your child is prescribed propranolol:

- Stop propranolol during illness or periods of reduced oral intake due to the risk of hypoglycaemia. Restart at the normal dose once feeding resumes.
- Dose may need adjusting as your child grows — your doctor will check this at paediatric clinic visits
- Ongoing management is coordinated by the paediatric service
- At the end of treatment, propranolol is weaned over 2 weeks to minimise the risk of rebound growth

### If parents decline referral

If families decline referral after being fully informed of the risks and benefits of non-treatment, the haemangioma can be monitored in general practice.
