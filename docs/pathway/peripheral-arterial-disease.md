# Peripheral Arterial Disease

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of peripheral arterial disease (PAD), including intermittent claudication and limb ischaemia.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

**Critical limb ischaemia** requires urgent specialist assessment. Signs include absent peripheral pulses with any of:

- Persistently recurring rest pain requiring analgesia for more than 2 weeks
- Ulceration
- Gangrene of the foot or toes

**Acute limb ischaemia** (sudden onset of pain, coldness, pallor, paraesthesia, and absent pulses) requires emergency vascular surgery assessment.

If you have any of these features, your doctor will arrange urgent or emergency assessment immediately.

---

## Background

The most common symptom of peripheral arterial disease is **intermittent claudication** — a cramp-like pain in the muscles of the calf, thighs, or buttocks brought on by walking and relieved by rest. It typically occurs at a fixed walking distance and can be bilateral, though usually worse in one leg.

People with PAD have a mortality rate 2 to 3 times higher than age- and sex-matched controls. Coronary heart disease is the major cause of death. Aggressive risk factor control leads to a significant reduction in cardiovascular events.

Most patients with intermittent claudication do not require surgical intervention.

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you by:

### History

- **Character and timing of the pain** — including onset, what makes it worse or better, and how far you can walk before it starts
- **Type of pain:**
  - Acute limb ischaemia: sudden onset pain, coldness, pallor, pins and needles, no pulses
  - Intermittent claudication: cramp-like pain on walking, relieved by rest, at a fixed distance
  - Rest pain: indicates severe disease
- Non-arterial leg pain tends to be variable day to day, or occurs when standing still, sitting, or lying flat
- **Other symptoms:** paraesthesia (pins and needles), impotence
- **Risk factors:** smoking, high cholesterol, diabetes, obesity, high blood pressure, family history of heart disease, stroke, or PAD
- **Quality of life impact:** walking distance less than 50 metres, ability to do household duties, employment, and sleep

### Examination

- Full cardiovascular examination including blood pressure
- Weight, height, and BMI
- Peripheral pulses and signs of limb ulceration or ischaemia (cold, pale, absent pulses, slow capillary refill)
- Lower back, hips, and knees to help rule out musculoskeletal or nerve-related causes

### Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- Blood tests: full blood count (FBC), fasting lipid levels, HbA1c (blood sugar), kidney function, C-reactive protein (CRP)
- **Ankle brachial pressure index (ABPI):** a measurement using a blood pressure cuff and handheld Doppler device that compares blood pressure at the ankle to the arm — this is more reliable than feeling for pulses alone. An ABPI below 0.9 is abnormal; below 0.5 indicates severe arterial disease
- If intervention is being considered, duplex scans and angiography will be arranged in secondary care

### Differential Diagnosis

Your doctor will consider other common causes of leg pain, including sciatica, spinal stenosis, hip or knee osteoarthritis, peripheral neuropathy, varicose veins, deep vein thrombosis (DVT), and other musculoskeletal conditions.

## Management

Management depends on severity. Your doctor will guide you through the most appropriate approach.

### Exercise Therapy

Exercise training is as effective as angioplasty for intermittent claudication and is recommended for all patients:

- Walk at least 3 times per week for 3 to 6 months
- Walk up to the point where claudication pain starts, rest, then continue
- Sessions of around 1 hour are recommended
- Simply being told to "walk regularly" without a structured plan is unlikely to be effective

### Cardiovascular Risk Factor Reduction

Your doctor may consider:

- **Smoking cessation** — stopping smoking improves symptoms and reduces disease severity
- **Diet and weight management** — correcting nutritional deficiencies has a positive impact on outcomes
- **Statin therapy** — recommended for all patients with PAD regardless of cholesterol levels, unless contraindicated
- **Blood pressure control** — treating high blood pressure reduces cardiovascular complications by around 25%. First-line is usually an ACE inhibitor (e.g., ramipril, perindopril) if not contraindicated; kidney function is monitored. Beta-blockers are continued if the patient has underlying cardiovascular disease
- **Diabetes management** — blood sugar control is important
- **Antiplatelet therapy:**
  - **Clopidogrel** is recommended by NICE guidelines for patients with established PAD
  - **Aspirin 100 mg daily** may be used — while evidence for PAD-specific benefit is limited, it may help reduce other cardiovascular complications
  - Dual antiplatelet therapy (clopidogrel + aspirin together) is not routinely recommended due to increased bleeding risk

### Drug Treatment for Claudication

There is currently no funded drug therapy in New Zealand that has been shown to improve the symptoms of intermittent claudication.

### Angioplasty or Surgery

Your doctor may consider referral for angioplasty or surgery if:

- Symptoms severely affect quality of life despite exercise and risk factor treatment
- Symptoms have not improved after 6 months of targeted exercise and risk factor reduction
- A young, otherwise healthy adult has claudication symptoms

Angioplasty is reserved for patients with a major impact on quality of life. Symptoms can recur within 1 to 2 years after the procedure. Surgery is considered for very disabling or life-threatening symptoms.
