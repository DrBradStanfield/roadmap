# Sepsis in Adults, Young People, and Pregnant Women

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the recognition and urgent management of sepsis in adults and young people aged 12 and over, including those who are pregnant or up to 6 weeks postpartum.

**Sepsis is a life-threatening emergency. If you or someone you are with is seriously unwell with a suspected infection, call 111 or go immediately to the emergency department. Do not wait. Your doctor will assess and manage this urgently.**

---

## What Is Sepsis?

Sepsis is a complication of infection where the body's immune response causes organ failure and severe circulatory impairment. It has a high mortality rate, but outcomes can be significantly improved with prompt recognition and treatment.

Māori and Pacific peoples have higher rates of sepsis and a higher risk of in-hospital death — a higher degree of suspicion is warranted when they present with infection.

---

## Red Flags — Call an Ambulance Immediately

These are features of **Red Flag Sepsis**. If a person has a confirmed or suspected infection AND any of these features, call an ambulance immediately (or call the St John healthcare professional line if calling from a clinic):

- Needs oxygen to keep oxygen saturation at or above 92%
- Responds only to voice or pain, or is unresponsive
- Respiratory rate 25 breaths per minute or higher
- Systolic blood pressure 90 mmHg or less, or a drop of more than 40 mmHg from normal
- Heart rate 130 beats per minute or higher
- Non-blanching rash (does not fade with pressure), mottled, ashen, or cyanotic (blue) skin
- Recent chemotherapy (within the last few weeks)

Additionally, the following contextual features increase concern:
- The patient appears very unwell
- Family or carer is very concerned
- The patient is being treated for infection but getting worse

---

## Amber Flags — Urgent Medical Review Required

**Amber Flag Sepsis** indicates a high risk of deterioration. Your doctor will assess urgently if any of the following are present alongside a confirmed or suspected infection:

- Family/carer concern that persists
- Altered mental state (confusion or disorientation)
- Respiratory rate 21–24 breaths per minute or difficulty breathing
- Heart rate 91–129 beats per minute or new irregular heart rhythm
- Systolic blood pressure 91–100 mmHg
- Temperature below 36°C
- If pregnant or up to 6 weeks postpartum:
  - Temperature above 39°C
  - Invasive procedure in the last 6 weeks (e.g. Caesarean section, forceps delivery, miscarriage, termination)
  - Waters broken for more than 24 hours
  - Close contact with group A Streptococcus
  - Malodorous vaginal discharge

More than one amber flag feature lowers the threshold for treating as Red Flag Sepsis.

---

## Who Is at Higher Risk?

Your doctor will consider the following risk factors when assessing for sepsis:

- Previous sepsis
- Māori or Pacific peoples
- Socio-economic deprivation
- Recently discharged from hospital
- Implanted medical device, IV line, or urinary catheter
- Recent surgery or invasive procedure in the last 6 weeks (including pregnancy-related procedures)
- Pregnant or up to 6 weeks postpartum
- Waters broken early (prolonged rupture of membranes)
- Recent or close contact with group A streptococcal infection
- Recent overseas travel
- Older than 60 years
- Drug or alcohol use disorder
- Diabetes (including gestational diabetes)
- Chronic kidney disease
- Immunosuppression (e.g. steroid therapy, splenectomy, cancer, chemotherapy, biological agents, DMARDs)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Ask whether the illness could be sepsis
- Assess mental state using AVPU (Alert / Voice / Pain / Unresponsive)
- Examine the skin for poor perfusion (pale, cool, mottled skin; slow capillary refill) and non-blanching rash
- Check vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, temperature)
- Look for an infection source (e.g. chest, urine, abdomen, skin, joints)
- Consider signs of meningitis (headache worsened by head movement, neck stiffness, Kernig's or Brudzinski's sign)

### Conditions that can look like sepsis

Your doctor will also consider: adrenal insufficiency, anaphylaxis, diabetic ketoacidosis, heart attack, pancreatitis, pulmonary embolism, and spinal cord injury.

---

## Immediate Management (Sepsis Six)

For Red Flag Sepsis, your doctor or paramedics will initiate the **Sepsis Six** bundle as quickly as possible:

1. **Emergency ambulance** — call 111 (or the St John healthcare professional line from a clinic: 0800-262-665)
2. **Oxygen** — if oxygen saturation is 92% or below, targeting 94% or above
3. **Blood cultures** — two sets from separate sites where possible, before antibiotics if this does not cause delay
4. **IV antibiotics** — ceftriaxone 2 g IV (or IM if IV access not yet established). Safe to use even in most patients with penicillin allergy
5. **Blood tests** — FBC, electrolytes, urea, creatinine, liver function, glucose, venous blood gas with lactate; samples from suspected infection source
6. **IV fluids** — if hypotensive or signs of poor circulation: sodium chloride 0.9% 500 mL bolus, repeated as needed up to 2000 mL. Smaller volumes used in elderly patients or those with heart failure.

**Antibiotics should not be delayed because of difficulty gaining IV access or obtaining investigations.**

---

## Amber Flag Management

For Amber Flag Sepsis, your doctor will:

- Lower the threshold for treating as Red Flag Sepsis if more than one amber flag is present
- Seek telephone advice from the relevant specialist or emergency department
- Assess and treat the underlying infection source
- Decide using clinical judgement whether community management is safe
- If managed in the community: observe, investigate, treat the infection, plan review at 24–48 hours (same day for pregnant patients with their lead maternity carer), and provide written guidance for family

---

## After Recovery — Post-Sepsis Syndrome

Most people recover fully from sepsis. However, some experience ongoing symptoms for 6–18 months, known as **post-sepsis syndrome (PSS)**. This is more common after ICU admission or prolonged hospitalisation.

**Physical symptoms** may include:
- Extreme tiredness, poor appetite, insomnia
- Repeated infections
- Reduced kidney function
- Joint and muscle pain
- Feeling cold or excessively sweaty
- Hair loss

**Psychological symptoms** may include:
- Poor concentration, short-term memory loss
- Mood swings, anxiety, depression
- Flashbacks, nightmares, PTSD

There is no single treatment for PSS — your doctor will address each symptom individually. Be aware that **cardiovascular risk is significantly elevated for at least 5 years after sepsis**.

Your doctor may:
- Offer a follow-up appointment one month after discharge
- Use the Post Sepsis Syndrome Screening Tool
- Address modifiable risk factors (diabetes, alcohol/drug use, immunosuppression)
- Refer to Health Improvement Practitioner, Health Coach, or Hauora Māori provider for support

Sepsis Trust NZ (septictrust.org.nz) provides information and support resources.
