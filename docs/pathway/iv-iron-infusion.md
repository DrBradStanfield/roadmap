# IV Iron Infusion

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers IV iron infusion for iron deficiency anaemia in patients aged 14 years and older. This is a clinical procedure managed entirely by your doctor — this information is for understanding what to expect.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Immediate Medical Help

- **Anaphylaxis during or after infusion:** Difficulty breathing, throat swelling, rash, blood pressure collapse, collapse — stop any infusion immediately and call emergency services.

---

## Who Is This For?

IV iron infusion is used when iron deficiency anaemia cannot be adequately treated with oral iron, or when a faster response is needed. Your doctor will assess whether you meet the criteria.

---

## Diagnosis Criteria

Your doctor will check your blood tests before proceeding:

**Anaemia is defined as:**
- Male — haemoglobin (Hb) below 130 g/L
- Female — haemoglobin (Hb) below 115 g/L (including in pregnancy)

**Iron deficiency is defined as:**
- Ferritin below 20 micrograms/L (adults, including pregnant women)
- Ferritin below 100 micrograms/L in patients with chronic inflammatory conditions (e.g., heart failure)

Iron deficiency and anaemia can occur separately or together.

---

## Contraindications

Your doctor will not proceed with IV iron infusion if you have:

- Known anaphylaxis or severe hypersensitivity to iron preparations (iron polymaltose, iron carboxymaltose, or iron sucrose) unresponsive to treatment
- Phosphate level below 0.8 mmol/L (infusion deferred until phosphate is corrected)
- First trimester of pregnancy (contraindicated — your doctor will seek gynaecology advice)
- Anaemia caused by something other than iron deficiency (e.g., B12 or folate deficiency, haemolytic anaemia)
- Iron overload (e.g., haemochromatosis)
- Polycythaemia vera
- Acute hepatitis or decompensated liver cirrhosis
- Acute kidney inflammation (e.g., glomerulonephritis)
- Age under 14 years (paediatric referral required)

---

## Special Situations

### Pregnancy

- **First trimester:** IV iron is contraindicated. Your doctor will seek specialist gynaecology advice.
- **Second and third trimesters:** IV iron may be given if benefits outweigh risks. Your doctor will seek obstetric advice if:
  - Hb below 80 g/L with ferritin below 20 micrograms/L (any stage of pregnancy)
  - Hb below 70 g/L and over 34 weeks gestation
  - Concerns about symptoms
- Fetal bradycardia (slowing of the baby's heart rate) may occur if the mother has an allergic reaction. Fetal monitoring is not routinely required but may be considered if there are clinical concerns.

### Other Special Situations

- **Breastfeeding:** Considered safe — negligible amounts of ferric carboxymaltose cross into breast milk.
- **Bariatric surgery:** IV iron is the preferred treatment after bariatric surgery (oral absorption is poor).
- **Inflammatory bowel disease:** IV iron is superior to oral iron in both effectiveness and tolerability.
- **End-stage renal failure:** Your doctor will seek nephrology advice for specific protocols.

---

## What Happens Before the Infusion

Your doctor will:

1. Check your weight, recent FBC and ferritin (within the past 4 weeks)
2. Check phosphate levels if indicated (see below)
3. Discuss side-effects and provide written information
4. Obtain your written consent
5. Calculate your dose based on your weight
6. Prescribe ferric carboxymaltose (Ferinject)

**Phosphate is checked if you:**
- Have had 2 or more iron infusions in the past 6 months
- Have received 1000 mg or more of ferric carboxymaltose and have symptoms of weakness, bone pain, or mental status change
- Are at risk of low phosphate (BMI below 18, poor nutrition, chronic diarrhoea)

---

## Dosage

**Weight 35 kg or more:**
A single dose of 1000 mg ferric carboxymaltose (Ferinject). This is standard for all patients in this weight range regardless of the degree of anaemia. No adjustment needed in pregnancy.

**Weight less than 35 kg:**
Your doctor will calculate the dose using the Ganzoni formula based on your weight, current haemoglobin, and target haemoglobin. The maximum dose per administration is 20 mg/kg. If more than 1000 mg is needed, multiple infusions are given with at least 7 days between them.

---

## How the Infusion Is Given

Ferric carboxymaltose (Ferinject) is given intravenously — either as a slow injection or as an infusion drip. It is not given by injection into muscle.

**Administration guide:**

| Iron dose | Diluted in | Minimum infusion time |
|---|---|---|
| 100–200 mg | 50 mL NaCl 0.9% | 3 minutes |
| 200–500 mg | 100 mL NaCl 0.9% | 6 minutes |
| 500–1000 mg | 250 mL NaCl 0.9% | 15 minutes |

Resuscitation facilities will be available throughout the procedure.

---

## Monitoring During the Infusion

Your doctor or nurse will:
- Record your baseline observations (blood pressure, temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate)
- Stay with you during the first 5 minutes (when serious reactions are most likely)
- Monitor you continuously throughout for signs of allergic or infusion reactions
- Repeat observations at the end of the infusion and 30 minutes after completion

**If a reaction occurs:**
- The infusion will be paused or stopped
- You may be given antihistamine and/or a corticosteroid
- If the reaction resolves, the infusion may be restarted
- If the reaction persists, the infusion will be abandoned and medical advice sought

---

## Side-Effects to Be Aware Of

**Immediate (during or shortly after infusion):**
- Up to 25% of patients experience mild to moderate reactions: nausea, vomiting, joint or muscle aches, headache, flushing, or chest discomfort
- Small risk of a serious reaction (anaphylaxis): shortness of breath, rash, throat swelling, blood pressure changes

**Delayed (days to weeks after infusion):**
- Up to 20% of patients experience delayed reactions: malaise, muscle or joint aches, headache, nausea, low-grade fever — may last several days
- Low phosphate (hypophosphataemia) — can be severe; causes weakness, bone pain, or fatigue; can occur weeks after infusion
- Approximately 1% risk of permanent skin discolouration at the infusion site (light to dark brown, or occasionally blue, black, purple, or grey). Risk increases with multiple infusions. May fade over time or with laser therapy.

---

## After the Infusion — Follow-Up

- **If taking oral iron:** Pause it for one week after IV iron, as oral iron absorption is reduced immediately after infusion. If oral iron is still needed, restart after at least 7 days.
- **Blood tests:** Your doctor will recheck FBC and ferritin approximately 4 weeks after the last infusion.
- **Ongoing monitoring:** Blood count and ferritin every 3–6 months, as iron deficiency anaemia can recur if the underlying cause has not been treated.
- **Underlying cause:** IV iron does not replace investigation of why you became iron deficient. Your doctor will continue to investigate and manage the underlying cause.

---

## When Your Doctor Will Seek Further Advice

- **Gynaecology:** Severe iron deficiency in the first trimester of pregnancy
- **Obstetrics:** Hb below 80 g/L with ferritin below 20 micrograms/L in pregnancy; Hb below 70 g/L at over 34 weeks; concerns about symptoms
- **Nephrology:** End-stage renal failure
- **Endocrinology:** Phosphate level below 0.8 mmol/L
- **Immunology:** Allergic reaction occurs despite pre-medication
- **Paediatrics:** Patient under 14 years
