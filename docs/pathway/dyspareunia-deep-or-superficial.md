# Dyspareunia (Deep or Superficial)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of pain experienced before, during, or after vaginal intercourse. Pain may be superficial (felt at the vulva or vagina) or deep (felt in the pelvis internally).

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Attention

Tell your doctor immediately if there is any concern about:

- Suspected genital tract or pelvic organ malignancy
- Sexual abuse or trauma (past or current)

---

## Background

Dyspareunia is defined as pain experienced before, during, or after vaginal intercourse. It is classified as **superficial** or **deep**, depending on whether the pain is felt externally (vulva or vagina) or internally (deep pelvic structures).

Physical and psychological causes are both possible and can co-exist.

### Common Terms

- **Vulvodynia** — pain, burning, or discomfort in the vulvar area without an identifiable cause. Likely represents a neuropathic pain syndrome. May affect a localised area or the entire vulva.
- **Vulvar vestibulitis syndrome (VVS)** — a subtype of vulvodynia. Localised pain with attempted penetration and areas tender to touch at the entrance to the vagina (vestibule).
- **Vaginismus** — involuntary spasm or reflexive contraction of the muscles surrounding the entrance to the vagina, making penetration impossible or painful.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a thorough history including:

- Duration of symptoms, and whether symptoms have been present since first becoming sexually active (primary) or developed later (secondary)
- Nature and location of the pain — whether the pain feels abdominal
- Pelvic floor muscle tension or spasm during attempts at penetration
- Hormonal factors — breastfeeding, menopause, contraception use, hormone replacement therapy
- Arousal factors — depression, anxiety, stress, antidepressants, foreplay adequacy, and lubrication use
- Gynaecological history — menstrual symptoms, pelvic pain, vaginal discharge, vulval or vaginal itch, urinary symptoms, symptoms of prolapse, irregular bleeding, past pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) or sexually transmitted infections (STIs)
- Obstetric history — number of pregnancies, vaginal tears and repair
- Sexual health history — sexuality, STI risk

Your doctor will also ask about psychosocial and psychosexual factors:

- Past sexual abuse or trauma
- Intimate partner violence and current safety
- Relationship issues or libido difficulties
- Fear of pregnancy, fear of penetration, or lack of sexual education
- Religious background or negative sexual attitudes
- Mental health conditions

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will examine the abdomen and pelvis, including:

- Appearance of skin around the genitals, vulva, vagina, and cervix
- Assessment for vestibulitis using a gentle cotton bud test
- Swabs of any fissures, ulcers, or blisters
- STI swabs if infection is suspected
- Bimanual palpation for masses, tenderness, and other abnormalities

Note: the extent of examination will be guided by the severity of symptoms and your comfort. Past history of sexual abuse may mean a full examination is not appropriate at the initial visit.

### Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- Pregnancy test
- Pelvic ultrasound (transvaginal ultrasound is most informative)
- Swabs for STI screening (chlamydia, gonorrhoea, bacterial vaginosis, candida, trichomonas)
- Viral culture and PCR for herpes simplex virus if ulcers are present
- Cervical screening if due

---

## Possible Causes

Your doctor will consider the following causes:

- **Sexually transmitted infections** — chlamydia, gonorrhoea, herpes, trichomonas
- **Vaginal infections** — candidiasis (thrush), bacterial vaginosis
- **Oestrogen-deficient states** — postpartum, breastfeeding, combined oral contraceptive pill, postmenopause
- **Skin conditions** — lichen sclerosis, eczema, psoriasis, contact dermatitis, vulvovaginitis
- **Vulvodynia or vulvar vestibulitis syndrome (VVS)**
- **Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)**
- **Endometriosis**
- **Childbirth trauma or post-surgical pain**
- **Vulval, vaginal, or cervical neoplasm**
- **Ovarian cyst, pelvic mass, or ectopic pregnancy**
- **Musculoskeletal causes** — hip osteoarthritis, lumbar pain

## Management

### Vulval Care Advice

Your doctor may advise the following to reduce irritation:

**Avoid:**
- Scented or dyed products — toilet papers, washing powders, baby wipes, lotions, deodorants, talcum powder, soaps and body washes, bubble bath, bath bombs, bath oils
- Tea tree oil
- Tight or synthetic clothing
- Abrasive activities such as cycling or horse riding
- Daily use of mini pads or panty liners
- Long baths

**Suggestions:**
- Use aqueous cream instead of soap on the vulva
- Short, warm water soaks
- Moisturise after washing with a bland emollient such as sorbolene or an unscented oil (vitamin E, jojoba, olive, or vegetable oil)
- Protect skin with a water-resistant barrier (e.g., zinc and castor oil) before swimming or when using pads

### Psychosexual Tips

Your doctor may discuss:

- Enhancing arousal and adequate foreplay to stimulate natural lubrication, reduce discomfort, and relax the vagina
- Being physically and emotionally ready for penetrative intercourse before it occurs
- Considering a change in sexual techniques — longer or different foreplay, different sexual positions, or sex without penetration
- Discussing concerns with your partner, and possibly bringing your partner to an appointment

### Medical Treatment

Your doctor may consider:

- **Topical oestrogen** for atrophic vaginitis if you are postmenopausal
- **Treatment of any identifiable cause** — for example, antibiotics for bacterial vaginosis or STIs, antifungals for candidiasis, or condition-specific management for endometriosis, fibroids, or pelvic organ prolapse
- **Antiviral treatment** (e.g., aciclovir) if herpes simplex virus is suspected, even if the PCR is initially negative
- **Topical anaesthetics** (e.g., lidocaine 5% ointment) applied 15 minutes before sexual activity and wiped off before intercourse, if vulvodynia or VVS is suspected
- **Neuropathic pain medications** — amitriptyline, gabapentin, pregabalin, or duloxetine — if vulvodynia or VVS is suspected (dosing as advised by your doctor)

### Referral

Your doctor may refer you to:

- **Gynaecology** — if there is abdominal pain, no treatable cause is identified, symptoms are not responding to treatment, or if severe pelvic pain is associated with dyspareunia. Some conditions (e.g., endometriosis, vulvodynia) are difficult to diagnose and cause significant morbidity.
- **Psychology or counselling** — experienced in sexual health, if psychosexual or relationship factors are contributing
- **Pelvic floor physiotherapist** — especially if vaginismus is present, for education on pelvic floor relaxation, biofeedback, and vaginal dilator use
