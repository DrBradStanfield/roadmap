# Health Assessment of Young People

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of young people aged 10 to 24 years in a primary care setting.

---

## Background

Adolescence and young adulthood involve three overlapping areas of development:

- **Physical** — growth and secondary sexual characteristics
- **Cognitive** — developing capacity for abstract and complex thinking
- **Social** — navigating friendships, family, and intimate relationships

The majority of young people navigate these changes well. However, the leading causes of death and illness in people aged 15–24 years are:

- Accidents and injury (including self-inflicted)
- Mental health problems (including depression and suicide)
- Behavioural problems, including substance use

Most health problems in this age group are **psychosocial rather than biological**, arising from health risk behaviours, mental health difficulties, and social or environmental risk factors. Co-morbidity is common — particularly mental health and substance use occurring together. Many risk behaviours established in early life (such as poor diet, smoking, and alcohol use) continue into adulthood.

## Assessment

Your doctor will approach the consultation with warmth and openness, as the first visit often sets the tone for future healthcare engagement.

### Barriers to Care

Young people may face specific barriers to accessing healthcare:

- Lack of self-confidence (not wanting to make a fuss)
- Fear of embarrassment
- Concerns about privacy and confidentiality
- Accessibility (transport, opening hours)
- Cost

### Confidentiality

Your doctor will explain the limits of confidentiality before talking to the young person alone:

- Health information is kept private within the treating team, unless there are safety concerns or the patient consents to sharing
- If safety concerns arise, others may need to be involved
- Parents and caregivers do not automatically have access to the young person's health information

### One-on-One Time

Your doctor may offer the young person some time alone during the consultation. This is routine practice — it helps young people learn to communicate about their own health and concerns.

### Developmental Stage

Your doctor will consider the young person's cognitive developmental stage, as this guides communication and management:

- **Early adolescence (~10–13 years):** Concrete thinking, reward-oriented, focused on the "here and now." Your doctor may use direct questions, simple explanations, and short-term goals.
- **Middle adolescence (~14–17 years):** Novelty-seeking, peer-focused, exploring identity, poor risk judgement. Your doctor may focus on medium-term outcomes and risk behaviours.
- **Late adolescence (~17–24 years):** Developing abstract thought, ability to reflect, beginning to plan for the future. Your doctor may use open-ended questions and focus on longer-term goals.

### Capacity to Consent

- At 16 years and older, young people are legally assumed competent to make their own medical decisions (Care of Children Act 2004).
- Younger patients may also be competent to consent if they have sufficient understanding — this is assessed individually, taking into account age, independence, level of schooling, and ability to understand and express their own wishes.

### Psychosocial Assessment (HEEADSSS)

Your doctor will undertake a broad psychosocial assessment using the **HEEADSSS framework** (Home, Education/Employment, Eating, Activities, Drugs, Sexuality, Suicide/Self-harm, Safety). This may take place over several consultations.

Key areas explored include:

- Home environment and family relationships
- Education, employment, and peer relationships
- Eating habits and body image
- Activities, hobbies, and recreation
- Drug, alcohol, and substance use
- Sexual health and relationships
- Mental health, mood, self-harm, and suicidal thinking
- Safety concerns

Your doctor will look for both **risk factors** and **resilience factors**:

- *Personal resilience:* Social skills, self-esteem, sense of control, sense of meaning
- *Family resilience:* Connection to at least one parent, closeness to siblings, family structure
- *External resilience:* Caring adults outside the family, involvement in school or community, friendships, strong cultural identity

If risk is identified, your doctor may assess further for suicide and self-harm.

### Assessment Tools

Your doctor may use structured tools to screen for common concerns:

- **GAD-7** (Generalised Anxiety Disorder Assessment) — screens for anxiety
- **K10** (Kessler Psychological Distress Score) — screens for psychological distress
- **SACS** (Substance Use and Choices Scale) — validated in New Zealand for young people to assess substance use

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will explain what they are going to do, obtain consent, and offer a chaperone where appropriate. The examination typically includes:

- Weight, height, body mass index (BMI), and blood pressure
- Mouth (dental caries) and neck (goitre, lymph nodes)
- Skin (acne, eczema, fungal infections)
- Growth and pubertal development — assessed where possible without undressing (height for age, hair washing frequency, acne, age of first period, voice change, facial hair in males)

Examination of breast or genital development is not routinely indicated. If a Tanner staging assessment is needed before specialist referral, young people can usually indicate their own stage from a chart.

### Preventive Activities

Your doctor may also:

- Check immunisations are up to date
- Ask about smoking and vaping
- Screen sexually active young people for sexually transmissible infections
- Assess for risk-taking behaviour

## Management

Your doctor will develop a management plan in conjunction with the young person and, where appropriate, with their parents or carers. This typically involves:

- Summarising the main concerns and issues identified
- Reinforcing the young person's strengths and successes
- Identifying areas where treatment or support may be needed
- Discussing risky behaviours and protective strategies
- Encouraging involvement of a trusted adult
- Setting realistic and achievable goals
- Advising parents on how to support the plan
- Discussing how results or reminders will be communicated to protect privacy
- Arranging follow-up, as it can take time to complete a full assessment and build trust

### Promoting Mental Health and Resilience

Your doctor may discuss:

- Positive coping skills (hobbies, sport, spirituality, friendships)
- Connectedness to friends, family, and community
- Problem-solving skills for peer, school, and family difficulties
- The difference between thoughts and feelings, and strategies for mood self-management

### Growth Concerns

Growth concerns are common and rarely require active intervention. However, your doctor will consider referral for specialist evaluation if secondary sexual characteristic development appears in:

- Female patients aged under 8 years
- Male patients aged under 9 years

Other growth-related concerns your doctor may consider include:

- Mild maturational delay
- Gynaecomastia in males
- Primary or secondary amenorrhoea
- Short stature

### Further Management by Concern

Depending on what is identified, your doctor may follow specific pathways for:

- General mental health in youth
- Child abuse and neglect
- Risk-taking behaviour
- Sexual health in youth
- Chronic conditions in youth
- Culturally and linguistically diverse (CALD) young people
- Acne
- Dysmenorrhoea
- Polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS)
