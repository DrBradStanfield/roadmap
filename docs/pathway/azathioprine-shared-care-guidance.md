# Azathioprine Shared Care Guidance

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This guidance supports GPs in monitoring and managing patients on azathioprine after a specialist has initiated therapy. It does not cover initiation — that must always be done by a specialist.

---

## What Is Azathioprine?

Azathioprine is an immunosuppressant (purine analogue). It is a prodrug, converted in the body to mercaptopurine.

**Indications:**
- Post-organ transplant
- Ulcerative colitis
- Crohn's disease
- Eczema
- Rheumatoid arthritis
- Other autoimmune conditions

**Contraindications:**
- Mercaptopurine sensitivity
- Very low TPMT (thiopurine methyltransferase) levels

---

## Before Starting — Specialist Will Have Checked

Prior to initiation, your specialist will have arranged:
- TPMT level — predicts risk of myelosuppression. Deficiency occurs in approximately 1 in 250 European people.
- FBC
- Liver function
- Electrolytes and creatinine
- Hepatitis B and C serology, HIV serology, varicella zoster virus (VZV) serology, and Quantiferon Gold (TB test)

---

## Dosing

- **Standard:** 50–150 mg orally daily (1–2 mg/kg per day)
- **Dermatology:**
  - Normal TPMT activity: 2–3 mg/kg per day
  - Intermediate TPMT activity: 1–1.5 mg/kg per day
- **Renal impairment:**
  - CrCl >50 mL/min: No adjustment needed
  - CrCl 10–50 mL/min: Give 75% of normal dose
  - CrCl <10 mL/min: Give 50% of normal dose
- Once stable, specialist follow-up annually

---

## When to Stop and Seek Urgent Advice

**Stop the drug and arrange urgent specialist consult if:**
- Hypersensitivity reaction
- Abnormal FBC
- Abnormal liver function — ALT or AST more than 2 times the upper limit of normal
- Deterioration in underlying condition
- Hypotension
- Interstitial nephritis

Your doctor will make this assessment and manage the situation — do not stop the medication without medical advice.

---

## Monitoring Schedule

Your doctor will review you every 3 months to check your condition is stable.

**Blood tests — FBC, liver function, electrolytes, creatinine:**
- Every 2 weeks until stable, then every 3 months
- If at risk of low TPMT or renal impairment: monthly monitoring

**At each review, your doctor will also:**
- Confirm you have read and understood your azathioprine information sheet
- Remind you about sun-smart behaviour and arrange annual whole-body skin checks (azathioprine increases the long-term risk of skin cancer)
- Check for symptoms of infection or inflammation that could suggest a lymphoproliferative disorder (lymphoma risk is increased)
- Consider TB screening (Quantiferon Gold) if not already done, particularly if you are a healthcare worker, migrant from a high-incidence country, or household contact of someone at increased TB risk

**For IBD patients:** Continue thiopurines before and during pregnancy — do not stop without specialist advice.

---

## Adverse Effects

| Category | What to watch for | Action |
|---|---|---|
| Hypersensitivity | Muscle pain and fever | Stop drug, see doctor urgently |
| General | Nausea, vomiting, flu-like illness, headache | Nausea may improve with divided doses or taking after food. Consult doctor if persistent |
| Blood | Bruising, bleeding, recurrent infection (signs of myelosuppression) | Stop drug, see doctor urgently |
| Liver | Jaundice, hepatotoxicity (rare) | Stop drug, see doctor urgently |
| Gut | Pancreatitis (rare) — severe abdominal pain | Stop drug, see doctor urgently |
| Kidney | Interstitial nephritis | Stop drug, see doctor urgently |
| Hypotension | Profound low blood pressure | Stop drug, see doctor urgently |
| Malignancy | Increased risk of non-melanoma skin cancer and possibly lymphoma (long-term) | Report new skin changes or lumps to your doctor |

*Note: Mild lymphocytopenia is an acceptable and expected effect.*

---

## Drug Interactions

Tell your doctor and pharmacist about all medications. Key interactions:

- **Allopurinol and febuxostat — avoid.** These dramatically increase azathioprine toxicity.
- **ACE inhibitors** — increased risk of myelosuppression
- **Warfarin** — azathioprine may reduce warfarin effect; dose adjustment may be needed
- **Methotrexate, cyclophosphamide, ciclosporin** — increased risk of myelotoxicity
- **Trimethoprim, co-trimoxazole, clozapine** — use with caution

Your doctor can check the full interactions list via the NZ Formulary or consult a pharmacist.

---

## Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

Azathioprine is a **Category D medication in pregnancy** — it may increase the risk of fetal malformation or other irreversible effects, but is not absolutely contraindicated.

- If you are planning a pregnancy or become pregnant, seek urgent specialist advice immediately
- IBD patients: do not stop azathioprine without specialist guidance — the risks of uncontrolled IBD may outweigh medication risks

---

## Vaccinations

While on azathioprine, your immune response is suppressed. Your doctor will advise:

- **Annual influenza (flu) vaccine** — recommended
- **Pneumococcal vaccine** every 5 years — recommended (may not be funded depending on your diagnosis)
- **Shingrix (shingles vaccine)** — consider; the older Zostavax vaccine is a live vaccine and is **absolutely contraindicated** while on azathioprine, especially with concurrent steroids or biologics
- **Hepatitis B vaccine** — consider if non-immune and in a higher risk group

**Live vaccines to avoid:**
- MMR (measles, mumps, rubella)
- Oral rotavirus, oral polio, oral typhoid
- BCG (tuberculosis)
- Varicella zoster (chickenpox and Zostavax)
- Yellow fever

---

## Surgery

Azathioprine may need to be stopped before surgery. Your doctor will discuss this with the surgical and prescribing teams — do not stop the medication without medical advice.

---

## Important Note

This information is a guide. Your individual management will be tailored by your doctor based on your condition, test results, and other medications. Always discuss changes or concerns with your GP or specialist.
