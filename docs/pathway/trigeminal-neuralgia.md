# Trigeminal Neuralgia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of trigeminal neuralgia in adults.

---

## What Is Trigeminal Neuralgia?

Trigeminal neuralgia causes recurrent, sudden episodes of severe, sharp, electric or jolting pain on one side of the face. Episodes are typically very brief (seconds).

Some people also have continuous background aching or burning pain in the same area between episodes.

**Common triggers:**
- Touch – touching the face, brushing teeth, blowing the nose, shaving
- Facial movement – chewing, talking, smiling
- Temperature – drinking hot or cold liquids, cold breezes, washing with cold water
- Medication use, alcohol

It is more common in people over 60 years of age.

Diagnosis is based on the history — there is no single diagnostic test.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history asking about:
- When the pain started, how often it occurs, and whether there was a rash around onset
- Which part of the face is affected — usually one side, rarely both
- The quality of pain (sharp/electric lasting seconds, or background aching between episodes)
- What triggers the pain

**Examination** your doctor will perform:
- Neurological examination — people with trigeminal neuralgia typically do not have sensory (feeling) loss; if sensory loss is present, a different diagnosis (trigeminal neuropathy) is considered
- Ear, nose, and throat examination
- Oral health check

**Conditions to distinguish from trigeminal neuralgia:**
- Trigeminal sensory neuropathy
- Dental pain
- Postherpetic neuralgia (nerve pain following shingles)
- Glossopharyngeal neuralgia (shooting pain in the tongue or throat)
- Idiopathic facial pain (dull, persistent cheek pain)

**Blood tests** are arranged before starting medication:
- Full blood count (FBC)
- Liver function tests (LFT)
- Urea and electrolytes

---

## Management

Management is guided by your doctor. Treatment is primarily with medication.

### First-Line Treatment: Carbamazepine

Carbamazepine is the standard first-line treatment for trigeminal neuralgia.

- The controlled-release (slow-release) tablet is better tolerated than immediate-release
- Typically started at a low dose (100 mg twice daily) and increased gradually up to 400 mg twice daily
- Most people who will respond see improvement fairly quickly
- Side effects are common and may limit how much the dose can be increased
- Once pain has been controlled for 4 weeks, the dose is gradually reduced and stopped
- Treatment can be restarted if pain returns

Your doctor will check for drug interactions and contraindications before prescribing.

### Second-Line Treatments: Other Anti-Seizure Medications

If carbamazepine is ineffective or not tolerated, other medications may be used. These include gabapentin, pregabalin, or lamotrigine — medications that calm nerve signalling and are generally well tolerated.

**Gabapentin** — usually started at a low evening dose and increased slowly. Maximum dose 2400 mg/day. Dose reduction needed in kidney disease. Caution with sedation (driving, falls) and in people with a history of substance misuse.

**Pregabalin** — started at 25–75 mg at night, increased gradually. Not recommended in pregnancy unless clearly necessary. Dose reduction needed in kidney disease. Same sedation and substance misuse cautions as gabapentin.

**Other options:** sodium valproate or topiramate.

Combinations of medications are occasionally needed but increase the risk of side effects — neurology advice is sought before combining.

### When Medication Doesn't Work

If medications fail or are poorly tolerated, your doctor may refer you for a specialist assessment to consider surgical or procedural options (neurosurgery review).

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will refer you to a neurologist if:
- There are neurological signs on examination
- You have sensory (feeling) loss in the face, rather than just pain
- Facial pain is unexplained after ENT and dental causes have been ruled out
- There is no response to first-line treatment
- The diagnosis is uncertain

Neurosurgery review is considered if medications have failed or are not tolerated.

---

> **Note:** This information is intended to help you understand the clinical approach to trigeminal neuralgia. Diagnosis and treatment decisions should always be made by your doctor, who will assess your individual situation.
