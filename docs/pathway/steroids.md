# IBD Medications (Steroids & Others)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the main groups of medications used to manage inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including ulcerative colitis (UC) and Crohn's disease (CD). Treatment is highly individualised — your doctor will decide which medicines are appropriate for you.

---

## Important Safety Note — Immunosuppression

Many IBD medications reduce immune system activity. If you are on steroids, thiopurines, methotrexate, or a biologic, your doctor should:

- Add an alert to your medical notes
- Recall you for annual flu vaccinations
- Discuss precautions around infection risk and vaccinations

---

## Aminosalicylates (5-ASA)

Examples include **mesalazine** (brands: Pentasa, Asacol), **sulfasalazine**, and **olsalazine**.

### What they are used for

- First-line treatment for ulcerative colitis (UC) — both for active flares and long-term maintenance
- Limited benefit in Crohn's disease

### Key points

- Mesalazine is generally well-tolerated
- Sulfasalazine and olsalazine are less commonly used due to side effects but remain useful in some situations
- Rectal formulations (suppositories, enemas) are used for disease affecting the lower bowel, either alone or alongside oral tablets

### Monitoring

- Your doctor may check kidney function (creatinine) and urine every 6 months

### Safety warning — blood disorders

Report any unexplained bleeding, bruising, purple spots on skin, sore throat, fever, or malaise to your doctor promptly. A blood count will be needed, and the drug may need to be stopped.

---

## Steroids

Example: **prednisone**

### What they are used for

- Used to bring IBD under control during a flare-up — for both ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease
- Not used for long-term maintenance

### Your doctor may consider

- Starting at a full dose (e.g., 40 mg per day) and reducing slowly over about 8 weeks
- A slow wean is important to prevent early relapse
- If you need steroids more than twice a year, or for prolonged periods, your doctor will likely refer you to a gastroenterologist to consider stronger maintenance treatments

### Monitoring

- Long-term or repeated steroid use can weaken bones. Your doctor may consider:
  - Vitamin D and calcium supplementation
  - Bone density monitoring (DEXA scan)

## Immunomodulators

### Thiopurines — azathioprine and mercaptopurine (6MP)

These are the first-line immunomodulator options.

**What they are used for:**
- When 5-ASA medicines are not effective enough in ulcerative colitis
- When repeated courses of steroids are needed
- For maintenance in Crohn's disease, particularly with severe or complex disease

**Key points:**
- These medicines are only started under specialist (gastroenterologist) supervision after a full discussion of risks and benefits
- They take 3 to 6 months to reach full effect
- Generally considered safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding — discuss with your doctor and specialist

**Side effects to be aware of:**
- Nausea and vomiting
- Flu-like symptoms (headache, muscle aches)
- Bone marrow suppression (low blood counts)
- Liver toxicity
- Long-term: small increased risk of skin cancer and lymphoma due to immune suppression

**Monitoring:**
- Regular blood tests (full blood count and liver function) — weekly for the first 4 weeks, then every 3 months long-term

**Vaccinations:**
- Live vaccines must be avoided while on these medicines. Examples include MMR, varicella (chickenpox), herpes zoster (shingles), oral polio, intranasal flu, BCG, oral typhoid, and yellow fever

---

### Methotrexate (second-line)

Used when thiopurines are not tolerated or not effective.

**Key points:**
- Taken once weekly, either orally or by injection under the skin
- **Must not be taken in pregnancy** — effective contraception is essential
- Men and women should wait at least 3 to 6 months after stopping before trying to conceive
- Folic acid 5 mg is co-prescribed weekly (on a different day) to reduce side effects

**Monitoring:**
- Full blood count and liver function tests every 3 months

## Biologics

Examples: **infliximab** (Remicade) and **adalimumab** (Amgevita, Humira)

These are potent anti-inflammatory medicines used when other treatments have not worked. They work by blocking a protein called tumour necrosis factor (TNF).

### What they are used for

- Reserved for moderate-to-severe IBD that has not responded to other treatments
- Initiated by gastroenterologists only

### Before starting

Your specialist will arrange screening tests including:
- Varicella zoster virus (chickenpox immunity)
- Hepatitis B and C
- Tuberculosis (TB) — as the medication can reactivate latent TB

### Your doctor may consider

- Withholding your dose if you develop an active infection
- Pausing treatment before major surgery (discuss with your surgeon and gastroenterologist)

### Key safety points

- Flu and pneumococcal vaccinations may be less effective while on biologics — still recommended
- Live vaccines must be avoided (same list as for thiopurines above)
- Regular review by a gastroenterologist (usually at least every 6 months once stable)
