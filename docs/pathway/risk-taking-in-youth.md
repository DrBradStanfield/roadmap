# Risk-taking in Youth

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers how doctors assess and manage risk-taking behaviour in young people, distinguishing normal adolescent behaviour from situations that require intervention.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

High-risk-taking behaviour in a young person who already has **multiple risk or vulnerability factors and few protective factors** warrants urgent review.

If there is an immediate mental health emergency or risk of serious harm to self or others, call **111** or go to the emergency department.

---

## Background

Risk-taking and novelty-seeking are a normal part of adolescent development — they help young people build resilience, self-worth, independence, and new skills. However, intervention may be needed if risk-taking:

- Interferes with normal development
- Poses a serious risk to health and safety
- Is characterised by impulsiveness, thrill-seeking, cognitive immaturity, or low self-worth

It can be difficult to prevent risk-taking outright. Reflecting on past episodes in a non-judgemental way can help promote safer choices in future.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess by distinguishing normal from concerning behaviours and identifying risk and protective factors.

### Normal Behaviours in Young People

- Moodiness and flare-ups
- Talkative with friends, monosyllabic with family
- A need to be like peers and to belong
- Sleeping in
- Being critical and argumentative
- Some experimentation with new things, including mild substance use

### Concerning Behaviours in Young People

- Wild mood swings
- Dramatic or sudden change in behaviour
- Isolation from peers
- Sudden deterioration in school performance
- Violent or aggressive behaviour
- Dangerous substance or alcohol use
- Excessive sleeping or withdrawal
- Loss of routine

### Assessment Framework (HEeADSSS)

Your doctor may use the [HEeADSSS Assessment framework](https://starship.org.nz/guidelines/adolescent-consultation/) to identify risky and protective behaviours. This tool covers Home, Education/Employment, Eating, Activities, Drugs, Sexuality, Suicide/Depression, and Safety.

### Cognitive Developmental Stage

Your doctor will tailor their approach based on the young person's developmental stage:

**Early adolescence (approximately 10–13 years)**
- Tends to be concrete in thinking, reward-orientated, focused on "here and now"
- Uses emotional reasoning
- Responds well to direct questions, reassurance, and short-term outcome framing

**Middle adolescence (approximately 14–17 years)**
- Novelty-seeking, peer-focused, exploring identity, poor risk judgement
- Starting to reflect and accept responsibility for consequences
- Responds to medium-term outcome framing and discussion of immediate concerns

**Late adolescence (approximately 17–24 years)**
- Abstract thought, ability to reflect, starting to plan for the future
- Responds to open-ended questions, reflection, and medium-to-long-term goal-setting

### High-risk Patient Characteristics

Young people at highest risk often:
- Rarely self-present — usually brought by concerned others (e.g., youth worker)
- Have chaotic lifestyles and are marginalised with few resources
- Are under-serviced and dealing with extreme circumstances without adequate support
- May take risks as a coping mechanism

### Risky Behaviours with Negative Consequences

Your doctor will look for:
- Early and/or high-risk sexual activity (e.g., unprotected sex when intoxicated or with strangers)
- Driving while intoxicated
- Substance or alcohol use
- Runaway behaviour
- Dropping out of school
- Criminal activity
- Severe dieting or disordered eating

### Protective Factors

These buffer and reduce the degree of risk:
- Presence of at least one stable, positive adult relationship
- Sense of belonging to school, friends, and family
- Belonging to a group
- Developing a skill, participating in sports, volunteering, or contributing to a cause

### Suicide Risk Assessment

Your doctor will screen for suicidal ideation or intent if relevant risk factors are present, including:
- Previous suicide attempts or self-harm
- Mental illness (especially depression, substance abuse, schizophrenia, eating disorders, personality disorder)
- Cultural disconnection
- Recent loss or isolation (e.g., relationship breakdown, custody, family court involvement)
- Family history of suicide, or recent suicide of a close friend or family member
- Chronic pain or chronic illness
- Expressed feelings of hopelessness

**High alert indicators include:**
- A suicide or self-harm plan, with or without preparation, or a recent attempt
- Current hopelessness or intense anger
- Isolation, inability to identify supports
- Inability to identify reasons for living
- Indirect references to own death
- Recent loss of an important relationship
- Substance use or withdrawal (increases disinhibition and impulsivity)

Asking about suicidal ideation does not create or worsen suicide risk. Suicide risk is dynamic — reassessment is needed if circumstances change.

### Overall Risk Stratification

Your doctor will determine one of four risk levels:

| Risk Level | Description |
|---|---|
| **No risk** | Not yet engaged in risk-taking; protective factors present; no vulnerability factors |
| **Low risk** | Safe, sporadic, experimental risk-taking (e.g., tried cannabis with peers but stable family/school); no vulnerability factors; protective factors present |
| **Moderate risk** | Risk-taking in the presence of vulnerability factors (e.g., depressed adolescent with low self-esteem smoking cannabis alone); few protective factors |
| **High risk** | Unsafe risk-taking with major disruption or risk to health (e.g., school-disengaged, little family support, antisocial behaviour, frequent substance abuse); few or no protective factors |

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following approaches depending on the situation and risk level.

### For All Patients

Your doctor will:
- Build therapeutic rapport — being open, non-judgemental, and using reflective listening
- Find out what the young person sees as the problem
- Encourage connection with family, friends, significant adults (e.g., sports coach), or local peer groups (e.g., school diversity group, church youth group)
- Help with problem-solving by brainstorming potential solutions and alternative choices

Your doctor uses the **BEARDS** approach to communication with young people:
- **B**ite your tongue — avoid telling the young person what to do
- **E**xpress empathy — affirm and reflect on what they are saying; ensure the young person does at least 50% of the talking
- **A**void argument — confrontation leads only to resistance
- **R**oll with resistance — emphasise personal choice and control; use "I am wondering..." to explore pros and cons
- **D**evelop discrepancy — raise awareness of negative consequences non-judgementally
- **S**upport self-efficacy — show belief in their ability to change; help set a realistic goal

### No or Low Risk

Your doctor may:
- Give positive feedback
- Identify a trusted adult in the young person's life and encourage their involvement
- Reinforce the young person's strengths and promote good mental health and resilience:
  - Discuss positive coping skills (hobbies, sport, spirituality, friendships)
  - Encourage connectedness to friends, family, and community
  - Discuss problem-solving skills for dealing with peer, school, and family problems
  - Help identify the difference between thoughts and feelings; promote positive thinking and mood self-management

### Moderate Risk

Intervention is needed to prevent escalation to high-risk activities. Your doctor may:
- Identify and reinforce strengths; promote good mental health and resilience (as above)
- Check whether the young person understands the health risks and possible consequences of their activities
- Help them set goals and identify potential barriers and enablers to behaviour change
- Offer referral to relevant specialist treatment and support services
- Consider a team approach
- Offer follow-up to review progress and provide further support
- Encourage involvement of a trusted adult

### High Risk

Your doctor may:
- Request an acute mental health assessment:
  - **Acute child and youth mental health assessment** if the young person is aged under 18 years
  - **Acute adult mental health assessment** if aged 18 years or over
- Check whether the young person understands the health risks and possible consequences of their activities
- Engage in a trusting, tolerant relationship to support access to other services
- Initiate collaborative case management with a team approach
- Strongly encourage involvement of a trusted adult — if there is a serious risk to self or others, your doctor may need to insist on this
- Reinforce strengths and promote mental health and resilience (as above)

### Specific Circumstances

Your doctor may also address:
- **Alcohol and drugs** — referral to alcohol and drug support services
- **Mental health** — follow the General Mental Health in Youth pathway; if immediate help is needed, request acute mental health assessment (above)
- **Transgender health** — follow the Gender Diversity and Transgender Health pathway
- **Sexual abuse** — follow the Sexual Abuse of a Child or Young Person pathway
- **Risk of offending** — contact the Oranga Tamariki duty social worker for advice on appropriate social service support
