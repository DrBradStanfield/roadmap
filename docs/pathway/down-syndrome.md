# Down Syndrome

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the diagnosis, ongoing management, and health monitoring for people with Down syndrome across the lifespan.

---

## About Down Syndrome

Down syndrome (DS) is the most common chromosomal disorder, occurring in approximately 1 in 1,000 births in New Zealand. It is caused by an extra copy of chromosome 21. There are three types:

- **Trisomy 21** (about 94%) — a complete additional copy of chromosome 21 in every cell
- **Mosaicism** (about 2–4%) — an extra chromosome 21 in some but not all cells; features may be milder
- **Translocation** (about 3–5%) — extra chromosome 21 material attached to another chromosome; may be inherited from a parent who is an unaffected carrier

The risk of trisomy 21 increases with maternal age, but most babies with Down syndrome are born to mothers under 35 because more babies are born in that age group overall.

People with Down syndrome do experience health challenges, but advances in healthcare, inclusive education, and community support have greatly improved quality of life and life expectancy.

---

## Common Features

Down syndrome is often recognisable at birth. Features include:

- Intellectual disability and hypotonia (low muscle tone)
- Short stature
- Flat face, depressed nasal bridge, small midface
- Eyes slanting upwards, epicanthal folds
- Small, low-set ears; short and wide neck; thickened nuchal skin fold
- Large tongue (macroglossia)
- Single palmar crease; fifth finger clinodactyly
- Increased gap between first and second toes
- Structural heart abnormalities (present at birth in up to 40%)
- Hearing and visual impairment
- Hypothyroidism and blood abnormalities

These features may not all be present, and some may appear later in the newborn period.

## Associated Health Conditions

People with Down syndrome have higher rates of several health conditions:

| System | Conditions |
|---|---|
| Cardiac | Congenital heart disease (40–50%), mitral valve prolapse, aortic regurgitation |
| Endocrine | Hypothyroidism (15%), obesity, diabetes; males usually infertile; females have reduced fertility |
| ENT | Hearing loss (38–75% in second decade), serous otitis media (50–70%), sleep apnoea (31%) |
| Eye | Cataracts (30–60%), severe refractive errors (50%), strabismus, keratoconus |
| Haematological | Blood dyscrasias including leukaemia; immune deficiency; increased autoimmune conditions (e.g. coeliac disease) |
| Neurological | Early-onset Alzheimer dementia, accelerated ageing, epilepsy (5–10%), high pain threshold with poor localisation |
| Orthopaedic | Atlantoaxial instability (15% — can cause spinal cord compression), joint instability, osteoporosis |
| Psychiatric | Psychiatric disorders in 22–38%, especially depression |
| Skin | Dry skin, eczema, hidradenitis suppurativa, cheilitis, vitiligo, alopecia |

**Important:** People with Down syndrome often have a high pain threshold and may not localise pain well. Always investigate pain reports from the person or their caregiver thoroughly.

## Diagnosis

Down syndrome may be diagnosed:

- **Antenatally** — through prenatal screening (blood tests, nuchal translucency ultrasound) and diagnostic testing (chorionic villus sampling or amniocentesis)
- **At birth or in infancy** — based on clinical features, confirmed with chromosomal analysis (karyotype)

If Down syndrome is suspected but not confirmed, your doctor will arrange urgent paediatric assessment.

---

## Initial Management and Family Support

A new diagnosis of Down syndrome — whether before or after birth — is often a difficult time for families. Your doctor can help connect you with:

- **Psychosocial support** — child and family support programmes, counselling
- **Financial support** — disability allowances and funding
- **Child development services** — speech and language therapy, occupational therapy, physiotherapy, dietitian
- **Needs assessment and service coordination**
- **Ministry of Education support** — for children's educational needs

If there is a family history of Down syndrome or you want to understand recurrence risk in future pregnancies, your doctor can refer you to a genetic health service for counselling.

## Health Monitoring by Age

Your doctor will coordinate regular health checks. The schedule below outlines what is monitored at each stage.

### Newborn (0–28 days)

- Complete physical examination by a paediatrician
- Newborn hearing check
- Blood tests — chromosomal analysis, full blood count (FBC), thyroid function tests (TFT)

### Infancy (1 month – 2 years)

- Watch for signs of atlantoaxial instability (neck pain, change in gait, bladder/bowel changes)
- Stool pattern (consider coeliac disease, Hirschsprung's disease)
- Cardiac examination (consider congenital heart disease)
- Respiratory symptoms (consider obstructive sleep apnoea)
- TFT at age 6 months and 12 months
- ENT assessment at 9–12 months
- Ophthalmology by age 9 months
- Formal audiology at 2 months; behavioural hearing test at 9–12 months
- First dental visit by age 2 years

### Early Childhood (2–5 years)

- Development and behaviour check (including autism screening)
- Atlantoaxial instability monitoring
- Stool pattern — coeliac disease, constipation
- Cardiac monitoring; cardiology referral as needed
- Sleep apnoea assessment; ENT referral for tonsillectomy/adenoidectomy if significant
- Seizure screening
- Joint and muscle tone issues (feet, ankles)
- Skin and hair (dry skin, alopecia areata)
- Coeliac HLA genotyping at age 2 and 4.5 years
- Iron and TFT every 2 years from 4.5 years
- Vision: ophthalmology at 18 months–2 years, and again at 4.5 years
- Hearing: tympanometry for CSOM (50–70% affected at this age); audiology at 4.5 years if no CSOM
- Dental visits every 6–12 months

### Middle Childhood (5–13 years)

- Development, behaviour, and autism monitoring
- Atlantoaxial instability
- Coeliac disease, constipation
- Cardiologist review if known heart defect
- Sleep apnoea
- Seizures and neurological issues
- Joint and muscle issues
- TFT every 2 years
- Vision: optometrist assessment at age 10 years
- Hearing: audiology at age 10 years
- Dental visits every 6–12 months

### Adolescence (13–21 years)

- Behaviour and mental health (depression common)
- Sexuality, puberty, and contraception
- Atlantoaxial instability
- Coeliac disease
- Cardiac review; echocardiogram may be recommended at age 18 years
- Sleep apnoea
- Seizures, TIAs
- Joint issues
- TFT annually; coeliac HLA and iron as needed
- Vision: optometrist every 2 years
- Hearing: audiology every 2 years
- Dental visits every 6–12 months

### Adults

Annual monitoring includes:

- Hearing and vision check in clinic (audiology and optometrist reviews every 2 years)
- Dental assessment every 6 months — note that some patients need endocarditis prophylaxis for dental procedures
- Cancer screening (bowel, breast, cervical, prostate) as per standard guidelines
- Memory and functioning check from age 35 (early-onset dementia screening)
- Bone density scan at menopause (females) or age 40 (hypogonadal males)
- Echocardiogram monitoring — mitral valve prolapse develops in about 50% of adults
- Annual blood tests: TFT, FBC, HbA1c, fasting lipids; screen for coeliac disease symptoms (low threshold for testing)
- Consider vitamin D supplementation, especially for those in institutional care

---

## Immunisations

People with Down syndrome should follow the New Zealand Immunisation Schedule, including the annual influenza vaccine and any additional funded vaccines for eligible special groups.

---

## Genetic Counselling

If you have a personal or family history of Down syndrome and want to understand recurrence risk for future pregnancies, your doctor can refer you to a genetic health service for counselling. The type of Down syndrome (trisomy 21, mosaic, or translocation) affects the recurrence risk.
