# Subcutaneous Foreign Bodies

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers soft-tissue (subcutaneous) foreign bodies — objects embedded under the skin, such as splinters, glass, or metal. It does not cover foreign bodies in hollow organs or body cavities.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Care

Go to an emergency department promptly if:

- The foreign body has been present for less than 48 hours **and** removal is likely to be complex (e.g., requires image intensification or is near vital structures distal to the elbow)
- There are signs of spreading infection (increasing redness, swelling, warmth, pus, red streaks, or fever)
- A puncture wound to the sole of the foot is infected — this can progress to septic arthritis or bone infection (osteomyelitis) if not treated promptly

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history to determine:

- The likely size of the foreign body
- What the material is, and whether it is solid enough to be completely removed
- How long it has been in place — foreign bodies present for more than two weeks are harder to remove

Your doctor will examine the area to:

- Determine how deep the foreign body is, and its relationship to nearby nerves and arteries
- Look for signs of infection — infection makes removal more urgent, and may actually make it easier once pus has formed around the object

Your doctor may consider investigations:

- **X-ray** — can identify some types of glass and metal objects. A normal X-ray does not exclude glass, but is important to arrange if there is any doubt about glass in a wound
- **Ultrasound** — can help locate both metallic and non-metallic foreign bodies

Investigations are not always necessary, but if needed may be funded by ACC. Your doctor will time any imaging so that removal can be carried out immediately after — the radiographer can mark the site on the skin.

---

## Management

### Removal in General Practice

If the foreign body is visible, or your doctor is comfortable removing it, your doctor may consider removal in the clinic:

- The site is marked before local anaesthetic is given (the anaesthetic can obscure the object)
- Local anaesthetic with adrenaline is used (except on fingers and toes) to reduce bleeding — your doctor will wait 10–15 minutes for full effect
- A small incision is made along the long axis of the foreign body; the object is located with the scalpel tip, then carefully grasped and removed with small forceps
- Long-embedded foreign bodies may be surrounded by fibrous tissue and require more effort to remove
- The wound is thoroughly cleaned with tap water and debrided before dressing

### When Your Doctor May Refer

- If your doctor is not confident or does not have the resources to remove it safely, they may refer you to a colleague with greater surgical experience
- If the foreign body has been present for less than 48 hours and is straightforward, your doctor may request an emergency department assessment — they will call ahead to arrange a time, and you may need to remain nil by mouth in case further surgical management is required
- If the foreign body has been present for more than 48 hours, or if an attempted removal in the community was unsuccessful, your doctor may refer to orthopaedics or plastic surgery for assessment

### Tetanus Prophylaxis

Your doctor will check whether you are up to date with tetanus vaccination, as puncture wounds carry a risk of tetanus infection.

### Antibiotics for Plantar Puncture Wounds

Antibiotics are **not** routinely recommended for plantar (sole of the foot) puncture wounds unless there is infection. However, your doctor may consider a 3-day course in certain situations:

- **Soil- or sewerage-contaminated wounds:**
  - Amoxicillin + clavulanic acid (Augmentin) 500/125 mg three times a day
  - If mild penicillin allergy: cefalexin 500 mg four times a day + metronidazole 600 mg twice a day
  - If severe penicillin allergy: infectious diseases advice will be sought

- **Puncture wound through a shoe** (risk of *Pseudomonas* infection):
  - Ciprofloxacin 750 mg twice a day

- **Other plantar puncture wounds:**
  - Flucloxacillin 1000 mg four times a day
  - If mild penicillin allergy: cefalexin 500 mg four times a day
  - If severe penicillin allergy: roxithromycin 300 mg daily

Note: There is no reliable evidence supporting coring, button-holing, or probing of plantar puncture wounds, and these carry risk of harm.
