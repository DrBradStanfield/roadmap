# Terminal Haemorrhage in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the anticipation and management of acute large haemorrhage (severe bleeding) in adult palliative care patients. This is a rare but serious event that can cause death within minutes.

---

## Background

Terminal haemorrhage in palliative care is:
- Usually caused by a tumour eroding a large blood vessel, or by a clotting disorder (coagulopathy).
- May be **external** (visible, large amount of blood) or **internal** (little to see externally, but the patient deteriorates rapidly).
- Natural history varies: death may occur within minutes, or there may be a more gradual deterioration over hours with increasing low blood pressure, restlessness, and pain.

---

## Recognising Internal Bleeding

Internal haemorrhage may not be immediately obvious. Signs to be aware of:
- Can present over several hours.
- Often begins with the patient appearing restless and uncomfortable but unable to describe why.
- There may have been multiple small bleeds over preceding days, or melaena (dark, tar-like stools indicating bleeding in the upper gut).

---

## Anticipatory Planning

Because terminal haemorrhage can occur suddenly, good advance planning is essential. Your doctor will discuss this with you and your family if significant bleeding is a risk.

**Your doctor will:**
- Discuss the possibility of haemorrhage with you and your family/whānau, sensitively and honestly.
- Create a written plan and emergency letter so everyone knows what to do.
- Document:
  - Goals of care and resuscitation status.
  - Who to call in the event of a haemorrhage.
  - Whether sedation medication (such as midazolam) is to be available, and whether carers are willing and able to administer it.
  - Note: **opioids (pain medications) are not appropriate if there is no pain** — they have no role in haemorrhage without pain.
- Ensure the following are available in the home:
  - Dark-coloured towels and sheets (to cover and absorb blood without appearing alarming to the patient)
  - Gloves, aprons, and clinical waste bags
  - Appropriate medications if prescribed

**About midazolam (sedation medication):**
- Midazolam may be prescribed in advance to ease distress and sedate the patient during a terminal haemorrhage.
- Having it available may help the patient and family feel more prepared and in control.
- However, administering it typically requires a healthcare provider to be called to attend, which takes time. This means the patient's family may need to leave the patient's side to make calls. Medications also need to be stored safely and disposed of if unused.
- Your doctor will seek specialist palliative care advice before prescribing midazolam for this purpose.

- Any potential contributors to bleeding (e.g. blood-thinning medications) will be reviewed and stopped if appropriate.
- All professionals and services involved in the patient's care will be informed of the plan.

---

## During a Haemorrhage

If a haemorrhage occurs:

1. **Remain calm and stay with the patient.** Massive haemorrhage is frightening for the patient if they are conscious, and for their family. The patient should not be left alone.
2. **Call for assistance.**
3. **Use dark-coloured towels to cover and absorb the bleeding** — this reduces the visual impact.
4. **Focus on comfort and presence** — if the patient is clearly dying, non-pharmacological support (holding hands, reassurance, calm presence) is more important than medication. The patient will usually lose consciousness rapidly.
5. **Use pre-planned medications** (e.g. midazolam) as documented in the emergency letter, if available and if appropriate.

Your doctor or specialist palliative care services can be contacted for:
- Anticipatory planning or acute management options.
- Arranging a joint visit.

---

## After a Haemorrhage

Witnessing a massive haemorrhage can be traumatic for families and healthcare staff. **Debriefing** is an important part of aftercare.

Your doctor can arrange:
- Support for family members.
- Debriefing and support for staff involved.

Specialist palliative care advice and support is available after an event.

---

## When Specialist Palliative Care Advice is Sought

Your doctor will seek specialist palliative care advice for:
- Anticipatory planning if significant haemorrhage risk is identified.
- Acute management options.
- Consideration of prescribing midazolam.
- Arranging a joint visit.
- Debriefing support for family after a haemorrhage.

*Always discuss your care and any concerns with your doctor.*
