# Hydroxychloroquine Shared Care Guidance

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This guidance supports ongoing management of hydroxychloroquine in primary care, following initiation or recommendation by a specialist. It is used in conjunction with your prescribing doctor's advice.

---

## Red Flags — Stop the Drug and Seek Urgent Specialist Advice

Contact your doctor urgently or go to the emergency department if you develop:

- Any change in vision — difficulty seeing entire words or faces, loss of part of your visual field
- Flashes of light or other unexplained visual symptoms
- Retinal or macular changes identified on eye examination
- Muscle weakness (myopathy)
- Numbness or tingling in the hands or feet (peripheral neuropathy)
- Palpitations or an abnormal heart rhythm (possible QT prolongation)
- Unexplained bruising, bleeding, or signs of blood disorder

---

## Background

Hydroxychloroquine (brand name: Plaquenil) is originally an antimalarial drug that is now widely used in rheumatology. Your doctor may prescribe it for:

- **Rheumatoid arthritis (RA)**
- **Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)**
- **Discoid lupus**
- **Malaria prevention or treatment**

It is generally considered a well-tolerated medication. The most significant long-term risk is retinal toxicity (retinopathy), which is rare and almost always only occurs after more than 5 years of use.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will check for factors that increase the risk of retinopathy, including:

- Duration of treatment (risk increases after 5 years)
- High daily dose
- Age over 60
- Kidney or liver problems
- Concurrent use of tamoxifen

### Contraindications

Hydroxychloroquine is generally not used in people with:

- Pre-existing maculopathy (disease of the central retina)
- Known hypersensitivity to 4-aminoquinoline compounds
- Long-term use in children
- Children under 6 years of age

---

## Monitoring

Your doctor will arrange:

- **Baseline ECG** — to assess for QT interval prolongation (at the start of treatment)
- **Eye review (ophthalmology)** — after 5 years of use, then annually to 10 years, then every 6 months. More frequent review may be recommended if risk factors are present. No baseline ophthalmology is required.
- **Baseline blood tests** — full blood count, creatinine, and liver function tests before starting. Ongoing blood monitoring is not generally required.
- **Vision check** — near vision recorded with reading glasses; any uncorrected visual impairment should be reviewed by an optometrist or ophthalmologist.
- **Cervical screening** — usual screening intervals apply; hydroxychloroquine is not classified as an immunosuppressant.

### Eye safety tips

- Wear sunglasses in bright sunlight
- Report any new or worsening visual symptoms promptly

---

## Management

### Dosage

Your doctor will prescribe an appropriate dose. The typical adult dose is:

- 200 to 400 mg orally once daily, up to a maximum of 5 mg/kg per day
- Dose may be lower if you have kidney or liver problems

### Adverse Effects

| System | Possible effects | What your doctor may do |
|---|---|---|
| Eyes | Retinopathy, maculopathy, visual field changes, flashes of light | Stop drug; refer to ophthalmologist |
| Blood | Blood disorders, low blood sugar | Consult specialist |
| Heart | Cardiomyopathy, prolonged QT interval | Stop drug; consult specialist |
| Nervous system | Peripheral neuropathy, neuromyopathy | Stop drug; consult specialist |
| Skin | Rash, skin pigmentation changes | Reduce dose; may need to stop |

### Drug Interactions

Your doctor will review your other medications. Key interactions include:

- **MAO inhibitors** — avoid combination
- **Digoxin** — hydroxychloroquine may increase digoxin levels; close monitoring required
- **Antidiabetic agents** — hydroxychloroquine may lower blood glucose; monitor carefully
- **Amiodarone** — increased risk of QT prolongation
- **Mefloquine** — increased risk of QT prolongation
- **Tamoxifen** — may increase risk of retinopathy

If you start any new medication, remind your prescriber or pharmacist that you are on hydroxychloroquine.

### Vaccinations

Your doctor may recommend the following vaccinations while on hydroxychloroquine:

- **Annual influenza (flu) vaccine**
- **Pneumococcal vaccine** — every 5 years (recommended; funding depends on your diagnosis)
- **Shingrix (shingles) vaccine** — consider if eligible. Note: the older Zostavax vaccine (a live vaccine) should be avoided
- **Hepatitis B vaccine** — if not immune and in a higher-risk group

**Live vaccines should generally be avoided** while on hydroxychloroquine. Examples of live vaccines include MMR, oral rotavirus, oral polio, oral typhoid, BCG, varicella (chickenpox), and yellow fever. Discuss with your doctor before any vaccination.

### Surgery

No need to stop hydroxychloroquine before surgery. Your doctor will confirm this is still appropriate based on your specific situation.

### Pregnancy

Hydroxychloroquine is considered safe to continue during pregnancy. Do not stop without discussing with your specialist, as untreated lupus or RA may carry its own risks during pregnancy.
