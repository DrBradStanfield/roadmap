# Bone and Musculoskeletal Health in Spinal Cord Impairment (SCI)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of bone and musculoskeletal conditions in people with spinal cord impairment (SCI), including osteoporosis, heterotopic ossification, nerve entrapments, fractures, spasticity, and contractures. Always discuss management with your doctor.

---

## Clinical Note (Updated 2025)

**Denosumab:** Special authority access for denosumab for osteoporosis was widened by Pharmac in June 2025. See Osteoporosis New Zealand recommendations for current guidance.

**Raloxifene (Evista) discontinued:** Stock expected to run out December 2025. Your doctor will discuss alternative osteoporosis treatments.

---

## Background

People with SCI commonly develop:

- **Osteopenia or osteoporosis** below the level of injury — bone density falls rapidly in the first years, particularly in the legs. This increases the risk of low-impact fractures, renal tract stones, and occasionally elevated calcium in the blood.
- **Heterotopic ossification (HO)** — abnormal bone growth around large joints (especially hips and knees) below the injury. It may cause no symptoms or can cause joint inflammation, restricted movement, or muscle damage.
- **Musculoskeletal pain** from contractures, spasticity, and poor posture below the level of injury.
- **Overuse injuries above the level of injury** — carpal tunnel syndrome and rotator cuff injuries are particularly common and debilitating for wheelchair users.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. **Take a detailed history** to distinguish musculoskeletal pain from neuropathic pain:
   - Neuropathic pain (below the injury): often poorly localised, diffuse, burning, stabbing, pins-and-needles, or electric shock sensations with abnormal skin sensitivity
   - Musculoskeletal pain: more localised, dull, or achy — typically above the injury site
   - Ask about recent changes in wheelchair, cushions, hoist, shower chair, or bed — even small equipment changes can cause pain
   - Ask about changes in activity level or function
   - Consider other causes: appendicitis, ovarian cysts, gallstones, fractures

2. **Examine** for pressure areas, range of motion, spasticity, and clonus.

3. **Arrange investigations:**
   - Bone density scan (DXA) after the first low-impact fracture (funded) and again at 2 years post-injury
   - Plain X-ray for suspected fractures or established heterotopic ossification (note: X-ray may be normal in early HO)
   - Alkaline phosphatase blood test (elevated in HO, though not specific)
   - If X-ray is negative but HO is still suspected: bone isotope scan or MRI

## Management

Your doctor will coordinate care with specialists experienced in SCI. Management decisions often involve specialist assessment.

### Osteoporosis

Managed as per standard osteoporosis guidelines, with one important exception: **avoid calcium supplements for the first 2 years after SCI** due to risk of hypercalcaemia.

### Heterotopic Ossification (HO)

Your doctor may prescribe:

- **Etidronate** (20 mg/kg/day for 2 weeks, then 10 mg/kg/day for 10 weeks) — most effective when bone scan is positive but X-ray is still normal
- **Indometacin CR 75 mg once daily for 3 weeks** for HO prevention following acute SCI
- NSAIDs help with HO-related pain and reduce further calcification
- Passive range-of-motion physiotherapy to maintain joint mobility
- Specialist assessment if conservative treatment is insufficient — surgical removal or radiation therapy may be considered in select cases

### Carpal Tunnel Syndrome and Rotator Cuff Injury

Early identification and prompt management are important to preserve independence. Your doctor will consider early surgical referral to maintain upper limb function.

### Insufficiency Fractures

Your doctor will seek orthopaedic advice. Conservative management is guided by specialist assessment. Additional carer support may be needed during recovery.

### Spasticity and Contractures

Management is guided by the specialist plan and may include:

- Stretching programmes and physiotherapy
- Night splints or serial casting
- A combination of approaches

### Equipment, Positioning, and Mobility Aids

Occupational therapy assessment is arranged based on the patient's location and funding source (DHB or ACC).

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor will refer to a spinal cord impairment specialist when:

- Heterotopic ossification is difficult to treat
- Carpal tunnel syndrome, shoulder pain, rotator cuff, or other tendinopathies are not responding to initial management
- Guidance is needed on managing insufficiency fractures
- Spasticity or contractures require ongoing management

Your doctor will check whether you already have a follow-up appointment scheduled before making a referral.
