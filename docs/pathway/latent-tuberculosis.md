# Latent Tuberculosis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway is for asymptomatic patients. If a patient has symptoms suggestive of TB, active tuberculosis needs to be excluded first.

---

## What Is Latent TB?

Latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) is infection with *Mycobacterium tuberculosis* without active disease. The immune system contains the infection and it remains dormant.

Key facts:
- Lifetime risk of developing active TB once infected: approximately 10%
- Risk is highest in the first 2–3 years after infection, and rises when immunity is low
- LTBI is **asymptomatic** and **not infectious** — the person poses no risk to others
- LTBI is not notifiable; active TB disease is notifiable

LTBI is identified in someone with no symptoms and a normal chest X-ray, confirmed by either:
- A positive interferon gamma release assay (IGRA, e.g. QuantiFERON Gold), or
- A positive tuberculin skin test (TST / Mantoux)

---

## Who Gets Screened?

Screening for LTBI is not routine. Your doctor will assess whether you need testing based on your situation.

**Screening is most commonly considered for:**
- Healthcare students undertaking clinical placements
- Individuals undergoing refugee health screening (under 16 years)
- Contacts of a confirmed pulmonary TB case (usually coordinated by Public Health, not arranged in primary care)
- Immunocompromised patients (e.g. those living with HIV, starting TNF-alpha inhibitors, undergoing organ transplant, on high-dose steroids equivalent to prednisone 15 mg/day or more, with renal failure, or with certain cancers) — testing is usually arranged by the treating specialist

**Screening is generally not recommended for:**
- General employment outside healthcare
- Recent migrants from high-TB-incidence countries (not routinely screened; unfunded testing may be considered after shared decision-making)

**Screening should be deferred if:**
- Previous TB infection or previous positive IGRA — your doctor will seek specialist advice before any retesting
- Recent fever or viral illness — wait until fully recovered
- Live vaccine administered within the past 4 weeks — wait at least 4 weeks (unless testing on the same day as vaccination)

---

## How Is LTBI Tested?

Two tests can detect LTBI. Your doctor will choose based on your age and circumstances.

### IGRA (QuantiFERON-TB Gold) — preferred for age 2 and over

A blood test that measures the immune response to TB antigens.

- More specific than TST — not affected by prior BCG vaccination or exposure to non-tuberculous mycobacteria
- At least as sensitive as TST for recently acquired LTBI
- May be slightly less sensitive for remotely acquired LTBI (more than 5–10 years ago)
- Not recommended for children under 2 years
- Must be done on a weekday (not available on weekends or public holidays)
- Can be done on the same day as live vaccines; if not, allow at least 4–6 weeks between vaccination and testing

### TST / Mantoux — used for children under 2

An intradermal injection of TB antigens into the forearm; the skin reaction (induration) is read 48–72 hours later.

- Interpretation depends on individual risk factors — your doctor uses specific cut-off criteria
- False positives can occur (e.g. prior BCG vaccination) and false negatives are also possible
- Size of reaction does not predict likelihood of active disease

**Neither test can diagnose active TB** — they only identify latent infection.

---

## What Happens If the Test Is Positive?

A positive IGRA or TST does not mean you have active TB. Your doctor will:

1. **Review for TB symptoms** — if any of the following are present, active TB must be excluded urgently before further LTBI workup:
   - Persistent cough for more than 3 weeks (with or without sputum)
   - Malaise and fatigue
   - Anorexia and weight loss
   - Haemoptysis
   - Fever and night sweats
   - Persistent swollen lymph nodes (neck, armpit, groin)
   - Other site-specific symptoms (e.g. abdominal pain)

2. **Arrange a chest X-ray** (posterior-anterior and lateral views) to exclude active pulmonary TB

3. **Arrange baseline blood tests** if active TB is excluded:
   - Full blood count (FBC)
   - HbA1c
   - Liver function tests
   - Renal function
   - Hepatitis B and C serology
   - HIV

## Management

If active TB has been excluded and LTBI is suspected, your doctor will:

- Refer you to an infectious diseases specialist for assessment (non-urgent)
- Provide reassurance: as long as you are asymptomatic, you pose no infection risk to others, and can continue your usual activities without isolating

**Treatment is decided and prescribed by a specialist — not in primary care.**

Not everyone with LTBI needs treatment. Those at highest risk of progressing to active TB (e.g. immunosuppressed) benefit most.

Treatment options (prescribed by specialist only):
- **Rifampicin** — preferred treatment in New Zealand where suitable
- **Isoniazid** — alternative
- **Rifampicin + isoniazid** — combination option

If you are on LTBI treatment, your prescribing specialist manages all medications, including repeat prescriptions.

**Potential side effects of TB medications your doctor will monitor for:**
- Rash
- Gastrointestinal upset
- Drug-induced hepatitis
- Gout
- Visual disturbance
- DRESS syndrome (drug rash with eosinophilia and systemic symptoms) — rare but serious

**Important:** Rifampicin has significant drug interactions. Always tell your doctor you are on TB treatment before any new medication is prescribed.

---

## When to See Your Doctor

Always discuss with your doctor if:

- You have been told you may have been exposed to TB (contact of a confirmed case)
- You have a positive TB test result and are unsure what to do next
- You are immunosuppressed and have not been screened
- You are experiencing side effects from TB medication
- You develop new symptoms while on or after LTBI treatment (cough, fever, night sweats, weight loss)
