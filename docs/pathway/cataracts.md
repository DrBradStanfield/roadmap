# Cataracts

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of patients with a suspected or diagnosed cataract.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

Contact your doctor urgently or seek emergency assessment if you have:

- Sudden vision loss or a marked change in vision

---

## Background

A cataract is an opacity (clouding) in the lens of the eye.

- Cataract formation is a normal part of ageing and is most common in people over 60 years old.
- It typically affects both eyes, though often one eye more than the other.
- Whether surgery is needed depends on your symptoms and how much they affect daily life.

### Types of cataract

- **Nuclear cataract** — causes gradual blurring and reduced colour vision; often tolerated well in older patients.
- **Posterior subcapsular cataract** — causes difficulty reading and marked glare, even when the opacity is small.
- **Cortical cataract** — commonly causes difficulty with night driving.

You can have more than one type in a single eye.

### Common symptoms

- Gradual reduction in visual sharpness
- Painless blurred vision
- Double vision in one eye
- Sensitivity to glare (e.g., car headlights, sunlight)
- Reduced vision in low light or mist
- Reduced colour vision

### Risk factors

- Increasing age
- Exposure to UV light and ionising radiation
- Diabetes
- Long-term steroid use
- Eye injury
- Smoking and excess alcohol
- Poor diet (low in antioxidant vitamins A, C, and E)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Your current level of vision and how your symptoms affect daily life — for example:
  - Can you read newspapers, labels, or price tags?
  - Can you see TV clearly, traffic signs, stairs, or kerbs?
  - Are you troubled by glare from headlights, glossy pages, or bright sunlight?
  - Do you have hazy, foggy, or blurry vision?
- Your medical history, including any previous eye conditions, injuries, or surgery
- Medications you are taking
- Risk factors (see above)

Your doctor will examine your eyes, including:

- **Visual acuity** — measuring how clearly you see in each eye, with glasses and using a pinhole to check if vision improves
- **Red reflex** — using an ophthalmoscope to assess the density and location of any cataract
- **Fundus examination** — checking the optic disc and macula (the back of the eye), looking for other causes of reduced vision such as macular haemorrhage

---

## Management

### If you have a cataract

**Your doctor may consider:**

- Referring you for an optometry assessment to determine whether surgery is appropriate
- Referral to an ophthalmologist (eye specialist) for further assessment or for the cataract surgery waiting list

If surgery is not yet appropriate — or if you prefer not to have surgery — your doctor may advise:

- Ensuring your glasses prescription is up to date (eye test within the last 2 years)
- Regular optometry reviews, as your cataract may progress over time
- Returning if your vision deteriorates
- Cataract prevention measures (see below)

### Cataract prevention

- Wear UV-protective sunglasses outdoors
- Stop smoking
- Keep alcohol within recommended guidelines
- Maintain a balanced diet — poor nutrition is a risk factor, though vitamin supplements have not been shown to prevent or treat cataracts

### If vision improves with a pinhole but no cataract is found

Your doctor will likely recommend an optometry assessment to check whether your glasses prescription needs updating.

### If you have reduced vision and no cataract is found

Your doctor will assess for other causes and may refer you to an optometrist or specialist depending on your age and findings.
