# Chronic Pelvic Pain

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of chronic pelvic pain — defined as intermittent or constant abdominal and/or pelvic pain lasting at least 6 months, not exclusively related to menstruation or intercourse, and not associated with pregnancy.

---

## Red Flags — See Your Doctor Promptly

The following features need prompt medical assessment to rule out serious causes:

- Onset of pain after menopause
- Pelvic mass
- Unexplained weight loss
- Irregular vaginal bleeding after age 40
- Postcoital bleeding (bleeding after sex)

---

## Background

Chronic pelvic pain is a common condition that can be difficult to diagnose because it is often multifactorial — meaning several different physical and psychological factors contribute at the same time.

Key points:
- Pain may be cyclical (linked to the menstrual cycle) or non-cyclical.
- The pain has often been present since adolescence and may have started as painful periods (dysmenorrhoea).
- Endometriosis is a common cause, but in many people no specific cause is found.
- Long-term complications can include opioid dependence, infertility, and adverse mental health outcomes.
- It is important to treat the chronic pain itself, not just search for a single underlying cause.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a thorough history including gynaecological, obstetric, and sexual history. They may ask you to record your symptoms over 2–3 months in a menstrual diary.

Things your doctor will ask about:
- Age of onset and how symptoms have progressed
- Pattern of pain — whether it is linked to your period, intercourse, bowel movements, or urination
- Any triggers, and whether movement or posture affects the pain
- Abnormal vaginal bleeding or discharge
- Weight loss, changes in bowel habit, or rectal bleeding
- Family history of gynaecological or gastrointestinal cancers
- All medications and treatments you have tried

Your doctor will also ask whether you have ever had pelvic mesh inserted during prolapse or incontinence surgery, as mesh complications can cause pelvic pain.

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will examine your abdomen and pelvis, and may also check the mobility of your spine and hips to rule out referred pain.

### Investigations

Your doctor may consider:
- Blood tests: full blood count (FBC), CRP, coeliac screen
- Urine tests: urinalysis, midstream urine (MSU), urine cytology if urological symptoms are present
- STI swabs and pregnancy test
- Bladder diary if you have urinary urgency or bladder-related symptoms
- Pelvic ultrasound (transvaginal if sexually active, transabdominal if not) — criteria include:
  - Chronic pelvic pain lasting at least 6 weeks, unrelated to the cycle, with PID excluded
  - Pelvic mass
  - Suspected ovarian cyst with persistent unilateral pain or tenderness

## Common Contributing Causes

Chronic pelvic pain is often multifactorial. Your doctor will consider causes across several categories:

### Gynaecological
- Endometriosis
- Adenomyosis
- Chronic pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)
- Vulvodynia
- Pelvic congestion syndrome

### Adhesions
- From endometriosis, previous surgery, or pelvic infection

### Urological
- Interstitial cystitis / bladder pain syndrome — a chronic bladder condition with pelvic pain, urinary frequency, urgency, and pressure, in the absence of proven infection
- Recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs)

### Gastrointestinal
- Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)
- Diverticular disease
- Coeliac disease
- Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)
- Constipation

### Musculoskeletal
- Pelvic floor myalgia (vaginismus)
- Coccydynia
- Chronic abdominal wall pain
- Referred pain from the back or after trauma

### Neurological
- Neuropathic pain (which may be associated with previous surgery)
- Pudendal neuralgia

### Psychological
- Depression
- Anxiety
- Sexual dysfunction
- Somatisation
- Opioid dependency

### Coexisting Pain Conditions
- Fibromyalgia
- Migraine
- Myalgic Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (ME/CFS)

## Management

Your doctor will take a holistic, multidisciplinary approach. The goal of management is to improve your ability to function and live a normal life — it may not be possible to completely eliminate the pain.

### Reassurance and Education

Your doctor will:
- Reassure you that chronic pelvic pain is a common problem. Being listened to and having your experience believed is itself therapeutic.
- Help you understand that normal test results do not mean the pain is not real — they mean there is no acute or dangerous cause, and that management should focus on living well with pain.
- Help you develop realistic expectations about what treatment can achieve.

### Non-Pharmacological Treatments

Your doctor may recommend one or more of the following, often started before or alongside investigations:

**Exercise:** Staying active helps manage pain. The specific type of exercise matters less than choosing something you enjoy — yoga and tai chi can also incorporate relaxation.

**Psychological therapies:** There is strong evidence that cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) can help with chronic pain. Your doctor may also suggest:
- Mindfulness
- Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT)
- Relaxation techniques
- Behavioural treatments

**Self-management:** Your doctor may encourage you to develop a self-management plan and a flare-up plan. Helpful strategies include:
- Meditation and breathing techniques
- Distraction
- Exercise
- Good sleep habits

**Acupuncture** may also be considered.

### Medications

Your doctor may consider:
- Simple analgesics such as paracetamol or NSAIDs — if these have not helped previously, ongoing use is discouraged.
- **Avoid opioid analgesics** for chronic non-malignant pelvic pain — opioids carry established risks of dependence, and opioid-induced hyperalgesia can paradoxically worsen pain.
- Neuromodulators such as **tricyclic antidepressants** (e.g., amitriptyline) or **gabapentin** — these are used as part of a multidisciplinary approach and may be prescribed off-label.
- For **interstitial cystitis**: avoiding dietary irritants (caffeine, alcohol, artificial sweeteners, hot pepper); a trial of NSAIDs or **oxybutynin** if not contraindicated.
- For **cyclical pain or suspected endometriosis**: ovarian suppression may be considered as a diagnostic and therapeutic tool.

Medications should be trialled one at a time, with a pain diary kept before and during use to assess efficacy.

### Managing Specific Identified Causes

If your doctor identifies a specific contributing cause, they will manage it appropriately:
- **Endometriosis / adenomyosis:** Hormonal and/or surgical management
- **Pelvic inflammatory disease:** Antibiotic treatment
- **Vulvodynia:** Specialist management
- **Pelvic congestion syndrome:** Specialist assessment
- **Urological causes:** Bladder-specific management
- **Gastrointestinal causes:** Treat the underlying condition (e.g., IBS management, gluten-free diet for coeliac disease)
- **Musculoskeletal / pelvic floor:** Physiotherapy
- **Psychological contributors:** Psychological therapy and mental health support

### Pain Flares

During a pain flare, your doctor will:
- Consider whether there are new symptoms or a new diagnosis
- Review psychosocial factors, activity, and exercise
- Review medications and their likely effectiveness
- Consider adjusting hormonal medications, such as increasing the dose of progestogen or considering early replacement of a levonorgestrel intrauterine device, or adding norethisterone 5 mg daily
