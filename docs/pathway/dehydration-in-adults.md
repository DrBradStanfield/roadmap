# Dehydration in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers dehydration in adults, including fluid loss secondary to diarrhoea and vomiting, and hyperemesis in pregnancy less than 20 weeks.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

See a doctor urgently or go to the emergency department if:

- Abdominal pain with no clear diagnosis
- Suspected intracranial causes (e.g. severe headache, confusion, neurological symptoms)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess the underlying cause at the same time as assessing dehydration. Relevant pathways include infectious gastroenteritis, migraine, nausea and vomiting in pregnancy, and pyelonephritis.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Symptoms of dehydration (timeline and severity): vomiting, diarrhoea, abdominal pain, fever, thirst, weakness
- Quantity of losses: volume and nature of vomit and stool (e.g. any bile, mucus, or blood), urine output
- Oral intake
- Other symptoms suggesting an underlying cause
- Risk factors that may mean hospital admission is more likely:
  - Older age
  - Poorly-controlled diabetes (e.g. finger prick blood sugar above 20 mmol/L — suggestive of DKA or hyperosmolar hyperglycaemic state)
  - End stage renal failure
  - History of heart failure
  - Significant other medical conditions
  - Prolonged duration of symptoms
  - Features of evolving illness
  - Recent overseas travel
  - Fever above 38°C
  - Abdominal pain that is not typical cramp-like pain
  - Immune suppression
  - On oral anticoagulants
  - History of inflammatory bowel disease (ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease)
  - On chemotherapy

### Examination

Your doctor will check:

- Pulse rate and volume
- Warmth of peripheries and capillary refill time
- Blood pressure lying and standing — postural hypotension is a drop of more than 20 mmHg systolic or 10 mmHg diastolic
- Weight
- Abdominal examination
- Signs of dehydration: dry mouth, sunken eyes, reduced skin turgor
- Additional examination depending on the clinical situation

### Investigations

- Urine dip for infection and ketones, finger prick glucose, and pregnancy test are often all that is required
- Your doctor may also consider:
  - FBC, creatinine and electrolytes (particularly in older patients or moderate/severe vomiting in pregnancy)
  - CRP, liver function tests
  - Other tests depending on the suspected underlying cause

### Severity Classification

| Severity | Features |
|---|---|
| **Mild** | May have no symptoms; mild thirst; concentrated urine; ketones 0 to + |
| **Moderate** | Weakness; light-headedness; postural hypotension; tachycardia; sunken eyes; dry mucous membranes; oliguria; ketones ++ |
| **Severe** | Significant weakness or apathy; shock; systolic BP below 90; tachycardia; low pulse volume; cool extremities; poor capillary refill; reduced skin turgor; confusion; oliguria; ketones +++ |

## Management

Management depends on severity. Your doctor will also treat any underlying cause simultaneously.

### Severe Dehydration or Life-Threatening Cause

If there are potentially life-threatening features (sepsis, unexplained abdominal pain, intracranial cause) or severe dehydration, your doctor will:

- Obtain IV access and resuscitate as necessary
- Arrange urgent hospital or specialist assessment

### Moderate Dehydration

Your doctor will decide on the appropriate setting for treatment based on your risk factors and clinical picture.

**If managed in the community:**

1. Antiemetics may be prescribed:
   - In pregnancy: follow the nausea and vomiting in pregnancy pathway
   - If not pregnant: options include Metoclopramide, Prochlorperazine, or Ondansetron

2. Trial of oral rehydration:
   - Fluid input and output recorded; observed in clinic for up to 60 minutes
   - If sufficient improvement: go home and drink 3–4 L of fluid over 24 hours
   - Signs of sufficient improvement: improved symptoms and clinical signs, adequate urine output, reduction in fluid losses, able to continue oral rehydration at home

3. If oral rehydration is insufficient, IV fluids are given:
   - 18–20 g IV cannula; normal saline 1000 mL immediately
   - Hydration status reviewed; up to a further 1000 mL normal saline if needed (maximum 2000 mL at one presentation)

4. If still insufficient improvement after 2000 mL IV saline, hospital assessment is arranged

**Follow-up for moderate dehydration:**
- Daily monitoring of fluid intake and losses until improving; treatment repeated as required
- Watch for: fluid overload, inadequate response, persisting losses, ketosis, deterioration, evolving illness
- Encourage 3–4 L oral fluids per day
- Patient given emergency contact details and instructed to seek help if any deterioration
- Renal function tests considered

### Mild Dehydration

- Antiemetics may be prescribed as above
- Advised to drink 3–4 L of fluid over 24 hours
- Advised to seek medical help if not improving

---

## When to Go to Hospital

Your doctor will arrange acute specialist assessment if:

- Life-threatening indications are present (sepsis, unexplained abdominal pain, intracranial cause)
- Severe dehydration
- Moderate dehydration that cannot be managed in the community
- Insufficient improvement after 2000 mL IV fluids

**Always discuss dehydration with your doctor** — they will assess the severity, identify the underlying cause, and determine whether you can be safely managed at home or need hospital-level care.
