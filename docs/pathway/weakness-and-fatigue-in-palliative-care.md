# Weakness and Fatigue in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Weakness and fatigue are among the most common and distressing symptoms in palliative care. They are often multifactorial. Your doctor will assess contributing causes and work with you and your whānau to develop an individualised support plan.

---

## Assessment

### Symptoms your doctor will ask about

- Low energy
- Poor concentration and motivation
- Reduced attention to environment or social stimuli
- Reduced mobility or muscle weakness
- Reduced independence
- Reduced self-esteem or altered body image
- Depressed or anxious mood
- Insomnia, hypersomnia, or unrefreshing sleep
- Short-term memory disturbance

### Reversible causes your doctor will look for

- **Medications:** opioids, benzodiazepines, tricyclic antidepressants
- **Metabolic:** low potassium, high sodium, low magnesium, hyperglycaemia, raised calcium, renal or liver failure, dehydration
- **Anaemia**
- **Infection**
- **Sleep disturbances**
- **Emotional causes:** anxiety, depression, fear, isolation, apathy, stress
- **Uncontrolled symptoms:** pain, nausea and vomiting, constipation
- **Corticosteroid-induced myopathy**
- **Recent surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy**
- **Cachexia or anorexia**

---

## Management

### Acknowledging the impact

Your doctor will acknowledge the reality and impact of these symptoms, which are often profoundly distressing. Explaining possible causes to both patient and carer is an important first step. A fatigue diary can help identify patterns or timing.

### Treating reversible causes

Where reversible causes are identified, your doctor will address these directly.

### Individual support plan

Your doctor will develop a plan tailored to your situation. This may include:

**Activity and energy conservation:**
- Rest in the daytime and aim for adequate sleep at night
- Practise good sleep hygiene
- Set realistic goals and remain as independent as possible
- Prioritise activities and delegate less important tasks
- Schedule important activities for when energy is best

**Planned exercise:**
Aerobic and resistance training of light to moderate intensity can improve fatigue and quality of life in palliative care. Your doctor may refer you to physiotherapy or occupational therapy.

**Emotional support:**
- Treating anxiety or distress
- Giving you opportunities to share feelings
- Addressing spiritual concerns if appropriate
- Supporting family or carers

**Nutritional support:**
Aggressive nutritional support is not appropriate in advanced illness. For appetite, your doctor may consider:
- Dexamethasone 2–4 mg once daily (which may also reduce fatigue) — trial for 1 week, stop if no benefit; use with caution in diabetes

**Physical and household support:**
Palliative care nursing can assess needs for services, equipment, and other supports.

### Corticosteroids for fatigue

Your doctor may consider a corticosteroid trial:

- Can improve appetite and energy, though there is no evidence of improved nutritional status
- May be effective in cachexia via anti-inflammatory effects
- Effect is often short-lived — days to weeks
- Preferred in patients with shorter life expectancy (weeks to a couple of months)
- **Dose:** Dexamethasone 2–4 mg per day
  - Use the lowest effective dose
  - Trial for 1 week; stop if no significant benefit
  - Maximum 2 weeks before specialist review (with prolonged use, appetite benefit may diminish and muscle catabolism risk increases)
- **Side effects include:** oral thrush, proximal myopathy, fluid retention, skin thinning or bruising, gastric upset

In palliative care, doses, indications, and routes of administration may be outside standard product approval.

---

## When to Seek Specialist Input

Your doctor will ensure close collaboration between all palliative care providers. Specialist palliative care support is sought for:

- Guidance on use of dexamethasone for fatigue
- Anticipatory planning or acute management
- Arranging a joint visit
- Considering admission to hospice
- Nutritional support decisions
- Physiotherapy or occupational therapy referral

Children's palliative care involves a separate specialist service.

---

*Always discuss your symptoms and any changes to medications with your doctor. Palliative care decisions are made together with the patient, family/whānau, and the care team.*
