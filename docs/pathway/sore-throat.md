# Sore Throat

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers sore throat including tonsillitis, quinsy (peritonsillar abscess), and pharyngitis.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

See a doctor urgently or call an ambulance if:

- Signs of peritonsillar abscess (quinsy) — severe one-sided throat pain with swelling
- Swelling causing acute difficulty breathing (upper airway obstruction)
- Severe dehydration due to difficulty swallowing

---

## Background

- Viral and bacterial causes of sore throats cannot be reliably told apart by symptoms, severity, or duration alone.
- Most sore throats are viral. Only a small number are caused by group A streptococcus (GAS).
- In people at higher risk, an untreated GAS sore throat can lead to acute rheumatic fever (ARF) — a serious complication affecting the heart and joints. ARF can appear 14–21 days after infection.
- GAS tonsillitis is uncommon under age 3 but very common in children aged 5–10 and young adults aged 15–25.
- A peritonsillar abscess (quinsy) is a possible complication of tonsillitis — a collection of pus beside the tonsil causing severe one-sided throat pain.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. Check vital signs, hydration status, and examine the throat, looking for signs of quinsy (redness and swelling on one side) and swollen or sore joints.
2. Assess your risk of rheumatic fever:
   - **High risk** — a personal, family, or household history of rheumatic fever/rheumatic heart disease; or meeting 2 or more of: Māori or Pacific peoples; aged 3–35 years (especially 4–19 years); living in crowded accommodation or deprivation.
   - **Occupational/school risk** — healthcare worker, residential care worker, food handler, teacher, childcare worker, or student.
   - **Low risk** — meets only 1 of the high-risk criteria above.
3. Take a throat swab if you are at high risk or occupational/school risk. In low-risk patients, a swab is usually only taken if you are very unwell — this helps avoid unnecessary antibiotic use.
4. In adolescents and young adults, also consider infectious mononucleosis (glandular fever) as a possible cause.

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following:

### Urgent referral

Your doctor will arrange urgent specialist (ENT/otolaryngology) assessment if there are signs of:
- Suspected quinsy or peritonsillar infection
- Acute upper airway obstruction due to swelling
- Severe dehydration from difficulty swallowing

### Antibiotics

Antibiotics are **not** needed for most sore throats — the majority are viral and get better on their own.

Your doctor will start antibiotics if you are at high risk of rheumatic fever or have an occupational/school risk of spreading GAS. Antibiotics may also be considered if you are at risk of other complications.

**If antibiotics are prescribed**, your doctor will choose from (in order of preference):

- **Penicillin V** (oral, 10 days):
  - Children under 20 kg: 250 mg two or three times daily
  - Children and adults 20 kg and over: 500 mg two or three times daily
- **Amoxicillin** (oral, 10 days):
  - Under 30 kg: 750 mg once daily
  - 30 kg and over: 1000 mg once daily
- **Benzathine penicillin** (long-acting intramuscular injection, single dose):
  - Children under 30 kg: 450 mg (600,000 U)
  - Children and adults 30 kg and over: 900 mg (1,200,000 U)

If there is a concern about penicillin allergy (IgE-mediated or anaphylactic):
- **Erythromycin ethylsuccinate** (oral, 10 days) — first choice
- **Roxithromycin** (oral, 10 days) — second choice

### Throat swab results

- **Positive GAS:** All positive cases are treated with 10 days of antibiotics to clear GAS and prevent rheumatic fever. Your doctor may also advise isolating at home for 24 hours after starting antibiotics and swabbing symptomatic household members.
- **Negative GAS:** Antibiotics are stopped if they were started before results were available.

### Recurrent GAS sore throats

If you have had 3 or more GAS-positive sore throats in the past 3 months, your doctor will follow specific guidelines — typically a single dose of intramuscular benzathine penicillin or directly observed oral amoxicillin, plus swabbing and treating symptomatic household members. Good hygiene practices and keeping the home warm and dry are also important.

### When to return

Return for review if you are getting worse after 24 hours. If you are at high risk of rheumatic fever, re-present sooner if you develop any of:
- Fever
- Painful, warm, or swollen joints
- Rash, skin sores, or lumps under the skin
- Breathlessness
- Jerky or uncoordinated movements
- Abdominal pain
- Unexplained fatigue

### Tonsillectomy

If you are getting recurrent tonsillitis, your doctor may refer you for a specialist ENT assessment to consider tonsillectomy. Criteria typically include:
- 6 or more episodes of acute tonsillitis in the past 12 months, or
- 4–5 episodes per year for 2 years, or
- 3 episodes per year for 3 years
- Obstructive sleep apnoea or chronic choking
