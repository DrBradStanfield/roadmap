# Behavioural Concerns in School Age Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of behavioural concerns in school age children, from mild difficulties through to severe presentations requiring specialist input.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Help

**Call emergency services (111) or go to the emergency department immediately if:**

- Your child is at immediate risk of significant self-harm
- Your child is at immediate risk of significant harm to others

**Contact your doctor urgently if:**

- The behaviour has escalated rapidly in severity
- You are concerned about your child's or family's safety

---

## Background

Significantly challenging behaviours occur in around 20% of children. These behaviours typically:

- Fall outside the range of normal developmental behaviour
- Cause considerable stress to the child and/or their family
- Have a negative impact on social or educational experience
- Persist over time

Mild to moderate behaviour problems may be developmentally appropriate — occurring as a child begins to negotiate a new area of independence — but can still cause significant family stress. Severe antisocial behaviour in childhood is a strong predictor of long-term negative outcomes in adulthood.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history, gathering information from several sources — including parents, the child (if maturity permits), teachers, and other caregivers.

**About the behaviour itself:**

- Nature of the behaviour and how often it occurs
- How long it has been a concern
- Impact on others
- What triggers the behaviour, and in which settings (behaviour occurring in 2 or more settings is of greater concern)
- How adults respond to the behaviour — whether this reinforces it
- What has been tried and whether it has helped
- Patterns of escalation

**Context and contributing factors your doctor will explore:**

- Recent life changes or stressors (trauma, loss, bullying, changes at home or school)
- Sleep quality and quantity
- Screen time and online activity (including possible exposure to harmful content or cyberbullying)
- Changes in family setting: living arrangements, household stability, history of family violence or abuse
- Family history: genetic conditions, ASD, parental mental illness, ADHD, or substance use
- Developmental and personal history: pregnancy, birth, milestones, trauma, consistency of caregiving
- Medical history: any chronic illness, neurodevelopmental disorders, learning difficulties, sleep apnoea
- Alcohol or drug exposure

**Physical examination may include:**

- Checking whether behaviours are appropriate for the child's developmental stage
- Observation of parent–child interaction
- Looking for dysmorphic features (including subtle features of fragile X, Turner syndrome, Klinefelter syndrome, foetal alcohol syndrome)
- Checking hearing and vision
- Examining the throat for tonsillar hypertrophy (linked to sleep apnoea)
- Skin examination for neurocutaneous signs (e.g., tuberous sclerosis, neurofibromatosis) and signs of trauma
- Neurological assessment
- Blood pressure and heart rate (to check for thyroid or other medical conditions)

Laboratory investigations are rarely needed but may be considered if a medical cause is suspected.

## Possible Underlying Causes

Your doctor will consider behaviour as a symptom, not a diagnosis in itself. Possible underlying causes include:

### ADHD (Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder)

Children with ADHD may be described as inattentive, hyperactive, impulsive, unable to concentrate, forgetful, fidgety, or constantly interrupting.

### Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)

Consider ASD if there are difficulties in:
- Social interaction (limited interest in others, overwhelmed socially, not wanting physical touch)
- Communication (unusual use of language, loss of previously acquired language skills)
- Restricted interests and repetitive behaviours (inflexibility, repetitive play or mannerisms, difficulty coping with change)
- Over- or under-sensitivity to sounds, movement, touch, or visual stimuli

### Anxiety

Anxiety may be the driver of difficult behaviour — particularly in children with a frightening or inadequate home environment. For all children with disruptive behaviour, your doctor will consider PTSD, domestic aggression, or exposure to threatening situations.

### Depression

Less common in children than adolescents. Features may include persistent sad or irritable mood, loss of interest in activities, changes in eating, sleeping, and activity, difficulty concentrating, and recurrent thoughts of death or suicide. Children may instead present with somatic complaints (abdominal pain, headaches) or withdrawn behaviour.

### Oppositional Defiant Disorder (ODD)

A pattern of angry, argumentative, or defiant behaviour toward authority figures that causes problems at home or school.

### Conduct Disorder (CD)

A more serious pattern involving aggression, rule-breaking, or deceitful behaviour.

### Other causes to consider

- Developmental concerns or intellectual disability
- Learning difficulties or hearing/vision problems
- Sleep apnoea (tonsillar hypertrophy)
- Substance use (including marijuana — some children begin very early)
- Child abuse or neglect, family violence, parental mental health or substance use issues
- Psychosis (rare)

## Management

### Immediate risk

- If there is immediate risk of serious self-harm or harm to others, emergency services should be contacted immediately.
- If there is risk of harm that is not immediate, your doctor may consider involving child welfare services.

### For mild to moderate behavioural concerns

Your doctor will provide advice on general behaviour strategies, which are usually adequate when there is no significant underlying cause:

**Behaviour strategies:**
- Be firm, fair, and friendly
- Distract an escalating child
- Use time out to remove the child from an escalating situation
- Reinforce desirable behaviours; avoid inadvertently reinforcing unwanted behaviour by giving it attention
- Use planned ignoring for inappropriate behaviour (unless safety is a concern)
- Remove privileges for difficult behaviours consistently
- Encourage caregivers to spend quality one-on-one time with their child in fun activities

**Lifestyle recommendations:**
- Healthy eating and regular physical activity
- Adequate sleep
- Limit screen time (recommended: up to 1 hour/day for children under 8, up to 2 hours/day for children over 8)
- Know what media your child is accessing and how

### If general strategies are not enough

Your doctor may consider referral to group parenting programmes. The **Incredible Years** programme (for parents of children aged 3 to 8 years) has the strongest evidence base for this age group — a 14-week, 2.5-hours-per-week parenting programme shown to be effective across cultural and socioeconomic groups.

### If severe behavioural challenges or suspected underlying cause

Your doctor may refer for specialist assessment:
- If possible ADHD: paediatric or specialist ADHD assessment
- If developmental or medical cause: non-acute paediatric assessment
- If psychiatric cause (e.g., ODD, CD, or severe behaviour without a clear medical or developmental cause): child and youth mental health assessment
- If confirmed intellectual disability or ASD with significant behavioural difficulties: needs assessment and service coordination

### Additional support

- Home-based parenting support
- Respite care (crisis care, school holiday care, after-school care)
- School support services: Resource Teachers Learning and Behaviour (RTLB), special education advisors or psychologists, Intensive Wraparound Service for children with very complex needs
- If the child has a parent with mental illness or addiction, community-based recovery support services may be involved
