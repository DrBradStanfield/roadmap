# Penis and Foreskin Problems in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers pre-adolescent boys. It addresses normal variation, phimosis, balanitis, and hypospadias.

---

## What Is Normal

Many parents present concerned about findings that are entirely normal:

- **Non-retractile foreskin:** The foreskin is normally not retractable before age 1 year. By age 4, most boys can retract their foreskin. Inability to retract before this age is not a problem.
- **Smegma:** A yellowish secretion that appears under the foreskin before it is fully retractable. It helps separate the foreskin from the glans (head of the penis) naturally. It looks like a cyst under the skin and does not need treatment — it will clear on its own.
- **Temporary redness and swelling:** Some boys experience brief pain and swelling during natural foreskin separation. This is normal and settles within a day or two.

Your doctor can confirm whether what you are seeing is a normal stage of development.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess:

- Whether the foreskin is retractable, and to what degree
- Whether smegma or normal adhesions explain the appearance
- Signs of phimosis (non-retractile, tight foreskin beyond the expected age)
- Signs of balanitis (redness, swelling, or discharge indicating inflammation or infection)
- Whether hypospadias is present (urethral opening not at the tip of the penis)

---

## Conditions and Management

### Phimosis (Tight Foreskin)

Phimosis means the foreskin cannot be retracted because it is tight. It may be mild or severe.

**Mild phimosis:**

Your doctor may consider:

- Applying a thin layer of **betamethasone valerate 0.1% ointment** to the tight, shiny part of the foreskin 3–4 times daily for one month
- Parents are shown exactly how and where to apply the ointment — only to the tight portion on gentle retraction
- Follow-up after one month to assess response
- If phimosis recurs after treatment, referral to paediatric surgery for assessment

After a successful course of steroid ointment, the foreskin should retract comfortably and most children have no further problems.

**Severe phimosis or scarring (including Balanitis Xerotica Obliterans / BXO):**

BXO causes the foreskin to become scarred, thickened, inelastic, and fissured. Steroid ointment is unlikely to help in this case.

Your doctor will refer for paediatric surgical assessment. Circumcision is the usual treatment.

---

### Balanitis (Inflammation of the Foreskin/Glans)

Balanitis is inflammation of the foreskin, with or without infection. It often occurs as smegma is released during natural foreskin separation and usually settles on its own within a few days without specific treatment.

Your doctor may consider antibiotics (**amoxicillin + clavulanic acid** or **flucloxacillin**) if:

- The balanitis is not settling spontaneously
- Redness extends down the shaft of the penis (uncommon)
- The child shows signs of systemic illness (very rare)

---

### Hypospadias

Hypospadias is a congenital condition where the urethral opening (where urine exits) is not at the tip of the penis but on the underside of the glans or shaft. It may be associated with a downward bend of the penis (chordee) and an incomplete foreskin (dorsal hood).

If hypospadias is identified:

- The child should **not** be circumcised — the foreskin tissue is used in surgical repair
- Your doctor will refer for paediatric surgical assessment

---

### Complications After Circumcision

If a child has previously been circumcised, your doctor will assess for:

- Ongoing bleeding
- Infection
- Poor urinary stream (may indicate meatal ulcer or meatal stenosis)
- Unsatisfactory appearance or poor healing beyond 6 weeks

Referral to paediatric surgery is appropriate if any of these are present.
