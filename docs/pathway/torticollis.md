# Torticollis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers non-acute torticollis in children.

---

## Background

- The most common cause of torticollis is congenital, due to tightness and shortening of one sternocleidomastoid muscle (SCM). This occurs in about 0.4% of births.
- At around 3 weeks of age, a visible or palpable swelling (called a sternomastoid tumour) may develop in part or all of the muscle. This swelling often persists for up to 1 year.
- The child typically presents with a preference to hold the head turned to one side.
- Most children present in their first year of life.
- Occasionally, older children present with a fibrotic, shortened SCM, thought to be the legacy of a previously unrecognised sternomastoid tumour.
- Over 90% of cases resolve spontaneously, irrespective of treatment.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will make a clinical diagnosis based on:

1. **History** — there is often a history of breech birth or difficult delivery.
2. **Examination:**
   - A tight and shortened SCM, or a hard spindle-shaped firm painless lump within it (the "sternomastoid tumour"), with restricted range of neck motion
   - Head tilted towards the shortened SCM, chin rotated to the opposite side
   - The affected SCM may feel tight at rest
   - If the SCM is **not** thickened or shortened, your doctor will consider an alternative diagnosis
3. **Secondary effects** — your doctor will also check for:
   - Plagiocephaly (flat or asymmetric head shape)
   - Hemifacial hypoplasia (facial asymmetry)
   - Scoliosis (postural compensation in older children)
4. **Investigations** — plain X-ray is not required if the SCM is clearly tight or shortened.

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following:

1. **Reassurance** — in 90% of infants, sternomastoid fibrosis resolves spontaneously regardless of treatment.
2. **Conservative management:**
   - **Physiotherapy** — a physiotherapist works with parents to demonstrate specific stretching and positioning techniques, and to help reduce the severity of any plagiocephaly.
   - **Home positioning strategies** to encourage the baby to turn their head in both directions:
     - Hold the baby so they need to rotate their chin to the non-preferred side during feeding
     - Position the cot so the baby must turn their head to the non-preferred side to view the room (babies prefer to look outward, which naturally stretches the tight neck muscles)
     - Place toys on the non-preferred side when the baby is lying on their back, front, or sitting — to view the toy, the baby must tilt and rotate their head, stretching the tight muscle
     - During play, use toys and sounds to encourage turning in both directions
3. **Surgery** — your doctor may consider surgical referral if:
   - SCM tightness persists and limits head rotation in a child older than 12–15 months
   - Persistent SCM tightness with progressive facial asymmetry
   - Diagnosis is made in a child older than 12 months
