# Permanent Contraception

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management for patients considering permanent contraception — vasectomy or tubal ligation.

---

## Background

Permanent contraceptive methods include tubal ligation and vasectomy.

**Long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs)** — intrauterine contraception and the contraceptive implant — are recommended as a first-line contraceptive choice for all eligible patients due to their excellent efficacy and safety. Your doctor will discuss these as an alternative before proceeding with permanent options.

### Vasectomy

- Generally performed under local anaesthetic
- Can be performed in primary or secondary care
- Failure rate: 1 in 300, dropping to 1 in 2000 if post-procedure semen analysis is clear
- Does not affect libido, testicular function, or the visible appearance of ejaculate
- No increased risk of testicular cancer; no conclusive evidence of increased prostate cancer risk
- Reversal is technically possible but not guaranteed to restore fertility

### Tubal Ligation

- Requires a general anaesthetic
- Performed laparoscopically or via open surgery (e.g. at the time of Caesarean section)
- Methods include: transection of the Fallopian tubes (electrocautery or cutting and tying), Filshie clip tubal occlusion, or salpingectomy
- Failure rate: approximately 1 in 200 with all methods
- If sterilisation fails, 40% of resulting pregnancies are ectopic

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a thorough history before recommending permanent contraception. This will cover:

- What has led you to consider permanent contraception
- Medications and allergies
- Previous surgery and any complications
- Social factors: relationship status, number of children
- Current contraceptive method
- Date of last cervical screening (if applicable)
- Concurrent medical conditions that may influence suitability (e.g. diabetes, heart disease, hypertension, lung disease, clotting disorders, psychiatric conditions, high BMI)
- Mental capacity to consent to a procedure that permanently removes fertility — if there is any doubt, legal advice will be sought

Physical examination includes checking weight and BMI, and offering scrotal or bimanual pelvic examination.

### Factors That Predict Regret

Your doctor will specifically check for factors associated with later regret. These include:

- Age younger than 30 years when permanent contraception is initiated
- Nulliparous or low parity (no children, or few children)
- Being unhappy in a relationship, or not currently in a relationship
- Remarriage or change of partner
- Death of a child
- Desire to have more children, including with a new partner
- Either partner giving a reason other than not wanting further children
- Psychological problems including psychosexual problems
- Coercion by a health professional, partner, or spouse
- Timing close to a pregnancy (e.g. after abortion, or in the postpartum period)
- Lack of information about the procedure and alternative contraceptive options

**Regret data:**
- Women aged 30 or younger have a cumulative regret rate of 20% after 14 years following tubal ligation
- Women older than 30 have a cumulative regret rate of 6% after 14 years
- Up to 20% of men desire more children after vasectomy; 2% proceed to reversal

### Comparing the Options

Your doctor will discuss both vasectomy and tubal ligation with you, even if you have a preference, covering:

| | Vasectomy | Tubal Ligation |
|---|---|---|
| Anaesthetic | Local | General |
| Failure rate | ~1 in 300 (1 in 2000 if tests clear) | ~1 in 200 |
| Surgical risk | Low | 1–3% complication rate |
| Onset of effect | After azoospermia confirmed (~12 weeks) | 7 days after procedure |
| Reversibility | Technically possible; ~60% pregnancy rate | Complex; ~50% pregnancy rate |

Long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) offer comparable efficacy to both methods without surgical risk or risk of regret.

## Management

### Vasectomy

Your doctor may consider the following management steps:

1. Provide written and verbal information about the procedure, including that vasectomy is safer and quicker than female sterilisation with less morbidity
2. Advise continuing reliable contraception until the procedure, and afterwards until azoospermia is confirmed on semen analysis
3. Discuss semen analysis timing — the first post-vasectomy semen analysis (PVSA) is best done 12 weeks after the procedure; a second test is not required if azoospermia is confirmed
4. If non-motile sperm persist, continue additional contraception until fewer than 100,000 non-motile sperm/mL are present; if this persists beyond 7 months, or if motile sperm are present at 7 months, the procedure is considered a failure
5. If planning vasectomy around a pregnancy, the procedure should be planned well in advance of delivery so that sperm is undetectable by 21 days postpartum
6. Advise condoms to prevent sexually transmitted infections (STIs)

**Vasectomy procedures available:**
- Traditional method — 2 incisions in the scrotum; each vas is located, cut, and the ends closed
- No-scalpel vasectomy (NSV) — a single keyhole entry to the scrotal midline; both vas are cut and tied or left open

Patients may choose to store sperm privately before the procedure. Sperm storage involves cryopreservation; thawed sperm have a 50–60% survival rate (lower for poor quality sperm).

**Post-vasectomy advice:**
- Rest and avoid strenuous activity; avoid lifting children who may inadvertently knock the groin
- Abstain from sexual activity until wounds have healed (usually 1–2 weeks)
- Wear tightly fitting underpants or athletic support for the first few days, including at night
- Use ice packs and regular paracetamol for 48 hours to reduce pain and bleeding
- Seek review if persistent pain or bleeding
- Seek urgent medical attention if there is a rapidly enlarging scrotal lump (haematoma)

**Managing post-vasectomy complications:**

- **Bruising** — will settle spontaneously
- **Pain** — rest, ice pack, and paracetamol
- **Skin infection** — appropriate antibiotics
- **Epididymo-orchitis** (hot, painful, enlarged scrotum) — rest, broad-spectrum antibiotics, and scrotal support
- **Abscess** (rare) — may require surgical drainage; seek advice from the clinician who performed the procedure
- **Small scrotal haematoma** — rest, elevation, support, and cold compress
- **Large scrotal haematoma** (very rare) — may require surgical drainage
- **Haematospermia or haematuria** — usually settles spontaneously

### Tubal Ligation

Your doctor may consider the following management steps:

1. Provide written and verbal information about the procedure, including that LARCs provide equivalent efficacy without regret, and vasectomy carries less morbidity than female sterilisation
2. Advise about contraception in the 7-day window after the procedure:
   - Retain a copper IUD (Cu-IUD) or levonorgestrel intrauterine system (LNG-IUS) for at least 7 days
   - Ensure the last contraceptive injection occurred within 13 weeks of the procedure
   - Continue progestogen-only contraceptive pill (POP) for at least 7 days after
   - If using the combined oral contraceptive pill (COC), omit the hormone-free interval if the procedure falls during this time, and continue active pills for at least 7 days after
   - The progestogen implant can be removed at the time of tubal ligation or any time after, within its licensed duration of use
   - Use condoms if no other contraceptive method is in use
3. Once a procedure date is confirmed, a pre-operative appointment will check blood pressure, BMI, and other surgical risk factors; cervical screening status; and exclude current pregnancy if indicated
4. Advise condoms to prevent sexually transmitted infections (STIs)

**Tubal ligation procedures available:**
- Transection of the Fallopian tubes (electrocautery, or cutting and tying)
- Filshie clip tubal occlusion
- Salpingectomy

The procedure is performed laparoscopically, as a mini-laparotomy, or through open surgery. It is usually performed under general anaesthetic. Patients can generally resume normal activities within 7–10 days unless performed as part of more major surgery.

**Complications (uncommon; 1–3% of cases):**
- Anaesthetic risk (e.g. allergic reaction)
- Injury to bowel, bladder, or ureters
- Infection, inflammation, and pain of wound or Fallopian tube(s)
- Haemorrhage
- Ectopic pregnancy (if sterilisation fails, 40% of resulting pregnancies are ectopic)

Patients who are obese or have a history of previous abdominal surgery are at increased risk of complications. If BMI is greater than 40, a long-acting reversible contraceptive (LARC) will be considered instead.

## Reversal

Reversal procedures are available only privately.

**Vasectomy reversal:**
- Performed using complex microsurgical techniques (vasovasostomy — re-anastomosis of the vas deferens)
- Return of sperm to the ejaculate occurs in 85–90% of men, but this does not guarantee pregnancy
- In practice, approximately 60% of couples achieve pregnancy after vasovasostomy

**Tubal ligation reversal:**
- Tubal re-anastomosis is a complex procedure performed by a small number of specialist surgeons
- Approximately 50% of women achieve pregnancy after reversal
- There is a higher rate of ectopic pregnancy after reversal of tubal occlusion

Assisted reproductive technology (IVF) may be used as an alternative to microsurgical reversal.
