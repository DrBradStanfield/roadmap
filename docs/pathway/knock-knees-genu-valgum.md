# Knock Knees (Genu Valgum)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of knock knees in children — distinguishing normal developmental variation from pathological causes that need specialist input.

---

## Background

- In the majority of children aged under 8 years, knock knees are a benign, developmental pattern that requires no investigation, treatment, or specialist referral.
- Leg alignment goes through a predictable progression from varus (bow legs) to valgus (knock knees) over the first 7 years of life.
- It is normal for children aged between 3 and 7 years to have 2.5 to 5 cm between the inner ankles (inter-malleolar distance) when standing upright with the knees together.
- An inter-malleolar distance of more than 8 cm is considered abnormal.
- In rare cases, knock knees can be pathological — due to congenital disorders, metabolic disorders (such as rickets or renal osteodystrophy), neuromuscular disorders, skeletal dysplasia, or asymmetric growth following fracture, infection, or neoplasia.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess knock knees by taking a history and performing an examination.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Growth and development
- Onset and progression of the knock knees
  - Physiological knock knees typically worsen between ages 2 and 4, then improve between ages 4 and 7
  - Pathological knock knees tend to worsen after age 4
- Any history of trauma, fracture, or infection in the lower limbs
- History of chronic disease (e.g., renal impairment, rickets)
- Associated symptoms such as pain or a limp
- Family history of knock knees, metabolic bone disease, or short stature conditions

Your doctor will also ask about **risk factors for vitamin D deficiency**, including:

- Darker skin pigmentation (particularly South Asian or African ethnicity)
- Prematurity (born before 37 weeks) or low birth weight (under 2,500 g)
- Reduced sun exposure
- Exclusive breastfeeding, mixed feeding, or delayed introduction of solids
- Low vitamin D diet (e.g., fussy eating, vegetarian, or vegan diet)
- Being born to a vitamin D-deficient mother
- Sibling of a vitamin D-deficient child
- Chronic disease: malabsorption syndromes (e.g., coeliac disease, pancreatic insufficiency), chronic liver or renal disease
- Medications affecting vitamin D metabolism or bone health, including:
  - Oral steroids
  - Some anticonvulsants (sodium valproate, carbamazepine, phenytoin)
  - Antiretrovirals, rifampicin, or some chemotherapy agents

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will:

- Plot height and weight and check growth percentiles — stature below the fifth percentile can be a sign of pathological knock knees; obesity can be associated with idiopathic knock knees
- Assess for symmetry — asymmetry suggests a pathological cause
- Measure the inter-malleolar distance (the gap between the inner ankles when knees are pressed together, standing upright with kneecaps facing forward) — more than 8 cm at any age is considered abnormal
- Observe gait, noting any medial thrust (a brief inward movement of the knee during the stance phase of walking) — this can indicate pathological conditions
- Perform a brief neurological examination of the lower limbs (checking for spasticity or weakness)

### Physiological vs Pathological

Your doctor will determine whether the knock knees are within the normal developmental range or pathological:

**Likely physiological (normal):**
- Child aged between 2 and 5 years
- Symmetric deformity
- Normal height and growth
- No pain or other symptoms

**Features suggesting pathological knock knees:**
- Age under 2 years, or significant knock knees persisting beyond age 8
- Progressive deformity, or no spontaneous improvement after age 4 to 5
- Asymmetrical deformity
- Pain
- Inter-malleolar distance greater than 8 cm
- Medial thrust (inward knee movement during walking)
- Height below the fifth percentile for age
- Knock knees that developed after a fracture or bone/joint infection
- History of chronic illness (e.g., poor renal function, rickets, prolonged steroid use)

### Investigations

Your doctor may consider:

- **X-ray** (long leg alignment, standing AP view) if features suggest pathological knock knees
- **Blood tests** if there are risk factors for vitamin D deficiency, an abnormal X-ray, or chronic disease is suspected:
  - Calcium, phosphate, albumin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), creatinine
  - FBC, iron studies, ferritin — iron deficiency often coexists with vitamin D deficiency (particularly in infants with prolonged breastfeeding and delayed solids)
  - 25-hydroxyvitamin D [25(OH)D] — the best marker of vitamin D body stores — if multiple risk factors are present

## Management

### If knock knees appear physiological

If there is no suspected underlying medical condition and the child is healthy, growing normally, and meeting developmental milestones, your doctor will:

- Monitor by observation until the child is aged 10 years
- Reassure parents: no early treatment changes the final leg position in adulthood — braces, special shoes, and orthotics do not make a difference to long-term leg appearance
- Arrange follow-up every 4 to 6 months to assess for expected resolution over time; a series of photographs can help document improvement

### If knock knees appear pathological

Your doctor may consider referral depending on the underlying concern:

- If there are concerns about development, growth, or neurological status — your doctor may refer for paediatric assessment
- If rickets is suspected — your doctor will investigate and manage vitamin D deficiency
- If there is a known chronic illness — your doctor will seek assessment from the relevant specialist
- If other features of pathological knock knees are present — your doctor may refer for paediatric orthopaedic assessment
