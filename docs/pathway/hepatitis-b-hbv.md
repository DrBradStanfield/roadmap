# Hepatitis B (HBV)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection — including who to test, how to interpret serology, managing acute and chronic HBV, antiviral treatment, and when specialist referral is needed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

Your doctor will urgently refer or seek advice for:

- Suspected **acute liver failure** (liver injury + altered consciousness + INR > 1.5)
- **Immune suppressive therapy** being started with known HBV infection
- Suspected **hepatocellular carcinoma** (liver cancer)
- **Pregnancy** with HBsAg positive status

---

## Background

### What Is Hepatitis B?

- Hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg) is the marker for HBV. If HBsAg persists longer than 6 months, the infection is defined as **chronic**.
- Infants and young children are most at risk of chronic infection after acute HBV — 90% of infants aged < 1 year progress to chronic infection. Universal neonatal vaccination has been available in New Zealand since 1987.
- In adults, acute HBV usually resolves spontaneously. Less than 5% progress to chronic infection.
- Chronic HBV can reactivate if a person becomes immunosuppressed — even after apparent resolution.

### Chronic Hepatitis B in New Zealand

- Almost 100,000 people in New Zealand have chronic hepatitis B, with the highest rates in Māori (6%), Pacific peoples (9%), and Chinese (9%).
- Chronic HBV is largely asymptomatic and often undetected without testing.
- Without monitoring and treatment, 15–25% of people with chronic HBV develop cirrhosis, liver failure, or liver cancer.
- Effective oral antiviral treatment is available and can be started in primary care without specialist endorsement in many cases.

---

## Who Should Be Tested

Your doctor will offer hepatitis B testing to:

- Families, household contacts, and sexual contacts of people with chronic HBV
- People of Māori, Pacific, or Asian ethnicity
- People born in high-endemic regions (Pacific Islands, Asia, Middle East, Africa, parts of Europe, South/Central America, Caribbean)
- Men who have sex with men (MSM)
- Sex workers or people with multiple sexual partners
- People who have ever injected drugs
- People who have been imprisoned
- People who have had tattoos or piercings in settings where infection control may have been inadequate
- Infants born to HBsAg-positive mothers
- People with abnormal liver function tests
- People living with HIV or hepatitis C
- Recipients of blood products or organ donation in New Zealand before 1992, or overseas at any time
- Those who received medical or dental treatment in countries where equipment sterilisation may have been inadequate
- After needle-stick injuries or non-consensual sexual intercourse
- Before starting immunosuppressive therapy (prolonged corticosteroids, biologics, chemotherapy, azathioprine, methotrexate)
- In pregnancy and pre-conception

## Assessment

### If Acutely Unwell

Your doctor will assess for:

**Acute hepatitis:** fever, abdominal pain, nausea/vomiting, jaundice, dark urine, pale faeces, tender enlarged liver or spleen, joint pains.

**Acute liver failure:** combination of liver injury + hepatic encephalopathy (confusion, altered consciousness) + coagulopathy (INR > 1.5). This is a medical emergency.

**Cirrhosis signs:** jaundice, white nails (leuconychia), spider naevi, palmar erythema, gynaecomastia, muscle wasting, enlarged or shrunken liver, splenomegaly, ascites.

### Blood Tests Your Doctor Will Arrange

**Initial hepatitis B serology:**
- HBsAg — screens for current HBV infection
- Anti-HBs — tests for immunity (from vaccination or resolved infection)
- Anti-HBc — tests for past exposure; particularly important before starting immunosuppressive therapy

**If HBsAg positive, further tests:**
- HBV DNA (viral load) — key for treatment decisions
- HBeAg — marker of viral replication and infectivity
- Liver function tests (ALT, AST) — note: upper limit of normal for ALT is lower in chronic HBV than in the general population (> 30 U/L men; > 19 U/L women)
- FBC, INR, alpha-fetoprotein (AFP)
- Co-infection screen: hepatitis C, HIV, syphilis, hepatitis A IgG

**Liver fibrosis assessment:**
- AST-to-platelet ratio index (APRI) calculated from blood tests
- If APRI ≥ 1.0 or clinical concern, a liver stiffness scan (FibroScan/elastography) may be arranged

### Interpreting Serology Results

| HBsAg | anti-HBc | anti-HBs | IgM anti-HBc | Interpretation |
|-------|----------|----------|--------------|----------------|
| Positive | Positive | Negative | Positive | Acute HBV infection |
| Positive | Positive | Negative | Negative/low | Chronic HBV or flare-up |

| HBsAg | anti-HBc | anti-HBs | Interpretation |
|-------|----------|----------|----------------|
| Negative | Negative | Negative | No infection or immunity — vaccination may be recommended |
| Negative | Negative | Positive | Immune from vaccination — no further action |
| Negative | Positive | Positive | Immune from resolved infection — risk of reactivation if immunosuppressed |
| Negative | Positive | Negative | Various possibilities (occult HBV, resolved, recovering) — your doctor may seek specialist advice |

## Management

### Acute Hepatitis B

More than 95% of adults with acute HBV clear the virus spontaneously. Your doctor will:

- Monitor weekly with blood tests (FBC, INR, liver function)
- Refer for hospital assessment if there are signs of liver failure, inability to stay hydrated, significant other health conditions, or immunosuppression
- Notify public health authorities (acute HBV is a notifiable disease)
- Advise on care during recovery

**What your doctor will advise you:**
- Rest, good nutrition, and stay well hydrated
- Avoid alcohol and hepatotoxic medications (including NSAIDs; use paracetamol at reduced dose with caution)
- How to avoid passing HBV to others (see below)
- Blood tests at 1 month, 3 months, and 6 months to confirm the infection has cleared

**Preventing spread to others:**
- Vaccinate close contacts — this is funded for household and sexual contacts
- Use condoms until partners are vaccinated and immune
- Cover cuts; clean blood spills with bleach
- Do not share razors, toothbrushes, or items that may carry blood
- Do not share drug-injecting equipment
- Tell healthcare workers (including dentists) about the infection
- Ensure tattooing or piercing equipment is properly sterilised
- Hepatitis B is **not** spread through coughing, sneezing, hugging, insect bites, shared bathroom facilities, shared cooking utensils, food preparation, or swimming pools
- Breastfeeding is safe, especially if the baby has been vaccinated

### Chronic Hepatitis B

All people with chronic HBV need regular, lifelong monitoring. Your doctor will:

**Refer to the Hepatitis Foundation of New Zealand** for ongoing free monitoring (6-monthly blood tests including HBV DNA, AFP, liver function; annual ultrasound if at increased risk of liver cancer; specialist referral if needed).

**If you decline Hepatitis Foundation monitoring**, your doctor will monitor you directly with:
- Blood tests every 6 months (liver function, HBsAg, HBeAg, HBV DNA)
- FBC annually
- Liver stiffness scan (FibroScan) every 5 years
- Liver cancer surveillance if you are at increased risk

**Liver cancer surveillance — who is at increased risk:**
- Moderate liver fibrosis or greater (liver stiffness > 8.1 kPa on FibroScan)
- Aged > 40 with a family history of HBV-related liver cancer
- REACH-B score ≥ 10 (if not on antiviral therapy)
- REAL-B score ≥ 5 (if on antiviral therapy; validated for Asians and Polynesians)
- Sub-Saharan African descent and aged ≥ 20 years

Your doctor will also advise you on:
- Reducing alcohol and cannabis use to protect your liver
- Not starting or changing medications without checking with your doctor or pharmacist
- Weight loss if you have fatty liver
- Seeking early advice if planning a pregnancy

### Antiviral Treatment for Chronic HBV

Treatment is not a cure but is very effective at reducing viral load, suppressing liver damage, and significantly lowering the risk of liver cancer. It consists mainly of daily oral tablets and is usually lifelong. **Do not stop treatment without medical advice** — stopping carries a risk of severe flare-up.

**Your doctor will discuss whether treatment is recommended for you.** Treatment is considered when:
- You have any detectable HBV DNA with cirrhosis, severe liver disease, or co-infection with HIV, hepatitis C, or hepatitis D
- You have extrahepatic manifestations (e.g., kidney inflammation)
- You are at risk of HBV reactivation (starting immunosuppressive therapy or chemotherapy)
- ALT is above the upper limit of normal AND HBV DNA > 2,000 IU/mL
- HBV DNA > 2,000 IU/mL AND aged > 30, or at risk of liver cancer

**Medications used in primary care (if specialist referral is not required):**
- **Entecavir 0.5 mg once daily** — taken at least 1 hour before or 2 hours after food, lifelong. Preferred if you have risk factors for kidney disease (aged > 60, hypertension, diabetes).
- **Tenofovir disoproxil 245 mg once daily** — taken with or without food, lifelong. Preferred for women of childbearing age. Can be taken safely during breastfeeding.

Your doctor will refer to a specialist rather than starting treatment in primary care if you have reduced kidney function (eGFR < 50), are pregnant or planning pregnancy, are immunosuppressed, have HIV, have hepatitis C or D co-infection, are on PrEP, are under 16 years old, or have decompensated cirrhosis.

**While on treatment, your doctor will monitor:**
- ALT and HBV DNA at 3 months, then 6-monthly
- Liver function, HBsAg, HBeAg, HBV DNA every 6 months
- FBC annually
- If on tenofovir: kidney function and phosphate every 6 months (may switch to entecavir if kidney problems develop)

Treatment is considered for stopping only if HBsAg becomes undetectable on two tests 3 months apart — and only after specialist advice.

### HBsAg Positive in Pregnancy

Hepatitis B serology is a routine part of antenatal blood tests. If you test HBsAg positive during pregnancy:

- Your doctor will refer you to both a gastroenterologist and an obstetrician
- Additional blood tests will be arranged (HBV DNA, HBeAg, liver function)
- If you are already on antiviral therapy, your doctor will continue it while seeking specialist advice — stopping risks a severe flare-up
- Breastfeeding is safe, including while on tenofovir for HBV

**Your newborn will receive:**
- Hepatitis B immunoglobulin (HBIG) ideally within 2 hours of birth
- Hepatitis B vaccine at or as close to birth as possible (within 12 hours)
- Further hepatitis B vaccines at 6 weeks, 3 months, and 5 months via the standard immunisation schedule
- A blood test at 9 months to check the baby's HBsAg and antibody levels

If HBIG or vaccine was accidentally delayed beyond 7 days, your doctor will seek paediatric advice promptly.

## When Your Doctor Will Refer or Seek Specialist Input

| Situation | Action |
|-----------|--------|
| Suspected acute liver failure | Urgent hospital assessment |
| Suspected decompensated cirrhosis | Urgent hospital assessment |
| Suspected liver cancer (HCC) | Urgent specialist referral |
| Chronic HBV with cirrhosis | Specialist referral |
| Chronic HBV requiring liver cancer surveillance | Specialist referral |
| Starting immunosuppressive therapy with HBV | Specialist input |
| Antiviral treatment needed but outside primary care criteria | Specialist referral |
| HBsAg positive and pregnant or planning pregnancy | Gastroenterology + obstetrics referral |
| Child with chronic HBV aged < 16 years | Paediatric gastroenterology |
| Uncertain whether acute infection or flare/reactivation | Urgent liver advice |
| Occupational exposure risk (healthcare worker) | Specialist management |

All people with chronic HBV should be referred to the **Hepatitis Foundation of New Zealand** for free long-term monitoring.
