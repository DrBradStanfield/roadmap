# Anxiety in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: August 2019.*

Anxiety is the most common of all the mental health disorders. Symptoms vary from unpleasant to completely debilitating. Earlier help can mean less impact on quality of life and less progression of symptoms.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Help

- **If you are at high risk of suicide, call 111 or go to the emergency department.** This needs an urgent (acute) mental health assessment.
- **Suicidal thoughts** are a red flag. Tell your doctor straight away.

---

## Background

- The lifetime chance of having an anxiety disorder in New Zealand is 24.9%.
- Anxiety often comes with other conditions: about 26% of people with anxiety have depression, and about 9% have a substance use disorder.
- Anxiety is a cluster of disorders that includes:
  - **Generalised anxiety disorder (GAD):** the most common anxiety disorder in primary care. It is chronic, excessive worry about events and activities that is hard to control. This can lead to avoiding triggers and to distress. It often causes physical symptoms, such as fatigue, muscle tension, restlessness, and other body symptoms.
  - Obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD).
  - Panic disorder.
  - Social phobia.
  - Specific phobias, such as animal or situational phobias.

### Anxiety in older people

- Significant anxiety affects 10 to 20% of older people. Most cases are GAD (90%), with worries across several areas such as finances, health, and relationships.
- GAD in older people is usually long-standing, with flare-ups caused by changes in social situation or loss of health and other resources.
- Fear of falling is common. It is linked with becoming housebound, worsening depression, and less mobility.
- Older people may show anxiety through physical symptoms, such as restlessness, fatigue, muscle tension, and insomnia.
- New-onset GAD is rare in older people. When it happens, it is important to rule out other disorders.

## Assessment

### Screening

Your doctor may screen people at higher risk using the **GAD-2** questions:

- During the past month, have you often felt nervous, anxious, or on edge?
- During the past month, have you not been able to stop or control worrying?
- Would you like help with this?

If the answer is yes to either of the first two questions, and yes to the third, your doctor may use the longer **GAD-7** questionnaire.

People at higher risk include:
- People with drug and alcohol misuse.
- Young people.
- People with multiple physical symptoms.
- People with multiple visits to their GP.
- People with chronic physical health problems, such as diabetes or chronic pain.

### Grading severity with the GAD-7

Your doctor may use the GAD-7 to grade severity and guide management.

| | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Cause | Anxiety in response to life events. | Anxiety not always confined to stressful life events. | Anxiety not always confined to stressful life events. |
| Worries | Worries 10 to 30% of the time. | Worries 30 to 60% of the time. | Constant, severe, complex. |
| Avoidance | Infrequent avoidance. | Regular avoidance of situations. | Marked avoidance. |
| Functioning | Slight impairment. | Significant impairment in work, relationships, or social life. | Marked impairment, such as unable to work, few relationships, trouble with daily activities, possible decline in physical health. |
| GAD-7 score | 5 to 9 | 10 to 14 | ≥ 15 |

Severe anxiety also includes moderate symptoms that have not responded to several previous therapies and treatments.

### What your doctor may ask about

- **Symptoms and impairment:** what you avoid because of anxiety, how severe and long-lasting the symptoms are, how distressed you feel, suicidal thoughts, and panic attacks. How much anxiety affects your daily life matters more than a count of symptoms.
- Older people may be asked about grief and loss, fear of falling, and taking part in social and physical activities.
- **Personal and family history:** past mental health disorders, family history of mental disorders, trauma, current or past treatments (medicines and talking therapies), other mental health conditions (especially depression), substance use, and medical conditions or medicines that may make anxiety more likely.
- **Other risk factors:** female sex, age (peak in young adults), ethnicity (Māori, Pacific, and Asian New Zealanders have higher rates of depression and anxiety disorders and are under-diagnosed), socio-economic deprivation, loss or stress (including divorce and unemployment), past physical or sexual abuse, substance use, and ongoing conflict (including spiritual, cultural, or sexual).
- Family violence.
- The needs of any dependent children.

### Other causes your doctor may consider

**Medicines and natural therapies that can cause anxiety:**
- Corticosteroids.
- Anticonvulsants.
- Stimulants, such as theophylline, ephedrine, norepinephrine, and ADHD medicines.
- Sympathomimetics, such as bronchodilators (salbutamol).
- Antihypertensives, such as captopril.
- NSAIDs, such as ibuprofen.
- Levothyroxine.
- Antiparkinson medicines, such as levodopa/carbidopa.
- Benzodiazepine withdrawal.
- Antibiotics, such as penicillin.
- Caffeine, caffeine products, and natural therapies.

**Medical causes:**
- Hormone disorders, such as an overactive or underactive thyroid, phaeochromocytoma, diabetes, low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia), and Cushing's disease.
- Heart disease, such as heart failure and abnormal heart rhythms.
- Lung conditions, such as COPD, pneumonia, and hyperventilation.
- Neurological disorders, such as seizures, tumours, balance (vestibular) problems, and encephalitis.
- Anaemia.
- Pulmonary embolus.
- Menopause.
- Drug withdrawal.
- Metabolic disorders, such as porphyria and vitamin B12 deficiency.
- Any physical illness that is life-threatening or causes pain or disability.

**Lifestyle causes:**
- Caffeine and caffeine products.
- Substances (such as amphetamines, cocaine, cannabis, hallucinogens) and alcohol.
- Nicotine withdrawal.

### Investigations

Many people will not need tests. Your doctor may talk with you about whether to investigate your health concerns. **Your doctor may consider:**
- Blood tests, such as FBC, TSH, sodium, potassium, creatinine, HbA1c, LFTs, and ferritin.
- A urine test in older people.

## Management

### For everyone with anxiety

- Anxiety is common and manageable.
- Self-management tools help improve mental health. These include breathing techniques to manage anxiety and panic attacks.
- Treating other mental health conditions (such as depression or substance use) and other health conditions will improve anxiety symptoms.
- **Lifestyle measures:**
  - Healthy eating.
  - Avoiding coffee and caffeinated drinks late in the day.
  - Physical activity. Exercise may be a useful treatment for anxiety. There is some evidence for yoga, especially when there is a history of trauma.
  - Stopping smoking.
  - Avoiding recreational drugs.
  - Good sleep habits (see below).
  - Time with family and friends.
- **Good sleep habits:**
  - Go to bed and get up at the same time every day. Get into bed when drowsy, tired, and ready to sleep.
  - Make a steady wake-up time the priority, to increase your chance of bright morning light.
  - Avoid daytime naps if possible. If you must nap, do so before 3.00 pm, and for only 30 minutes.
  - Do relaxing things to wind down from work or the day, such as listening to music or having a bath (not too hot).
  - Write things down to let go and let your mind relax.
  - Avoid caffeine and alcohol before bed.
- **Self-help resources** (see Patient Resources below).

### Mild anxiety

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Self-help resources and self-treatment modules.
- Community mental health support for education about anxiety and peer support. This improves anxiety and worry scores, shows no evidence of harm, and has low dropout rates.
- One-on-one counselling through community counselling, psychology, or a workplace employee assistance programme (EAP), for acute stress and anxiety.
- Referral to a physiotherapist for relaxation or therapeutic exercise programmes.
- A review after 2 to 3 months. If anxiety does not improve or gets worse, care follows the moderate or severe steps.

### Moderate anxiety

- There is little benefit from medicines in moderate generalised anxiety disorder or specific phobias. Your doctor may consider medicines for OCD or panic disorder.
- **Talking therapies come first. Options may include:**
  - Brief intervention therapy through primary care mental health services.
  - Anxiety NZ Trust peer support groups for general and social anxiety, or one-on-one therapy (paid).
  - A community alcohol and drug service therapy group, if substance use is also a problem.
  - ACC sensitive claims support, if there is a history of sexual abuse.
  - Support services at your tertiary education provider, if you are an enrolled student.
  - A workplace employee assistance programme (EAP).
- The consultation itself and regular follow-up with your doctor have therapeutic benefits.
- **Follow-up:**
  - After 2 to 4 weeks, your doctor may reassess. If there is improvement, routine care continues. If not, your doctor may consider stepping up or changing the measures above.
  - After 4 to 6 weeks, if there is still no improvement, care follows the severe anxiety steps.

### Severe anxiety

- **Talking therapies come first**, with the same options as for moderate anxiety.
- Regular follow-up, by phone and in person, is key to good outcomes whatever the treatment.

#### Short-term benzodiazepines

Your doctor may consider a benzodiazepine for short-term support during a crisis, and should discuss the risks and side effects.
- Benzodiazepines can reduce how well some therapies work, such as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT).
- Shorter-acting benzodiazepines carry a higher risk of dependence and misuse.

**Clonazepam:**
- Not used if there is a past history of substance misuse.
- Usual adult dose is 0.25 to 0.5 mg two to three times a day.
- Taken at regular times, so it does not become the only way of coping with distress.
- Reduced slowly after 5 to 14 days.

**Diazepam:**
- Usual adult dose is 2 mg three times a day.
- If necessary, increased to 15 to 30 mg per day in divided doses.
- Half the usual adult dose if elderly or frail.

**Lorazepam:**
- Usual adult dose is 1 to 4 mg per day in divided doses.
- Half the usual adult dose if elderly or frail.

#### SSRIs (first-line medicines for anxiety)

Your doctor may discuss starting an SSRI if lifestyle measures, community support, and talking therapies are not available or have not helped.

**Choosing an SSRI:**
- In women of child-bearing age, escitalopram and sertraline are safer and easier to stop.
- SSRIs are linked to a higher risk of bleeding. Your doctor may consider adding a stomach-protecting medicine if you take NSAIDs or aspirin.
- Citalopram has fewer drug interactions for most people, including those with chronic health issues. Like all SSRIs, it has been linked to dose-related QTc prolongation (a heart rhythm change), so the maximum dose should not be exceeded. Escitalopram does not appear to have this risk.

**Typical SSRI doses:**

| Medicine | Starting dose, days 1 to 4 (half dose) | Full dose | Maximum per day |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Sertraline | 25 mg | 50 mg per day | 200 mg |
| Escitalopram | 5 mg | 10 mg per day | 20 mg |
| Citalopram | 10 mg | 20 mg per day | 40 mg |

- Start at the lowest dose possible and increase slowly. The aim is the lowest dose that treats symptoms.
- Some people have a mild, short-lived rise in anxiety in the first week before anxiety slowly eases. People with anxiety often have nocebo effects (side effects driven by expectation).

#### Monitoring after starting an SSRI

Your doctor may monitor the dose for the following 6 weeks:
- **1 to 2 weeks:** a check in person, by phone, text, or email. This includes a repeat GAD-7 score, progress, and support to keep taking the medicine. The medicine starts at half dose for the first 4 days, then full dose if tolerated. If not tolerated, your doctor may change to another medicine. Your doctor will also consider the increased risk of suicide.
- **2 to 4 weeks:** if there is only a partial response, your doctor may consider increasing the dose. If there is little or no response, or side effects are unacceptable, your doctor may consider another SSRI, or changing to or adding a talking therapy.
- **4 to 6 weeks:** after 4 weeks, a repeat GAD-7 score. If you have responded, routine care continues. If not, your doctor may consider a higher dose, another SSRI, or changing to or adding a talking therapy.
- **6 to 8 weeks:** if there is no improvement after 6 weeks, your doctor may suggest trying another SSRI. If you cannot tolerate, or have not responded to, 2 SSRIs, your doctor may consider buspirone.

#### Buspirone (second-line medicine)

- As effective as benzodiazepines for GAD, but less effective in people who have recently used benzodiazepines.
- It does not cause withdrawal symptoms and has a low potential for misuse or physical dependence. It has no effect on depression that occurs alongside anxiety.
- Not used in epilepsy, acute porphyria, acute angle closure glaucoma, or myasthenia.
- Starts at 5 mg three times a day, increased as needed by 5 mg every two to three days. The usual range is 20 to 30 mg daily in divided doses.

---

## When to See a Specialist

- **High risk of suicide:** call 111 or go to the emergency department for urgent mental health assessment.
- **No response to treatment after 8 to 10 weeks:** your doctor may seek mental health advice or refer you for a non-urgent specialist mental health assessment.
- **Severe anxiety that continues despite a trial of medicine:** your doctor may refer you for a non-urgent specialist mental health assessment or seek mental health advice.
