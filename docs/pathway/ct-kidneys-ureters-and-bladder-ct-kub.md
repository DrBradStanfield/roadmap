# CT Kidneys, Ureters, and Bladder (CT KUB)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

A CT KUB is a specialised X-ray scan of the kidneys, ureters, and bladder. It is most commonly used to investigate suspected renal (kidney) colic — the pain caused by a stone passing through the urinary tract. Your doctor will decide whether this scan is right for you based on your symptoms, risk factors, and other test results.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

If you have suspected renal colic (loin-to-groin pain) and any of the following, **do not wait — call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately:**

- Blood test showing creatinine greater than 160 µmol/L or eGFR less than 45 mL/min (signs of kidney impairment)
- Solitary kidney (only one functioning kidney)
- Temperature above 38°C (fever suggesting infection)
- Signs of peritonitis (rigid or very tender abdomen) or sepsis
- Known stones in both ureters at the same time

These features mean the situation is potentially life-threatening and needs hospital assessment before any imaging is arranged.

---

## Background

CT KUB is the preferred imaging test for confirming a kidney or ureteric stone when:

- It is your first episode of suspected renal colic with no red flags, or
- You are having follow-up imaging for a known renal stone as recommended by a specialist, or
- You have pyelonephritis (kidney infection) with flank pain that is not improving after 24 hours of IV antibiotics or 48 hours of oral antibiotics, and kidney stone is being considered as an alternative diagnosis.

### When ultrasound is preferred instead

Your doctor may recommend an ultrasound of the kidney rather than CT KUB if you have suspected renal colic with no red flags and you are:

- A woman aged under 35 years, or
- Pregnant (your doctor will also seek obstetric advice before arranging any imaging)

Ultrasound avoids radiation exposure, which is particularly important for younger patients and during pregnancy.

---

## Radiation and Contrast Considerations

CT scans use ionising radiation. Your doctor weighs this risk against the benefit of the information the scan provides.

- CT should generally be avoided during pregnancy unless the scan is critical for your care
- For patients under 40, doctors often consider whether an alternative (such as ultrasound) is appropriate, especially if frequent follow-up scans may be needed over time
- CT KUB for kidney stones does **not** typically require IV contrast (dye injected into a vein), so contrast risks usually do not apply

**If IV contrast is used** (for other CT scan types), your doctor needs to know if you:

- Have had an allergic reaction to IV contrast before
- Have an eGFR less than 30 mL/min (kidney impairment)
- Take metformin (a diabetes medication)
- Have hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you for renal colic based on:

- The character of your pain — typically severe, cramping pain in the loin (side of the back, between ribs and hip) that may radiate into the groin, inner thigh, or genitals
- Associated symptoms — nausea, vomiting, blood in the urine (haematuria)
- Whether you have had kidney stones before
- Blood tests — kidney function (creatinine, eGFR), full blood count, C-reactive protein (CRP)
- Urine tests — looking for blood, infection, or crystals
- Temperature and observations — to check for infection or sepsis

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following depending on your situation:

- **Pain relief:** Strong pain relief is usually needed — this may include anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs such as diclofenac or ibuprofen) or opioids (such as morphine) if pain is severe. Nausea medication is often given alongside.
- **Hydration:** Drinking adequate fluids helps. Your doctor will advise whether you need IV fluids.
- **Antibiotics:** If there is evidence of infection alongside the stone (e.g., fever, positive urine culture), antibiotics are required. Common choices include trimethoprim, nitrofurantoin, or cefalexin for uncomplicated infection; broader-spectrum antibiotics such as gentamicin or ceftriaxone may be needed if infection is more serious.
- **Observation and stone passage:** Small stones (typically less than 5 mm) often pass on their own. Your doctor may arrange a follow-up CT KUB to confirm passage.
- **Specialist referral:** Larger stones, stones that are not passing, or stones causing significant obstruction or infection require urology (specialist kidney/bladder surgery) assessment.
- **Emergency hospital admission:** Required if any red flags are present — see above.

Most people with a straightforward first episode of renal colic can be managed in the community with pain relief, close monitoring, and arranged imaging, as long as no red flags are present.
