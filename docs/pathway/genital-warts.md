# Genital Warts

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of genital warts (caused by genital human papillomavirus, HPV) in primary care. It uses the terms male, female, men, and women to refer to genital anatomy, recognising that gender identity may not match the sex assigned at birth.

---

## Background

Genital warts are caused by specific strains of the human papillomavirus (HPV). The virus is common and most sexually active people are exposed at some point in their lives.

- Over 40 HPV types can infect the anogenital and oropharyngeal mucosa.
- 90% of genital warts are due to HPV types 6 and 11.
- The high-risk types 16 and 18 do not usually cause genital warts but are associated with pre-malignant conditions (e.g. cervical intraepithelial neoplasia, vulval intraepithelial neoplasia, anal intraepithelial neoplasia).
- The average duration of HPV infection is approximately 18–24 months, but can be longer. Long-term latent infection is possible.
- Infection with multiple different HPV types, at the same time or over time, is possible.
- Transmission is via skin-to-skin contact. Condoms are not fully protective but are helpful.
- Genital warts are often present in pregnancy and can increase in size and number during pregnancy.
- HPV vaccination has decreased prevalence in younger age groups and is funded in New Zealand for people aged 9–26 years.

## How Your Doctor Will Assess You

Your doctor will only assess for genital HPV if you report lesions in the anogenital area.

### Where genital warts are found

- **People with a penis:** Commonly on the glans penis, coronal sulcus, meatal orifice, shaft, or perianal area.
- **People with a vagina:** Commonly vulval or perianal; may also involve the vaginal introitus or (less commonly) the vaginal walls or cervix.
- Lesions may cause itch, irritation, discomfort, or bleeding.
- Perianal lesions are not necessarily associated with anal intercourse.

### Complicated presentations

Your doctor will look for features that require specialist input before starting treatment:

- Disseminated or extensive infection
- A lesion larger than 1.5 cm
- Urethral or intra-anal warts
- You are immunocompromised or have diabetes

### Distinguishing genital warts from other conditions

Diagnosis is made clinically. Your doctor will consider:

- **Benign anatomical variants** — e.g. pearly penile papules, vulvar papillae, Fordyce spots, Tyson glands, sebaceous cysts, skin tags, seborrhoeic keratoses
- **Molluscum contagiosum** — another viral skin infection
- **Condylomata lata** — lesions of secondary syphilis (a blood test is important to exclude this)
- **Pre-malignant or malignant lesions** — e.g. vulval or anal intraepithelial neoplasia, genital squamous cell carcinoma

Your doctor may take a biopsy (punch or shave) if:
- Lesions are atypical, unusually pigmented, ulcerated, or attached to underlying structures
- Lesions recur frequently after treatment or do not respond to treatment
- You have chronic lesions, especially if you smoke or are immunosuppressed

### Other assessments your doctor may perform

- Screen for other sexually transmitted infections (STIs)
- Cervical screening test if due
- Assessment of psychological and psychosexual wellbeing — guilt, shame, and anxiety are common emotional responses to a diagnosis of genital HPV

## Do Genital Warts Always Need Treatment?

No. Treatment is cosmetic rather than curative — it does not eradicate the virus from the body. This means no treatment is a valid option at any stage, particularly for asymptomatic warts.

- **30% of patients will experience spontaneous clearance** of warts over a 6-month period.
- The goal of treatment is to trigger immune recognition of the virus, which may encourage earlier resolution of visible symptoms.
- Genital warts can cause significant emotional distress due to stigma and aesthetic concern — your doctor will take this seriously when discussing treatment options.

Your doctor will consider several factors when selecting a treatment:

- Your preferences
- The size and number of warts
- The anatomical site
- Wart morphology
- Cost and convenience
- Adverse effects
- Provider experience

## Treatment Options

### 1. Patient-applied topical creams

These are applied at home. Your doctor will prescribe and instruct you on how to use them.

#### Imiquimod cream (5%)

Imiquimod works by stimulating local immune response. It is suitable for minimally keratinised warts (e.g. vaginal, perianal, sub-preputial area).

- Apply at bedtime, 3 times per week (alternate days)
- Leave on skin for 6–10 hours, then wash off in the morning
- Can be used for up to 16 weeks; most patients who respond do so within 8 weeks
- Common side effects: redness, irritation, and ulceration at the site — mild side effects are expected
- If moderate to severe side effects occur, take a break from treatment and reintroduce gradually
- **Contraindicated in pregnancy**
- **Affects the integrity of condoms and diaphragms**
- Use with caution if you are immunosuppressed — your doctor may seek specialist advice first

#### Podophyllotoxin solution (Condyline 0.5%)

Podophyllotoxin is suitable for small numbers of warts on keratinised skin (area less than 4 cm squared), at sites the patient can see and reach.

- **Not suitable for:** pregnant or breastfeeding women, perianal warts, or mucosal tissue (inner labia, introitus, vagina, anal canal)
- Women of childbearing age should use effective contraception during treatment
- Treatment should be under medical supervision
- The cream formulation is not available in New Zealand

Application:
- Apply twice daily on 3 consecutive days per week, for up to 5 weeks
- Apply only to the lesion — contact with surrounding skin can cause significant ulceration
- If mild skin irritation occurs, protect surrounding skin with Vaseline before applying
- Wash off before sexual contact
- Periodic review is needed to assess tolerability and response

---

### 2. Clinician-applied cryotherapy

Cryotherapy uses liquid nitrogen or CO2 to freeze visible warts. It is safe in pregnancy.

- 2 freeze-thaw cycles are applied per session. The wart is frozen until surrounding skin whitens for 2 mm.
- Lidocaine (lignocaine) gel 2% or Emla cream can be used for pre- and post-treatment discomfort (apply 30–45 minutes before treatment). These are unfunded but available to purchase.
- Repeat weekly for up to 6 weeks.
- If warts persist after 6 treatments, your doctor will review the treatment plan.

---

### 3. Surgical treatment

Surgery may be considered for more extensive warts (e.g. perianal or large vulval lesions) to improve function and appearance and to de-bulk lesions. Options include diathermy, laser, or excision.

- Surgical treatments have higher recurrence rates than other options.
- Your doctor may seek sexual health specialist advice before recommending surgery.

---

### 4. Combination treatment

More than one treatment may be used at the same time or in sequence — for example, cryotherapy or surgical de-bulking of larger lesions followed by imiquimod cream. Monitoring for side effects is important.

---

### 5. Conservative management (watchful waiting)

30% of patients experience spontaneous clearance of warts over a 6-month period. No treatment is always a reasonable choice, particularly for asymptomatic warts.

## Treatment for Specific Sites

Your doctor will guide treatment based on the location of the warts:

- **Cervical or vaginal warts** — high rate of spontaneous resolution. If not extensive, no treatment may be offered initially, with follow-up at 6 months.
- **Intra-anal warts** — sexual health specialist assessment is recommended.
- **Urethral warts** — there is a risk of stenosis (narrowing) with aggressive treatment. Sexual health specialist assessment is recommended.

---

## Treatment for Specific Patient Groups

### Pregnant patients

- Warts may grow rapidly during pregnancy and become pedunculated.
- **Topical medications (imiquimod and podophyllotoxin) are contraindicated in pregnancy.**
- Safe options include expectant management (lesions often resolve after delivery), cryotherapy, or surgical removal if warts are particularly painful.
- Infection of the baby is very rare. Genital warts are not a reason for Caesarean section.
- Your doctor may seek obstetric or sexual health specialist advice if uncertain.

### Breastfeeding patients

- Cryotherapy or surgical options are recommended.
- Topical medications are not recommended.

### Immunocompromised patients (including HIV)

- Warts may be harder to treat, may require longer treatment, and are more likely to recur.
- Your doctor will consider seeking sexual health specialist advice before starting treatment.
- Imiquimod is often used as first-line treatment in this group.

## Skin Care Between Treatments

Your doctor may advise the following to soothe skin and support healing:

- Salt water baths between treatments
- Avoid soaps and detergents on the affected area — use water or soap substitutes
- Avoid perfumed products and any topical preparations not prescribed
- Avoid exposure to urine, faeces, and menstrual blood
- Avoid scratching or rubbing — a single scratch can keep itching going for a week
- Avoid wet swimwear or clothing for prolonged periods
- Prefer tampons or menstrual cups over pads or panty liners, especially scented products
- Keep cool, especially at night
- Wear loose-fitting, natural fibre underwear and clothing
- Cold compresses can help reduce the urge to scratch

## Follow-Up

Your doctor will review you regularly to assess treatment efficacy and tolerability:

- **Cryotherapy** — typically weekly reviews
- **Topical treatments** — typically monthly reviews

Follow-up continues until there are no visible warts. Relapses are treated according to the site and size of recurring warts.

HPV is not a notifiable condition and contact tracing is not required — infection may have occurred 12 months or more before warts became visible. You may choose to disclose to current partners, who can be assessed and treated if needed.

If you are experiencing psychological distress related to your diagnosis, your doctor can refer you to psychological therapy.

---

## Prevention

Your doctor may advise the following to reduce the risk of HPV transmission:

- **Condoms** — consistent use may reduce the risk of HPV acquisition and genital warts by 30–60%. Transmission is via genital skin contact, not just penetrative sex.
- **HPV vaccination** — recommended for all eligible people:
  - Prevents 90% of anogenital warts
  - Funded in New Zealand for people aged 9–26 years
  - Protects against HPV types causing most anogenital warts, as well as 7 oncogenic (cancer-causing) HPV types
  - Recommended even if already sexually active
- A general sexual health check may also be offered.

---

## When Your Doctor May Refer You

Your doctor will refer you to a sexual health specialist if:

- You have a complicated presentation (extensive infection, lesion > 1.5 cm, urethral or intra-anal warts, you are immunocompromised or have diabetes) — referral before starting treatment
- Cervical or vaginal warts are still present after 6 months
- Lesions persist despite treatment
- You have urethral or intra-anal warts

Your doctor may also seek specialist advice (without necessarily referring you) if:

- You are immunocompromised or have diabetes
- Surgical treatment is being considered

For pregnant patients with painful warts or uncertain management, your doctor may request an obstetric or sexual health assessment.
