# Differentiating Asthma from COPD

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Asthma and COPD can look similar — both cause breathlessness, wheeze, and cough. This pathway covers how your doctor distinguishes between them using your symptoms, history, and breathing tests (spirometry).

> **Note (December 2025):** New Zealand COPD Guidelines were updated in 2025. COPD pathways are currently under review in line with these guidelines.

---

## Key Features Suggesting Asthma

Your doctor will consider asthma more likely if you have:

- A pattern of symptoms that suggests asthma — wheeze, waking at night with breathing difficulty, and symptoms that vary a lot from day to day
- No smoking history, but a personal or family history of allergies (atopy) or hay fever
- Breathing tests (spirometry) showing that airflow obstruction is completely or substantially reversible — specifically, a bronchodilator response much greater than +200 mL or +12% of FEV1 and/or FVC

**Important:** Some patients will not show full reversibility on a single test. If spirometry is inconclusive, your doctor may recommend a steroid trial:

- Prednisolone 20–30 mg daily for 2 weeks, or
- Beclomethasone 100 micrograms, 2 puffs twice daily for 6 weeks

Spirometry is repeated after the trial to reassess.

---

## Key Features Suggesting COPD

Your doctor will consider COPD more likely if:

- There is airflow limitation that is **not fully reversible**
- You have persistent symptoms of breathlessness, cough, and sputum production
- Post-bronchodilator spirometry shows FEV1/FVC ratio below 0.7 **and** FEV1 below 80% predicted

---

## Asthma-COPD Overlap Syndrome (ACOS)

Some people have features of both conditions. This is called ACOS. Your doctor may consider this if:

- You have COPD but your airflow obstruction shows some (incomplete) reversibility
- You have had asthma since childhood and smoke, which increases COPD risk
- You have COPD with steroid-responsive airflow obstruction caused by eosinophilic (allergy-type) airway inflammation

Distinguishing ACOS from pure asthma or pure COPD matters because it affects treatment choices — particularly decisions about inhaled corticosteroids.

---

## Other Clinical Considerations

Your doctor will keep in mind:

- A negative bronchodilator response on spirometry **does not** mean steroids won't help — a steroid trial is still worthwhile
- Inhaled steroid trials are generally preferred over oral steroid trials
- Even if spirometry shows little change after a bronchodilator, bronchodilator therapy can still provide real benefits — including less air trapping and less breathlessness during exercise (dynamic hyperinflation)

---

## What to Discuss with Your Doctor

If you are being assessed for possible asthma or COPD, ask your doctor:

- Which condition is more likely based on your symptoms and test results?
- Will I need a steroid trial, and what will it involve?
- Do I have features of both conditions (ACOS)?
- What does my spirometry result mean for treatment?

Your doctor will interpret all findings in the context of your full history and examination. Spirometry results alone do not make the diagnosis.
