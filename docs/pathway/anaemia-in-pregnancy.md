# Anaemia in Pregnancy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of anaemia discovered during pregnancy, most often due to iron deficiency.

---

## Background

- Anaemia in pregnancy is defined as haemoglobin (Hb) **< 100 g/L**.
- It is common and often picked up incidentally on routine antenatal blood tests.
- Anaemia in pregnancy is associated with preterm delivery, small-for-gestational-age infants, increased risk from postpartum haemorrhage, and iron deficiency in infants under 2 years of age.
- **Iron deficiency is the most common cause (> 90%)** and can usually be managed in the community.
- B12 deficiency can occur in women who are vegetarian or vegan.
- Mean corpuscular volume (MCV) and mean corpuscular haemoglobin (MCH) are useful to identify the type of anaemia because they are not affected by pregnancy haemodilution.

---

## Who Is at Increased Risk of Iron Deficiency?

- Past history of anaemia or iron deficiency
- History of malabsorption or coeliac disease
- Vegetarian or vegan diet
- History of heavy periods or bleeding in pregnancy
- Multiple pregnancy
- Giving birth within 1 year of a previous birth
- Grand-multiparity
- Smoker
- Adolescent
- Low socio-economic status or poor diet
- Obesity

---

## Symptoms of Anaemia in Pregnancy

- Fatigue and reduced energy
- Palpitations
- Shortness of breath on exertion
- Apathy or inability to concentrate
- Dizziness or light-headedness
- Poor sleep
- Irritability
- Pica (eating non-nutritive substances)
- Restless legs

Many women are asymptomatic and anaemia is found only on blood tests.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Advice

Your doctor will seek obstetric advice or refer for further assessment if:

- Hb **< 80 g/L** with ferritin < 20 micrograms/L at any stage of pregnancy
- Hb **< 70 g/L** and > 34 weeks gestation
- Significant or concerning symptoms (e.g., severe palpitations, shortness of breath)

**Note:** Intravenous iron infusion is contraindicated in the first trimester of pregnancy.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. Take a history including diet, medical history, and risk factors for iron deficiency.
2. Examine you, especially if you have significant symptoms such as palpitations or shortness of breath.
3. Review blood test results:
   - **Iron deficiency anaemia** is likely if: Hb < 100 g/L, MCV < 80 fL, and ferritin < 20 micrograms/L.
   - **Note on ferritin:** Ferritin is an acute-phase protein — it can appear falsely normal in the presence of inflammation, even when iron deficiency is present.
   - **Note on MCV:** A low MCV in women with thalassaemia trait is common in New Zealand and should be considered.
4. Consider other causes if results are not typical for iron deficiency:
   - B12 or folate deficiency if Hb < 100 g/L and **MCV > 100 fL**
   - Other non-pregnancy-related causes of anaemia

---

## Management

### Iron Deficiency Anaemia (Hb < 100 g/L, ferritin < 20 micrograms/L)

Your doctor may consider:

- **Oral iron supplementation** — this is the standard first-line treatment for iron deficiency anaemia in pregnancy.
- If gastrointestinal side-effects are a concern, alternate-day dosing or different iron preparations may be tried.
- Follow-up bloods (FBC and ferritin) are typically repeated in 3–4 weeks to check response.

### Symptomatic Iron Deficiency Without Anaemia (Hb > 100 g/L, ferritin < 20 micrograms/L)

If you have symptoms of iron deficiency but your haemoglobin is above 100 g/L, your doctor may still consider oral iron supplementation and will reassess in 3–4 weeks.

### B12 or Folate Deficiency (Hb < 100 g/L, MCV > 100 fL)

Your doctor will investigate and manage B12 or folate deficiency according to standard guidelines.

### Dietary Advice

Your doctor will discuss dietary sources of iron and may refer to a dietitian if there is significant anaemia with malnutrition.

- **If you are vegan or vegetarian:** B12 supplementation is recommended throughout pregnancy and while breastfeeding.
  - Pregnancy: 2.6 micrograms/day
  - Lactation: 2.8 micrograms/day
