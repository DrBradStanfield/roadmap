# Non-acute Wheeze and Asthma in Preschool Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and ongoing management of preschool children (aged 1 to 4 years) with recurrent wheeze or possible asthma. It does not cover acute asthma episodes.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Medical Review Promptly

Consult your doctor promptly if your child has any of the following, as these may suggest a more serious or alternative diagnosis:

- Frequent wet or productive cough (not just dry wheeze)
- Faltering growth (failure to thrive)
- Persistent ear, nose, or sinus infections
- Daily symptoms of wheeze or shortness of breath

---

## Background

- Most wheeze in this age group is episodic and triggered by viral respiratory tract infections (RTIs). Many children grow out of wheeze by school age.
- Some children have more frequent or severe episodes, or symptoms between viral illnesses — this pattern is more consistent with preschool asthma, which may respond to regular inhaled corticosteroids (ICS).
- Asthma is a chronic respiratory condition involving reversible narrowing of the small airways.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your child by:

### History

- Features of the wheeze: timing, whether constant or episodic, frequency, duration, work of breathing, triggers (exercise, cold air, allergies, viral illness)
- Typical asthma symptoms, especially between viral illnesses:
  - Expiratory wheeze (the most sensitive and specific symptom)
  - Breathlessness
  - Chest tightness
  - Cough (isolated cough alone is unlikely to be asthma)
  - Symptoms worse at night or early morning, triggered by cold air, allergens, exercise, or stress, recurrent or seasonal, improving with a reliever puffer
- History of atopy (eczema, hayfever, food allergy)
- Family history of asthma
- Exposure to tobacco smoke
- Whether symptoms have been present since birth (this would not be consistent with asthma)
- Whether there are persistent ear, nose, or sinus infections (which may suggest an alternative diagnosis)

### Examination

Your doctor will look at:

- Work of breathing (e.g., intercostal or subcostal recession, tracheal tug)
- Breath sounds (wheeze, reduced air entry, crackles)
- Growth (plotted on a growth chart)
- Signs of allergic rhinitis, nasal polyps, or eczema
- Hands for digital clubbing
- Chest shape (e.g., barrel chest, Harrison's sulcus)

Note: examination between episodes of wheeze is usually normal. Abnormal findings in a well child between episodes may prompt consideration of alternative diagnoses.

### Alternative Diagnoses to Consider

Your doctor may reconsider the diagnosis if your child has atypical features, such as:

- Constant or highly predictable wheeze (may suggest fixed airway narrowing, e.g., tracheomalacia)
- Inspiratory stridor (may suggest upper airway obstruction — e.g., croup)
- Frequent wet or productive cough (may suggest bronchiectasis)
- Cough without wheeze
- Wheeze associated with feeding, vomiting, or lying down (may suggest aspiration)
- No response to bronchodilators at all
- Sudden-onset or one-sided wheeze (may suggest an inhaled foreign body — there may or may not be a choking history)
- Persistent ear/nose/sinus infections (may suggest cystic fibrosis or immune deficiency)
- Faltering growth or clubbing (may suggest bronchiectasis, cystic fibrosis, congenital heart disease, immunodeficiency, or tuberculosis)

---

## Diagnosis: Viral Wheeze vs Preschool Asthma

Your doctor will use a trial of therapy to clarify the diagnosis.

### Features Suggesting Viral Wheeze

- Infrequent episodes (less than every 6–8 weeks) only during viral illnesses
- No severe episodes
- No symptoms between viral illnesses

### Features Suggesting Preschool Asthma

- Frequent episodes of wheeze (more than every 6 weeks)
- Severe episodes requiring medical review
- Typical asthma symptoms occurring between viral illnesses
- Regular night waking with cough or wheeze
- Pattern and triggers typical of asthma (diurnal variation, cold air, allergens, atopy)

### Trial of Therapy

If features suggest preschool asthma, your doctor will conduct an **8-week trial** of low-dose inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) plus reliever therapy, with a review appointment scheduled at 8 weeks.

- If symptoms improve significantly with this trial: preschool asthma is likely
- If symptoms do not improve: your doctor will check inhaler technique and adherence, and reconsider the diagnosis

---

## Management

### For Acute Episodes

If your child is having an acute wheeze episode (now), this pathway does not apply — seek urgent medical care.

---

### Viral Wheeze Management

Your doctor may consider the following approach:

- Stop any ICS if an 8-week trial showed no benefit
- Use a **short-acting bronchodilator (SABA) reliever** — salbutamol 1–2 puffs via metered dose inhaler (MDI) and spacer — as needed during wheezing episodes
- Explain that viral wheeze is not asthma, and most children grow out of it
- Teach, check, and reinforce inhaler and spacer technique at every visit
- Offer an **annual influenza vaccine** (funded for children with qualifying chronic illness)
- Create a personalised **Pre-school Wheeze Action Plan** — [KidsHealth Wheeze Action Plan](https://www.kidshealth.org.nz/sites/default/files/2025-03/kidshealth-pre-school-wheeze-action-plan-v1-5.pdf)

**Environmental factors that may help:**

- Better home insulation
- Avoid cold, damp, mouldy, or crowded housing
- Avoid unflued gas heaters
- Eliminate household smoking (inside or outside, including in the car) — complete cessation is needed
- Dietary changes are unlikely to help unless a specific food allergy has been confirmed

**Review the diagnosis** if symptoms worsen, become more frequent, or atypical features develop.

---

### Preschool Asthma Management

Your doctor may consider the following stepwise approach, reviewed every 3 months:

#### Inhaler Device

- The metered dose inhaler (MDI) with a spacer is the gold standard
- Children under 2 years always need a spacer with a mask; children aged 2–4 years may or may not need a mask
- Spacers should be replaced annually (or after 6 months of regular use)

#### Stepwise Medication Management

| Step | Maintenance therapy | As-needed |
|------|--------------------|-----------|
| Step 1 (viral wheeze pattern) | None | Salbutamol (SABA) 1–2 puffs |
| Step 2 (preschool asthma) | Low-dose ICS, or consider montelukast | Salbutamol (SABA) 1–2 puffs |
| Step 3 (poor control on Step 2) | Low-dose ICS + montelukast | Salbutamol (SABA) 1–2 puffs |
| Step 4 (poor control on Step 3) | Low-dose ICS + montelukast + consider standard-dose ICS | Salbutamol (SABA) 1–2 puffs + specialist referral |

**Titration guidance your doctor will follow:**
- Step up after 1–2 months if not controlled
- Step down after 3 months of good control
- Always check inhaler technique and adherence before stepping up

#### Low-Dose ICS Options

Your doctor may consider:
- Fluticasone propionate (Flixotide) 50 micrograms twice daily, or
- Beclometasone dipropionate (Beclazone) 100 micrograms twice daily, or
- Beclometasone dipropionate ultrafine (Qvar) 50 micrograms twice daily

#### Standard-Dose ICS Options (Step 4)

Your doctor may consider:
- Fluticasone propionate (Flixotide) 100–125 micrograms twice daily, or
- Beclometasone dipropionate (Beclazone) 200–250 micrograms twice daily, or
- Beclometasone dipropionate ultrafine (Qvar) 100 micrograms twice daily

#### Montelukast

Your doctor may consider montelukast (a leukotriene inhibitor taken once daily as a chewable tablet):
- Dose: 4 mg once daily for children under 5 years
- Usually well tolerated; most common side effect is headache
- May be particularly useful for exercise-induced asthma, concurrent hayfever, obstructive sleep apnoea, or when ICS adherence is difficult
- **Important:** montelukast can cause behaviour change and sleep disturbance in children. Stop and seek medical review if these occur. It is not effective in all patients — your doctor will reassess after 6 weeks.

#### Intermittent ICS

Your doctor may consider intermittent rather than regular ICS in some children — started at the first sign of a respiratory infection, rather than taken every day.

#### Asthma Action Plan

Your doctor should provide a personalised **Child Asthma Action Plan** — [Asthma + Respiratory Foundation NZ](https://www.asthmafoundation.org.nz/resources/child-asthma-plan) (available in English, te reo Maori, Samoan, and Tongan).

#### Environmental Factors

Your doctor may advise:
- Improve home insulation
- Avoid cold, damp, mouldy, or crowded housing
- Avoid unflued gas heaters
- Reduce house dust mite exposure if your child is confirmed to be sensitised (requires impermeable bed casings)
- Eliminate household smoking or vaping — complete cessation is needed
- Dietary changes are unlikely to help unless a specific food allergy has been confirmed
- Address psychosocial triggers and stress/panic responses

#### Review Schedule

Your doctor will plan reviews:
- Every 3 months for asthma control
- 6 weeks after any change in medication
- After any acute wheeze episode requiring emergency or after-hours care
- If two or more oral steroid prescriptions have been needed in 2 months

At reviews, your doctor will check inhaler technique, adherence, growth (height and weight), and whether the Child Asthma Action Plan is current.

---

## Risk Factors for Severe Asthma

Your doctor will ask about factors that increase the risk of a severe episode or death from asthma:

**Asthma-related:**
- Poor asthma control or poor inhaler technique
- Underuse of preventer ICS
- Hospitalisation or emergency department visit for asthma in the past year
- Using more than one reliever inhaler per month
- History of sudden attacks or intensive care admission
- Needing frequent oral steroids (more than 14 days in 12 months)

**Other factors:**
- Tobacco smoke exposure
- Cold, damp, mouldy, or crowded housing
- Socio-economic disadvantage or financial hardship
- Gaps in medical care

---

## Specialist Referral

Your doctor may refer your child to a paediatric specialist if:
- Asthma remains poorly controlled after 1–2 months of good adherence to Step 3 treatment
- Step 4 is being considered
