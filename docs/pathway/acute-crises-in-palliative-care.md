# Acute Crises in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the immediate management of acute crises in adult palliative care patients — situations where a patient develops sudden severe distress, pain, breathlessness, delirium, or haemorrhage that requires urgent treatment.

---

## What Counts as an Acute Palliative Care Crisis?

Examples of acute crises include:
- Acute, uncontrolled haemorrhage
- Acute bronchial obstruction
- Superior vena cava obstruction (causing head/neck swelling and stridor)
- Hypercalcaemia
- Massive haemoptysis (coughing blood)
- Seizure
- Ruptured aortic aneurysm (where surgery is not an option)
- Extreme pain, agitation, restlessness, or delirium in the terminal phase

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:
- Identify the likely cause of the crisis.
- Assess the degree of distress in both the patient and their family.
- Determine the cause of distress: pain, breathlessness, anxiety, delirium, or a combination.
- Review current medications, doses, and routes of administration, and what has worked in the past.

---

## Management

### Route of Administration

In an acute crisis, oral medications are often not appropriate. Your doctor will typically use:
- **Subcutaneous** (under the skin) — preferred route; rapid onset, less painful, well absorbed.
- **Buccal or sublingual** (inside the cheek or under the tongue) — used for some medications such as midazolam.
- **Syringe driver** (continuous subcutaneous infusion) — may be needed if symptoms persist.

### If the Patient is Distressed (not due to pain)

If distress is not caused by pain, your doctor may use a **benzodiazepine** to provide temporary sedation until the underlying cause can be addressed — rather than increasing opioid doses.

**Midazolam** is the most commonly used medication for crisis or severe distress in palliative care:
- Given buccally (into the side of the mouth) or sublingually (under the tongue), or subcutaneously.
- Doses are carefully titrated and repeated as needed.
- There is a daily maximum dose; if more is required, specialist palliative care advice is sought.

*Note: In palliative care, doses, indications, and routes of administration may differ from standard prescribing guidelines.*

### If the Patient Has Delirium

Your doctor may use:

**Haloperidol** (first-line for delirium):
- Given subcutaneously initially, then regularly twice a day or via continuous subcutaneous infusion.
- Doses are carefully titrated.

**Levomepromazine (Nozinan)** (second-line):
- Has antiemetic and anti-psychotic properties but causes more sedation.
- Given subcutaneously or orally, then via syringe driver if needed.

If higher doses or different medications are needed, specialist palliative care advice is sought.

### If the Patient is in Pain

Your doctor will use **opioids** (e.g. morphine, oxycodone) given subcutaneously or intravenously. The dose depends on:
- The clinical situation and cause of pain
- Current opioid use
- Other medications being taken
- Degree of kidney (renal) impairment

For patients not previously on opioids, a low starting dose is used. Pain is reassessed every 30 minutes and further doses given if needed. If pain persists after multiple doses, specialist palliative care advice is sought.

**Important — watch for opioid problems:**
- **Opioid excess**: sedation, pinpoint pupils, slow breathing — reversed with naloxone.
- **Opioid toxicity**: hallucinations, delirium, jerking movements — managed by reducing the dose.

**Opioid conversion (oral to subcutaneous):**
- Morphine: approximately 2:1 (10 mg oral = 5 mg subcutaneous)
- Oxycodone: approximately 2:1 (10 mg oral = 5 mg subcutaneous)

### If the Patient is Breathless

Your doctor will:
1. Look for and treat any reversible underlying causes (e.g. heart failure, COPD, asthma, anaemia, anxiety, pleural effusion, pulmonary embolus, superior vena cava obstruction).
2. Consider low-dose **morphine** to reduce the sensation of breathlessness:
   - Morphine reduces respiratory effort and the distressing feeling of breathlessness.
   - Lower doses are used than for pain.
   - If kidney function is significantly reduced, alternatives to morphine may be used.
   - If more than 2 doses per day are regularly needed, your doctor may switch to a slow-release form or continuous subcutaneous infusion.
   - 30 mg per day of oral morphine is generally sufficient when used for breathlessness alone.

---

## After the Crisis

- Your doctor will provide you and your carers with an **emergency letter and instructions** for future crises.
- If symptoms persist despite initial management, specialist palliative care support and advice will be sought.

---

## When Specialist Palliative Care Advice is Sought

Your doctor will contact specialist palliative care services if:
- Higher medication doses are required.
- Significant pain persists after repeated opioid doses.
- Symptoms or crises do not settle with initial management.
