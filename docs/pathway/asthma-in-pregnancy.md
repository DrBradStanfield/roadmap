# Asthma in Pregnancy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of asthma during pregnancy, including medication safety and monitoring.

---

## Background

- Asthma control improves in about one third of pregnant women, worsens in one third, and stays the same in one third.
- If symptoms worsen, the peak period is around the sixth month of pregnancy.
- The course of asthma tends to be similar in successive pregnancies.
- Acute asthma during labour is rare.
- There is no evidence that avoiding specific food allergens in pregnancy prevents asthma or allergies in the child.

> **Important:** Poorly controlled asthma, asthma exacerbations, and severe asthma are strongly associated with adverse outcomes for both mother and baby. Good asthma control throughout pregnancy matters.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess:

1. **Asthma control** — using the GINA (Global Initiative for Asthma) criteria (see below)
2. **Risk factors for flare-ups:**
   - Severe asthma
   - Time of year and exposure to viral infections
   - Non-adherence to preventer medications
   - Obesity and gastroesophageal reflux
3. **Immunisation status** — pertussis and influenza vaccines in pregnancy
4. **Smoking status**

### GINA Asthma Control Criteria

Based on symptoms over the previous 4 weeks:

| Good Control | Partial Control | Poor Control |
|---|---|---|
| All of: daytime symptoms ≤2 days/week; reliever use ≤2 days/week; no activity limitation; no night-time symptoms | One or two of: daytime symptoms >2 days/week; reliever use >2 days/week; any activity limitation; any night-time symptoms | Three or more of: daytime symptoms >2 days/week; reliever use >2 days/week; any activity limitation; any night-time symptoms |

*Not including reliever taken before exercise — record this separately.*

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following:

### Monitoring and Education

- Asthma control should be reviewed every 4–6 weeks during pregnancy.
- Education on the safety of asthma medications in pregnancy — most inhaled medications are safe and it is important not to stop them.
- The importance of maintaining good asthma control to reduce the risk of exacerbations.
- Using an asthma self-management (action) plan.

### Medications

- **Inhaled corticosteroids:** Safe in pregnancy. Keep the dose at the minimum needed to control symptoms and maintain normal lung function.
- **Salbutamol (reliever):** The most extensively studied short-acting bronchodilator in pregnancy and considered safe.
- **Long-acting beta-2 agonists (e.g., salmeterol, formoterol):** Evidence is limited, but animal studies and human studies have not identified major concerns.
- **Prednisone (short course):** Safe in pregnancy when clinically needed.
  - If multiple courses of prednisone are required, there is a higher risk of gestational diabetes — early screening is recommended.
  - If recurrent courses are needed, stress-dose steroids (hydrocortisone) during labour and delivery may be considered.

### Managing Contributing Factors

- **Heartburn or GORD:** Antacids containing aluminium (e.g., aluminium hydroxide) or calcium (e.g., calcium carbonate) are preferred in pregnancy. Omeprazole 10–20 mg once daily may be considered if needed.
- **Nasal symptoms:** Saline nasal spray is first-line; ipratropium nasal spray or a low-dose steroid nasal spray (e.g., budesonide) are options.
- **Smoking cessation:** Your doctor can provide support and referral.
- **Environmental factors:** Minimise exposure to known allergens and irritants.

### Influenza Vaccination

Influenza vaccination is recommended for all pregnant women in line with current guidelines.

### Hospital Delivery

Hospital delivery is recommended if:
- The patient has had recent asthma exacerbations
- Asthma has not been stable during the pregnancy
