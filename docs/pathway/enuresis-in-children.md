# Enuresis in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of bedwetting and daytime wetting in children aged 5 years and over.

---

## Background

Enuresis is involuntary loss of urine in a child aged over 5 years, in the absence of congenital or acquired defects of the central nervous system or urinary tract.

**Types of enuresis:**

- **Primary enuresis** — the child has never been consistently dry; usually idiopathic
- **Secondary enuresis** — the child was consistently dry for more than 6 months before wetting returned; underlying causes are more common (though not always present). Note: children with infrequent wetting (e.g., monthly) are often considered dry by their parents but still have enuresis — symptoms often increase when the child is tired, stressed, or out of routine
- **Mono-symptomatic nocturnal enuresis** — night-time wetting only, without daytime symptoms; has a 15% natural resolution rate per year (0.5 to 2% of adults continue to have enuresis)
- **Non-monosymptomatic nocturnal enuresis** — night-time wetting with daytime symptoms (e.g., urgency, frequency, or daytime incontinence)
- **Daytime incontinence** — can occur alone or alongside nocturnal enuresis

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history covering:

**Enuresis features:**
- Amount and frequency of wetting
- Whether primary (never sustained dryness) or secondary onset

**Bladder and bowel symptoms:**
- Daytime symptoms as well as night-time wetting
- Voiding pattern — stream, hesitation, urgency, abnormal frequency (fewer than 4 or more than 7 times per day)
- Past history of urinary tract infections (UTI)
- Family history of urinary incontinence
- Bowel habits — frequency and consistency (constipation is often missed; parents may report daily motions that are actually overflow)

**Fluid intake:**
- Type, volume, frequency, and whether caffeinated drinks are consumed

**Other relevant factors:**
- Co-morbidities
- Snoring and sleep disorders
- Developmental delay
- Mental health history or behavioural concerns (e.g., ADHD, autism spectrum disorder, oppositional defiant disorder)
- School and family situation
- Impact on child and family (attitudes, punishment)
- History of abuse

**Possible causes your doctor will consider:**

*Medical:*
- Primary mono-symptomatic nocturnal enuresis (idiopathic — the most common cause)
- Chronic constipation reducing functional bladder volume
- Urinary tract infection (UTI)
- Acute illness affecting continence
- Conditions causing polyuria (renal failure, diabetes insipidus or mellitus)
- Bladder overactivity — bladder contracts before it is full, causing sudden urgency; the most common cause of daytime urinary incontinence; can be associated with constipation
- Vaginal reflux — urinary leakage 5–10 minutes after voiding with no other lower urinary tract symptoms; caused by posture when voiding; common in prepubertal girls
- Congenital abnormality of the urinary tract or neurological disorder (e.g., spina bifida, neurogenic bladder)
- Dysfunctional voiding — incomplete bladder emptying due to contraction of the external urinary sphincter during voiding; usually shows large post-void residuals on ultrasound; associated with increased UTI risk
- Stress incontinence — rare in neurologically normal children; caused by increased intra-abdominal pressure (e.g., cystic fibrosis, urological surgery)

*Social / lifestyle:*
- Giggle incontinence — voiding to completion during laughter; caused mostly by overactive bladder or voiding postponement
- Inadequate fluid intake
- Infrequent voiding (fewer than 4 times per day) or voiding postponement

**Physical examination** typically includes:
- Abdomen
- Spine
- Lower limb neurology
- Perineum

**Investigations:**
- Urine dipstick and culture — to exclude UTI and diabetes mellitus

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following approaches, depending on the type and cause of wetting:

### 1. Treat any underlying cause first

- If UTI is present, this will be treated.
- Constipation is actively sought and treated — it is often not recognised by the child or family. Even an empirical trial of laxatives may be considered, as 80% of daytime wetting can improve even without an obvious constipation history.
- Daytime wetting symptoms are addressed before night-time wetting.

### 2. Practical advice for daytime wetting

Your doctor may recommend:

- **Fluids:** Encourage age-appropriate fluid intake. Fluid restriction is not effective. Avoid caffeinated drinks (tea, chocolate, fizzy drinks). Typical daily needs: ages 1–4 about 4 cups/day; ages 5–13 about 5–6 cups/day.
- **Regular voiding:** Encourage the child to toilet every 2–3 hours. Teachers can help by ensuring easy toilet access and checking for fears around using toilets at school.
- **Bowel habits:** Ensure daily, soft, easy bowel movements.
- **Attitude:** Do not punish the child — encourage them to take shared responsibility (e.g., helping change the bed). Star charts and reward systems can be effective.
- **Toilet posture:** Foot and buttock support when sitting, with relaxed abdominals. For dysfunctional voiding, a relaxed, regular voiding pattern with double voiding may help; physiotherapy may assist with muscle re-education.
- **Vaginal reflux:** The child should try straddling the toilet backwards, or sitting with legs apart and an anterior pelvic tilt.
- **Stress incontinence:** Pelvic floor physiotherapy (similar to adult management).

### 3. Behavioural interventions for nocturnal enuresis

These are tried before alarms or medications:

- **Rewards and incentives:** Star charts and reward systems using positive reinforcement. Only reward behaviours the child can control (e.g., going to the toilet, helping change the bed — not dry nights themselves if that is too ambitious). Negotiate rewards with the child and family.
- **Scheduled waking:** Waking the child to urinate at a set time. This can keep beds dry but does not train the child to stay dry independently. Lifting a sleeping child is less useful, as it encourages voiding without waking.
- **Pull-ups:** May help families cope, particularly if blame or punishment is a feature. Funding may be available through Disability Allowance if the child qualifies.

Mono-symptomatic nocturnal enuresis has a 15% natural resolution rate per year — younger children or families not troubled by the condition may choose to wait.

### 4. Alarm programmes

For children aged 7 years and over with primary monosymptomatic nocturnal enuresis where simple measures have not worked, your doctor may refer for a continence assessment and consideration of an alarm programme.

- Alarms have a 65–80% success rate in motivated children who can follow the programme requirements.
- Success is higher when behavioural management strategies are also in place.
- Professional support reduces relapse risk.
- Relapse responds well to repeating the programme.

### 5. Medications

Medications are rarely indicated and your doctor will usually seek specialist advice before prescribing. Options include:

**Desmopressin**
- An antidiuretic that reduces overnight urine production.
- Around 60–70% of children respond.
- Response is not sustained when the medication is stopped — not recommended for long-term use.
- Useful for specific short-term situations (e.g., sleepovers, school camps).
- Safety note: there are concerns about water intoxication (low sodium, cerebral swelling, convulsions). Oral tablets are safer than nasal spray. Risk is minimised by using the lowest effective dose and restricting fluids from 1 hour before to 8 hours after the dose.
- A 1–2 week trial at home is suggested first to assess response.

**Oxybutynin**
- An antispasmodic used when daytime wetting is associated with an overactive bladder.
- Not appropriate for all children — contraindicated in bladder obstruction or retention. Your doctor will assess carefully before prescribing.
- Usually prescribed after specialist review.
- Used as a short-term (3–4 months) adjunctive treatment alongside behavioural strategies.
- Doses are increased gradually to reduce side effects, and weaning is done gradually.

**Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs)**
- Not recommended for nocturnal enuresis.
- Safety concerns at therapeutic levels and especially in overdose; considered contraindicated in this age group.

### 6. Secondary enuresis

If there is a clear precipitant (constipation, UTI, social stressor), this is treated first. If no clear cause is identified, your doctor may consider an empirical trial of laxatives before referral.
