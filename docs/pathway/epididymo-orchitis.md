# Epididymo-orchitis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of epididymo-orchitis — inflammation or infection of the epididymis and testis — in adults.

---

## About Epididymo-orchitis

Epididymo-orchitis is clinically diagnosed based on history, risk of sexually transmitted infection (STI), physical examination, and preliminary investigations. Patients usually present with **unilateral testicular pain** with or without urethral discharge or dysuria.

**Common causes:**
- *Chlamydia trachomatis* and *Neisseria gonorrhoeae* — most likely in sexually active people of any age
- Enteric organisms (e.g., *Escherichia coli*) — more likely in those aged > 35 years, recent urinary tract instrumentation or surgery (including vasectomy), or those who engage in insertive anal sex
- There is overlap between these groups; in a significant number of cases no organism is identified

**Less common associations:**
- Systemic disease
- Immunosuppression
- Mumps
- Amiodarone use

---

## Red Flags — Requires Urgent Assessment

The following require urgent assessment and should not be managed as epididymo-orchitis without urgent evaluation by your doctor:

- **Testicular torsion** — sudden severe pain, especially in those aged 10–25 years; requires emergency urology
- **Testicular cancer** — swelling confined to the body of the testis
- **Strangulated inguinal hernia** — swelling extending above the scrotum, may have abdominal involvement
- **Fournier's gangrene** — pain disproportionate to signs, skin erythema/oedema/duskiness, unable to palpate testis, fever; this is a surgical emergency

> **If you have sudden severe testicular pain, go to an emergency department immediately.** Testicular torsion is a surgical emergency — the testicle can be lost if treatment is delayed.

---

## What Your Doctor Will Assess

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- **Sexual history:** last sexual activity, number and gender of partners in last 3 months/12 months, type of sex (vaginal, oral, anal), condom use, previous STI diagnosis
- **Symptoms:** onset and character of testicular pain and swelling, urethral discharge, urinary symptoms (dysuria, frequency), abdominal or suprapubic pain, fever
- **STI risk factors:** multiple or new sexual contacts, sexually active and aged < 30 years, men who have sex with men (MSM), partner with known STI
- **Other risk factors:** strenuous physical exertion, bicycle riding, prolonged sitting
- **Medical history:** previous UTI or urological procedure, recent genitourinary surgery, history of mumps, immunosuppression (e.g., HIV, diabetes), renal tract abnormalities, amiodarone use

### Examination

Your doctor will examine for:

- Swelling, induration, erythema, and tenderness of the epididymis (most common signs)
- Urethral discharge
- Suprapubic tenderness
- Position of the testis — normal position in scrotum, cremasteric reflex, and Prehn's sign (relief of scrotal pain on elevation suggests epididymo-orchitis rather than torsion)
- Signs of sepsis (high fever, severe systemic illness)

### Investigations Your Doctor Will Arrange

- **Mid-stream urine (MSU)** for culture — your doctor will include clinical details so the lab cultures the sample even if leucocytes are negative
- **First-catch urine** for chlamydia and gonorrhoea PCR/NAAT testing (first 20–30 mL, ideally ≥ 1 hour after last void)
- **Gonorrhoea culture swab** if urethral discharge is present (done in parallel with PCR, not as an alternative)
- **HIV and syphilis serology** — offered to all patients
- **Testicular ultrasound** if there is a testicular mass or enlargement, or a scrotal lump that cannot be confirmed clinically

### Determining the Most Likely Cause

Your doctor will categorise the most likely underlying cause:

| Category | Typical features |
|---|---|
| **STI-associated** | STI risk factors present and/or urethral discharge |
| **Urinary-pathogen-associated** | Aged > 35, low sexual risk, previous UTI or urological procedure, no discharge, positive urine dipstick |
| **Non-infective** | Physical exertion or prolonged cycling, irritative voiding with normal urinalysis |

> **Note:** Dual pathology is possible — STIs can co-exist with torsion, tumours, trauma, or other infections.

## Treatment

> **All treatment decisions should be made with your doctor.** Antibiotic choice and dose depend on which cause is most likely, local resistance patterns, and your individual circumstances including allergies and other medications.

### If Sepsis Is Suspected

If you appear very unwell, your doctor will follow the Sepsis pathway and arrange urgent hospital assessment.

### Acute Urology Assessment (Emergency Referral)

Your doctor will arrange urgent urology assessment for:
- Suspected testicular torsion
- Severe genital infection (Fournier's gangrene, bacteraemia, scrotal abscess)
- Strangulated inguinal-scrotal hernia
- Sepsis

### STI-Associated Epididymo-orchitis

Your doctor will typically prescribe:

- **Ceftriaxone 500 mg** intramuscular injection (single dose), AND
- **Doxycycline 100 mg** orally twice a day for **14 days**

**Sexual health advice:**
- Abstain from sex (including oral sex) or use condoms until 7 days after treatment AND until 7 days after sexual contacts have been treated
- All sexual contacts in the last 3 months should be notified and tested
- Contacts are typically treated empirically with doxycycline 100 mg twice daily for 7 days
- Patient-delivered partner therapy is not legal in New Zealand — contacts need to see their own doctor or a sexual health clinic

### Urinary-Pathogen-Associated Epididymo-orchitis

Your doctor will check prior urine cultures, then typically prescribe:

- **First line:** amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 625 mg orally three times a day for 10 days
- **Second line:** trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole 960 mg twice a day for 10 days
- Treatment is adjusted once the MSU culture result is available
- Quinolones (e.g., ciprofloxacin) are reserved for infections resistant to other antibiotics
- Contact tracing is not required for non-sexually acquired infection

### Non-Infective Cause

Avoid or modify the causative activity.

### Supportive Care (All Types)

- Rest
- Analgesia
- Scrotal support
- NSAIDs (anti-inflammatories) if not contraindicated

Symptoms typically take **4 to 5 days to start resolving**; swelling may take **several weeks** to fully resolve.

---

## Follow-Up

Your doctor will arrange review at **7–14 days**.

**For STI-associated epididymo-orchitis, your doctor will:**
- Discuss test results
- Confirm you completed the antibiotic course without problems
- Ask about condomless sex since treatment (re-treatment needed if re-exposed to an untreated contact)
- Confirm sexual contacts have been notified
- Check a full STI screen has been done
- Arrange **rescreening at 3 months** to detect reinfection
- Refer for sexual health specialist advice if symptoms persist

**For urinary-pathogen-associated epididymo-orchitis:**
- If symptoms persist beyond 2 weeks despite appropriate antibiotics, there may be a renal tract stone; your doctor may arrange renal ultrasound and urology review

**Scrotal ultrasound** will be arranged for any patient with persisting pain, lump, or swelling after treatment, to exclude complications or dual pathology.

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor may refer you to urology or sexual health if:

- Testicular torsion is suspected (emergency)
- Severe genital infection requiring surgery
- STI-associated epididymo-orchitis with persisting signs or symptoms → **sexual health advice**
- Urinary-pathogen-associated with persisting signs or symptoms → **urology advice or non-acute urology assessment**
