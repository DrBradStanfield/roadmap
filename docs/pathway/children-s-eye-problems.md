# Children's Eye Problems

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of eye problems in children, including vision concerns, strabismus, red reflex abnormalities, and watering eyes.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Attention

Contact your doctor urgently or go to the emergency department if your child has:

- Absent or very dull, white, or markedly asymmetric red reflex in a young child
- Photophobia (will not open eyes until all lights are off) in a young child
- Eyelid swollen shut
- New, sudden onset strabismus (squint) or nystagmus (flickering eye movements)
- A hazy cornea

---

## Background

- Many common and significant eye problems present in children aged under 4 years, including strabismus (squint), abnormal eye movements, decreased vision, and abnormal red reflex.
- A squint (esotropia or exotropia) may begin at any age up to around 5 years, most commonly between 18 and 36 months. Early treatment gives the best outcomes.
- Red eyes in children are common and usually represent conjunctivitis — this is generally of little concern unless associated with other findings.
- Vision problems, needing glasses, reading difficulties, and failing the pre-school vision test are common and usually occur after age 3 years.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your child's eyes, which typically includes:

### Vision

- 0 to 3 months — visual interest and fixation is variable at this age.
- 3 to 12 months — should be interested in a face and fixate well.
- 1 to 4 years — should fixate on toys, pictures, and small objects (e.g., hundreds and thousands at 50 cm).
- Older than 4 years — vision is measured objectively.

### Red Reflex

Your doctor will check the red reflex (the orange-red glow seen in each eye with an ophthalmoscope). Abnormal findings include:

- Absent reflex (black, no glow)
- Very different between each eye
- One reflex unusually bright or pale
- Black blobs or streaks within the reflex (possible cataracts)

A hazy cornea is a serious finding in any child and requires urgent referral.

### Strabismus (Squint)

- 0 to 3 months — some variable alignment is normal, but no large constant turn should be present.
- 3 to 12 months — your doctor will assess the corneal light reflex.
- Over 12 months — light reflex and a cover test are used.

A squint visible on examining the corneal reflex in any child under 1 year requires prompt attention. Children do not grow out of a significant misalignment after 3 months of age, and early intervention is more effective.

### Eye Movements

Your doctor will look for:

- Obvious misalignment in different gaze positions
- Nystagmus (flickering or oscillating eye movements)
- Whether both eyes move together (conjugate movement)

### Neurological Signs

Important signs your doctor will check for include:

- A difference in pupil size greater than 1 mm (especially if not present at birth), with mild drooping of the eyelid on the same side (Horner syndrome)
- New onset nystagmus
- Strabismus with restricted eye movement suggesting a nerve palsy

## Management

Management depends on what your doctor finds:

- **Lens opacity or absent/white red reflex in an infant (especially under 3 months):** Your doctor will arrange urgent ophthalmology assessment. Surgery at 6 weeks of age gives the best chance of a good visual outcome; every effort is made to complete surgery before 12 weeks.

- **Strabismus in a child under 1 year:** Your doctor will request urgent ophthalmology assessment to check for infantile esotropia. Better outcomes for stereoscopic vision (depth perception) if surgery is performed within the first year.

- **Watering and crusting or recurrent eye infections in a child under 1 year:** 95% resolve on their own. Your doctor may recommend nasolacrimal duct massage, or treat conjunctivitis or eyelid infections with topical or oral antibiotics as appropriate.

- **Recent vision loss in a child under 13 years with normal examination findings:** If the red reflex, cornea, and iris are all normal with no other neurological signs, your doctor may refer to a community optometrist as a first step rather than hospital.

- **Neurological cause of poor vision:** If signs such as abnormal optic disc, nystagmus, cranial nerve palsy, or non-eye findings are present, your doctor will request ophthalmology and possibly paediatric assessment.

- **Significant eye trauma with signs present** (e.g., red eye, loss of red reflex, iris abnormality): Your doctor will arrange urgent ophthalmology assessment.
