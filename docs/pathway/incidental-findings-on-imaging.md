# Incidental Findings on Imaging

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Incidental findings — also called incidentalomas — are lesions, masses, or unexpected findings detected on imaging performed for an unrelated reason. As cross-sectional imaging has become more common, these findings are increasingly frequent. This pathway provides guidance on how your doctor will approach them.

---

## Background

The significance of an incidental finding depends on its type, size, and your personal health history. Some findings require no action; others need further investigation or specialist review. Your doctor will review the radiology report in the context of your symptoms, risk factors, and other test results before deciding on next steps.

---

## Abdominal Findings

### Mesenteric Panniculitis

A non-specific inflammatory finding usually seen incidentally on abdominal CT, appearing as a mass-like area of increased fat density. It has limited clinical significance in most cases.

**Background:**
- May occur alongside malignancy, but in most cases the malignancy is already known or visible on the same scan
- As an isolated finding, it is unlikely to represent cancer and has not been shown to be a useful predictor of future cancer

**Your doctor may consider:**
- If you have genitourinary symptoms, arranging appropriate investigations and specialist urology review
- If you have gastrointestinal symptoms, arranging appropriate investigations and specialist general surgery review
- If found on a CT-KUB (kidneys, ureters, and bladder) confirming a renal stone with no gastrointestinal or systemic symptoms, no further imaging is required
- If lymph nodes in the mesentery are larger than 12 mm without a visible solid tumour, investigation for lymphoma may be needed and haematology specialist review arranged

---

### Gallbladder Polyps

Polypoid lesions of the gallbladder are common, occurring in up to 9.5% of people. Gallbladder cancer is rare (prevalence approximately 0.06%), and the incidence is similar whether or not polyps are present.

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Ultrasound surveillance based on polyp size and characteristics, following current guidelines
- The vast majority of gallbladder cancers arise from flat dysplastic epithelium, not polyps; your doctor will interpret the finding in this context

## Cardiac Findings

### Coronary Artery Calcification (CAC)

CAC can be found incidentally on CT chest imaging performed for non-cardiac reasons. It is a marker of atherosclerosis and correlates with coronary heart disease risk, including in people with no known history of coronary disease.

**Background:**
- Visual grading (none / mild / moderate / severe) correlates well with formal Agatston scoring and mortality risk
- Moderate or severe CAC indicates high cardiovascular risk regardless of standard risk assessment tools

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Reviewing your cardiovascular history and symptoms
- Performing a formal cardiovascular risk assessment
- Managing modifiable risk factors such as blood pressure and cholesterol
- Referring for cardiology review if the CT reports severe CAC
- Discussing primary prevention strategies for cerebrovascular disease if moderate or severe CAC is present

## Neurological Findings

### Cerebral Infarction and Ischaemia

Unexpected brain infarction or ischaemia found on imaging requires review by your doctor. The significance and management depend on the type of finding:

**Embolic infarctions** (single or multiple bilateral infarcts in different vascular territories):
- These typically have a central source of embolism as the cause
- Your doctor will review you, arrange an ECG, and start secondary prevention treatment
- Cardiology or stroke team advice may be sought to determine whether further heart assessment (e.g. echocardiogram) is recommended

**Lacunar infarctions** (small, less than 15 mm, caused by occlusion of small penetrating brain arteries):
- Individual infarctions may be clinically silent
- Repeated episodes are associated with vascular dementia
- Your doctor will review you and manage modifiable risk factors, and start secondary prevention treatment

**Small vessel ischaemic changes / deep white matter ischaemia:**
- Associated with increased risk of vascular dementia and stroke with repeated episodes
- Your doctor will review and manage modifiable cardiovascular risk factors

**Unexpectedly severe changes in younger patients:**
- Your doctor will seek neurology specialist advice

### Chiari Malformation

Chiari malformation is defined as cerebellar tonsillar descent 5 mm or more below the foramen magnum, which can impair cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow. It is usually present from birth and almost always asymptomatic.

**Symptoms to be aware of (if symptomatic):**
- Headache — most common, classically occipital or upper cervical, worsened by Valsalva, cough, or straining
- Cranial nerve symptoms or signs (most commonly vertical nystagmus)
- Central sleep apnoea
- Cerebellar symptoms and signs
- Weakness
- Paraesthesia (pins and needles)
- Loss of pain sensation

**Your doctor may consider:**
- In children (any age, symptomatic or asymptomatic): neurosurgery specialist assessment
- In adults, if asymptomatic: no follow-up or further imaging required; return if any new symptoms develop from the list above
- In adults, if symptomatic: neurosurgery specialist assessment

---

### Intracranial Aneurysm

Incidental intracranial aneurysms occur in approximately 1% of the population. Management (conservative observation versus surgical or radiological intervention) is nuanced and depends on multiple factors.

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Neurosurgery specialist assessment for all patients with an incidental intracranial aneurysm

---

### Intracranial Arachnoid Cysts

Arachnoid cysts are congenital fluid-filled lesions that occur in approximately 0.5% of the population. Almost all are asymptomatic; if symptoms develop, they usually do so in childhood.

**Possible symptoms (if symptomatic):**
- Headache
- Raised intracranial pressure (from obstructive hydrocephalus)
- Focal neurological signs
- Seizures
- Endocrine dysfunction
- Visual dysfunction

**Your doctor may consider:**
- In patients aged 15 years or younger: neurosurgery specialist assessment regardless of symptoms
- In patients aged 16 years and older, if asymptomatic: no follow-up or imaging required; return if any new symptoms develop
- In patients aged 16 years and older, if symptomatic: neurosurgery specialist advice or assessment depending on severity

---

### Meningiomas

Meningiomas are the most common primary intracranial tumour, and most remain asymptomatic throughout life. Incidence on MRI is 0.5–1% overall, rising to approximately 2% in those aged over 75 years.

**Background:**
- Between one-third and one-half show no growth over time
- Risk factors for growth include younger age, female sex, T2 hyperintensity on MRI, absence of calcification, and surrounding oedema
- Factors associated with no growth include age over 65 years and calcified lesions
- The differential diagnosis includes metastases and solitary fibrous tumour

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Neurosurgery specialist assessment; appropriate follow-up will be arranged by the specialist

---

### Pineal Cysts

Pineal cysts are a common incidental finding, seen on approximately 4% of MRIs and in 20–40% of autopsy studies. The natural history of simple cysts is regarded as benign.

**Your doctor may consider:**
- If simple cyst (less than 15 mm, thin-walled, not causing compression): no further imaging required
- If concerning features (greater than 15 mm, thick-walled, enhancement, or tectal/aqueduct compression): neurosurgery specialist assessment
- If significant abnormalities (e.g. hydrocephalus) or clinical symptoms: urgent neurosurgery assessment as hospital admission may be needed

## A Note on Responsibility

If an incidental finding is identified on an imaging test arranged by another clinician, the requesting clinician holds primary responsibility for arranging further management. However, any clinician copied into the result who notices a finding of significant clinical importance should ensure it has been acted upon, to avoid results being missed.

If you are unsure whether your incidental finding has been followed up, contact your GP or the specialist who arranged the imaging.
