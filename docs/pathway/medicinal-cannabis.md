# Medicinal Cannabis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the therapeutic use of cannabis medicines for medical conditions. It does not cover cannabis screening, dependency, or withdrawal.

---

## Contraindications

Your doctor will check for conditions that rule out medicinal cannabis before considering it.

**Absolute contraindications (apply to all medicinal cannabis products):**

- Pregnancy, planning pregnancy, or breastfeeding
- Unstable cardiovascular or cardiopulmonary disease
- Allergy or hypersensitivity to carrier oils (e.g., coconut, MCT oil, hemp seed, sesame, peppermint, canola, sunflower)

**Additional absolute contraindications for THC-containing products:**

- Previous history of psychosis
- Concurrent severe active mood or anxiety disorder

**Relative contraindications (your doctor will weigh up carefully):**

- Children and adolescents under 18 years — generally avoided due to limited safety and efficacy data
- History of nicotine dependence, alcohol dependence, or illicit drug use — especially relevant for THC products
- Severe liver or renal disease — cannabinoids are primarily cleared by the liver; use lowest effective dose in chronic kidney disease
- Frail elderly with a risk of falls

Previous recreational cannabis use is not a contraindication, but your doctor will be cautious about dependence risk with THC products.

## Background

Medicinal cannabis products contain cannabinoids derived from the cannabis plant. The two main cannabinoids are:

- **Tetrahydrocannabinol (THC)** — the primary psychoactive cannabinoid; responsible for euphoria and dysphoria. May have antiemetic properties.
- **Cannabidiol (CBD)** — affects neurological function including seizure activity, sedation, and dizziness. Psychosis has not been reported with CBD.

Products may contain CBD only, THC only, or a combination of both. They can be taken orally (drops, capsules, lozenges, tea), as oral spray, by vaporisation (non-combustible), or as a topical balm.

All medicinal cannabis products in New Zealand are prescription medicines. Most are unapproved medicines (prescribed under Section 29 of the Medicines Act). The exception is Sativex (CBD + THC oral spray), which is Medsafe-approved for spasticity in multiple sclerosis.

Medicinal cannabis is **not considered first-line therapy for any indication**. Your doctor will only consider it if evidence-based, approved alternatives are ineffective, contraindicated, or not tolerated.

## Assessment

Your doctor will complete a comprehensive clinical assessment before prescribing. This includes:

- Your presenting symptoms and the underlying diagnosis being treated
- Past medical history, particularly cardiovascular, pancreatic, liver, and renal disease
- All conventional treatments already tried, how long they were used, and why they were stopped
- Current medications — especially those metabolised by cytochrome P450 enzymes, which may interact with cannabis
- Mental health history — particularly mood disorders, psychosis, or schizophrenia
- Family history of mental illness, especially schizophrenia
- Any history of substance use disorder or at-risk behaviours
- Social history: family responsibilities, child safety, whether you drive or operate heavy machinery
- Employment, especially in high-risk occupations (heights, heavy machinery)
- Your own ideas, concerns, and expectations about the treatment
- Whether you are already using illicit cannabis for medicinal purposes

## Management

Your doctor may consider medicinal cannabis only after conventional treatments have been exhausted and a thorough assessment has been completed.

### Evidence by condition

Your doctor will consider the available evidence for your specific condition. Conditions with the strongest evidence include:

- **Multiple sclerosis with moderate to severe spasticity** not responding to other anti-spasticity medication — Sativex (CBD + THC oral spray) is approved for this
- **Chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting** — THC has evidence here
- **Chronic non-cancer pain** — THC and combination products have evidence
- **Paediatric epilepsy syndromes** (e.g., Lennox-Gastaut syndrome, Dravet's syndrome) — CBD has moderate evidence

### Product type: CBD-only vs THC-containing

**CBD-only products** contain cannabidiol, less than 2% THC, and no other controlled drug. They come as oral liquids, sublingual solutions, or dried flower. CBD products are generally a good starting point — they are not euphoria-inducing or intoxicating. They are not controlled drugs and can be prescribed on a standard prescription.

**THC-containing products** come as oral liquids, dried flower, or oral spray (Sativex). Combination products come in various THC:CBD ratios (e.g., 1:1 "balanced", or lower THC ratios such as 5:20). THC-only oils come in different strengths (e.g., 10 mg/mL, 25 mg/mL). Products containing THC (other than dried flower) are Class B1 controlled drugs and require a controlled drug prescription.

### Route of administration

The route affects how quickly the medicine works and how long it lasts:

- **Oral (drops, capsules, tea):** Slowest onset; effects peak at 2–4 hours and can last 6–24 hours. Most variable.
- **Oral mucosal spray:** Effects likely peak at 90–120 minutes and can last 6–24 hours.
- **Vaporisation (dried flower):** Fastest onset — maximum effect at 15–30 minutes, lasting 2–4 hours.
- **Topical (patches, gels):** Mostly CBD-based; onset and duration vary substantially between individuals.

### Dosing principles

There are no established dosing guidelines for medicinal cannabis. The general rule is **"start low, go slow"**. Dosing is highly individualised and relies on careful titration.

Commonly prescribed doses (once titrated to effect):

- **CBD:** Commonly 100–200 mg/day. Maximum 1200 mg/day.
- **THC:** Up to 20–30 mg/day. Maximum 50 mg/day.
- **Dried flower:** Up to 3–5 g/day.

For specific conditions:

- **CBD for paediatric epilepsy:** 2.5 mg/kg as the starting dose, titrated up to 10 mg/kg twice daily
- **Sativex for MS spasticity:** 1–12 sprays/day in two divided doses — start at the lowest end
- **THC for chronic non-cancer pain:** Typically 2.5 mg twice daily, titrated up to 15 mg twice daily
- **THC for chemotherapy-induced nausea and vomiting:** Usually 2.5–5 mg twice daily, titrated up to 20 mg/day in 3–4 divided doses

### Adverse effects

Common adverse effects include:

- Gastrointestinal upset, nausea, diarrhoea, appetite changes
- Dry mouth
- Dizziness
- Blurred vision
- Headache
- Cognitive effects

Less common or rare adverse effects include:

- Orthostatic hypotension
- Ataxia or coordination problems
- Psychosis or paranoia
- Tachycardia
- Cannabis hyperemesis

Neurological and cognitive adverse effects are more common with THC-containing products, but CBD products can also uncommonly cause undesirable neurological and behavioural changes.

### Drug interactions

Both CBD and THC are metabolised before excretion. Interactions are possible with drugs metabolised by cytochrome P450 enzymes. Your doctor will check for interactions on the NZ Formulary Interaction Checker.

Key interactions to be aware of:

- **Warfarin:** THC and CBD may increase warfarin levels. More frequent INR testing is needed.
- **Antiepileptics:** Cannabis may increase levels of drugs such as valproate and clobazam.

### Driving

Both THC and CBD can impair driving. Your doctor will advise:

- Do not drive while impaired or while doses are being adjusted
- Starting with a low dose taken in the evening is least likely to impair driving the following day

**Roadside saliva drug testing (as of December 2025):** NZ Police can now conduct random roadside oral fluid drug testing. The test checks for THC, cocaine, methamphetamine, and MDMA. If a saliva test is positive, a second test is taken — a second positive result means a 12-hour driving ban. The sample is also sent to a lab; if THC is confirmed above the threshold, an infringement notice (fine and demerit points) is issued.

A medical defence is available if you were taking prescribed medication according to your doctor's instructions — but this can only be applied after an infringement notice is issued, not at the roadside.

### Workplace and drug testing

- Consider how medicinal cannabis may affect your employment, especially in roles involving driving or heavy machinery
- THC-containing products are much more likely to be detected in workplace drug testing (particularly urine tests) and may be detectable for days to weeks after use
- CBD-only products generally will not be detected in standard drug testing
- Cannabinoids are a prohibited substance in competition for most sports

### Monitoring and review

Your doctor will arrange regular reviews to monitor:

- Adverse effects
- Progress towards agreed treatment goals

The medicine should be stopped if:

- The desired effect is not apparent after 4–12 weeks
- Psychoactive or other side effects are prohibitive (particularly with THC preparations)

If stopping, most patients can simply cease medicinal cannabis without withdrawal symptoms. Some patients may prefer to taper.

### Cost

Medicinal cannabis products are **not funded**. Costs depend on the type and strength of product used.
