# Gastroenteritis in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of gastroenteritis (stomach bug) in children — including recognising dehydration and knowing when emergency care is needed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately if:

- Non-blanching rash (a rash that does not fade when pressed)
- Bile-stained (green) vomiting
- Blood in stool or vomit
- Child is aged under 6 months
- Any significant existing health condition (e.g., diabetes, heart disease, immunocompromised, inflammatory bowel disease)
- Signs of shock: child is limp, drowsy, or unresponsive; rapid weak pulse; low blood pressure; fast shallow breathing; cool, blue, or mottled skin; not passing urine

---

## Background

Gastroenteritis is an acute infection causing diarrhoea, which may be accompanied by vomiting, fever, or abdominal pain. It is most commonly caused by a virus (rotavirus, norovirus, adenovirus), but can also be caused by bacteria (such as Campylobacter) or ingested toxins.

The main goal of treatment is safe rehydration. Oral rehydration therapy (ORT) is the preferred approach for mild to moderate dehydration — it is as effective as intravenous fluid, safer, and less traumatic.

If your child has been on antibiotics recently, consider the possibility of *Clostridioides difficile* infection.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your child for gastroenteritis if there is an acute onset of vomiting and/or diarrhoea, possibly with fever or abdominal pain.

**History your doctor will ask about:**

- Frequency and duration of diarrhoea and/or vomiting
- Whether the vomiting is bile-stained (green)
- How much fluid the child has taken in and how much urine they are passing
- Whether others in the household are unwell (contact history)
- Recent weight, if known
- Recent food (to identify possible food poisoning or toxins)
- Travel history, including rural or international travel
- Any known health conditions that increase risk

**Examination typically includes:**

- General appearance, alertness, and hydration status
- Vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, breathing rate) — reassessed at 30 and 60 minutes if needed
- Capillary refill, skin elasticity, mucous membranes
- Abdominal tenderness, distension, or masses
- Skin check for non-blanching rash
- Weight measurement (acute weight loss is the most accurate measure of dehydration)

**Dehydration severity:**

| Severity | Symptoms | Signs |
|----------|----------|-------|
| Mild | Thirsty, restless | Slightly dry mouth |
| Moderate | Lethargic, irritable | Dry mouth, sunken eyes and fontanelle, decreased urine output, prolonged capillary refill, signs of ketosis |
| Severe | Limp, drowsy | Altered consciousness, shock, capillary refill >2 seconds |

As a rough guide: mild dehydration (~5%) = ~50 mL/kg deficit; severe dehydration (10–15%) = at least 100 mL/kg deficit.

**High risk of becoming dehydrated** (two or more of the following):

- Poor oral intake
- Frequent vomiting
- Frequent loose stools
- Aged under 6 months

**Investigations:**

Your doctor will generally only request a stool specimen if:

- Diarrhoea has not resolved after 7 days
- The child is severely unwell, febrile, or has bloody diarrhoea
- The child is immunocompromised
- The child attends childcare
- There is a history of overseas travel to a country with poor water or sanitation

## Management

### Emergency Management (Shock or Severe Dehydration)

If the child is in shock or severely dehydrated, your doctor will call an ambulance for urgent hospital assessment and intravenous rehydration.

If blood glucose is below 4 mmol/L, an ambulance will be called for immediate transfer to the emergency department.

### Oral Rehydration Therapy (ORT)

For mild to moderate dehydration, your doctor will recommend ORT. Funded options include Pedialyte Bubblegum and Enerlyte sachets. Hydralyte ice blocks or refrigerated ORT may be more palatable.

**In the acute phase (first 2–4 hours):**

- Offer small amounts frequently — about 5 mL per minute by cup, bottle, syringe, spoon, or medicine cup
- Continue even if the child is vomiting — vomiting is not a reason to stop ORT, as most children still absorb fluid given frequently in small amounts
- Continue breastfeeding — do not withhold breast milk to give ORT if the child is tolerating it; formula can continue at full strength
- Your doctor will review hourly to check how well rehydration is working

For children who are not dehydrated, a normal diet with extra fluids is recommended.

### Medications

Your doctor may consider the following, though medications generally have a limited role in childhood gastroenteritis:

- **Paracetamol** — for pain or fever
- **Ondansetron** — your doctor may consider a single dose (0.1–0.15 mg/kg, available as 4 mg or 8 mg dispersible wafers) if ongoing vomiting is preventing the child from tolerating ORT and there is clinical dehydration. It is not routinely used, can prolong diarrhoea, and is not recommended in children under 6 months or under 8 kg, or those with long QT syndrome.
- **Antidiarrhoeal medications and other antiemetics** — not recommended in children
- **Over-the-counter, herbal, or traditional Chinese medicine** — not recommended

### Antibiotics

Most gastroenteritis in children is viral or self-limiting bacterial infection and does not require antibiotics. Your doctor will seek specialist advice before using antibiotics if:

- There is bloody diarrhoea, the illness is prolonged (over 1 week and not improving), or the child is clinically severe
- *Verotoxin- or Shiga toxin-producing E. coli* (VTEC/STEC) is confirmed — antibiotics are **not** used, as they increase the risk of haemolytic uraemic syndrome (HUS), a serious complication affecting the kidneys and blood
- *Clostridioides difficile* is confirmed — specialist advice is sought; if the child is on a proton pump inhibitor (PPI), stopping it may be considered

*Dientamoeba fragilis* and *Blastocystis* spp. are very likely non-pathogenic — no treatment is indicated.

### Once Rehydrated — Advice for Parents and Caregivers

Your doctor will advise:

- Resume normal feeding after the acute rehydration phase — children recover more quickly when fed throughout illness
- Return for review if there are any concerns about fluid intake, or if the child becomes more unwell
- Stay home: children should be symptom-free for at least 48 hours before returning to school or childcare, and 2 weeks before swimming in a pool

---

## When to Seek Further Medical Assessment

See your doctor or seek emergency care if:

- ORT is failing — the child is refusing all fluids, has intractable vomiting or profuse diarrhoea, or dehydration is worsening despite fluid intake
- Another condition is suspected (e.g., appendicitis, meningitis, UTI, diabetic ketoacidosis)
- You are concerned about your ability to manage rehydration at home
