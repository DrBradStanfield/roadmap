# Emollients (Moisturisers)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the use of emollients (moisturisers) for dry skin conditions, including how different formulations work, how to use them correctly, and important safety considerations.

---

## Background

Emollients are essential for managing dry skin conditions but are often underused. They work by reducing transepidermal water loss, which improves the skin's barrier function.

Eczema with dry skin is the most common reason for prescribing an emollient.

Key points:
- The choice and frequency of application depends on the skin condition and patient preference.
- The effects of emollients can be short-lived — large amounts and frequent applications are often needed.
- Emollients may contain preservatives or other substances that rarely cause contact dermatitis.

---

## Formulation Types

Emollients are available as ointments, creams, and lotions. They differ in their oil, alcohol, and water content:

| Formulation | Oil Content | Alcohol Content | Notes |
|-------------|-------------|-----------------|-------|
| Ointment | Highest | None | Greasiest, most effective for dry skin; preferred on broken skin |
| Cream | Intermediate | Low | Good all-rounder; may be preferred during the day |
| Lotion | Lowest | Highest | Easiest to apply to hair-bearing areas; avoid on broken skin (alcohol will sting) |

General principles:
- The greasier the formulation, the more effective it tends to be for treating dry skin.
- Creams are overall better than lotions.
- When skin is broken, use ointments — the alcohol in creams and lotions will sting.
- Lotions are easiest to apply to hair-bearing areas.

---

## How to Use Emollients

Your doctor may advise the following:

- **Apply frequently** — at least 3 or more times a day when skin is very dry.
- **Avoid contamination** — use a spoon or spatula to scoop from a tub rather than fingers.
- **After a skin infection** — discard the current tub and replace with a new one.
- **As a soap substitute** — take care, especially with babies (skin becomes slippery) and when bathing or showering.

### Prescribing quantities

Approximately 30 g of emollient is needed to cover all the skin of an average adult. For total body moisturising twice daily for 2 weeks, the following amounts are typically needed:

| Age group | Quantity |
|-----------|----------|
| Adults | 500–1000 g |
| Children 3–12 months | 150 g |
| Children 1–3 years | 300 g |
| Children 3–6 years | 375 g |
| Children 6 years and older | 500 g |

Your doctor will prescribe adequate amounts to ensure the treatment is effective.

## Paraffin-Based Emollients — Fire Safety

Some paraffin-based emollients can soak into clothing and bedding, creating a fire hazard. Medsafe requires warning labels on products that pose this risk.

If you are using a paraffin-based emollient:
- Change clothing, bedding, and bandages frequently.
- Refrain from smoking or being near people who are smoking.
- Avoid being near naked flames.
