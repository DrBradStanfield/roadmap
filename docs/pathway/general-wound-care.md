# General Wound Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: March 2022.*

This pathway covers general care of all acute and chronic wounds. A wound more than 2 weeks old is treated as chronic.

> **Always discuss your wound and any change in treatment with your doctor or nurse.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department if you may have critical limb ischaemia** (a severe lack of blood supply to a limb). Signs are pain in the limb at rest and/or tissue loss, with absent pulses. This needs hospital assessment by the vascular surgery team.

**Signs of spreading or body-wide infection need same-day medical review:** severe, excessive, or increasing pain out of proportion to the wound (which may be a sign of necrotising fasciitis), redness, hardening, swelling, or crackling under the skin (crepitus) spreading more than 2 cm from the wound edge, red streaks following a blood vessel (lymphangitis), feeling generally unwell, fever, chills, or shaking (rigors), a fast heart rate, or an abscess forming.

**See your doctor or nurse early if a leg wound is at risk of becoming an ulcer.** This includes an injury to the lower leg in someone with chronic venous insufficiency, leg swelling (oedema), or a past venous ulcer, and any wound that has not got smaller within 2 weeks.

## Background

- Clinicians often assess wounds with the TIMES model: Tissue, Infection, Moisture balance, Edge advancement, and Surrounding skin. It helps create the best environment for healing.
- Factors that may slow healing:
  - **Smoking** affects healing and is a significant risk factor for arterial ulcers.
  - **Other conditions**, e.g. diabetes, heart failure, immune deficiency, bleeding disorders, incontinence, peripheral vascular disease, obesity.
  - **Nutrition.** Adequate nutrition is needed for all stages of healing. For most people, a well-balanced diet is enough. Poor nutrition is an independent risk factor for wound healing. Very large tissue loss with heavy wound fluid (exudate) causes a major loss of protein.
  - **Medicines**, e.g. steroids, immunosuppressants, warfarin, aspirin.
  - **Infection** in the wound or elsewhere, which may slow healing through competing inflammation.
  - **Lack of rest and sleep.**

## Assessment

### History

Your doctor or nurse may ask about:
- Where the wound is, and how and where it happened.
- When the injury happened. This helps decide whether the wound is acute or chronic.
- Symptoms: pain, itch, irritation, tingling, numbness, weakness. Excessive pain may point to poor blood supply (ischaemia) or necrotising fasciitis.
- Possible contamination or a foreign body in the wound.
- Any blood loss and roughly how much.
- What has been done so far: first aid, pain relief, removal of foreign bodies, previous dressings, home remedies (e.g. toothpaste, honey, plant extracts).
- Recent antibiotics.
- Previous slow-healing wounds.
- Tetanus immunisation status.
- Allergies to dressings, latex, local anaesthetic, tape, antibiotics, or antiseptics.

Your doctor will also consider whether an injury may have been inflicted (non-accidental). Indicators include a delay in seeking help, an account that changes over time, a history that does not fit the wound or a child's stage of development, a past inflicted injury, inappropriate behaviour by the patient or caregivers, scalds with glove-and-stocking immersion lines, and patterned injuries.

### Examination

- Location and appearance of the wound, temperature, and pulse.
- Checks for damage to tendons, arteries, nerves, joints, and bones.
- Wound size and depth (deep or shallow), measured with a ruler across the widest parts, by tracing, or with photos taken with consent at the same angle and distance with a ruler for scale.
- The TIMES assessment:
  - **Tissue:** dead tissue (necrotic tissue or slough), and blood supply, including the pulses and the circulation around the wound.
  - **Infection:** the wound breaking down or opening, getting bigger, or healing slowly; a bad smell; new or worse pain; more, changed, or pus-like discharge; a deep red or grey wound bed; excess granulation tissue (hypergranulation); pockets or bridging in the wound bed; spreading redness, hardness, swelling, or warmth around the wound; and undermining of the wound.
  - **Moisture balance:** moderate to high exudate can damage the skin around the wound, leading to water-logged skin (maceration) and slower healing. Too little moisture can also delay healing and cause eschar. Sweat, incontinence, and swelling add moisture.
  - **Edge advancement:** whether new skin is growing in from the edge. Abnormal edges (rolled, hypergranulating, undermined) may need further tests such as a biopsy.
  - **Surrounding skin:** thickness, colour, warmth, fragility, hard skin (hyperkeratosis), callus, dry skin, swelling in the lower limb (dependent oedema), infection, pustules, scratches or abrasions from repeated tape or dressings, maceration, and signs of chronic venous insufficiency. Pustules can develop under occlusive dressings and may be infected hair follicles (folliculitis) or a fungal infection.
- Signs of chronic venous insufficiency include varicose veins, ankle flare (tiny varicose veins on the inner foot and ankle), atrophie blanche, red-brown haemosiderin staining, lipodermatosclerosis (hard, woody skin at the ankle), venous eczema, pitting oedema, and venous ulcers. Venous ulcers are typically on the inner leg or between the ankle and knee, with sloped edges. 95% of venous ulcers occur in the gaiter area, between the ankle and knee on the inner leg. A past deep vein thrombosis, significant leg injury, or infection can predispose to venous disease.

### Diagnosis

Your doctor will work out:
- the type of injury.
- whether the wound is acute or chronic (more than 2 weeks old).
- whether it is infected.
- whether there are signs of critical limb ischaemia (rest pain and/or tissue loss with absent pulses).
- whether it is at risk of ulceration.
- the goal of care: healing, maintaining the wound, preparing it for surgery such as a skin graft, or a wound not expected to heal.

Bites, burns, skin tears, foot wounds in diabetes, and leg ulcers each have their own assessment and tests.

## Management

### First priorities

Your doctor may consider:
- **Suspected critical limb ischaemia:** hospital vascular surgery assessment. Call 111 or go to the emergency department.
- **Lower leg wound at risk of ulceration:** early referral to specialist wound nursing.
- **Signs of local, spreading, or body-wide infection:** treatment as a wound infection.
- **Tetanus-prone wound:** tetanus protection, if needed.
- **Pain:** pain relief, reassessed at each visit.

### Helping the body heal

- Better management of other conditions: heart failure, diabetes, and risk factors for peripheral vascular disease and chronic venous insufficiency.
- A review of medicines that may affect healing, where possible.
- Stopping smoking.
- Good nutrition. If intake of protein, vitamins, or trace elements is poor, your doctor may suggest nutritional supplementation or a dietitian assessment.
- Enough rest and sleep.

### Cleaning the wound

- Hands are washed meticulously first.
- Most chronic wounds can be cleaned with sterile saline or tap water that is safe to drink.
- Sterile saline is used for surgical procedures, when tendon or bone is visible, if there may be a sinus or tracking, or if the person is severely immunocompromised.
- Enough fluid is used to irrigate the wound thoroughly, and the surrounding skin is always cleaned.
- Newly forming granulation tissue should not be damaged. A sinus or deep cavity is irrigated only once it is known where it leads and that irrigation is safe.
- A clean or a sterile technique is used.

### Removing dead tissue (debridement)

Dead tissue (necrotic tissue or slough) may be removed to leave a clean wound bed, if within the clinician's scope of practice. Some dressings soften it over time (autolytic debridement). Debridement is not done for:
- facial wounds (specialist wound nursing or plastic surgery advice is sought instead).
- suspected or confirmed ischaemia (the wound is kept dry).
- a necrotic pressure injury on the heel.
- a diabetic foot.
- wounds close to blood vessels, nerves, tendons, implants, or dialysis fistulae.
- suspected or proven cancer.
- wounds of unknown origin or diagnosis.
- a patient who has not consented.

### Choosing a dressing

The main dressing depends on how much fluid (exudate) the wound produces and what the wound needs:
- **Removing dead tissue:** hydrogels for dry wounds, or hydrocolloids for dry shallow wounds (shallow is less than 5 mm deep). Hydrofibres, alginates, or cadexomer iodine for wet wounds. Composite foam is also an option for wet shallow wounds, and hydrocolloids for wet shallow necrotic wounds. Hydrocolloids should not be used on infected wounds.
- **Promoting granulation:** hydrocolloids, low-adherent contact layers, or foams for dry shallow wounds. Hydrofibres, alginates, or foams for wet shallow wounds. Hydrofibre or alginate rope for deep wounds. Negative pressure wound therapy (NPWT) can be used on deep wounds once slough is removed, through specialist wound nursing.
- **Promoting new skin (epithelialisation) by keeping the wound moist:** hydrocolloids, films, or low-adherent contact layers for dry shallow wounds. Foams for wet shallow wounds.
- **Reducing bacteria:** povidone-iodine, silver, or PHMB (polyhexamethylene biguanide) dressings, and cadexomer iodine for wet wounds.
- **Controlling odour:** the cause of the odour is found and treated first (e.g. infection, dead tissue, or the dressing itself). Activated charcoal dressings absorb odour. Metronidazole (as a cream, gel, lotion, or intravenous solution) is another option. These are used for malodorous and infected wounds, fungating cancers, and malodorous wounds with moderate to heavy exudate.

### Protecting the wound and surrounding skin

- Exudate can damage the surrounding skin and cause maceration. At each dressing change, the skin is washed with warm water or saline and moisturised with a simple fragrance-free emollient.
- Products that wick fluid away from the wound, barrier wipes, or a barrier cream can protect the skin.
- To reduce skin damage: avoid rubbing hard, use a remover product for adhesive dressings, and remove the dressing clockwise from the edge towards the centre.
- Every wound needs a non-toxic covering to prevent injury, act as a barrier to the outside, and keep it warm.
- The outer (secondary) dressing is chosen by exudate level and the state of the surrounding skin: a low-adherent layer or film for low exudate, an absorbent pad or foam held with a crepe bandage for moderate exudate, and a super-absorbent dressing for high exudate.
- Bandages hold dressings in place. To avoid a tourniquet effect, a crepe bandage on the arm runs from hand to elbow, and on the leg from the base of the toes to the knee.

### Monitoring

Regular checks look at how the wound is progressing and whether treatment is working. If the new skin is not growing over the wound, your doctor or nurse may look at:
- the underlying cause, reassessing the person, not just the wound.
- infection or colonisation, possibly with a wound swab, with or without a biopsy.
- whether the wound is too dry (needs a moisture-donating product) or too wet or macerated (needs a more absorbent dressing).
- damage at dressing changes, prevented by protecting the edges with a barrier product or "picture framing" the wound.
- callus, which may need offloading or debriding.
- fragile new skin, which may need a simple protective dressing.

If healing is inadequate or the wound has not got smaller within 2 weeks, care depends on the wound type. A leg wound is managed as a leg ulcer. For other wounds, your doctor may consider a biopsy (if the wound is atypical or cancer is suspected) or early specialist wound nursing.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you for:
- **Hospital vascular surgery assessment:** suspected critical limb ischaemia. Call 111 or go to the emergency department.
- **Specialist wound nursing:** a wound at risk of ulceration, inadequate healing, or a wound that has not got smaller within 2 weeks.
- **Dietitian assessment:** poor intake of protein, vitamins, or trace elements.
