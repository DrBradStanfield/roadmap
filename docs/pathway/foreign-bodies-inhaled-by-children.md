# Foreign Bodies Inhaled by Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of suspected inhaled foreign bodies in children, including when emergency care is needed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately if:

- Airway obstruction is present
- Unexplained or recurrent respiratory symptoms following a recent episode of choking, especially if aged under 4 years

---

## Background

- Children aged 6 months to 4 years are at greatest risk of inhaling a foreign body.
- Peanuts are a common inhaled foreign body in younger children.
- Symptoms depend on where the object has lodged:
  - **Larynx or main bronchus** — may cause a sudden and catastrophic event, especially if obstruction is complete.
  - **Below the main bronchus** — tends to cause persistent respiratory symptoms following a choking episode.
- Many episodes are unwitnessed and may appear to resolve quickly — the object may still be present even if the child seems fine.
- Missing the diagnosis can lead to serious harm. Many suspected cases will be false alarms, but a high index of suspicion is important.
- Inhaled foreign bodies typically require removal by rigid bronchoscopy.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will be especially suspicious of children under 4 years who choke on small, hard food items, even if they become asymptomatic afterwards. If the object was not recovered, this warrants further evaluation.

**History your doctor will take:**

- Type and size of the item inhaled
- Whether there was a sudden episode of choking, coughing, or wheezing while eating or playing
- Symptoms present at the time and any symptoms remaining now, including:
  - Drooling or dribbling
  - Respiratory distress (stridor, wheeze, chest recession, difficulty breathing)
  - Cyanosis (bluish colour)
  - Pain on swallowing or chest pain
- In children with unexplained or recurrent chest infections, whether there was any preceding choking episode
- Unilateral wheeze (wheeze on one side only)

**Physical examination typically includes:**

- Signs of respiratory distress: stridor, wheeze, suprasternal or subcostal recession
- Neurological status
- Gagging, choking, drooling, or pooling of secretions
- Chest examination: asymmetrical chest movement, tracheal deviation, crepitations, focal wheeze, decreased breath sounds, or unequal air entry

**Investigations:**

- Routine X-rays and scans (including CT and MRI) are **not** recommended in the community to exclude an inhaled foreign body.
- A plain chest X-ray (inspiratory and expiratory views) may be considered if looking for an opaque foreign body, segmental or lobar collapse, or localised emphysema. However, the chest X-ray is frequently normal even when a foreign body is present.
- Rigid bronchoscopy is required regardless of X-ray results — your doctor will determine whether this is needed based on the clinical picture.

---

## Management

Your doctor will manage this based on the clinical findings:

- **If airway obstruction is present:** Emergency management of airway obstruction (choking) is initiated immediately.
- **If there are signs of respiratory distress, stridor, wheeze, or unexplained respiratory symptoms** (especially in a child under 4 years): Your doctor will arrange immediate specialist assessment and keep the child nil per mouth (nothing to eat or drink).
- **If the foreign body may still be present, even if the child is now asymptomatic:** Your doctor will seek urgent paediatric ENT/otolaryngology (ear, nose, and throat specialist) advice. Deciding whether bronchoscopy is needed requires specialist judgement — the consequences of missing the diagnosis can be severe.
