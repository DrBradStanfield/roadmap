# De Quervain Tenosynovitis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

De Quervain tenosynovitis is a condition causing radial-sided wrist pain due to compression of two tendons (abductor pollicis longus and extensor pollicis brevis) as they pass through a narrowed tunnel at the wrist. Tendons become thickened from acute or repetitive trauma, which restricts their normal gliding movement.

Most cases settle with conservative treatment.

---

## Background

De Quervain tenosynovitis presents as pain on the thumb side of the wrist. It is most common in:

- Young postnatal women with an infant aged 6 to 12 months
- Early childcare workers who frequently lift infants
- People with a history of direct injury to the wrist

Bilateral symptoms are common.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Location of pain and when it started
- Whether there was a direct blow to the wrist
- Pain on moving the thumb or wrist
- Your occupation and activities (especially lifting infants or repetitive hand movements)

On examination, your doctor will look for:

- Tenderness and swelling or thickening just above the radial styloid (the bony prominence on the thumb side of the wrist)
- Pain caused by thumb movement, particularly with the wrist bent sideways
- Crepitus (a grating sensation — uncommon)
- **Hitchhiker sign:** pain when you abduct your thumb against resistance (as if hitching a lift)
- **Finkelstein test:** your doctor folds your thumb across your palm and bends your wrist toward the little finger side — sharp pain is a positive result

---

## Differential Diagnoses

Your doctor will consider other conditions that can cause similar symptoms:

- **Osteoarthritis of the first carpometacarpal joint** — arthritis at the base of the thumb
- **Intersection syndrome** — pain higher up the forearm (5–8 cm above the wrist), caused by inflammation where two groups of tendons cross
- **Wartenberg syndrome** — numbness, tingling, or burning near the base of the thumb caused by compression of a nerve branch; not worsened by hand or finger movement

An X-ray may be arranged if a different diagnosis is suspected (e.g., osteoarthritis).

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following, depending on severity and how long symptoms have been present:

1. **NSAIDs** (anti-inflammatory pain relief such as ibuprofen or naproxen), unless there is a reason to avoid them
2. **Rest** — resting the hand completely if possible, or modifying activities that trigger pain (e.g., changing how you lift an infant)
3. **Hand therapy or physiotherapy** — for splinting and graded exercises, particularly if:
   - You are having difficulty recovering
   - There is loss of strength, range of movement, or hand function
   - Pain is a significant feature
   - A combined thumb and wrist splint (prescribed by a hand therapist) gives a more effective period of rest than off-the-shelf options
4. **Steroid injection** — for cases that have not settled after 2–6 weeks of conservative treatment. Steroid injection combined with splinting has better outcomes than splinting alone; injection relieves pain in approximately 70% of cases
5. **Surgical referral** — if conservative treatments have failed or symptoms recur after one steroid injection, your doctor may refer you for surgical release under a nerve block, which has a high success rate

Mild cases may resolve over a few weeks without treatment. Some recover with rest or splinting alone.
