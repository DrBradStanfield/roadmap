# Nausea and Vomiting in Pregnancy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to nausea and vomiting during pregnancy, from mild morning sickness through to severe hyperemesis gravidarum.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

Contact your doctor or seek emergency care urgently if:

- Signs of **Wernicke's encephalopathy** — confusion, unsteady gait, or abnormal eye movements. This is a life-threatening thiamine-deficiency emergency caused by severe or prolonged vomiting.
- Suspected **gestational trophoblastic disease (molar pregnancy)** — hyperemesis combined with vaginal bleeding and/or excessive uterine enlargement.

---

## Background

Nausea and vomiting is common in pregnancy, affecting 60–70% of pregnancies. Severe and persistent vomiting (hyperemesis gravidarum) affects 1–2%.

**Typical timeline:**
- Usually begins between 4 and 7 weeks of pregnancy
- Peaks around 9 weeks
- 90% resolve by 16–20 weeks
- Nausea and vomiting starting at or after 12 weeks is unlikely to be pregnancy-related — other causes should be investigated

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you by taking a history, examining you, and arranging investigations.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Pregnancy dates and whether symptoms fit the expected timeline
- Fluid intake, food intake, weight loss, and urine output
- Symptoms of dizziness, light-headedness, thirst, and energy levels
- What you have already tried for symptom relief
- Medical history, medications, and allergies

Symptoms that may suggest another diagnosis include:
- Nausea and vomiting starting at or after 12 weeks
- Abdominal pain
- Fever
- Headache
- Neurological symptoms
- Urinary symptoms

Other possible diagnoses your doctor will consider: diabetes in pregnancy, gallstones, gastroenteritis, peptic ulcer disease, hepatitis, pancreatitis, urinary tract infection, thyroid disease, or adrenocortical insufficiency.

### Examination

Your doctor will assess:

- **Degree of dehydration:** heart rate, blood pressure (including postural drop), mucous membranes, skin turgor, peripheral circulation, and sunken eyes
- Weight, temperature, blood pressure
- Neurological signs of Wernicke's encephalopathy (confusion, unsteady walking, nystagmus)

### Investigations

For all patients:
- Urine sample (MSU) — urinary tract infection may be asymptomatic in pregnancy
- Urine dipstick for ketones and glucose finger prick

### Severity — PUQE-24 Score

Your doctor may use the PUQE-24 scoring system to assess severity. It is based on your last 24 hours:

| Question | Scoring |
|---|---|
| How long have you felt nauseated? | Not at all (1) → More than 6 hours (5) |
| How many times have you vomited? | None (1) → 7 or more times (5) |
| How many times have you had dry heaves without vomiting? | None (1) → 7 or more times (5) |

**Score interpretation:**
- **Mild:** 4–6
- **Moderate:** 7–12
- **Severe:** ≥ 13

Your doctor will also consider whether you can tolerate fluids, have signs of dehydration, have biochemical changes or weight loss, and whether you are coping at home.

---

## Management

*Your doctor will direct management based on the likely cause and severity. The following describes the approach they may take.*

### Non-Pregnancy-Related Causes

If another cause is identified (e.g., urinary tract infection, gastroenteritis), your doctor will manage that condition directly.

### First or Second Trimester Pregnancy-Related Nausea and Vomiting

Your doctor will:

- Advise continuing or starting **folic acid** and **iodine** supplementation
  - Folic acid: 0.8 mg daily for most; 5 mg daily if you have a personal or family history of neural tube defects, BMI over 30, diabetes, coeliac disease, sickle cell anaemia, epilepsy, or are on certain medications
- Recommend pregnancy-specific multivitamins — avoid any containing vitamin A
- Consider **non-pharmacological measures** (see below)
- Consider starting **pyridoxine (vitamin B6)** 25 mg three times daily — there is limited evidence it helps mild to moderate nausea; it does not significantly reduce vomiting

### Non-Pharmacological Measures

Your doctor may suggest:

- Drinking small amounts of fluid frequently
- Eating small, frequent meals — high in carbohydrate, low in fat
- Avoiding smells or foods that trigger symptoms
- **Ginger** — as ginger tea, ginger-containing foods, or ginger capsules. A 2014 systematic review found ginger improved nausea compared with placebo, though it did not significantly reduce vomiting episodes
- **Wrist acupressure** — some people find this helpful; there is no evidence of harm, though evidence of benefit is limited

### Management by Severity

#### Mild (PUQE score 4–6)

Your doctor may recommend:

- Non-pharmacological measures above
- If symptoms continue: **antiemetics** (anti-nausea medications) such as:
  - **Cyclizine** 50 mg three times daily
  - **Metoclopramide** 10 mg three times daily (not to be used regularly for more than 5 days)
  - **Prochlorperazine** (e.g. Stemetil) — dose depends on route; available as buccal, intramuscular, or oral
  - **Promethazine** (e.g. Phenergan) — useful at night due to its sedative effect

Return for review if symptoms worsen despite these measures.

#### Moderate (PUQE score 7–12)

If moderate symptoms continue despite treatment for mild symptoms, your doctor may arrange:

- Urine dipstick for ketones
- Blood tests: full blood count (FBC), creatinine, electrolytes, and liver function tests (LFT)
  - LFTs are abnormal in 50% of hospitalised patients; if significantly abnormal, specialist assessment will be sought
  - If potassium is below 3.0 mmol/L, specialist assessment will be sought
- **Antiemetics** as above
- **Intravenous (IV) fluids** if dehydrated — normal saline (not dextrose in pregnancy); up to 2000 mL may be given

After IV rehydration, if you have improved and can tolerate small amounts of fluid, you may be discharged home with:

- A prescription for oral antiemetics — taking these regularly or early in symptoms may prevent the need for further IV or intramuscular treatment
- **Oral potassium supplementation** if potassium is 3.0–3.4 mmol/L (e.g. Span K or Chlorvescent tablets)
- A follow-up review with your lead maternity carer (LMC) or GP in 2–3 days, or sooner if needed
- Consideration of medications for gastro-oesophageal reflux (GORD) if relevant

If you have needed IV fluids more than once, your doctor will also:

- Consider checking thyroid function tests (TFTs) if there are signs of hyperthyroidism
  - Note: hCG can cause a temporary biochemical hyperthyroidism that resolves by 18 weeks without treatment
- Arrange a pregnancy ultrasound to check for multiple or molar pregnancy
- Consider referral to a dietitian if:
  - More than 5% weight loss or BMI below 18.5
  - Prolonged nausea and vomiting for more than 2 weeks
  - Unable to take prescribed vitamin supplements
  - Oral intake less than 50% of normal
  - Multiple pregnancy
  - Previous moderate or severe hyperemesis requiring nutrition support
  - Severe or persistent hyperemesis with no weight gain by second trimester

#### Severe or Persistent (PUQE score ≥ 13, or not responding to treatment)

Severe or persistent vomiting means one or more of:
- Not responding to IV fluids or antiemetics in the community
- More than 5% weight loss
- Biochemical abnormalities

Your doctor will seek specialist (gynaecology or obstetric) assessment. If managed in the community after specialist advice, your doctor may:

- Give IM or IV antiemetics
- Consider **ondansetron** 4–8 mg twice daily for severe hyperemesis not responding to other antiemetics
  - **Important:** Ondansetron is associated with an increased risk of cleft palate and possibly cardiac and renal malformations when used in the first trimester. It is reserved for cases where other treatments have failed, and where the benefits clearly outweigh the risks
- Give IV fluids (normal saline, up to 2000 mL)
- Prescribe **thiamine** 50 mg once daily to prevent Wernicke's encephalopathy
- Arrange a pregnancy ultrasound (if not already done) to check for multiple or molar pregnancy
- Check FBC, creatinine, electrolytes, and LFTs
- Consider TFTs if hyperthyroidism is suspected
- Consider GORD medications if reflux symptoms are present
- Arrange dietitian input and social worker support if needed

### GORD as the Underlying Cause

If nausea and vomiting appears to be secondary to gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD), your doctor will manage it accordingly. Medications that may be considered include:

- **Alginates** — if heartburn is the predominant symptom
- **Antacids** — aluminium- or calcium-containing antacids are preferred; avoid bismuth-containing products especially in the second and third trimesters
- **Omeprazole** 10–20 mg daily — considered safe in pregnancy; use the minimum effective dose for the shortest time

### Third Trimester Nausea and Vomiting

Additional causes are considered in the third trimester, including pre-eclampsia, gastroenteritis or food poisoning, and acute fatty liver of pregnancy (AFLP). Your doctor will manage according to the identified cause and may seek urgent specialist assessment.
