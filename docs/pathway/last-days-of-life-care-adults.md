# Last Days of Life Care (Adults)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: September 2022.*

"Last days of life" is the time when a person's death is thought to be imminent. They may have hours or days to live. This pathway is for adults. Children (usually aged less than 16) have a separate palliative care pathway.

> **Always discuss your loved one's care, symptoms and medicines with their doctor or care team.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Background

- New Zealand's national guidance for this stage is [Te Ara Whakapiri: Principles and Guidance for the Last Days of Life](https://www.tewhatuora.govt.nz/publications/te-ara-whakapiri-principles-and-guidance-for-the-last-days-of-life/). It provides a care plan and a toolkit of checklists, flow charts, tools and patient resources.
- Healthcare teams can use it in all care settings to help them assess the person regularly.
- Recognising that someone is dying can be complex. Sometimes a person thought to be dying lives longer or dies sooner than expected. This uncertainty should not stop an early conversation about the last days of life.

---

## Signs That the Last Days of Life May Have Begun

Your doctor looks for new or worsening signs, such as:
- Profound weakness.
- Eating and drinking less.
- More drowsiness, reduced consciousness, confusion (delirium), or restlessness near the end of life (terminal restlessness).
- Incontinence.
- Changes in breathing and heart rate, e.g. changing patterns of breathing, rattling or noisy breathing, a fast or slow heart rate.
- Cold hands and feet as the circulation shuts down.
- Being unable to swallow medicines by mouth.

---

## Assessment

### Deciding whether further tests or treatment are right

Your doctor may consider:
- What the person and their family/whānau want.
- Whether the decline is sudden or unexpected.
- Whether there are causes that could be reversed, such as:
  - drug toxicity (e.g. from opioids or benzodiazepines)
  - kidney failure
  - a high calcium level (hypercalcaemia)
  - infection
- Whether further tests or treatment are appropriate.

### Physical assessment

Your doctor may check:
- Current symptoms such as pain, restlessness, nausea, breathlessness, or confusion.
- The mouth, and whether mouth care is needed.
- Bowels, bladder, and any pressure areas.

---

## Management

### Talking early

- Your doctor may raise the last days of life early. Uncertainty should not stop an early conversation that plans ahead.
- They take into account the person's advance care plan, if there is one.
- They may ask the person how much they want to know, and who their spokesperson or main contact is.
- Guided by the person's wishes, the doctor may tell them and/or their family/whānau that the person appears to be dying and in the last days or hours of life.
- The conversation can cover:
  - the wishes, fears and concerns of the person and their family/whānau about dying
  - cultural or spiritual needs
  - the signs of the last days of life and the common symptoms to expect
  - medicines that may be needed
  - who to call and what to do when the person dies
  - written information about the last days of life

### Priorities of care

- Support for family/whānau.
- Simpler medicine regimens.
- Managing symptoms while keeping the person comfortable and preserving their dignity.
- Watching for restlessness or agitation near the end of life, which is common.
- Checking for constipation or urine retention (being unable to pass urine). Relieving these may remove the need for more medicine.

### Care plan

Your doctor or care team may start a care plan for the last days of life. It keeps all the records in one place and helps make sure all the steps have been taken.

### Common symptoms to treat and plan for

The most common symptoms are:
- Agitation, delirium and restlessness.
- Breathlessness.
- Nausea and vomiting.
- Pain, including pain in people with kidney impairment.
- Respiratory tract secretions.

### Medicines

Your doctor may:
- Make sure there is enough anticipatory ("as needed", or PRN) medicine on hand for symptoms that may arise after hours or at the weekend.
- Where possible, change essential medicines to injections under the skin (subcutaneous), either with a syringe driver or as single injections (boluses).

### Physical care

- **Mouth care** as needed.
- **Continence care:**
  - Many dying people pass less urine. The person may need incontinence pads or a urinary catheter.
  - Bowel motions become less frequent.
  - If the person is agitated, restless or distressed in the last days of life, the care team may consider urine retention or faecal impaction (hard stool blocking the bowel).
- **Pressure care:**
  - Early advice from an occupational therapist can help with good pressure-relieving care.
  - A person who cannot tolerate turning (e.g. because of pain) may not need turning if they are on a pressure-relieving mattress.
  - If the person can tolerate it, they are turned 2 hourly.

---

## When to Get More Help

Your doctor may seek specialist palliative care support and advice for:
- symptoms that are difficult to manage
- planning ahead, and urgent management if needed
- arranging a joint visit
- considering admission to a hospice

---

## After Death

- The doctor may visit to view the person if a cremation certificate will be needed, or to reassure relatives if the death happened at home.
- The person does not need to be moved straight away.
- Family/whānau should know to contact the funeral director.
