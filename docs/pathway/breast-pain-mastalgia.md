# Breast Pain (Mastalgia)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Breast pain (mastalgia) is a common symptom experienced by most women at some point in their lives. In isolation, it is rarely a symptom of breast cancer, though this should always be considered. This pathway covers how doctors assess and manage breast pain.

---

## Red Flags — See a Doctor Promptly

The following features may suggest breast cancer and require prompt medical assessment:

- Discrete breast or axillary (armpit) lump, ulceration, skin dimpling, or breast distortion
- Persistent nipple eczema, ulceration, or nipple retraction/distortion that has developed within the last 3 months
- Bloody or serous (clear, watery) nipple discharge from one breast — note: green or yellow discharge is not a red flag
- Breast inflammation or infection not responding to treatment, especially without fever or abnormal blood results
- Repeated consultations about the same breast symptom other than pain

If you notice any of these features, see your doctor promptly.

---

## Background

Breast pain most commonly affects women aged 30 to 50 years and is often located in the upper outer area of the breast.

- **Cyclical breast pain** is linked to the menstrual cycle. It resolves spontaneously within 3 months in 20–30% of women, but up to 60% develop recurrent symptoms within 2 years.
- **Non-cyclical breast pain** responds poorly to treatment but may resolve on its own in about 50% of women.
- About 1% of breast cancer occurs in men. If breast cancer is suspected in a male patient, assessment follows the same approach as for female patients.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history and examine you. This typically includes:

### History

- Type of pain: cyclical or non-cyclical, localised or diffuse, one or both breasts, severity, duration, any associated itch or inflammation
- Whether pain is related to activity or follows a trauma (may suggest musculoskeletal cause)
- Breast history: any lumps (site, size, duration), nipple discharge or changes, previous breast disease, surgeries, or implants, date of last imaging, family history of breast disease
- Medication history — some drugs can cause breast pain, including:
  - Hormonal medications (HRT, combined oral contraceptive pill, Depo-Provera, progestogen implant)
  - Antidepressants (sertraline, venlafaxine, mirtazapine)
  - Antipsychotics (e.g. haloperidol)
  - Cardiovascular medications (e.g. digoxin, spironolactone)
  - Some antibiotics and antifungals (e.g. metronidazole, ketoconazole)
- Reproductive history, including breastfeeding
- Impact on daily life — sleep, work, social activities

If the pain pattern is unclear, your doctor may ask you to keep a breast pain diary/calendar.

### Examination

Your doctor will examine both breasts (in upright and lying positions), check the armpit and collarbone area for lymph nodes, and assess the chest wall. They will look for:

- Any lumps, skin changes, nipple changes, or signs of malignancy (skin tethering, nipple inversion)
- Chest wall tenderness (costochondral joints, trigger points)
- Whether your bra is providing adequate support

### Investigations

Most patients with a normal examination do not need imaging. Imaging is generally **not required** for:

- Female patients without breast lumps or red flags
- Female patients with cyclical bilateral breast pain
- Male patients with gynaecomastia but no palpable lump

Your doctor may arrange:

- Breast screening (if you are of screening age and not up to date)
- Private breast imaging if you are anxious or outside screening age, though there are no concerning features — imaging can identify benign causes such as fibrocystic disease, breast cysts, or fat necrosis
- A pregnancy test if clinically indicated

## Possible Causes

### Cyclical

Related to hormone fluctuations with the menstrual cycle. Pain typically:

- Starts 1 to 3 days before a period and settles by the end of menstruation
- Is diffuse and affects both breasts (though one side may be more severe)
- May be associated with generalised swelling and lumpiness but no distinct lump
- Menopausal women taking HRT may also experience similar symptoms

### Non-Cyclical

Pain is more likely to be one-sided or localised. Causes include:

- Medications (see above)
- Mastitis or breast abscess
- Pregnancy
- Gynaecomastia (breast tissue enlargement in males)

### Musculoskeletal

Suggested by the location and radiation of pain, history of trauma, or activities that aggravate it. Causes include:

- Costochondritis (inflammation of cartilage connecting ribs to the breastbone)
- Tietze syndrome (similar to costochondritis, with visible swelling)
- Chest wall trauma or rib fracture
- Fibromyalgia
- Cervical or thoracic spine disease (spondylosis or radiculopathy)
- Shoulder pain

### Extramammary (Non-Breast Causes)

Uncommon, but include:

- Post-herpetic pain (after shingles)
- Angina or coronary artery disease
- Pericarditis
- Pulmonary embolism
- Pleurisy
- Gastro-oesophageal reflux (GORD)
- Gallstones or gallbladder inflammation (cholecystitis)

### In Males

- Young boys and adult men may develop breast pain related to physiological gynaecomastia — this usually resolves without treatment
- Elderly men may develop gynaecomastia secondary to diuretics or cardiac medications; discomfort is usually worst in the first 3 to 6 months
- Musculoskeletal and extramammary causes should also be considered

### In Transgender Patients

- Transgender women (male to female): hormone therapy may cause breast pain
- Transgender men (female to male): residual breast tissue after mastectomy, or breast binders that are too small or tight

## Management

Education and reassurance are central to managing breast pain. Your doctor will reassure you that **breast pain alone is rarely a symptom of breast cancer**.

If red flags or abnormal findings are identified, further assessment will be arranged.

### Conservative Treatment Options

Your doctor may consider the following:

- **Well-fitted bra:** A supportive sports bra or non-underwire bra relieves breast pain for many women
- **Warm compresses, ice packs, or gentle massage** may help some women
- **Topical NSAID gel** (e.g. diclofenac gel): First-line treatment for both cyclical and non-cyclical breast pain — applied directly to the breast. Benefits generally outweigh the risks of adverse effects
- **Paracetamol or oral NSAIDs** may also help
- **Review of hormonal medications:** Your doctor may consider stopping, reducing, or changing hormonal therapy (e.g. the pill, HRT, Depo-Provera, or progestogen implant)
- **Lifestyle modifications:**
  - Maintain a healthy body weight
  - Increase soy products in the diet
  - Reduce caffeine and salt intake
- **Evening primrose oil:**
  - Some patients find 3000 mg or more per day helpful for mastalgia
  - 4 months of continuous treatment is needed to assess effectiveness
  - Contraindicated in people with epilepsy

### Follow-Up

Your doctor will review you:

- At 3 to 4 months if breast pain persists
- Sooner if any new symptoms arise
