# Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of suspected or confirmed deep vein thrombosis (DVT) — a blood clot forming in a deep vein, usually in the leg.

---

## Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if you have:

- Suspected pulmonary embolism (PE) — sudden chest pain, shortness of breath, low oxygen levels, collapse, or coughing blood. A DVT can dislodge and travel to the lungs.
- Acutely unwell with a known or suspected DVT.

---

## Symptoms — When DVT Is Suspected

Your doctor will consider a DVT if you have any of the following in one limb:

- Unilateral (one-sided) leg or arm swelling
- Redness of the affected limb
- Pain and tenderness
- Warm or heavy feeling in the limb, or hot to touch
- Dilated surface veins

---

## Risk Factors for DVT

Your doctor will ask about factors that increase your risk. These are cumulative — the more you have, the higher your risk.

**Major risk factors:**
- Previous blood clot (DVT or pulmonary embolism)
- Recent surgery (within 4–6 weeks)
- Recent trauma to the limb, paralysis, or leg in a plaster cast
- Cancer (known or newly diagnosed)
- Family history of thrombosis or blood clotting disorders (thrombophilia)
- Immobility (e.g., stroke, prolonged bed rest)
- Postpartum (after childbirth)
- Older age

**Other risk factors:**
- Pregnancy or oestrogen therapy (e.g., combined oral contraceptive pill, hormone replacement therapy)
- Long-haul travel (> 6 hours in cramped conditions)
- Obesity
- Varicose veins

---

## What Your Doctor Will Do

### Physical Examination

- Measure the calf circumference and look for signs of DVT: redness, heat, pitting swelling, prominent veins, or tenderness along the deep venous system.
- Check heart rate, respiratory rate, and oxygen saturation.
- Consider alternative diagnoses (cellulitis, heart failure, lymphoedema, ruptured Baker's cyst, muscle injury, superficial vein clot).

### Well's Score

For most patients, your doctor will calculate a **Well's score** — a validated tool that uses clinical features to estimate the probability of DVT. The score determines whether you need an immediate ultrasound scan or a blood test (D-dimer) first.

- **Well's score ≥ 2 (higher probability):** Ultrasound scan arranged immediately.
- **Well's score < 2 (lower probability):** D-dimer blood test arranged first.

**Situations where ultrasound is arranged immediately without Well's score:**
- Suspected upper limb (arm) DVT
- Pregnancy
- Superficial vein clot near the deep system
- Advised by a specialist

### D-Dimer Blood Test

D-dimer is a substance released when a blood clot breaks down. Your doctor interprets the result using **age-adjusted thresholds**:

| Age | Positive D-dimer (microgram/L) |
|-----|-------------------------------|
| Under 50 | ≥ 500 |
| 51–60 | ≥ 600 |
| 61–70 | ≥ 700 |
| 71–80 | ≥ 800 |
| 81–90 | ≥ 900 |
| Over 91 | ≥ 1000 |

- **Negative D-dimer:** DVT is very unlikely (about 99% sensitivity when combined with low Well's score). No further investigation needed.
- **Positive D-dimer:** Ultrasound scan arranged.

A D-dimer can be falsely positive in pregnancy, older age, recent surgery, malignancy, heart failure, severe infection, or recent trauma — your doctor will interpret it in context.

## Anticoagulation While Waiting for Ultrasound

If there is a high probability of DVT and the ultrasound will be delayed more than 6 hours, your doctor may start short-term anticoagulation (blood-thinning medication) to reduce the risk of the clot extending while you wait.

**First choice — rivaroxaban (a tablet):**
- A single 15 mg dose provides 12 hours of cover.
- A second 15 mg dose can be given if the scan is delayed beyond 12 hours (unless your kidney function is reduced or you are aged ≥ 75 years).
- Not used if you are pregnant, breastfeeding, have very poor kidney function, or have a high risk of bleeding.

**Alternative — enoxaparin (Clexane, an injection):**
- Used when rivaroxaban is contraindicated, especially in pregnancy or poor kidney function.
- Dose is calculated based on your weight and kidney function.

## Management After Diagnosis

### Based on Ultrasound Result

The management your doctor recommends depends on where the clot is:

- **Upper limb (arm) DVT or proximal leg DVT (at or above the popliteal vein behind the knee):** Your doctor will seek specialist (thrombosis service or haematology) advice. These are higher-risk clots.
- **Distal (below-knee) DVT:** Anticoagulation therapy started; follow-up with the thrombosis service arranged.
- **Superficial vein clot (no DVT on ultrasound):** Anticoagulation only if the clot is extensive (≥ 8 cm or within 5 cm of the deep system); thrombosis service follow-up arranged.
- **Negative ultrasound but high suspicion remains:** A repeat scan is arranged 10 days later. Your doctor will discuss whether you need anticoagulation in the meantime. Report any worsening leg pain, swelling, chest pain, or breathlessness promptly.

### Anticoagulation Medications

Your doctor will choose the most suitable anticoagulant based on your circumstances. **DOACs (direct oral anticoagulants) are now preferred over warfarin** for most people.

#### Rivaroxaban (preferred DOAC)
- Does not require bridging with heparin injections.
- Dosing: **15 mg twice daily for the first 3 weeks**, then **20 mg once daily**.
- Must be taken **with food** (absorption is much lower on an empty stomach).
- Available patient information leaflets in English, te reo Māori, Cook Islands Māori, Samoan, Tongan, Hindi, Korean, and Chinese.

#### Dabigatran (preferred DOAC)
- Requires **5 days of enoxaparin (Clexane) injections first**, then switch to dabigatran tablets.
- Standard dose: **150 mg twice daily** (or 110 mg twice daily if aged ≥ 80 years, or if there is moderate kidney impairment and higher bleeding risk).
- Not suitable if kidney function is significantly impaired (creatinine clearance < 30 mL/min).
- Kidney function monitored every 6–12 months during long-term treatment.

#### Warfarin
- Requires starting enoxaparin injections at the same time, continued until the INR (blood test) reaches ≥ 2 — usually 5–7 days.
- Regular blood tests (INR) needed to ensure the dose is in the correct range (target INR 2–3).
- Many food and drug interactions.
- Not suitable in pregnancy.

**In pregnancy:** Oral anticoagulants are contraindicated. Long-term low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) injections are used instead. Specialist input is always sought.

### Duration of Anticoagulation

- **Provoked DVT** (caused by a clear, reversible trigger such as surgery, travel, immobilisation, or the contraceptive pill): Usually **3 months**.
- **Unprovoked DVT** (no clear cause): Usually **6 months**; haematology referral arranged.
- **Recurrent DVT:** Lifelong anticoagulation may be considered; haematology referral arranged.

Your doctor will seek thrombosis service advice if there is any uncertainty about duration.

Consider wearing a **medical alert bracelet** while on anticoagulants.

## Further Management

### Looking for an Underlying Cause

Investigations to search for the underlying cause are not routinely needed after a first DVT. Your doctor will assess you with:

- A careful history and full physical examination.
- Routine blood tests (full blood count, kidney and liver function, calcium, electrolytes).
- Chest X-ray.
- In men aged over 50: PSA (prostate screening).
- Ensuring cancer screening (e.g., bowel screening, mammogram, cervical smear) is up to date.

Aggressive investigations for hidden cancer are not recommended.

### Thrombophilia Testing (Inherited Clotting Disorders)

Thrombophilia testing is not routinely done for a first DVT. Your doctor may arrange it only in specific situations, such as:
- Unprovoked clot in someone under 45 years old
- Strong family history of unprovoked clots
- Clot in an unusual site (e.g., brain, bowel, liver veins)
- If your doctor is uncertain, they will seek haematology advice before arranging tests

### Compression Stockings

Routine compression stockings to prevent post-thrombotic syndrome are **no longer recommended**. However, they may help relieve symptoms. Your doctor can advise.

### Mobilisation

Mobilising (getting moving) as soon as possible is encouraged.

### Long-Term Monitoring

If you are on long-term anticoagulation:
- Review every 12 months (or every 3–6 months if high risk, e.g., aged ≥ 65 years).
- Blood tests (FBC, creatinine, LFT) at reviews.
- A final review is arranged when you complete your anticoagulation course.

### DVT and Future Long-Haul Travel

If you have a history of DVT and are planning long-haul travel, discuss this with your doctor. General advice includes:
- Move around frequently during the flight.
- Stay well hydrated; avoid alcohol and sleeping tablets.
- If at increased risk (previous clot, cancer, recent surgery, pregnancy, thrombophilia, limited mobility) and not currently anticoagulated: consider below-knee graduated compression stockings (20–30 mmHg).
- If at low bleeding risk, your doctor may recommend 10 mg rivaroxaban on the day of travel, with a further 10 mg 24–48 hours later for flights of 8 hours or longer.
- If uncertain about risk level or medication choice, your doctor will seek thrombosis service advice.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor will refer you to or seek advice from specialists in the following situations:

- **Acute general medicine:** If PE is suspected, you are acutely unwell, or the ultrasound is delayed > 24 hours with any clinical concerns.
- **Thrombosis service or haematology:** Upper limb or proximal leg DVT; negative scan but high clinical suspicion; uncertainty about anticoagulation choice or duration; recurrent or unexplained DVT; bilateral leg swelling.
- **Non-acute haematology:** Unprovoked or recurrent DVT.
- **Non-acute thrombosis service:** Distal DVT or superficial thrombosis already on anticoagulation; recurrent or unexplained DVT not yet on treatment.
- **Non-acute vascular surgery:** Recurrent superficial thrombophlebitis with no DVT on scan.
- **Haematology or thrombosis service (pregnancy):** All DVTs in pregnancy — management is specialised.
- **Paediatric haematology (under 15 years):** Any suspected or confirmed DVT.
