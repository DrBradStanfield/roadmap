# Travel Consultation

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the pre-travel health consultation — preparing for international travel with appropriate vaccinations, medications, and risk management. Seek advice from a health professional **6 to 8 weeks before departure**.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will discuss your travel plans, including:

**Itinerary and activities**
- Dates, duration, and season of travel
- Countries and specific regions visited
- Rural or urban settings; type of accommodation
- Activities (altitude trekking, diving, rafting, animal contact, disaster relief)
- Mode of travel (cruise, cycling, air travel)
- General hygiene standards and healthcare access at destination

**Reason for travel**
- Tourism, business, visiting friends and relatives (VFR), volunteer or aid work, pilgrimage, or other

**Travel insurance**
- Your doctor will recommend travel insurance for all travellers
- Insurance should cover pre-existing conditions, planned activities, and pregnancy where relevant
- Check your policy for exclusions, medical evacuation, and repatriation
- Reciprocal healthcare agreements exist between some countries

**Travel advisories**
- Check current advisories before departure (SafeTravel, CDC, WHO, ProMED)

**Medical history review**
- Pregnancy or breastfeeding
- Disability or immunocompromise
- Older age or recent surgery
- Psychiatric conditions
- Diabetes (time zones and diet changes can affect diabetic control)
- Current medications and allergies

**Fitness to fly**
- Patients with unstable medical conditions or communicable disease (e.g., varicella, COVID-19, measles) should not fly
- Many airlines do not allow pregnant patients to fly after 36 weeks gestation (or 32 weeks for twin pregnancy or significant preterm labour risk)
- Patients with serious health conditions (cardiovascular, respiratory, neurological, severe psychiatric illness), or who need oxygen or CPAP, may need an airline medical clearance form

**Medications for travel**
- Carry prescription medications in original packaging in hand baggage
- Bring a copy of your prescription or a doctor's letter using generic drug names
- Carry enough medication for the whole trip split across two bags
- Medications, expressed breast milk, and infant formula are exempt from carry-on liquid restrictions — declare at security
- Check the laws of countries being visited regarding transporting medications

**Allergies**
- Your doctor will update your allergy action plan and may prescribe a spare autoinjector
- A travel plan stating autoinjectors are required in-flight should be provided

**Previous immunisation history**
- Review routine and travel vaccines received
- Malaria prophylaxis history and altitude experience

## Risk Management by Travel Type

Your doctor will address risks based on your specific itinerary:

### Altitude Sickness (Acute Mountain Sickness)

Relevant if travelling above 2,500 metres (8,000 feet). Commonly visited high-altitude destinations include Everest Base Camp, the Annapurna Circuit, Mount Kilimanjaro, the Inca Trail, Mount Fuji, and cities such as Lhasa, La Paz, Cusco, and Quito.

**Prevention:** Gradual ascent is the best prevention. Above 3,000 metres, sleep no more than 300–500 metres higher per night. Build in an acclimatisation day for each 1,000 metre gain.

**Your doctor may consider:** Acetazolamide (off-label) as prophylaxis in moderate or high-risk situations — 125 mg twice daily, starting one day before ascent and continuing at least two days after reaching maximum altitude. A trial dose before travel is recommended to check for side effects (diuresis, tingling, nausea, taste disturbance).

**Symptoms of Acute Mountain Sickness (AMS):**
- Headache (any headache at altitude must be assumed to be AMS)
- Loss of appetite, nausea or vomiting
- Fatigue, flu-like symptoms, dizziness
- Poor sleep and irregular breathing during sleep

**Serious altitude illness — descend immediately and seek urgent care:**
- High-Altitude Cerebral Oedema (HACE): severe headache, unsteadiness, vomiting, drowsiness, confusion or appearing intoxicated
- High-Altitude Pulmonary Oedema (HAPE): breathlessness at rest, extreme weakness, fast pulse, bluish skin colour (cyanosis)

**Key rules:** Never ascend to sleep at a higher altitude when experiencing any AMS symptoms. Descend to the last altitude where you felt well if symptoms worsen at rest.

---

### Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) and Pulmonary Embolism (PE)

Risk is increased for 2 weeks after a long-haul flight of 6 or more hours.

**All passengers should:**
- Drink plenty of non-alcoholic fluids during flights
- Move their ankles and stretch the calves at least hourly
- Wear loose, non-restrictive clothing
- Seek medical advice for calf pain after flight

**Higher-risk patients** (previous DVT/PE, active cancer, surgery within the past 4–6 weeks):
- Your doctor may consider below-knee graduated compression stockings (20–30 mmHg)
- If at low bleeding risk, your doctor may consider rivaroxaban 10 mg on the day of travel, with a further 10 mg 24–48 hours later for flights of 8 or more hours, then 10 mg once daily for two days after arrival
- Declare any history of DVT, PE, or thrombophilia to your travel insurer

---

### Travellers' Diarrhoea

Travellers' diarrhoea is common and usually self-limiting. Oral rehydration is the primary treatment.

**Prevention:** Follow food and water precautions. Bismuth (Pepto-Bismol) is an effective non-antibiotic option for prevention — avoid if taking aspirin, warfarin, or if you have a salicylate allergy.

**Your doctor may prescribe before travel:**
- Loperamide (antidiarrhoeal)
- Oral rehydration sachets

**Management by severity:**

*Mild to moderate (tolerable or distressing but not incapacitating):*
- Start oral rehydration therapy (WHO formula: 6 teaspoons sugar + half teaspoon salt per 1 litre water; or dilute sports drink 50:50 with water)
- Loperamide 4 mg immediately, then 2 mg after each loose motion, up to 8 mg in 24 hours
- Do not use loperamide if there is bloody diarrhoea or fever

*Severe (incapacitating):*
- Start oral rehydration therapy
- Seek medical consultation
- Avoid loperamide if bloody diarrhoea
- Your doctor may consider azithromycin (off-label): 1,000 mg as a single dose or 500 mg once daily for 3 days — do not self-purchase antibiotics without medical advice
- Seek medical advice if symptoms persist on return

---

### Diving

Some medical conditions increase diving risk, including diabetes, asthma, COPD, history of spontaneous pneumothorax, coronary artery disease, arrhythmias, cardiomyopathy, seizures, and claustrophobia. Your doctor may seek specialist advice if any of these apply.

Consider obtaining specific diving insurance (e.g., DAN — Dive Accident Insurance).

---

### Jet Lag

Commonly affects travellers crossing more than 3 time zones, particularly eastbound travel.

- Avoid coffee and alcohol; drink plenty of fluids
- Your doctor may consider melatonin or short-acting hypnotics in some circumstances

---

### Malaria Prophylaxis

No antimalarial medicine is completely effective — avoiding mosquito bites from dusk to dawn is essential.

**Personal protective measures:** Activity timing, long and light-coloured clothing, insect repellent, treated mosquito nets, avoiding perfume and aftershave.

**Prophylaxis options your doctor may prescribe:**
- Doxycycline (funded, off-label)
- Atovaquone + proguanil (Malarone, unfunded)
- Mefloquine or tafenoquine (off-label, unfunded — usually from travel clinics)
- Hydroxychloroquine (funded — effective only in Haiti)

Malaria prophylaxis recommendations vary by country and risk level. Your doctor will tailor advice to your specific itinerary.

**Important:** Migrants returning to visit friends and relatives in malarious areas are at high risk — prior immunity is lost rapidly after leaving an endemic area.

If travelling to a remote, high-risk location, your doctor may discuss emergency standby treatment (e.g., Riamet/artemether-lumefantrine).

Seek prompt medical care for any fever during or after travel in a malarious area.

---

### Motion Sickness

- Avoid activities requiring close concentration (e.g., reading) if prone to motion sickness
- Avoid large meals before or during the journey
- Your doctor may prescribe prophylactic medications for children and adults

---

### Parasites

- Avoid walking barefoot — parasites can enter intact or damaged skin
- Avoid wading or swimming in fresh water in areas with risk of schistosomiasis or leptospirosis

---

### Pregnancy

- Carry your antenatal record when travelling
- Air travel is generally safest in the second trimester for healthy women with uncomplicated pregnancies
- Air travel at 34–37 weeks is associated with increased risk of preterm birth — check airline restrictions
- Some medical conditions are contraindications to travel during pregnancy
- Venous thrombosis risk is increased in pregnancy during long-haul travel — use support stockings, move regularly, stay hydrated, avoid alcohol
- Travel to malaria-risk areas should be avoided if possible during pregnancy — malaria is associated with miscarriage, preterm birth, low birth weight, and maternal/neonatal death
- Live vaccines (MMR, BCG, yellow fever) are generally contraindicated in pregnancy
- Pregnant women or those planning pregnancy should delay travel to Zika-affected areas
- Pregnant women travelling to South America should be advised of oropouche virus risk — use protection against midge and mosquito bites

---

### Rabies

- 99% of human rabies cases result from dog bites
- Avoid all contact with potential animal hosts (dogs, bats, raccoons, monkeys, foxes, cats, cattle, horses)
- If bitten, scratched, or licked on the face by a potential animal host:
  - Wash the wound immediately for at least 5–10 minutes with running water and apply antiseptic (e.g., Povidone-iodine)
  - Seek medical assistance as soon as possible and within 7 days for Human Rabies Immunoglobulin (HRIG) and vaccination if indicated
- Your doctor may consider pre-travel rabies vaccination

---

### Sexual Health and Blood-Borne Pathogens

- Use condoms to reduce the risk of sexually transmitted infections — consider bringing condoms from home to ensure quality
- Avoid tattoos or acupuncture in facilities with uncertain hygiene practices
- Your doctor may consider hepatitis B vaccination and mpox vaccination
- For patients at moderate to high risk of HIV, your doctor may discuss pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP)

---

### Vector-Borne Diseases (Dengue, Yellow Fever, Zika, Chikungunya, Japanese Encephalitis)

- Use insect repellent at all times — day-feeding mosquitoes transmit dengue, chikungunya, yellow fever, and Zika; evening/night-feeding ones transmit Japanese encephalitis and malaria
- Day-biting mosquito protection is essential in dengue-endemic areas

**Dengue (January 2026 alert):** There is an ongoing dengue outbreak in the Pacific, particularly the Cook Islands, Samoa, American Samoa, Kiribati, Nauru, and Tuvalu. Advise mosquito-bite prevention. If you develop fever within 3 weeks of returning from the Pacific or any dengue-endemic region, seek medical review promptly.

**Zika virus:**
- Pregnant women, or those planning pregnancy, should delay travel to Zika-affected areas
- After travel to a Zika-affected area:
  - Men: use condoms or abstain from sex for 6 months; avoid getting partner pregnant for 6 months; do not donate sperm for 6 months
  - Women: use barrier methods or abstain from sex for 2 months after return; if male partner also travelled to a Zika-affected area, avoid pregnancy for 6 months
- Your doctor can advise on Zika testing if pregnancy is unavoidable

**Oropouche virus:** Travellers to South America should adopt protection against midges and mosquito bites.

---

### Visiting Friends and Relatives (VFR)

VFR travellers and their children are at significantly increased risk of travel-related illness due to longer stays, greater exposure to local conditions, and reduced awareness of preventive measures.

- VFR travellers have 8–10 times the malaria risk of standard tourists
- Higher risk of STIs, typhoid, TB, and hepatitis A (especially for New Zealand-born children)
- Greater risk of food- and water-borne illness due to social pressure to eat local food

Discuss pre-travel care proactively with patients planning to visit friends and relatives overseas.

## Vaccinations

Your doctor will review and discuss routine, recommended, and required vaccines, balancing risks and costs.

**Routine vaccines:** Ensure up to date, especially MMR. COVID-19 and influenza are the most common vaccine-preventable diseases in travellers. Babies aged 4–12 months can receive a funded MMR vaccine before travel to areas of measles risk.

**Recommended vaccines** (based on destination): May include hepatitis A, Japanese encephalitis, rabies, typhoid, yellow fever, and cholera. Dengue, tick-borne encephalitis, and chikungunya vaccines are available overseas.

**Required vaccines:** Yellow fever and polio vaccinations are required in some countries, including stopovers. Meningococcal vaccination is required for Hajj and Umrah.

Yellow fever vaccination must be administered at an authorised yellow fever vaccination centre.

## First Aid Kit

Your doctor may recommend preparing a travel first aid kit including:

- Alcohol-based hand rub
- Analgesics/antipyretics
- Antidiarrhoeal medication (e.g., loperamide)
- Antifungal cream
- Antiseptic cream and cleanser
- Bite and burns cream
- Condoms
- COVID-19 RAT tests
- Decongestant or saline nasal spray
- Dressings and bandages
- Ear plugs for in-flight use (e.g., Ear Planes)
- Insect repellent
- Mild steroid cream
- Motion sickness medication
- Rehydration salts
- Scissors and safety pins
- Sunscreen
- Tape
- Thermometer
- Water purification tablets

---

## Management Notes

Where possible, your doctor will provide written materials about insurance, safety, insect bite avoidance, food and water precautions, and other relevant topics. This may require two or more appointments.

Contact your doctor or seek medical care if you become unwell during travel or on return.
