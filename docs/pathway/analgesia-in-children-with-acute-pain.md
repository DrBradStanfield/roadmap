# Analgesia in Children with Acute Pain

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: December 2021.*

This pathway covers pain relief for children with acute (short-term) pain, such as injuries, burns, and painful procedures.

> **Always discuss your child's symptoms and any medicines with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

- If your child is injured and the pain keeps going despite pain relief by mouth, or seems bad enough to need strong (opioid) pain relief, see a doctor promptly; the doctor may call an ambulance.
- If a child who has been given an opioid becomes very drowsy or their breathing slows, this can be life-threatening. Call 111 or go to the emergency department.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor may:
- Estimate how severe the pain is, based on how the injury happened, how long the pain is expected to last, how it is expected to progress, and the pain level.
- Check the pain before and after pain relief, to see whether it is working.
- Use non-drug pain strategies, including explanation, relaxation, distraction, and splinting.

---

## Management

### General principles

- Pain relief should start as early as possible.
- Children with acute injuries, such as fractures or burns, should always be given pain relief before X-rays, dressings, or transfer to hospital.
- **Codeine is not recommended in children.**
- Pain relief follows a step-up approach (the WHO analgesic pain ladder). Each step adds a medicine to the one used at the level before.
- If a child's weight is not known, your doctor may estimate it for children aged 1 to 8 years: weight (kg) = (2 × age in years) + 10. This is called the "best guess formula".

### Mild pain: paracetamol

**Your doctor may consider paracetamol by mouth:**
- 15 mg/kg (to a maximum of 1 g per dose) every 4 hours, up to 4 times per day (to a maximum of 60 mg/kg/day).
- To avoid dosing errors, your doctor may prescribe only the 250 mg/5 mL strength and supply a syringe for accurate measuring.

**Paracetamol suppositories (rectal):**
- The dose is the same as by mouth, rounded to the nearest suppository size.
- It is slower to work (2 to 4 hours) than paracetamol by mouth (about 20 minutes).
- The maximum is still 60 mg/kg/day, and no more than the normal adult dose of 1 g per dose for ongoing doses.

### Moderate pain: adding ibuprofen or tramadol

Your doctor may consider adding these to paracetamol.

**Ibuprofen by mouth:**
- Only for children aged more than 3 months.
- 6 mg/kg (to a maximum of 400 mg per dose) every 6 to 8 hours (to a maximum of 3 doses per day).
- Local specialists recommend 6 mg/kg rather than the standard 5 to 10 mg/kg dose because it makes dosing easier. The volume per dose of ibuprofen (100 mg/5 mL strength) then matches paracetamol (250 mg/5 mL strength), which may reduce dosing errors. For example, a 20 kg child needs 6 ml of each.
- Avoid in hypovolaemia (low body fluid volume).
- Use with caution in children with brittle asthma, as it may bring on wheeze.

**Tramadol:**
- Not for children aged less than 12 years.
- Can be added to other pain relief for moderate pain, but is not suitable on its own.
- 1 to 2 mg/kg (to a maximum of 100 mg per dose) every 6 hours.
- A reduced dose (0.5 to 1 mg/kg, to a maximum of 100 mg, every 6 to 8 hours) is used after tonsillectomy, adenoidectomy, or throat surgery.
- Given as a 10 mg/mL liquid made up by pharmacists.

### Severe pain: opioids

Your doctor may consider these for severe pain.

**Intranasal fentanyl (into the nose):**
- Only for children more than 10 kg.
- Children more than 10 kg and less than 15 kg get 20 micrograms.
- The usual dose is 1.5 micrograms/kg (to a maximum of 100 micrograms), given with a nasal atomiser.

**Morphine by mouth:**
- 200 micrograms/kg (0.2 mg/kg), to a maximum of 10 mg per dose.
- Not for children aged less than 2 years.
- The child must be alert, with a breathing rate for their age of:
  - 2 to 8 years: at least 14 breaths per minute
  - Over 8 years: at least 12 breaths per minute
- It is likely to work slowly and unpredictably, and is not suitable on its own.

**Intravenous (IV) morphine:**
- Useful for children with severe injuries or in significant pain.
- Best given by experienced clinicians, and only if the child can be closely monitored for slowed breathing (respiratory depression).
- Naloxone can reverse too much sedation or life-threatening slowed breathing. Its effect is shorter than most opioids, so the child is observed closely.

### Numbing creams and gels for painful procedures

For more painful procedures, such as blood tests (venepuncture), drips (cannula insertion), and wound repair, your doctor may consider a numbing (topical anaesthetic) option:

**Lidocaine + prilocaine cream:**
- Applied as a thick layer to intact skin at least one hour (or up to 2 hours) before the procedure. It is not rubbed in, and is covered with a dressing.
- Numbs to a depth of 3 to 5 mm for up to 2 hours.
- For children with atopic dermatitis, it is applied for 15 to 30 minutes before the procedure.
- Local reactions (paleness, redness, swelling) are common but pass.
- Too much prilocaine absorbed can cause methaemoglobinaemia. It is not applied to broken skin, burns, mucous membranes, or areas of eczema. Infants can also absorb more.
- It may be applied to small, shallow cuts to help with cleaning or gluing.

**Lidocaine 4% cream:**
- For children aged 1 year or more.
- 1 to 2.5 g is applied to cover a 2.5 cm × 2.5 cm area where the needle will go, then covered with a dressing.
- It usually works after 30 minutes.
- It may be difficult to obtain, so arrange it in advance.

**Tetracaine 4% gel:**
- Applied under a dressing for 30 minutes before a blood test, or 45 minutes before a drip is inserted.
- Not funded. Available from pharmacies. It may be difficult to obtain, so arrange it in advance.

**Lidocaine, tetracaine, and adrenaline solution:**
- A numbing solution for open wounds or mucous membranes. It is not used on fingers or toes.
- Useful to reduce discomfort before cleaning wounds that don't need stitches, stitching small wounds without injected local anaesthetic, or injecting local anaesthetic into larger wounds.
- The solution is warmed to room temperature to reduce stinging, and the wound is gently rinsed first.
- A gauze soaked in the solution (maximum dose 0.1 mL/kg) is placed over the wound under a dressing for 20 minutes before the procedure. The skin edges go pale when it is working.

All local anaesthetics, including creams and gels, can cause serious toxicity. Safe maximum doses must not be exceeded.

## Key Concepts for Parents

- Start pain relief as early as possible.
- Codeine is not recommended in children.
- Measure liquid medicines with a syringe, and never go over the maximum daily dose.
- Stronger pain relief is added step by step, on top of the medicines already given.
- Always discuss any new symptoms or medicine questions with your doctor.
