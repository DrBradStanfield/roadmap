# Lead Poisoning

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of lead exposure — who should be tested, what the results mean, and how lead levels are managed.

---

## Background

- Lead is a naturally occurring heavy metal found in homes, workplaces, and the environment.
- There is no known safe level of lead exposure.
- Lead is bioaccumulative — low-level, prolonged exposure over time can result in significant concentration in the body.
- Young children (under 5 years), people with pica, and pregnant women are especially vulnerable. Lead exposure in children can cause irreversible neurobehavioural and developmental effects.
- Common sources of exposure in New Zealand include lead-based paint, occupational exposure (painters, smelting/metal refinery workers), and contaminated soil.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department immediately if lead encephalopathy or acute poisoning is suspected:

- Severe confusion or altered consciousness
- Convulsions or seizures
- Collapse or unresponsive
- Acute illness in someone with known or suspected significant lead exposure

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history to identify potential sources of lead exposure and whether you are in a higher-risk group.

### Sources of lead exposure include:

- Lead-based paint (especially in homes built before 1980)
- Old or imported painted toys
- Shooting or smelting lead bullets
- Contaminated soil (near old paint, industrial sites, or mining)
- Imported traditional medicines (e.g., Ayurvedic, Chinese), cosmetics, and jewellery
- Old household pipes soldered with lead
- Rainwater tanks contaminated with lead dust

### High-risk activities:

- Renovating a house built before 1980
- Painting
- Occupations: mining, smelting, soldering, automotive repair, battery manufacturing, metal recycling
- Hobbies: target or indoor rifle shooting, pottery glazing, stained glass making, making fishing sinkers
- Pica (eating non-food substances)
- Use of unregulated cosmetics, supplements, or traditional medicines
- Children playing with old or overseas-manufactured toys

### Higher-risk groups:

- Pregnant women (lead crosses the placenta — risk of stillbirth, miscarriage, premature birth, low birth weight)
- Infants and children under 5 years (irreversible neurodevelopmental effects)
- People with pica behaviour
- Adult males (higher rates of lead absorption than females)
- Pacific males (triple the notification rate compared to other ethnic groups in New Zealand)

### Signs and symptoms:

Most people are asymptomatic or have non-specific symptoms, especially early on. Lead poisoning can be difficult to diagnose on symptoms alone.

**Nervous system:**
- Mood changes (depression, irritability)
- Developmental delay (in children)
- Memory impairment and concentration difficulties
- Sleep disturbance
- Headaches
- Tingling and numbness in fingers and hands
- Muscle weakness or wrist drop
- Seizures (rare)

**Gastrointestinal:**
- Lack of appetite, nausea
- Diarrhoea or constipation
- Stomach pains
- Weight loss

**Other effects:**
- Kidney damage and increased blood pressure
- Decreased sperm count and quality
- Miscarriage
- Anaemia

**At higher blood lead levels (BLL 3.38 micromol/L or higher in adults; 2.17 micromol/L or higher in children and pregnant women):**
- Irritability, agitation, confusion
- Poor attention span, headaches
- Uncoordinated movement, drowsiness
- Convulsions, coma

## Testing — Blood Lead Levels (BLL)

Your doctor may order a blood lead level (BLL) test if you have risk factors or symptoms. BLL testing is indicated in:

- Patients with risk factors who have not been previously screened
- Children under 5 years and pregnant patients with a household member who has BLL ≥0.24 micromol/L
- Children aged 9–48 months who live in or regularly visit pre-1945 housing with peeling paint, have been present during renovations of older painted homes, have parents with occupational exposure, or live near lead-smelting sites or historically heavy-traffic roads
- Children under 18 years with pica, siblings with elevated BLL, or other significant exposure
- Anyone with symptoms and high-risk activities or exposure

**Important limitations of BLL testing:**
- BLL measures lead in the blood only — it does not detect lead stored in bones, teeth, or organs.
- In most cases, BLL alone will not be diagnostic of a specific clinical problem.
- In children with developmental or behavioural problems, elevated BLL likely contributes to (rather than solely causes) those problems.

---

## Management

Management depends on the blood lead level. Your doctor will guide this.

### Any detectable lead level

Your doctor will:
- Advise that there is no safe level of lead in the blood, and that adverse health effects can occur even below the notification threshold
- Advise on avoiding high-risk activities (see above)
- Advise on reducing potential lead exposure (see below)
- Arrange BLL testing for household members under 5 years or who are pregnant
- Inform your lead maternity carer (LMC) if you are pregnant
- Notify Public Health as required (this is done automatically by the laboratory in New Zealand if BLL ≥0.24 micromol/L)

### Reducing potential lead exposure

- In homes built before 1980: frequently wash hands with soap and water; regularly clean floors, stairs, and windowsills; keep children away from peeling paint and bare soil; regularly wash pets and toys
- During renovations of pre-1980 homes: ensure pregnant women and young children are not present during paint removal
- Remove old painted or imported toys, recalled toys, and old painted cots from children
- Consider soil testing if contamination is suspected

### Ensuring good nutrition

Children deficient in iron or calcium are more susceptible to lead toxicity:
- Iron-sufficient diets reduce lead absorption
- Calcium competes with lead and can inhibit its absorption

### Management by BLL range

Your doctor may consider the following approach based on BLL:

| BLL (micromol/L) | Your doctor may consider |
|---|---|
| Less than 0.24 | Not notifiable; no monitoring required. Advise on exposure reduction if relevant. |
| 0.24–0.47 | Repeat BLL in 3 months. If still ≥0.24, repeat at 6 months then annually. |
| 0.48–0.71 | Repeat BLL in 6 weeks. If still ≥0.24, repeat at 6 months then annually. |
| 0.72–0.95 | In children under 16: test for iron deficiency anaemia; seek paediatric advice about neurodevelopmental testing. Repeat BLL in 6 weeks. |
| 0.96–2.16 | In children under 16: seek acute paediatric medicine advice about general management and possible neurodevelopmental testing. Repeat BLL in 6 weeks. |
| 2.17 or higher (child) | Seek acute paediatric medicine advice and arrange assessment and management as guided. |
| 2.17–3.39 (pregnant) | Urgent obstetric assessment; seek clinical toxicologist advice (National Poisons Centre). |
| 3.4 or higher (adult 16+) | Seek acute general medicine advice about the need for acute assessment and **chelation therapy**. If admitted, the team will consult directly with a clinical toxicologist. |

**Note:** BLL ≥0.24 micromol/L is legally notifiable. In New Zealand, the laboratory automatically notifies Public Health when this threshold is met, provided clinical information is included on the request form. The Public Health Unit will contact the patient directly to provide remediation and abatement advice.

### Chelation therapy

Chelation therapy may be considered at very high BLL (≥3.4 micromol/L in adults). This is a specialist-initiated treatment. If given, monitoring frequency will be coordinated by the treating specialist.

---

## What to Expect After Reducing Exposure

- Blood lead levels will slowly decrease over the following months.
- After abatement (lead removal from the environment), BLL may temporarily rise — this happens because disturbed lead can enter the bloodstream during clean-up.
- If BLL remains above 0.24 micromol/L after 3–6 months, or rises again, further investigation will be needed.
