# Varicose Veins and Chronic Venous Insufficiency

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: February 2020.*

Varicose veins (also called varices or varicosities) are swollen, twisted veins, often found on the lower legs and feet. Chronic venous insufficiency happens when the valves in the leg veins do not close properly (often showing as varicose veins), or when major veins are blocked. This causes long-term high pressure in the leg veins.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any treatment changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to See Your Doctor Promptly

Your doctor may arrange an **urgent** vascular surgery assessment if:
- You have varicose veins or venous disease together with poor circulation in the arteries of the leg (peripheral arterial disease) **and** a leg ulcer.
- An ankle brachial pressure index (ABPI) test is less than 0.5, which indicates severe arterial disease.

---

## Background

- Varicose veins run in families.
- Factors that can make them worse include pregnancy, smoking, a previous blood clot (venous thromboembolism), and obesity.
- Venous disease and venous ulcers cause considerable illness and cost. This can be prevented or reduced with correct management.

---

## Assessment

### History

Your doctor may ask about:
- Infections, including cellulitis.
- Thrombophlebitis or deep vein thrombosis (DVT) in the calf or thigh.
- Skin changes, including brown pigmentation and varicose (gravitational) eczema.
- Bleeding and ulcers.
- Whether you want treatment for appearance (cosmetic treatment).

### Examination

Signs your doctor may look for:
- **Varicose veins** in the great and small saphenous veins.
- **Ankle flare:** tiny varicose veins on the inner foot and ankle.
- **Atrophie blanche:** lacy white areas of skin with tiny red spots.
- **Haemosiderin staining:** brown discolouration of the skin. It can be small patches, or can cover most of the inner leg between the ankle and knee. This is called the gaiter region, where 95% of venous ulcers occur.
- **Lipodermatosclerosis:** hard, woody, scarred skin on the inner or outer ankle. When extensive, the leg looks like an upside-down champagne bottle.
- **Varicose (gravitational) eczema**, also called venous eczema. It can be wet or dry.
- **Venous oedema:** swelling of the lower leg that leaves a dent when pressed.
- **Venous ulcers:** usually on the inner leg or gaiter region, with sloped edges.

### Severity (CEAP classification)

Your doctor may grade the severity of venous disease using the CEAP classification:
- C0: No visible or palpable signs of venous disease
- C1: Telangiectases or reticular veins
- C2: Varicose veins
- C3: Oedema (swelling)
- C4: Pigmentation, eczema, lipodermatosclerosis, atrophie blanche
- C5: Healed venous ulcer
- C6: Active venous ulcer

Each grade is also marked as asymptomatic (CA) or symptomatic (CS).

Your doctor will also consider:
- Whether your symptoms can be controlled with medical management, including compression hosiery.
- Whether you can carry out normal activities with conservative measures.
- Your body mass index (BMI). A BMI more than 35 affects management options and the success of surgery on the superficial veins.

### Investigations

**Your doctor may consider:**
- **Ankle brachial pressure index (ABPI):** compares the blood pressure at the ankle with the arm, using a blood pressure cuff and a handheld Doppler device. It shows whether significant arterial disease is present. It is measured only if arterial disease is suspected from absent pulses and compression hosiery is being prescribed.
  - ABPI less than 0.9 is abnormal. With intermittent claudication, it is usually between 0.5 and 0.9.
  - ABPI less than 0.5 indicates severe arterial disease and needs urgent vascular surgery assessment.
  - In people with heavily calcified arteries, the ABPI will be more than 1.2 and should be disregarded.
- **Duplex ultrasound** to assess backflow in the veins (venous reflux). This is needed only if vein ablation therapy is being considered. The vascular surgeon will arrange it as part of the referral.

## Management

### Uncomplicated varicose veins

If varicose veins cause no symptoms and no complications, they are harmless. Early treatment is not needed, other than for appearance. Raise your legs when resting.

### Compression hosiery

Your doctor may prescribe compression hosiery if appropriate, taking the ABPI into account. Points to know:
- Compression in people with known or suspected arterial disease (particularly with diabetes) can cause complications.
- Stockings are expensive and do not suit everyone.
- Unusually shaped legs need experienced fitting.
- Swollen legs shrink with compression, so smaller hosiery will be needed.
- Elderly people and people with arthritis cannot manage hosiery.

If your BMI is more than 40, you are best managed by a specialist nursing team, which can provide compression after an ABPI check.

### Controlling swelling

- Raise the foot of the bed, and raise the leg high when resting.
- Simple elevation is an essential part of managing venous (stasis) ulcers.
- Walking is encouraged if you are also wearing graduated elastic compression.

### Lifestyle

Lifestyle changes should include:
- Exercise to improve the calf muscle pump.
- Weight management.
- Stopping smoking.
- Leg elevation.

### Varicose eczema

Your doctor may consider soap substitutes, regular moisturisers (emollients), and topical corticosteroids, as for other eczema.

### Ulcers and wounds

If ulcers or wounds are difficult to treat, your doctor may refer you for specialised wound care assessment.

### Before a referral for surgery

- A trial of compression therapy for at least 3 months, to control swelling and improve symptoms. Evidence is limited, and 25% of patients cannot tolerate compression.
- If your BMI is more than 40, your doctor may first refer you to a specialist nursing team for compression management after an ABPI check. This can speed up ulcer healing.

### Vein ablation therapy

Options include:
- **Chemical ablation (sclerotherapy)**, including ultrasound-guided sclerotherapy.
- **Thermal (endovenous) ablation:** laser or radio-frequency ablation, and other technologies such as vein glue.
- **Surgical ablation** (open varicose vein surgery).

What the evidence shows:
- Long-term results of thermal ablation are equivalent to surgery, with lower morbidity, less pain, and an earlier return to work.
- Thermal and surgical ablation both have a short recovery time and reduced complications. Radio-frequency ablation appears less painful than surgery.
- Chemical ablation often needs to be repeated, and recurrence rates are high.
- Surgical and thermal ablation work better and are more cost-effective than compression or chemical ablation.
- Superficial vein treatment in people with a BMI more than 35 has limited short-term benefit.

The vascular surgeon can advise which option is appropriate for you.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you for a vascular surgery assessment:
- **Urgently**, if you also have peripheral arterial disease and a leg ulcer.
- If you have peripheral arterial disease as well.
- To consider vein ablation therapy (and a duplex ultrasound) if you have:
  - Venous hyperpigmentation.
  - Varicose eczema.
  - Thrombophlebitis.
  - Bleeding varicose veins.
  - Venous ulcers (healed or active).

## Key Concepts for Patients

- Varicose veins without symptoms or complications are harmless.
- Leg elevation, exercise, weight management and stopping smoking are part of managing venous disease.
- Compression hosiery can help but does not suit everyone. Your doctor will check it is safe for your circulation first.
- Skin changes, eczema, bleeding veins or ulcers are reasons to ask your doctor about specialist assessment.
- Always discuss any new symptoms or treatment questions with your doctor.
