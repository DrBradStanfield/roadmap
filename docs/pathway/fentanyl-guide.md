# Fentanyl Guide (Palliative Care)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This guide applies to adults. Fentanyl is a controlled drug that must be prescribed and managed by a doctor. All prescribing decisions should be discussed with your doctor or palliative care team.

---

## What Is Fentanyl?

Fentanyl is a synthetic opioid approximately **100 times more potent** than oral morphine. It has high fat solubility and is absorbed quickly through skin and mucous membranes.

- **Fast-acting forms** (sublingual, intranasal, buccal, injection): onset within minutes, effective for about 1 hour — useful for breakthrough pain or pain that comes on quickly
- **Patches (transdermal):** slower onset — at least 12 hours to reach peak effect — for continuous background pain control

---

## Fentanyl Patches — Key Points

### Who should NOT start fentanyl patches immediately

Your doctor will not start patches in patients who:
- Are opioid-naïve (have not previously taken regular opioids)
- Have unstable or poorly controlled pain

In these cases, a short-acting opioid should be used for at least a few days first, or specialist palliative care advice sought.

### Important properties of fentanyl patches

- **Safe in renal failure** — unlike morphine, fentanyl does not accumulate in kidney disease
- **12.5 micrograms/hour patch ≈ 30 mg oral morphine per 24 hours**
- Takes **12 to 18 hours to reach peak effect** — a short-acting opioid is needed to cover this gap when starting
- Changed every **72 hours** — if pain worsens on day 3, this usually means the dose needs increasing, not that the patch is faulty
- After removal, a depot of fentanyl remains in the skin — blood levels take approximately **17 hours to fall by 50%**

### Breakthrough medication

Any short-acting opioid can be used for breakthrough pain alongside a fentanyl patch. Your doctor will choose based on your kidney function and other factors:

- **Avoid oral morphine** in poor renal function
- **Oxycodone** is acceptable in mild to moderate renal impairment
- **Intranasal, sublingual, or buccal fentanyl** are available but limited by volume (dilute solution: 100 micrograms per 2 mL)

## Intranasal Fentanyl

Intranasal fentanyl is used off-label (not officially approved by the medicines regulator for this route). It must be arranged through the palliative care team.

**Key facts:**
- Made by compounding pharmacies — no commercial preparation available in New Zealand
- Useful for breakthrough pain, especially incident pain (pain on movement)
- Short-acting (up to 60 minutes)
- Not suitable for patients needing moderate or high breakthrough doses
- Not funded — may cost approximately $20
- Must be prescribed on a Controlled Drug form (H572)

**How to use:**
- Prime the bottle once at first use (spray until mist appears)
- With head upright, insert nozzle into nostril and press the pump firmly — do not sniff
- Do not tilt head back while spraying
- Give 1 spray at a time into each nostril
- Do not blow nose for 1 hour after a dose

## Calculating the Fentanyl Patch Dose

Your doctor calculates the correct patch dose using the **Morphine Equivalent Daily Dose (MEDD)** — the total oral morphine equivalent taken in the past 24 hours. This requires the opioid dose to have been stable for at least 3 to 5 days.

### MEDD Conversion Table

| Oral morphine (mg per 24 hours) | Fentanyl patch (micrograms per hour) |
|---------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Less than 60 | 12.5 |
| 60 to 134 | 25 |
| 135 to 224 | 50 |
| 225 to 314 | 75 |
| 315 to 404 | 100 |
| 405 to 494 | 125 |
| 495 to 584 | 150 |
| 585 to 674 | 175 |
| 675 to 764 | 200 |
| 765 to 854 | 225 |
| 855 to 944 | 250 |
| 945 to 1,034 | 275 |
| 1,035 to 1,124 | 300 |

If the dose needed is not available in a single patch, multiple patches can be combined (e.g. 100 + 75 micrograms/hour to give 175 micrograms/hour).

Dose adjustments should use immediate-release opioids alongside the patch — patches alone cannot be titrated quickly, as they take several days to reach steady state.

### Switching from morphine to fentanyl

Switching can cause **opioid withdrawal symptoms** despite adequate pain relief — shivering, diarrhoea, bowel cramps, nausea, sweating, restlessness. Your doctor will prescribe a short-acting opioid as needed to manage these symptoms for a few days.

## Fentanyl at the End of Life

When a patient is deteriorating:

- **If already using a fentanyl patch:** leave in place and continue changing every 72 hours. Additional analgesia is given on top — either orally (with another opioid) or via a subcutaneous fentanyl infusion.
- **Transdermal and subcutaneous fentanyl are equivalent in dose** (1:1 bioavailability).

### Fentanyl via syringe driver (continuous subcutaneous infusion)

Your doctor will calculate and prescribe this. General starting points:

- **Opioid-naïve patients:** 100 micrograms fentanyl per 24 hours (equivalent to approximately 10 mg oral morphine per 24 hours)
- **Breakthrough dose:** 25 to 50 micrograms per hour as needed (equivalent to 2.5 to 5 mg oral morphine)
- **Titration:** increase in increments of 200 to 300 micrograms per 24 hours
- **Upper limit:** switch to or add another opioid if exceeding 2,100 micrograms per 24 hours (due to syringe volume constraints)

Seek palliative care advice if guidance is needed, or if a subcutaneous bolus dose of more than 100 micrograms is required.

---

## Working with Your Doctor

Fentanyl is a powerful opioid that requires careful prescribing, dose calculation, and monitoring. Your doctor will manage initiation and titration, and will involve the palliative care team for complex situations. Do not adjust patch doses or breakthrough doses without medical advice.
