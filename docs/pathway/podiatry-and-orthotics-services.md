# Podiatry and Orthotics Services

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers what podiatrists treat, when foot problems need urgent attention, and how orthotics services are accessed.

---

## What Is Podiatry?

Podiatrists are lower limb specialists trained in the prevention, diagnosis, treatment, and rehabilitation of foot and lower limb conditions. They can:

- Treat problematic toenail conditions including ingrown, thickened, and fungal nails
- Differentiate between and treat corns, callus, and verrucae
- Perform minor surgery for nail problems and verrucae
- Manage musculoskeletal injuries of the foot and lower leg (e.g., shin pain, anterior knee pain)
- Provide specialist treatment for heel pain, arch pain, and forefoot pain
- Diagnose and manage paediatric foot conditions (e.g., intoeing, curly toes, heel pain)
- Manage foot conditions for people with diabetes through monitoring, treatment, and education
- Manage the arthritic foot with orthotic therapy for shock and pressure absorption
- Manage mild to moderate knee osteoarthritis through exercise and shoe or orthotic interventions
- Manufacture prefabricated and custom-made orthoses
- Prescribe footwear and provide gait re-education

A small number of suitably qualified podiatrists — podiatric surgeons — perform bone surgery on the foot and ankle.

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Care

Contact your doctor or go to the emergency department promptly if you have:

**Severe or spreading foot infection**, which includes:
- Rapid deterioration of an ulcer
- Deep abscess
- Cellulitis of the foot or ankle region
- Feeling systemically unwell (fever, rigors, confusion)

**Critical limb ischaemia** — absent peripheral pulses with any of:
- Persistent rest pain requiring regular analgesia for more than 2 weeks
- Ulceration
- Gangrene of the foot or toes

**Suspected acute Charcot foot** — unexplained hot, swollen foot with or without pain (especially in someone with diabetes or peripheral neuropathy)

## Diabetic Foot Disease — Risk Stratification

Your doctor will assess diabetic foot risk to determine the right level of care.

### Active Foot Disease (Highest Priority)

Criteria include:
- Active foot ulceration or open wound
- Unexplained hot, swollen foot (suspected acute Charcot foot)

Your doctor will refer you to specialist diabetic podiatry services. If you also have severe infection or signs of critical ischaemia, urgent vascular surgery assessment is needed first.

### High-Risk Foot Disease

Criteria — any of the following:

**In remission (previous serious problem):**
- Previous amputation
- Previous foot ulcer
- Consolidated Charcot foot

**Or 2 or more of these risk factors:**
- Loss of sensation
- Peripheral arterial disease
- Significant callus
- Significant deformity
- Pre-ulcerative lesion
- eGFR less than 15 (CKD stage 5)
- Māori ethnicity

Your doctor may consider referring you to a specialist diabetic podiatry or PHO diabetes high-risk foot scheme.

### Moderate-Risk Foot Disease

Criteria — one of the following risk factors:
- Loss of sensation
- Peripheral arterial disease (more than one pulse absent, claudication, pallor, dependent rubor, or previous lower limb arterial interventions)
- Significant callus
- Significant deformity
- Pre-ulcerative lesion
- eGFR less than 15 (CKD stage 5)

Your doctor may consider referral to community podiatry via a PHO diabetes foot scheme.

### Low-Risk Foot Disease

No risk factors present — normal sensation, intact pulses, no peripheral arterial disease signs. Your doctor will provide advice on ongoing foot care and monitoring. There is no publicly funded podiatry for low-risk diabetic feet.

---

## Non-Diabetic Active Foot Disease

Publicly funded podiatry may also be available for non-diabetic patients with active foot disease who have:
- End-stage renal disease
- Rheumatologic conditions
- Significant peripheral vascular disease
- Neurological conditions
- Significant immunocompromise

Your doctor can discuss whether a referral is appropriate.

---

## Orthotics Services

Orthotics are devices that support, align, or improve the function of a limb or joint. Your doctor will determine whether your need falls under a disability support service or a personal health condition.

### Disability Support Orthotics

Typically funded when the orthotic need:
- Will last more than 6 months, **and**
- Is due to a functional impairment from a long-term disability (e.g., a shoulder sling for hemiplegia from a long-standing stroke)

Your doctor may consider a referral to an orthotic centre for assessment.

### Personal Health Orthotics

Generally funded for:
- Chronic conditions such as diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, osteoarthritis, and vascular disease
- Conditions more commonly associated with ageing (e.g., arthritis)
- Any orthotic need likely to last less than 6 months
- Post-surgery patients
- Exacerbation of medical conditions
- An injury (within the first 6 weeks post-discharge if ACC-covered)

Your doctor can refer you to an orthotic service. Referrals need to be made by a GP or relevant registered health professional.

### Prosthetic Limb Services

Funded for amputation — including lower limb, upper limb, and finger amputation. Your doctor will refer you to the prosthetic limb service.

---

## ACC and Private Podiatry

**ACC:** Lower limb injuries are covered by ACC. Consultations generally require a co-payment, including for orthotics. In many cases ACC covers orthotics costs up to $150 without prior approval if the provider holds an orthotics service contract.

**Private podiatry:** For conditions not eligible for public funding, private podiatrists are available throughout New Zealand. Initial consultation and treatment costs typically range from $45 to $150, and orthotics from $75 to $700 depending on the type.
