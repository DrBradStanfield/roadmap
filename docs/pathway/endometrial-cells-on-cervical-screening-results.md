# Endometrial Cells on Cervical Screening Results

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers how to interpret and manage endometrial cells reported on cervical screening (smear) results — including benign-appearing cells and atypical glandular cells of endometrial origin.

---

## Red Flags — Urgent Assessment Required

The following results require urgent gynaecology assessment:

- **Atypical endometrial cells (AG2)**
- **Endometrial adenocarcinoma (AC2)**

---

## Background

- Benign-appearing glandular endometrial cells are reported in women aged 45 years and older.
- In premenopausal women, benign endometrial cells are rarely associated with significant pathology — they are usually the result of physiologic shedding, especially in the first half of the menstrual cycle.
- In women who are postmenopausal, symptomatic, or at high risk, even normal-appearing glandular endometrial cells are more likely to be associated with pathology.
- Atypical glandular cells (AGC) of endometrial origin — referred to as atypical endometrial cells (AG2) — have a high correlation with the presence of endometrial hyperplasia or cancer.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. **Take a history**, including:
   - Menstrual history and any abnormal bleeding patterns
   - Abnormal vaginal discharge or pressure symptoms suggesting a pelvic mass
   - Risk factors for endometrial cancer or hyperplasia (see below)

2. **Perform an examination** including abdominal and pelvic examination (bimanual and speculum)

### Risk Factors for Endometrial Cancer or Hyperplasia

**Equity considerations:**
- Māori and Pacific peoples have significantly higher rates of endometrial hyperplasia and cancer (relative risk of 2.47 and 5.11 respectively compared with other ethnicities). Your doctor will consider investigations to exclude endometrial cancer and hyperplasia at, or soon after, the initial presentation of abnormal uterine bleeding.

**Clinical risk factors:**

Excess estrogen exposure (endogenous):
- BMI 30 or higher — risk is directly proportional to BMI
- Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
- Chronic anovulation (fewer than 4 menstrual cycles in 12 months)
- Nulliparity in women older than 35 years
- Early menarche (before age 10–12 years) or late menopause (after age 55 years)
- Estrogen-secreting tumour

Excess estrogen exposure (exogenous):
- Long-standing unopposed estrogen menopause hormone therapy (MHT)
- Tamoxifen therapy

Other risk factors:
- Diabetes mellitus
- Iron deficiency anaemia (Hb less than 100)
- Family history of endometrial, ovarian, breast, or bowel cancer

---

## Management

### Atypical Endometrial Cells (AG2)

Your doctor will:

1. Arrange a pelvic ultrasound scan within 1–2 weeks and a pipelle biopsy (an in-office sampling of the uterine lining), regardless of ultrasound results
2. Request urgent non-acute gynaecology assessment, including the investigation results even if normal. If results are abnormal, the referral will be annotated as "high suspicion of cancer" to facilitate faster triage
3. If there is also a co-existing reason for colposcopy referral (e.g. HPV 16/18 detected, HPV detected with immune deficiency, or HPV detected with high-grade cytology), colposcopy will be requested via gynaecology assessment

> **Important:** Your doctor will not delay the referral if the pelvic ultrasound and pipelle biopsy cannot be done within 1–2 weeks.

---

### Endometrial Adenocarcinoma (AC2)

Your doctor will request urgent non-acute gynaecology assessment, marked as "high suspicion of cancer," with all investigation results included.

---

### Benign Endometrial Cells

A person with symptoms of uterine pathology requires investigation regardless of cytology result.

**No investigation needed if** the patient is premenopausal, not high risk, asymptomatic, and examination is normal — they can return to the regular cervical screening interval.

**Your doctor will arrange a pelvic ultrasound scan and pipelle biopsy if the patient:**
- Has a suspected mass on pelvic examination (urgent — within 2 weeks), **or**
- Is postmenopausal (no period for 12 consecutive months), **or**
- Is symptomatic (e.g. intermenstrual, postcoital, irregular or heavy bleeding), **or**
- Has one or more risk factors for endometrial cancer or hyperplasia (listed above)

**If investigations are normal:**
- For asymptomatic patients with a normal examination — return to the regular cervical screening interval, with advice to report any abnormal bleeding
- For symptomatic patients — your doctor will manage according to the appropriate pathway (e.g. abnormal uterine bleeding, postcoital bleeding, or postmenopausal bleeding) and arrange follow-up in 3 months
- If abnormal bleeding persists after 3 months of management, your doctor will request non-acute gynaecology assessment

**Abnormal results** requiring urgent gynaecology assessment include:
- Pipelle showing hyperplasia or cancer
- Ultrasound showing endometrial thickness ≥ 5 mm in postmenopausal women or ≥ 12 mm in premenopausal women

**If the pipelle sample is inadequate,** your doctor will seek gynaecology advice.
