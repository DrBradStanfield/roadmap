# Proteinuria in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to finding protein in a child's urine — assessing whether it is transient and benign, or persistent and requiring further investigation.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

Your doctor will arrange urgent (same-day) paediatric assessment if any of the following are present:

- Nephrotic range proteinuria (3+ on dipstick, or urine protein:creatinine ratio > 200 mg/mmol)
- Proteinuria associated with haematuria, hypertension, renal dysfunction, or low albumin (hypoalbuminaemia)
- Rapid or unexplained weight loss or weight gain
- Oedema (swelling — including early periorbital swelling, which can be mistaken for hay fever)
- Features of hypertensive encephalopathy (seizures, confusion, or reduced consciousness)

---

## Background

Proteinuria may be found incidentally during a routine urine test, or when deliberately checking for it in certain clinical situations.

- Anything more than a trace of protein on dipstick needs further evaluation.
- **Persistent proteinuria is abnormal** and should be investigated further — it does not need to be in the nephrotic range to be significant.
- Persistent proteinuria is a recognised marker of kidney damage and a well-known risk factor for progression to chronic kidney disease (CKD) in both children and adults.

**Transient proteinuria** is common (affecting 5–15% of children) and is often due to:
- Fever
- Recent strenuous exercise
- Menstruation
- Urinary tract infection (UTI)
- Orthostatic (postural) proteinuria (see below)

**Orthostatic proteinuria** is a benign form most common in growing children and adolescents. If a daytime urine test shows proteinuria but the first morning protein:creatinine ratio is normal, a diagnosis of orthostatic proteinuria can be made. It is usually mild and disappears as the child grows older.

## Assessment

Your doctor will carry out a detailed assessment including the following.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

**Birth history:**
- Prematurity, birth asphyxia, small for gestational age, polyhydramnios, placentomegaly, or poor growth

**Current symptoms:**
- Oedema (swelling)
- Rapid weight changes
- Urine output changes
- Blood in the urine (macroscopic haematuria)
- Current or recent viral illnesses
- Rashes
- Joint symptoms

**Factors that may cause transient proteinuria:**
- Recent strenuous exercise
- Menstruation
- Symptoms of urinary tract infection
- Fever
- Timing of the urine sample (to assess for orthostatic proteinuria)

**Past medical history:**
- Hearing loss
- Ophthalmological diagnoses (some genetic forms of glomerular disease, such as Alport's Syndrome, are associated with renal failure, hearing loss, and visual loss)
- Renal or urinary tract anomalies
- Acute kidney injury
- Recurrent urinary tract infections
- Medications — NSAIDs, antibiotics, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), antivirals, and anti-epileptics are all associated with interstitial nephritis

**Family history:**
- Renal disease, deafness (relevant to conditions such as Alport's Syndrome)

### Examination

Your doctor will:

- Take the child's temperature
- Record height and weight (plotted on age-appropriate growth charts)
- Assess for rapid weight gain or loss
- Measure blood pressure using the correct cuff size for the child's age and arm size
- Compare blood pressure to age-appropriate screening values
- Check for oedema — early periorbital swelling may be misdiagnosed as hay fever
- Check for vasculitic rash and joint swelling

## Management

Your doctor will use the following step-by-step approach, though the exact pathway will depend on your child's specific findings.

**1. Trace protein on dipstick**
If protein is only a trace on dipstick, this is likely insignificant and can be ignored.

**2. Proteinuria ≥ 1+ on dipstick**
Your doctor will check for transient causes first by repeating the test on 3 additional first morning urine samples collected on separate occasions:
- For isolated 1–2+ proteinuria: repeat monthly
- For isolated 3+ or higher: recheck within one week

**3. If proteinuria persists**
Your doctor will send an early morning urine sample for a protein:creatinine ratio (PCR) when the child is well.

- **PCR < 23 mg/mmol (normal):** If there are no concerning features, no additional tests are needed. Concerning features that would prompt specialist advice include oedema, haematuria, hypertension, history of being systemically unwell for weeks to months, or rapid weight changes.

- **PCR abnormal:** Your doctor may consider:
  - Urine microscopy and culture (to check for UTI and haematuria)
  - Repeat blood pressure measurement
  - Blood tests, which may include: full blood count (FBC), electrolytes, urea, creatinine, albumin, anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), C3 and C4, streptococcal serology, and lipids
  - Renal ultrasound (to look for dysplasia, scarring, or cystic kidney disease)

**4. Referral thresholds**
Depending on the PCR result and clinical findings, your doctor will consider paediatric or nephrology specialist assessment:

- **PCR 23–100 mg/mmol:** Paediatric nephrology advice
- **PCR ≥ 23 mg/mmol with persistent microscopic haematuria:** Paediatric assessment or nephrology advice
- **PCR > 100 mg/mmol:** Paediatric assessment or nephrology advice
- **Persistent isolated proteinuria (PCR > 23 mg/mmol):** Specialist review. "Isolated" means no haematuria, normal blood pressure, and normal creatinine in an otherwise well child. The PCR should be rechecked on at least 3 separate occasions over 6 weeks to 3 months.
- **PCR > 200 mg/mmol with oedema, low albumin (< 25 g/L), or high cholesterol (hyperlipidaemia):** Urgent paediatric assessment
- **Proteinuria with impaired renal function, macroscopic haematuria, or hypertension:** Urgent paediatric assessment

**Glomerulonephritis — when to suspect it**
Your doctor may suspect glomerulonephritis if a child has haematuria (ranging from microscopic to gross), proteinuria, hypertension (in up to 80% of cases), volume overload (which may cause heart failure or pulmonary oedema), or reduced urine output.
