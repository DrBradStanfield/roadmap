# Facial Palsy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of facial palsy, including Bell's palsy (idiopathic) and Ramsay Hunt syndrome (herpes zoster). It is intended as a clinical reference — always discuss diagnosis and treatment decisions with your doctor.

---

## Important Safety Note (May 2025)

Aciclovir and valaciclovir doses must be **reduced in renal impairment** — aciclovir is 80% renally cleared and accumulation can cause neurotoxicity. Ensure adequate hydration and monitor for lethargy, confusion, agitation, and hallucination in at-risk patients.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

These features suggest a cause other than Bell's palsy and require prompt specialist review:

- **Forehead sparing** — if only the lower face is weak (forehead movement preserved), this suggests an upper motor neurone (central) lesion such as stroke or TIA, not Bell's palsy
- **Bilateral facial palsy** — not Bell's palsy; requires urgent neurology assessment (consider sarcoidosis, Guillain-Barré syndrome, myasthenia, myopathy)

---

## Background

Facial palsy involves the VII cranial nerve (facial nerve), causing unilateral weakness of the upper and lower face. Key points:

- **Most common cause (~50%):** Idiopathic — Bell's palsy
- **Other causes:** Herpes zoster (Ramsay Hunt syndrome), trauma, middle ear infection, parotid gland tumour
- **Typical Bell's palsy course:**
  - Rapid onset over hours to days
  - Some improvement within 6 weeks
  - Recovery usually complete by 6 months (incomplete in some patients)

---

## Assessment

### History — Typical Features of Bell's Palsy

- Rapid onset (hours to days)
- Unilateral paralysis of **both upper and lower face**
- Impaired eye closure or blinking
- Eyebrow sag
- Otalgia (ear pain)
- Drooping of the mouth corner, drawn to the opposite side on smiling
- Subjective numbness or tingling (objective sensory loss suggests an alternative cause)
- In severe cases: loss of taste over the front of the tongue; intolerance to loud or high-pitched sounds

### Neurological Examination

- **Confirm both upper and lower facial muscles are involved.** Lower facial weakness alone (with preserved eye closure and forehead movement) indicates an upper motor neurone disorder — follow the Stroke pathway.
- Test by asking the patient to: close eyes, elevate eyebrows, frown, show teeth, pucker lips, and tense neck soft tissues.
- Examine all cranial nerves and peripheral nerves — involvement beyond VII nerve suggests an alternative diagnosis.
- Limb neurological abnormalities → consider TIA or stroke.

### ENT Examination

Examine the ear, nose, and throat to exclude other causes:

- **Herpes zoster vesicles** in the mouth, pinna, ear canal, or eardrum → Ramsay Hunt syndrome (see below)
- Parotid gland tumour
- Cervical or cranial lymphadenopathy

### Ramsay Hunt Syndrome

Herpes zoster infection of the VII cranial nerve. Key features:

- Vesicles and scabs on the face, pinna, ear canal, pharynx, or upper neck
- Worse prognosis than Bell's palsy:
  - Untreated with complete palsy → 10% achieve full recovery
  - Untreated with partial palsy → 68% achieve full recovery
- Maximum palsy typically within 1 week; late denervation can occur up to 14 days after onset
- Other cranial nerves may be involved (VIII, IX, X)
- May cause swallowing impairment
- Complications: permanent facial paralysis, postherpetic neuralgia, hearing loss

### Investigations

Blood tests and imaging are **not routinely needed** for typical Bell's palsy.

## Management

Your doctor will manage facial palsy as follows:

### When to Refer Urgently

**Arrange acute neurology assessment if:**
- Bilateral facial nerve palsy
- Diplopia, dysphagia, or dizziness
- CNS cause suspected

**Arrange non-acute neurology assessment (marked urgent) if:**
- Lower motor neurone palsy with hearing loss or other cranial nerve involvement
- Vesicles in the tympanic membrane with otalgia or otorrhoea
- Perineural spread from cutaneous squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) suspected

**Follow the Stroke pathway if:**
- Facial nerve paralysis with other lateralising signs, preserved forehead wrinkling (forehead sparing), or dysphasia

### Reassurance

For typical Bell's palsy, reassure the patient that most cases resolve completely, though recovery may take several months.

### Medications

**Bell's palsy:** Treat with a short course of corticosteroids, ideally started within 3 days of onset:
- Prednisone 60 mg daily for 5 days, then taper over 5 days
- A lower dose may be appropriate for some patients

**Ramsay Hunt syndrome:** Low threshold to treat with prednisone **plus** an antiviral:
- Valaciclovir 1000 mg three times daily for 7 days, **or**
- Aciclovir 800 mg five times daily for 7 days

*(Reduce antiviral doses in renal impairment — see Important Safety Note above)*

### Eye Protection

If the eyelid cannot close fully, eye protection is essential to prevent corneal damage:

- Lubricating eye drops frequently during the day (e.g., dextran + hypromellose)
- Eyepad or protective glasses during the day
- Paraffin-based eye ointment overnight (e.g., liquid paraffin + lanolin)
- Tape upper and lower lid together before sleep

If incomplete eye closure persists beyond 6 weeks, or corneal damage is a concern, your doctor will arrange an ophthalmology review.

### Follow-Up

- Review promptly if new symptoms or signs develop
- Review at 2 weeks and 6 weeks
- At 6 weeks: if concern it may not be typical Bell's palsy (other cranial nerve involvement, other systems involved), arrange non-acute neurology assessment
- If facial nerve function is incompletely recovered at 3 months → seek neurology advice for further investigation
- If poor functional or cosmetic outcome at 3 months → consider plastic surgery referral (may require private referral)

## When to See a Specialist — Summary

| Situation | Action |
|---|---|
| Bilateral facial palsy, diplopia, dysphagia, dizziness, or CNS cause suspected | Acute neurology assessment |
| LMN palsy + hearing loss, vesicles in ear, or perineural SCC spread | Urgent non-acute neurology assessment |
| Forehead sparing or other lateralising signs | Stroke pathway |
| Not resolving as expected at 6 weeks | Non-acute neurology assessment |
| Incomplete recovery at 3 months | Neurology advice |
| Persistent eyelid closure failure or corneal damage | Ophthalmology assessment |
| Poor cosmetic/functional outcome at 3 months | Plastic surgery (may need private) |
