# Acute Bilateral Red Eye

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment of acute bilateral red eye — identifying the likely cause and determining urgency.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

Go to the emergency department immediately if:

- **Chemical injury to the eye** — flush immediately with copious water and seek emergency care without delay
- **Sudden significant eye pain with reduced vision and haloes around lights** — may indicate acute angle closure glaucoma (a medical emergency)
- **Severe eye pain with marked redness, photophobia, and reduced vision** — may indicate infectious keratitis or iritis

---

## Differential Diagnosis — Key Features

Your doctor will use the following features to determine the likely cause:

### Adenoviral (Epidemic Viral) Conjunctivitis

- Eyes affected sequentially (not simultaneously)
- Watering, gritty pain, puffy eyelids
- Vision and corneal/iris examination normal
- Symptoms worsen over about 1 week, usually resolve by 2 weeks; some patients have ongoing mild irritation
- Highly contagious — strict hand hygiene and equipment cleaning required

### Allergic Conjunctivitis

- Seasonal (often with hay fever) or perennial
- Significant itch with mild redness
- Rubbing eyes, watery discharge, tearing
- May be associated with atopic history or hay fever-type symptoms
- Vision and corneal/iris examination normal
- Can also occur as a reaction to eye drop preservatives, chemicals, or other allergens

### Bacterial Conjunctivitis

- Mucopurulent (thick, sticky) discharge
- Vision and corneal/iris examination normal
- If a sexually transmitted infection is possible, or if not improving with topical antibiotics, eye swabs (NAAT/PCR) for chlamydia and gonorrhoea are indicated

### Chemical Injury

- Most urgent of all eye conditions
- Alkalis penetrate deeper than acids and cause more harm — both require immediate copious irrigation
- Significant pain, watering, and irritation
- Redness variable; severe injuries may cause white eyes (vessel blanching)
- Corneal damage ranges from minor punctate staining to severe epithelial loss and corneal opacity

### Acute Angle Closure Glaucoma

- Significant deep aching eye pain
- Reduced visual acuity and haloes around light sources
- Nausea and vomiting common
- Bilateral presentation is rare
- More common in older patients and those of Asian ancestry

### Iritis / Uveitis

- Usually unilateral, but may be bilateral in a minority of cases
- Photophobia
- Poorly reactive and/or distorted (irregular) pupil
- Constant deep pain
- Mild to moderately reduced vision
- Hypopyon (pus in the anterior chamber) may be visible in severe cases

### Infectious (Bacterial) Keratitis

- White area visible with a torch or direct ophthalmoscope
- Fluorescein dye taken up on corneal surface, with significant conjunctival redness
- Significant pain and photophobia
- Risk factors: contact lens wear, trauma involving organic material

### Scleritis

- Usually unilateral, but may be bilateral in a minority of cases
- Severe, deep aching pain — often painful enough to wake the patient at night
- Marked redness of the eyeball itself (not the eyelids), diffuse or localised
- Common in patients with underlying connective tissue disease
- Nodular and necrotising forms exist — dark areas visible through the sclera suggest necrotising scleritis and require urgent referral

### Episcleritis

- Redness without significant discomfort or discharge
- Pain not prominent — not severe enough to interrupt sleep
- If there is a painful deep ache, assume scleritis rather than episcleritis

## Management

Management depends on the identified cause. Your doctor may consider:

- **Adenoviral conjunctivitis:** Supportive care; lubricating eye drops for comfort; strict hygiene to limit spread. No antiviral treatment routinely needed.
- **Allergic conjunctivitis:** Topical antihistamine or mast cell stabiliser eye drops; avoiding the allergen; oral antihistamines if associated hay fever.
- **Bacterial conjunctivitis:** Topical antibiotic eye drops. Eye swabs if STI possible or no response to treatment.
- **Chemical injury:** Immediate copious irrigation (water or saline) before anything else; urgent ophthalmology assessment.
- **Acute angle closure glaucoma:** Emergency ophthalmology referral; intraocular pressure-lowering treatment.
- **Iritis/Uveitis:** Ophthalmology assessment; topical steroids and cycloplegics typically used.
- **Infectious keratitis:** Urgent ophthalmology review; intensive topical antibiotics.
- **Scleritis:** Ophthalmology assessment; NSAIDs or systemic steroids depending on severity.
- **Episcleritis:** Usually self-limiting; lubricants or mild topical NSAIDs for comfort.

Always discuss your symptoms and treatment options with your doctor. Some of these conditions — particularly chemical injury, acute angle closure glaucoma, keratitis, and scleritis — require urgent specialist assessment and should not be managed with self-treatment alone.
