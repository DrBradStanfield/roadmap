# Chronic Red Eye

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Chronic red eye is a common presentation. It is often bilateral but can be unilateral. In many cases, no specific diagnosis is required — if a careful history, vision check, and examination of the external eye are all normal, the condition may be non-specific.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Care

See your doctor promptly or seek emergency care if you have:

- Sudden loss of vision or significant change in vision
- Severe eye pain
- Eye injury or chemical exposure
- A red eye with headache, nausea, or vomiting (possible acute glaucoma)
- Photophobia (sensitivity to light) with a painful red eye

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history and examine your eye. This typically includes:

- How long the redness has been present and whether it is getting worse
- Whether one or both eyes are affected
- Associated symptoms: burning, itching, grittiness, discharge, tearing, crusting, blurred vision
- Time of day symptoms are worst (e.g., worse in the morning suggests blepharitis or dry eye)
- Any known allergies, hay fever, or atopic history
- Any skin conditions such as rosacea
- Thyroid history
- Medications (some can cause dry eye)

Physical examination typically includes:

- Visual acuity check
- Examination of the eyelid margins and lashes
- Assessment of the conjunctiva and cornea
- Tear film assessment

## Common Causes

### Blepharitis

- Extremely common
- Burning sensation, especially in the morning
- Redness of the lid margin and lids
- Crusting, scaling, and possible loss of lashes
- Dryness and gritty irritation (intermittent); lubricants may help symptoms but redness often remains

### Dry Eye

- Often used as a broad diagnosis for intermittent sharp eye pain or irritation
- Pain and irritation that may be severe, with blurring of vision
- Related to a wide range of exacerbating factors
- Can occur as an isolated condition or as part of Sjogren's syndrome
- Blepharitis and eyelid problems are commonly involved, causing tear film instability

### Allergic Conjunctivitis

- Seasonal (often with hay fever) or perennial
- Significant itch with mild redness
- Rubbing eyes, watery discharge, and tearing
- May be associated with atopic history or hay fever-type symptoms
- Visual acuity and corneal/iris examination are normal

### Rosacea Keratitis

- Blepharitis with peripheral corneal changes
- Associated facial rosacea
- Redness of lid margins, injection of the bulbar conjunctiva, possibly involving the cornea

### Ectropion / Entropion

- Malposition of the lower eyelids
- Ectropion (lid turned outward) can cause inferior corneal exposure and redness
- Entropion (lid turned inward) causes ongoing mechanical irritation and places the eye at risk of corneal infection

### Pterygium / Pinguecula

- Pterygium: wedge-shaped growth of conjunctival tissue that can encroach onto the corneal surface and affect vision
- Pinguecula: similar growth but confined to the conjunctiva — does not encroach on the cornea
- Both are often bilateral and can be chronically red
- Redness alone is not an indication for removal; threatened vision from corneal encroachment (pterygium) is

### Thyroid Eye Disease

- Usually associated with retracted eyelids and/or proptosis (eye bulging)
- Your doctor may check thyroid function — often hyperthyroid

## Management

Management depends on the identified cause. Your doctor may consider:

- **Blepharitis:** Lid hygiene (warm compresses, lid scrubs), lubricating eye drops, and in some cases topical or oral antibiotics (e.g., doxycycline for rosacea-associated blepharitis)
- **Dry eye:** Preservative-free lubricating eye drops, managing contributing factors (blepharitis, medications, environment)
- **Allergic conjunctivitis:** Avoiding known allergens, topical antihistamine or mast cell stabiliser eye drops (e.g., olopatadine, ketotifen), oral antihistamines if systemic symptoms are present
- **Rosacea keratitis:** Treating the underlying rosacea (topical metronidazole or azelaic acid for skin; oral doxycycline for moderate-to-severe blepharitis component)
- **Ectropion / entropion:** Referral to ophthalmology for surgical correction
- **Pterygium / pinguecula:** Lubricating drops for comfort; ophthalmology referral if vision is threatened by corneal encroachment
- **Thyroid eye disease:** Referral to ophthalmology and endocrinology

If a careful history, vision check, and external eye examination are normal and no specific diagnosis is identified, reassurance and lubricating drops are appropriate first steps.
