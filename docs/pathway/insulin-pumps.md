# Insulin Pumps

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the primary care management of patients using insulin pumps — including prescribing consumables, troubleshooting problems, and knowing when to refer to specialist diabetes services.

> **Note:** Initiating and changing insulin pumps is outside the scope of primary care. This is highly specialised and managed by diabetes specialist teams. Your doctor can help with consumables prescribing and troubleshooting, but pump initiation requires specialist referral.

---

## What Is an Insulin Pump?

Insulin pumps provide a continuous basal rate of rapid-acting insulin and the ability to bolus for meals and correction of high blood glucose.

Most pumps are simple infusion devices that:

- administer insulin at planned basal rates, and
- enable boluses to be calculated from glucose levels and carbohydrate intake, which are manually entered.

Patients can run their pumps manually by inputting glucose levels from a continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) device, or self-monitored blood glucose (finger pricks).

### Automated Insulin Delivery (AID)

Pumps can be used in combination with a CGM to run automated insulin delivery (AID), also known as a closed-loop or artificial pancreas system. AID has three components:

- An insulin pump
- A continuous glucose monitoring (CGM) device
- A control algorithm that automatically modulates insulin delivery based on real-time glucose values (on the pump itself or on a smartphone app)

AID systems reduce or stop insulin when glucose is predicted to be low, and increase insulin when glucose is predicted to be high. Studies have shown AID can improve glycaemic control.

**Important:** While AID delivers automated boluses, best practice recommends still giving a manual bolus before meals, particularly large ones — this proactively prevents post-meal glucose spikes rather than waiting for the AID system to react.

**Funded AID systems in New Zealand:**

- **Tandem t:slim X2** with Dexcom G6 or G7 CGM — operates Basal IQ or Control-IQ (algorithm on the pump)
- **mylife YpsoPump** with Dexcom G6 or Freestyle Libre 3 Plus CGM — operates CamAPS FX (Android app; iOS compatibility expected early 2025)

**No longer funded (from 1 January 2025):** Medtronic MiniMed 770G/780G (consumables funded until 1 October 2026).

**Pumps are not funded for type 2 diabetes.**

## Funded Pump Types

### Tandem t:slim X2

- Water resistant to 0.91 m depth for up to 30 minutes
- Rechargeable battery, up to 7 days
- Touch screen; weighs 112 g
- Cartridge holds up to 300 units of rapid-acting insulin (single use; must be filled by patient)
- When cartridge is changed, infusion set must also be changed
- CGM integration: Dexcom G6 or G7

### mylife YpsoPump

- Waterproof to 1 m for 60 minutes
- Standard AAA battery, up to 30 days
- Touch screen; weighs 83 g
- Cartridge holds up to 160 units of rapid-acting insulin (single use; must be filled by patient)
- Cartridge can be changed independently of infusion set (infusion sets still changed every 3 days)
- CGM interoperability: Dexcom G6 or Freestyle Libre 3 Plus (via compatible smartphone)

## Special Authority

Insulin pumps and consumables are funded under Special Authority (SA) criteria. From January 2026, SAs are **lifelong** (no renewal required).

**Eligibility for a funded pump:**

- Type 1 diabetes, permanent neonatal diabetes, some types of monogenic diabetes with insulin deficiency, Type 3c diabetes (e.g. post-pancreatectomy, insulin-requiring cystic fibrosis or pancreatitis), or some atypical inherited forms of diabetes
- The diabetes specialist team considers the patient will benefit from AID
- HbA1c thresholds are no longer required to qualify

The diabetes specialist issues the SA and provides the initial prescription. Thereafter, primary care can prescribe repeat consumables using the SA number.

## Prescribing Consumables in Primary Care

Your doctor can issue repeat prescriptions for consumables using the Special Authority number (obtained from the diabetes specialist at initiation).

**Common pitfall:** Prescribing a similar but not identical consumable — always check the patient is getting the exact type they use, including cannula length and tubing length.

### How Much to Prescribe

| Item | Annual limit | Max per script |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| Infusion sets (Tandem or YpsoPump) | 19 packs (10 sets/pack) | 5 packs |
| Tandem insulin cartridges | 19 packs (10/pack) | 5 packs |
| YpsoPump reservoirs | 36 packs (10/pack) | 9 packs |

Unless the patient requests less, prescribe the maximum per script (5 packs Tandem, 9 packs YpsoPump).

### Tandem t:slim X2 Infusion Sets

- **TruSteel** — steel cannula, manual straight insert. 6 mm or 8 mm cannula; 60 or 81 cm tubing. Good for those with dexterity issues, plastic cannula allergy, or bent cannula history.
- **Autosoft 90** — soft Teflon cannula, 90-degree auto-inserter. Cannula 6 mm or 9 mm; tubing 60 cm or 110 cm.
- **Autosoft 30** — soft Teflon cannula, 30-degree auto-inserter. 13 mm cannula; 60 cm or 110 cm tubing.

Tandem cartridges hold 300 units; changed with each infusion set change.

### mylife YpsoPump Infusion Sets

- **Mylife Orbit micro (steel):** 5.5 mm or 8.5 mm needle, straight insert; tubing 45–80 cm
- **Mylife Inset Soft (Teflon):** 6 mm or 9 mm needle, flexible insert; tubing 46–80 cm

YpsoPump reservoirs hold 160 units; can be changed without changing infusion set.

### Insulin to Prescribe

Rapid-acting insulin for pump use is usually **NovoRapid (insulin aspart)** or **Humalog (insulin lispro)** (YpsoPump also supports Apidra). Usually a 3 mL cartridge ("penfill"); some patients use a 10 mL vial. Prescribe what the patient is used to.

## Safety and Hygiene

- **Insulin storage:** Refrigerate at 2–8°C (never freeze). Check expiry. Insulin in cartridges can remain unrefrigerated for up to 28 days.
- **Infusion set:** Replace every 3 days.
- **Tandem reservoir:** Change every 48–72 hours.
- **YpsoPump reservoir:** Change at least every 7 days.
- **Rotate cannula sites** every time the cannula is replaced to prevent lipohypertrophy (which reduces insulin absorption). Check sites for lipohypertrophy or infection at appointments.
- **Prescriptions:** Advise patients to request repeat scripts well before supplies run out — not all pharmacies stock these items.

### Emergency Kit

Every patient should have an emergency kit at home containing:

- 2 spare cartridges and infusion sets
- Spare batteries or charged power pack
- Glucometer with test strips (e.g. CareSens Dual Meter with CareSens PRO strips)
- Ketone meter with test strips (e.g. KetoSens strips)
- 1 Glucagen Hypokit
- 2 cartridges or pens of basal insulin (e.g. Lantus, Protaphane, or Humulin N)
- 1 insulin pen for rapid-acting insulin
- Insulin needles

## Troubleshooting

### Technical Pump Problems

For all pump issues, notify the patient's diabetes nurse specialist as soon as possible.

If there are technical problems, contact the supplier 24-hour helplines:

- **Tandem pump:** NZMS — 0508-634-103
- **Mylife YpsoPump:** Pharmaco — 0800-458-267
- **Medtronic pump:** Intermed — 0800-633-487

### Pump Failure

Contact the supplier helpline; they should provide a replacement within 24 hours.

In the meantime, the patient must give insulin by pen **within 4 hours** of the pump stopping. Guided by pump records (or use these defaults):

- **Basal:** Give Lantus at a dose equal to the daily basal total delivered by the pump (e.g. 24 units once daily if usual basal rate was 1 unit/hour)
- **Mealtime bolus:** Use the same insulin:carbohydrate ratios, insulin sensitivity factor, and target blood glucose levels as the pump settings

Notify the diabetes nurse specialist. Ensure the patient has an emergency kit.

**Pump expiry:** Apply to the patient's diabetes service for a funded replacement. Advise the patient to contact their diabetes service well in advance of expiry to ensure HbA1c and other criteria are up to date.

### Low Blood Glucose (less than 4 mmol/L)

If presenting acutely, your doctor will manage according to hypoglycaemia protocols. If not acute, seek diabetes advice.

### High Blood Glucose (greater than 15 mmol/L)

Check ketones if there is no clear precipitant (e.g. recent large meal):

- **Ketones > 1.0 mmol/L (ketotic):** Likely occlusion or infusion set problem. Give rapid-acting insulin by pen using the sick-day management plan. Seek diabetes advice.
- **Ketones ≤ 1.0 mmol/L (not ketotic):** Bolus insulin using the pump as usual. Consider replacing the infusion set if blood glucose does not respond.

### Corticosteroid Use

Corticosteroids may require a temporary increase in basal infusion rate. Your doctor will advise you to contact your diabetes nurse for individual guidance.

### Unwell Patient

- Follow the sick-day management plan if available.
- If mildly unwell without a sick-day plan, or if concerned despite following the plan, seek diabetes advice.
- If moderately to severely unwell, vomiting, or showing signs of DKA, arrange emergency assessment.

### Surgical Procedures

Your doctor will liaise with the diabetes team and anaesthetist to plan continuous insulin during the procedure. Do not stop the pump without specialist guidance.

### Travelling with an Insulin Pump

Your doctor can write a travel letter on practice letterhead covering your diagnosis, insulin pump management, and all medications. Key travel advice:

- Always carry insulin in hand luggage (not checked baggage — it may freeze)
- Do not disconnect the pump at the airport
- Do not put the pump through X-ray machines or body scanners
- Carry enough supplies (insulin, infusion sets, cartridges) plus a buffer, and keep the emergency kit as carry-on

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor will refer you to a diabetes specialist for:

- **Initiating a pump** — pump therapy requires specialist assessment and initiation; primary care can refer and support the wait
- **Changing to a different pump** — pumps are not interchangeable; specialist education is required for any device change (including transition away from MiniMed 770G/780G before October 2026)
- **Starting AID (automated insulin delivery)** — highly specialised; initiated and managed by specialist teams
- **Troubleshooting** if: blood glucose is high and ketotic; you are mildly unwell with no sick-day plan; or your sick-day plan is not working
- **Emergency assessment** if moderately to severely unwell, vomiting, or suspected DKA

If requesting specialist assessment, include recent HbA1c, future pregnancy planning, ethnicity, time since diagnosis, and a CGM printout if available.
