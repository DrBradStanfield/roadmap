# Intestinal Parasites

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers selected intestinal parasites that may present in general practice, including strongyloidiasis, amoebiasis, giardiasis, cryptosporidiosis, threadworms, other intestinal worms, tapeworm, and delusional infestation.

---

## Strongyloidiasis

### Background

Strongyloidiasis is caused by *Strongyloides stercoralis*, a microscopic roundworm common to almost all tropical and subtropical countries.

Transmission occurs when larvae in contaminated soil penetrate human skin. Autoinfection is a unique feature that leads to persistent infection if untreated.

**Who is at risk:**
- Long-term residents of, or travellers to, endemic countries
- Immigrants, refugees, expatriates, and military personnel on long deployments
- Risk is life-long, even decades after leaving an endemic country
- Short-term or urban-only travel is generally not considered a risk

**Endemic regions include:** Southeast Asia, Pacific Islands, Sub-Saharan Africa, Latin America, Northern Australia.

### Symptoms and signs

Most patients have no symptoms. If present, symptoms are mostly non-specific.

Acute infection (within months of exposure) may cause:
- Localised skin swelling or hives at the site of larval entry (usually the foot)
- Dry cough, or cough with fever and shortness of breath (eosinophilic pneumonitis)
- Diarrhoea or constipation
- Abdominal pain
- Loss of appetite

Chronic strongyloidiasis is usually asymptomatic, but may include:
- Epigastric pain or dyspepsia, nausea or vomiting
- Diarrhoea, pruritis ani
- Less commonly: rash (urticaria, larva currens), cough, wheeze, gastrointestinal bleeding, weight loss

### Severe (disseminated) strongyloidiasis

Disseminated disease can occur in people who are immunocompromised, on systemic steroids (even short courses), have alcohol dependency, or malnutrition.

High-risk groups include those with solid organ or bone marrow transplantation, haematologic malignancy, or multimodal immunosuppression.

Severe strongyloidiasis typically causes severe abdominal pain, diarrhoea, and gastrointestinal bleeding. Additional features may include fever, signs of sepsis, ileus, petechiae or purpura, pneumonia, or meningitis.

**Suspected severe strongyloidiasis requires urgent hospital assessment.**

### Investigations

Your doctor will decide which investigations are needed based on your circumstances:

- **Asymptomatic screening before planned immunosuppression (in someone from an endemic region):** FBC (looking for eosinophilia) and Strongyloides serology
- **Any risk factors for severe disease, or symptomatic:** FBC, Strongyloides serology, and 3 stool specimens for faecal parasites taken on 3 separate days
- Additional tests as needed: chest X-ray, endoscopy

### Treatment

Your doctor will seek infectious diseases advice before treating if you are immunocompromised and symptomatic, have positive stool microscopy, have an eosinophil count greater than 5, or have any contraindications to ivermectin.

**Ivermectin contraindications** include: residence or travel in a Loiasis-endemic country, pregnancy, breastfeeding, age under 1 year, or weight under 10 kg.

Treatment with ivermectin (dose 200 mcg/kg, maximum 21 mg) — the number of doses depends on immune status. Ivermectin requires a Special Authority, which a GP can apply for.

**Follow-up is important:** your doctor will check eosinophilia resolution at 1 month, and repeat Strongyloides serology at 6 months (a halved titre suggests cure).

## Amoebiasis

### Background

Amoebiasis is caused by *Entamoeba histolytica*, a protozoa found worldwide. It spreads via faecal-oral transmission: contaminated food or water, or oral-anal sexual contact.

Risk factors include travel to or former residence in endemic regions (tropical/subtropical Central and South America, Asia, Africa), immunosuppression, and men who have sex with men.

### Symptoms and signs

Many patients are asymptomatic. The two main clinical forms are:

- **Amoebic colitis:** gradual onset of bloody diarrhoea, abdominal pain and tenderness, fever, weight loss, loss of appetite
- **Amoebic liver abscess:** fever and pain in the right upper quadrant

### Investigations

Your doctor will arrange 3 stool samples for ova, cysts, and parasites if you have symptoms of amoebiasis (dysentery or liver abscess), or as part of refugee health screening. Serology for *E. histolytica* may also be requested.

### Treatment

If your stool sample is positive for *E. histolytica*, your doctor will assess for extraintestinal disease. Treatment involves a course of metronidazole followed by paromomycin to eliminate intestinal carriage. Infectious diseases advice is needed to obtain a Special Authority for paromomycin.

If you have symptomatic colitis, you will need to stay away from work, school, and early learning services until 48 hours symptom-free. Food handlers and healthcare workers may need clearance testing.

## Giardiasis

### Background

Giardiasis is caused by the parasite *Giardia duodenalis*. It spreads through contaminated food or water, recreational water (pools, streams, lakes), or the faecal-oral route.

Incubation period is usually 7 to 10 days (range 3 to 25 days or longer). A person remains infectious for weeks to months.

### Symptoms and signs

May be asymptomatic. If present: diarrhoea, abdominal cramps, bloating, flatulence, nausea, weight loss, and malabsorption.

### Investigations

Testing is usually not needed in acute gastroenteritis unless:
- Diarrhoea not resolving after 7 days
- Severely unwell, febrile, or bloody diarrhoea
- Food handler, childcare or healthcare worker, immunocompromised
- Travel to an area with poor water or sanitation

If needed, a single faecal sample is sent requesting "parasites."

### Treatment

Giardia is a notifiable disease — your doctor will notify the Medical Officer of Health.

Treatment is with ornidazole (preferred) or metronidazole. All symptomatic household contacts should also be treated.

- Ornidazole: adults or children over 35 kg — 1.5 g single dose; children under 35 kg — weight-based dosing
- Metronidazole: adults — 400 mg three times daily for 5 days, or 2 g once daily for 3 days; children — 30 mg/kg once daily for 3 days

**Important:** You remain infectious for several months after onset of symptoms. Do not swim in pools (public or private) for at least 2 weeks after symptoms resolve. Secondary lactose intolerance is common after giardiasis — temporarily avoiding dairy may help.

## Cryptosporidiosis

### Background

Cryptosporidiosis is caused by the protozoa *Cryptosporidium*. It spreads person-to-person by faecal-oral route, from infected animals, or from contaminated water or food. Incubation is on average 7 days (range 1 to 12 days). Infectious oocysts are shed in faeces for several weeks after symptoms resolve.

### Symptoms and signs

Acute illness with diarrhoea (may be profuse and watery) and abdominal pain. May be asymptomatic.

### Investigations

Same thresholds as giardia — testing is not routinely needed in mild acute gastroenteritis. A single faecal sample requesting "parasites" is sent if indicated.

### Treatment

- Cryptosporidium is notifiable — your doctor will inform the Medical Officer of Health.
- **Antibiotics are not indicated** for cryptosporidiosis.
- Stay home from work/school/childcare until 48 hours symptom-free.
- Do not swim in pools for at least 2 weeks after symptoms resolve.
- If you are immunocompromised or have chronic diarrhoea, your doctor will seek specialist advice.

## Threadworms (Pinworms)

### Background

Threadworms (*Enterobius vermicularis*) are common intestinal parasites, especially in school-aged children. They appear as small, thin, white-beige threads visible to the naked eye. Humans are the only host — they are not carried by animals.

Spread occurs by transferring eggs to the mouth after scratching the perianal area, or through contact with contaminated surfaces, clothing, or bedding.

### Symptoms and signs

- Perianal itching, typically worse at night, causing sleep disturbance
- Less commonly: vulvovaginitis, abdominal pain, loss of appetite
- 40% of those infected have minimal or no symptoms

### Investigations

Lab confirmation is not needed before treatment if you have typical symptoms, especially if worms are visible (thin, white, wriggling threads).

The sticky tape test can confirm infection: press clear adhesive tape against the skin around the anus first thing in the morning before washing, then place tape sticky-side-down on a glass slide and send to the laboratory.

Stool examination is not recommended for pinworm diagnosis.

### Treatment

- **Adults (non-pregnant):** mebendazole 100 mg as a single dose, repeated in 2 weeks
- **Children 6 months and older:** mebendazole 100 mg single dose, repeated in 2 weeks (can be halved and crushed, take with food)
- Good hand hygiene is important. Close contacts with symptoms should also be treated.
- Seek your doctor's advice if pregnant, under 6 months of age, or if treatment fails.

## Other Intestinal Worms

**Giant roundworm** (*Ascaris lumbricoides*), **whipworm** (*Trichuris trichuria*), and **hookworm** (*Necator americanus*, *Ancylostoma duodenale*)

These infections are very rare in New Zealand. They occur in tropical and subtropical regions with inadequate sanitation. Spread is through contaminated soil (eggs swallowed, or hookworm larvae entering through skin). There is no direct person-to-person transmission.

Most infected patients are asymptomatic, though high worm burdens may cause abdominal pain.

### Treatment

- **Aged 12 months or older, not pregnant:** mebendazole 100 mg twice daily for 3 days
- **Aged 6 to 11 months:**
  - Under 10 kg: albendazole 200 mg single dose
  - 10 kg or over: albendazole 400 mg single dose
  - Note: albendazole requires a special authority from an infectious diseases consultant or clinical microbiologist
- **Pregnant, under 6 months, or treatment failure:** seek infectious diseases advice

## Tapeworm (Taeniasis)

Tapeworm infection is caused by eating raw or undercooked beef or pork contaminated with tapeworm eggs. It is very rare in New Zealand; most cases occur in travellers or migrants from Asia, Latin America, Eastern Europe, Russia, or Eastern Africa.

Adult tapeworms can shed eggs in the intestine for up to 25 years.

Infection is usually asymptomatic, but may cause insomnia, nervousness, loss of appetite, weight loss, abdominal pain, or gastrointestinal disturbance. Tapeworm segments (proglottids) may be visible on the perineum, clothing, or in faeces.

**Important:** Ingestion of pork tapeworm eggs (*Taenia solium*) can lead to cysticercosis — cysts forming in muscles and brain. This is a serious complication and a common cause of adult-onset epilepsy in some regions.

### Investigations

Your doctor will arrange 3 stool samples. If you are passing segments, these will also be sent to the lab for examination.

### Treatment

- **Tapeworm is notifiable** — your doctor will inform the Medical Officer of Health.
- *Taenia solium* (pork tapeworm): your doctor will assess for cysticercosis and will seek infectious diseases advice before treating, as treatment can in rare cases trigger cerebral oedema.
- *Taenia saginata* (beef tapeworm): for adults and children aged 4 years or older who are not pregnant — praziquantel 10 mg/kg as a single dose, taken with food. Seek infectious diseases advice if pregnant, under 4 years, or treatment fails.

## Delusional Infestation

### Background

Delusional infestation is a rare condition where a person has a fixed belief that they are infested by living organisms (e.g. lice, fleas, worms, fungi) when no actual infestation exists.

It may be a primary psychiatric condition, or secondary to other disorders (dementia, schizophrenia, depression), medical conditions (hypothyroidism, B12 deficiency, neuropathy, diabetes, stroke), recreational drug use (amphetamines, alcohol withdrawal), or rarely certain prescribed medications (amantadine, levodopa, topiramate, gabapentin, ropinirole).

### Symptoms and signs

- Crawling sensations under the skin, or reported sighting of organisms others cannot see
- Scratching and skin excoriations (dermatitis artefacta) from the belief
- Patients often bring in "specimens" (skin flakes, scabs) as evidence of infestation

### What your doctor will do

Your doctor will take the symptoms seriously while carefully examining the skin and ruling out actual parasitic infestation or inflammatory skin conditions. Any specimens brought in will be sent to the laboratory.

Blood tests will be arranged to look for treatable secondary causes: FBC, vitamin B12, ferritin, renal and liver function, HIV, and syphilis serology. A stool sample may also be requested to exclude gastrointestinal parasites.

### Management approach

- Your doctor will treat any identifiable secondary cause.
- Referral to dermatology, infectious diseases, or mental health may be recommended depending on the clinical picture.
- If a fixed delusion is causing significant distress, a second-generation antipsychotic (e.g. risperidone, olanzapine, or quetiapine) may be recommended after specialist advice — these medications can significantly improve symptoms.
- Topical antipruritics (e.g. crotamiton cream) and emollients can help manage the itch.
- Patients are often reluctant to see a psychiatrist; your doctor will aim to explain treatment in practical terms around symptom relief.
- Involving family or support people in discussions and treatment can be helpful.
