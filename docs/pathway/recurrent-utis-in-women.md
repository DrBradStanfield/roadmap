# Recurrent UTIs in Women

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of recurrent urinary tract infections (rUTIs) in women — defined as at least 3 episodes per year, or 2 episodes in the last 6 months.

---

## Background

Recurrent UTIs include both lower tract infection (cystitis) and upper tract infection (pyelonephritis). Repeated pyelonephritis should prompt further investigation.

Risk factors vary by life stage:

**Young and premenopausal women:**
- Sexual intercourse
- Use of spermicide
- A new sexual partner
- A mother with a history of UTI
- History of UTI during childhood

**Postmenopausal and elderly women:**
- History of UTI before menopause
- Urinary incontinence
- Atrophic vaginitis due to oestrogen deficiency
- Cystocele
- Increased post-void urine volume
- Urinary catheterisation or functional status deterioration

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history asking about:

- Number of UTIs in the past year or 6 months
- Pain — including dyspareunia and loin pain
- Current pregnancy
- Urological history: stones, haematuria, previous surgery, lower urinary tract symptoms
- History of pelvic cancer or radiation therapy

Physical examination typically includes:

- Palpable bladder
- Pelvic organ prolapse
- Malodorous discharge
- Atrophic vaginitis

Your doctor will arrange investigations including:

- **Midstream urine (MSU)** for urinalysis and microbiology/culture when indicated
- **HbA1c** (blood sugar test)
- **Ultrasound of the kidney, ureter, and bladder** if UTIs have been associated with:
  - Not responding to prophylactic antibiotics
  - Refractory UTI with significant symptoms not settling despite antibiotics
  - Urine culture showing *Proteus*, *Klebsiella*, or *Pseudomonas* (associated with high risk of stones)
  - Any episode of urosepsis
  - Suspected renal stone (loin pain or upper tract symptoms)
  - History of childhood UTIs
  - Previous urinary tract or incontinence surgery
  - Immunosuppression or diabetes
  - Spinal cord injury

## Management

Most women with recurrent UTIs can be managed in primary care with preventive strategies and/or prophylactic antibiotics.

**Important:** Asymptomatic bacteriuria (bacteria in the urine without symptoms) should not be treated unless you are pregnant. There are no benefits to treating asymptomatic bacteriuria in non-pregnant women, even in elderly patients.

### Preventive strategies

Your doctor may advise:

- **Good hydration** — drinking plenty of fluids, especially important for elderly patients
- **Bowel hygiene** — normalising bowel habit, avoiding constipation, wiping front to back after a bowel motion, and treating faecal incontinence
- **Reducing intercourse-related infections** — considering a lubricant, and avoiding spermicides, spermicide-coated condoms, and diaphragms

Note: There is mixed evidence for cranberry products in reducing recurrent UTIs, and they are not routinely recommended.

### Vaginal estrogens (postmenopausal women)

If you are postmenopausal, your doctor may consider prescribing vaginal estrogens, which can reduce the risk of recurrent UTIs. Vaginal estrogens are an effective treatment for genitourinary syndrome of menopause and can also assist with urinary incontinence.

- **Estriol vaginal cream** (Ovestin 1 mg/g) — applied vaginally at bedtime every night for two weeks, then twice a week ongoing
- Contraindicated in active endometrial cancer or undiagnosed vaginal bleeding
- Considered safe for women with previous venous thromboembolism or gynaecological cancer survivors
- If you have a history of oestrogen-dependent breast cancer and persistent symptoms despite non-hormonal therapy, low-dose vaginal estrogens can still be considered — discuss with your doctor

### Prophylactic antibiotics

Your doctor may consider prophylactic (preventive) antibiotics if there is a clear trigger or pattern of infections:

- **Post-intercourse:** A prophylactic antibiotic taken within 2 hours (e.g., trimethoprim 150 mg, or cefalexin 250 mg). Low-dose continuous or post-coital nitrofurantoin 50–100 mg is an alternative.
- **During menstruation:** Trimethoprim 150 mg daily during menstruation
- **Continuous low-dose prophylaxis:** Typically a 6-month course after treating any current infection and confirming resolution with MSU. Options include:
  - Nitrofurantoin 50 mg once daily (your doctor will monitor for potential liver and lung reactions with prolonged use)
  - Trimethoprim 150 mg once daily
  - Quinolone antibiotics are avoided for prophylaxis

If you develop a breakthrough infection while on prophylaxis: stop the prophylactic antibiotic, take a 2-week course of an appropriate antibiotic, then restart prophylaxis.

Your doctor may also provide a standby (self-treatment) course of antibiotics. Seek medical attention if symptoms persist for more than 48 hours.

### Antibiotic alternatives

If you want to avoid long-term antibiotics, your doctor may consider:

- **Methenamine hippurate (Hiprex)** — acidifies urine and has antibacterial activity. Typically 1 g twice daily for 6 months, with liver function tests (LFTs) checked every 3 months. Avoid taking with urinary alkalinisers (e.g., Ural) or sulphonamides.
- **D-mannose** — competitively binds to *E. coli*. Available as tablets or powder. Some evidence of reduced frequency of infections. A typical dose is 1 g twice daily for prophylaxis.

### When referral to a specialist is needed

Your doctor will refer you to a urologist if you have:

- Previous urinary incontinence surgery
- Any episode of urosepsis
- Previous radiation treatment or pelvic cancer (due to risk of fistula, radiation cystitis, or bladder cancer)
- Abnormal upper tract ultrasound or CT scan
- Macroscopic haematuria (visible blood in urine)
- Spinal cord injury
- History of urinary tract reflux
- Lower urinary tract symptoms between UTI episodes
- Not responding to prophylactic antibiotics
- Persistent bladder pain
