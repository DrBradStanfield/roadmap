# Speech and Language Difficulties in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of speech and language difficulties in children, from early red flags through to developmental milestone review across age groups.

---

## Red Flags — See Your Doctor Promptly

Contact your doctor if your child shows any of the following:

- Minimal babbling in the first year
- Not responding to their name at any point before 12 months
- No indication of first words by 15 months

---

## Significant Concerns — Discuss With Your Doctor

Your doctor will also look for these markers, which may indicate a speech or language difficulty:

- No two-word combinations by age 2 years
- Fewer than 50 words between 18 and 22 months
- Lack of interest in communicating
- Effortful, groping oral movements when trying to form sounds or words
- Persistent word or sound repetitions, prolongations of sounds, or blocking
- Husky or hoarse voice and/or persistent voice loss
- Otitis media with effusion (glue ear)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your child against developmental milestones appropriate for their age. The milestones below are a guide — individual children develop at different rates, and your doctor will consider the full picture.

### Birth to 12 Months

**Receptive language (understanding)**
- Birth–3 months: Attends to surroundings; quiets or smiles when spoken to; startles to loud sounds; recognises familiar voices
- 3–6 months: Moves eyes toward sounds; responds to changes in tone; pays attention to music
- 6–8 months: Attends to one thing at a time; early turn-taking; understands intonation
- 8–12 months: Understands object permanence; recognises familiar words (mum, dad, cup); begins responding to simple requests

**Expressive language**
- Birth–3 months: Makes pleasure sounds (cooing); cries differently for different needs
- 3–6 months: Smiles, babbles, gurgling; vocalises excitement and displeasure
- 6–8 months: Increased vocal play and babbling; develops intention to communicate
- 8–12 months: Imitates adults (e.g., waving); adds consonants and vowels to babbling; says first words (mama, dada, no)

### 12 to 24 Months

**Receptive language**
- 12–18 months: Follows simple one-step commands; points to pictures in a book; understands new words rapidly
- 18–24 months: Follows longer instructions with less reliance on context; points to some body parts when asked

**Expressive language and speech**
- 12–18 months: Uses approximately 30–50 words by 18 months; commonly uses jargon (unintelligible made-up words)
- 18–24 months: Uses two words together (e.g., "bye mum", "more drink"); can ask and answer what/where questions; uses negatives (e.g., no)

### 2 to 3 Years

**Receptive language**
- Follows 2 and 3-part instructions
- Understands concepts (hot/cold, big/small, on/under)
- Follows sequential instructions; listens to short stories

**Expressive language and speech**
- Uses sentences of three or more words (not yet grammatically correct)
- Vocabulary of several hundred words; talks about things not present
- Asks increasing questions; speech may not be understood by unfamiliar adults

### 3 to 4 Years

**Receptive language**
- Understands basic time concepts (now, soon, later)
- Carries out two-step related commands
- Identifies objects by use

**Expressive language and speech**
- Uses increasingly complex sentences
- Uses location words (in, on, under)
- Refers to past and future events; asks and answers who/what/where/why
- Speech becoming more adult-like but still developing

### 4 to 5 Years

**Receptive language**
- Understands concepts of number, size, weight, colour, texture, distance, position, and time
- Understands order of daily routines
- Follows two unrelated directions

**Expressive language and speech**
- Uses complete sentences (minor grammatical immaturities are common, e.g., "I runned")
- Asks meanings of words; tries new vocabulary
- Speaks clearly by 5 years; some immaturities may persist (e.g., /r/, /l/, /th/)

### 5 to 7 Years

**Receptive language**
- Understands complex 2–3 part instructions
- Understands relationships between word meanings

**Expressive language and speech**
- Uses sound and letter links when reading and spelling
- Uses complex sentences with joining words (because, so, then)
- Tells a story with events in order; consistently speaks clearly

### 7 to 9 Years

**Receptive language**
- Understands complex multi-part sequential instructions
- Listens and identifies relevant information; understands inferred meaning

**Expressive language and speech**
- Speaks clearly, fluently, and accurately about past, present, and future
- Tells a story with structure and a distinct plot; speech sounds fully developed

### 9 to 11 Years

**Receptive language**
- Understands sarcasm, simple jokes, and idioms
- Identifies when a sentence is grammatically incorrect

**Expressive language and speech**
- Continually growing vocabulary; long and complex sentences
- Tells elaborate, well-structured stories; uses language to negotiate and organise socially

## Other Assessment Considerations

### Bilingual and Multilingual Children

- If parents have no concerns about development in the first language, any delays in second language acquisition are likely normal
- If parents have concerns about first language development, your doctor may consider a speech-language therapy assessment

### Hearing

Your doctor will check whether your child has had newborn hearing screening. Hearing assessment (audiometry) may be arranged if there are concerns about:
- Hearing
- Receptive language (understanding)
- Expressive language
- Speech sound development

### Broader Developmental Concerns

If your child is not reaching milestones across multiple areas (not only speech and language), your doctor will consider other possible causes such as:
- Global developmental delay
- Cognitive impairment
- Autism spectrum disorder
- Dyslexia
- Dyspraxia

### Voice Difficulties

Your doctor will assess for voice difficulties, which include:
- Frequent or constant hoarse voice, even when the child does not have a cold
- Excessively loud or quiet voice
- Unusual pitch or quality (e.g., nasal tone)

### Stuttering

- "Normal non-fluency" typically occurs between ages 2 and 4 years
- Stuttering can develop at any age but is most common in the pre-school years

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following approaches based on their assessment:

- **Glue ear with speech delay:** Your doctor will follow the otitis media with effusion (glue ear) pathway
- **Partial milestone achievement (only 2 or 3 milestones met):** Your doctor may review again in 6 to 8 weeks
- **Normal non-fluency (repeating whole words, not parts):** Your doctor may review in 3 months
- **Stuttering with any of the following criteria:** Your doctor may refer for a speech-language therapy assessment:
  - Family history of stuttering
  - Non-fluency persisting longer than 3 months
  - Signs of anxiety or frustration about speech
  - Signs of struggle when speaking (e.g., stamping feet, grimacing)
  - Each block, hesitation, or repetition lasts more than just a fleeting moment
- **Voice difficulties or concerns with feeding, swallowing, or oral motor skills:** Your doctor may request a child speech-language therapy assessment
- **Significant developmental deficits across multiple domains:** Your doctor may request a paediatric medical assessment

For isolated speech and language difficulties, most children are referred to a speech-language therapy service. Your doctor can advise on both publicly funded and private options.
