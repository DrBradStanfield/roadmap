# New Patient to Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach when a patient with a life-limiting illness is identified as needing a palliative care approach. It applies to adults.

---

## Who Needs a Palliative Care Approach?

Your doctor may use the **surprise question** as a starting point: "Would I be surprised if this patient died in the next 12 months?" If the answer is no, introducing a palliative care approach is appropriate.

Other indicators:
- Patient expresses a preference to prioritise comfort goals or reduce/stop certain treatments.
- Clinical indicators of advanced disease for cancer, organ failure, frailty, or dementia (e.g. using the Supportive & Palliative Care Indicators Tool, SPICT).

Palliative care is a core part of care provided by all health professionals and should be available to everyone regardless of the setting.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a holistic approach covering:

**Physical health**
- Physical symptoms (pain, breathlessness, nausea, constipation, delirium, low mood/anxiety)
- Activities of daily living
- Medications and current treatments

**Emotional and psychosocial**
- Fears, anxieties, loss of meaning, family dynamics
- Caregiver distress and capacity

**Social concerns**
- Relationships, family issues, financial and legal concerns

**Spiritual concerns**

Your doctor will also explore what barriers exist to caring for the patient at home or in the community, including emotional distress, difficulty coping, financial concerns, and cultural needs.

---

## Early Conversations Your Doctor Will Have With You

Early discussions with the patient and their family or caregivers typically cover:

- Understanding of the diagnosis and prognosis.
- Current stage of disease and treatment options.
- Preferred place of care (home-based or hospital-based).
- Who the patient wishes involved in their care.
- Specific treatment preferences, including cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR).

**About CPR:** Your doctor will explain CPR clearly:
- CPR is pushing on the chest to move blood around the body and helping you breathe.
- CPR does not restart the heart; it supports the body while a treatable cause is addressed.
- CPR works best when the heart stops suddenly due to a reversible cause. When the circulation arrests as the final event in dying, CPR is not effective.

## Management

Most people with palliative care needs can be managed in the community or in an aged care facility by their usual GP or nurse practitioner, who coordinates specialist palliative care input when required.

### Advance Care Planning

Your doctor will check whether you have an existing **Advance Care Plan (ACP)**. If not, they will offer to help create one. An ACP records your wishes for future care and treatment.

### Enduring Power of Attorney (EPA)

Your doctor may encourage you to consider appointing an **Enduring Power of Attorney**:
- An EPA gives legal authority to a trusted person (the attorney) to make decisions on your behalf if you become mentally incapable.
- EPA Welfare covers care and welfare decisions; EPA Property covers financial decisions.
- An EPA is activated by a formal assessment process by a relevant health practitioner.
- This is a legal process with associated costs.

### Medications

Your doctor will:
- Document all regular medications.
- Arrange **anticipatory medications** for likely symptoms (pain, nausea, constipation) so these are available at home if needed.
- Consider building a "medication toolkit" in the home, with education on when and how to use them.
- Complete a medication card listing all medications and their indications.
- Withdraw medications that are no longer appropriate.

### Symptom Management

Your doctor will manage symptoms including:
- Pain
- Constipation
- Nausea and vomiting
- Breathlessness (dyspnoea)
- Delirium
- Low mood or anxiety

If symptoms cannot be managed in the community, your doctor will seek specialist palliative care advice.

### Other Supports

Your doctor can help arrange:
- **Equipment** for the home.
- **Community supports** such as district nursing or allied health input.
- **After-hours planning** — a plan for who to call outside of clinic hours, including an emergency letter.
- **Carer support** and a needs assessment.
- **Practical support** — a communication book in the home can help coordinate everyone involved.

### Syringe Drivers

If medications need to be given continuously via a syringe driver (a small pump delivering medication under the skin), your doctor will use specific authority forms and refer to the Syringe Drivers pathway.

### End of Life Choice Act

If you raise questions about assisted dying, your doctor can discuss the End of Life Choice Act with you.

---

## When to Seek Specialist Palliative Care

Your doctor will seek **adult palliative care support and advice** if:
- Symptoms persist despite community management.
- You need urgent 24-hour telephone advice.
- You have complex psychosocial needs beyond the capacity of community services.
- A joint visit or review is needed.
- Hospice admission is being considered.
