# Epistaxis (Nosebleed)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of epistaxis (nosebleeds) in adults and children.

---

## When to Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if:

- Bleeding cannot be controlled after firm compression and first-aid measures
- There is heavy or rapidly worsening blood loss

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Frequency and duration of episodes
- Underlying medical conditions and bleeding history
- Any medications (particularly anticoagulants or antiplatelet drugs)

Physical examination typically includes:

- Inspecting the nasal cavity (using a headlight and nasal speculum if needed) to identify the bleeding site — most commonly Little's area on the anterior nasal septum
- Checking for a nasal mass or septal perforation

If bleeding is brisk or recurrent, your doctor may consider blood tests — full blood count, renal function, liver function, and a coagulation screen.

---

## Possible Causes

- Trauma (nose-picking, rubbing, sneezing) — most common
- Allergic rhinitis or rhinosinusitis / upper respiratory tract infection
- Bleeding disorders (e.g., thrombocytopenia, Von Willebrand's disease, haemophilia)
- Anticoagulant or antiplatelet medications
- Foreign body in the nose (especially in children — button batteries are an emergency)
- Hereditary haemorrhagic telangiectasia or granulomatosis with polyangiitis (GPA)
- Liver disease
- Nasopharyngeal polyp or tumour — juvenile nasopharyngeal angiofibroma (recurrent unilateral bleeding in adolescent males)
- Malignancy (e.g., leukaemia, nasopharyngeal cancer)

---

## Management

### Immediate first aid — compression

1. Sit upright with mouth open and head tilted forward at about 45°
2. Pinch the soft part of the nose (the nasal alae) firmly with fingers for **10 minutes** without releasing
3. Breathe through the mouth

### What your doctor may consider

- **Vasoconstrictor/local anaesthetic:** Cotton wool pledgets soaked in lidocaine with adrenaline, or oxymetazoline, placed in the nostril for at least 5 minutes to reduce bleeding and numb the area before further treatment
- **Silver nitrate cautery:** Used for anterior nosebleeds once bleeding is controlled — a silver nitrate stick is applied precisely to the bleeding vessel for 1–2 seconds. Only one nostril is treated per session to avoid the risk of septal perforation
- **Topical creams or ointments** (applied twice daily to both nostrils for one week):
  - If no infection: Vaseline or petroleum jelly
  - If infection is present: mupirocin (Foban) three times daily for 1–2 weeks
- **Nasal pack or tampon:** If the above measures fail to control bleeding, a nasal pack may be inserted before sending the patient to the emergency department

### After treatment

- Continue nasal ointment for a few weeks after any cautery procedure
- Most children grow out of nosebleeds — conservative management is often appropriate
- Discourage nose-picking; in children, keep fingernails short and clean

---

## When Your Doctor May Refer

Your doctor may consider specialist ENT (ear, nose, and throat) assessment if:

- Nosebleeds recur despite the above treatment
- A nasal pack was required
- A nasal mass or septal perforation is found
- You need definitive treatment to safely continue anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication
