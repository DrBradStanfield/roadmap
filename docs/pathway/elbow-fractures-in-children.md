# Elbow Fractures in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of elbow fractures in children, including when urgent specialist assessment is required.

---

## Background

Elbow fractures are common in children, accounting for 10% of all childhood fractures.

Complications can occur because of the close proximity of the median and ulnar nerves and the brachial artery to the fractured bone(s). These complications may not occur immediately but can develop some hours after trauma.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if there are signs of:

- Loss or reduced pulse in the hand or fingers (neurovascular compromise)
- Signs of compartment syndrome: increasing pain despite immobilisation, swelling, pain on passive movement of the fingers, or loss of sensation
- Open fracture (skin wound over the fracture site)
- Suspected supracondylar fracture grade 2 or 3 (significant displacement)
- Suspected lateral condylar fracture

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Mechanism of injury — particularly fall onto outstretched hand (FOOSH) or direct elbow trauma
- Site(s) of pain
- Hand dominance

Physical examination will include:

- Swelling, decreased range of motion, effusion suggesting bleeding into the joint (haemarthrosis)
- Skin wounds — a skin wound may suggest an open fracture
- **Neurovascular status** — checked before and after any treatment:
  - Neurological impairment below the injury (sensation and movement)
  - Peripheral pulses below the injury
  - Perfusion (colour, temperature, capillary refill) below the injury
- Other injuries — wrist, shoulder

Your doctor will arrange an X-ray. Children under 12 years are almost always X-rayed after elbow injury, as they rarely sprain ligaments.

### X-ray Interpretation

X-ray changes can be subtle, and there may be more than one fracture site or an associated dislocation.

**Radial head position:** In normal alignment, a line drawn along the radial neck touches the capitellum on both AP and lateral views. The radial head can be dislocated or subluxated without an obvious fracture if there is plastic deformation of the ulna.

**Ossification centres (C-R-I-T-O-L):** There are 6 ossification centres around the elbow that appear in strict order — Capitellum, Radial, Internal (medial epicondyle), Trochlea, Olecranon, Lateral epicondyle — at approximately 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, and 11 years of age. These can be confused with fracture fragments.

**Fat pad sign:** On a lateral X-ray with the elbow flexed at 90°, a positive fat pad sign (elevated anterior fat pad, or a visible posterior fat pad) indicates blood in the joint from an intra-articular fracture. A visible posterior fat pad without an obvious fracture should be treated as an occult fracture.

## Fracture Types

### Supracondylar Fracture

The most common elbow fracture in children aged 3–11 years. Usually caused by a fall onto an outstretched hand when the elbow is locked in extension. There is often tenderness and swelling over the distal humerus.

Graded by displacement:
- **Grade 1** — minimal or no displacement (may only show a positive fat pad sign on X-ray)
- **Grade 2** — angulated but no translation
- **Grade 3** — fully displaced, translated, or rotated

Almost all children with this fracture need hospital assessment because of the risk of compartment syndrome. Your doctor will always check the brachial artery pulse and median nerve function.

**Compartment syndrome** is a serious complication where increased pressure within the forearm causes loss of blood supply. It typically presents within 48 hours with increasing pain despite immobilisation, swelling, pain on passive finger movement, or numbness. This requires urgent surgical decompression.

### Lateral Condylar Fracture

Common in children aged 4–10 years. Occurs when a varus force is applied to the extended elbow (e.g., falling off monkey bars onto an outstretched arm). Can be difficult to diagnose as X-ray changes are subtle and can be confused with ossification centres. If displaced, it can be a serious injury.

### Medial Epicondylar Fracture

Common in adolescent boys. Usually caused by a fall onto an outstretched hand. This is typically an avulsion injury and can easily be missed on X-ray — look carefully. May be associated with an elbow dislocation. Comparison X-ray with the other elbow may be needed.

### Olecranon Fracture

May be associated with other injuries including radial head dislocation, radial neck fracture, lateral condyle fracture, or Monteggia fracture-dislocation. Always check AP and lateral views.

**Monteggia fracture-dislocation** — dislocation of the radial head combined with fracture of the ulna. Peak incidence ages 4–10 years. Reduction is always required. The most frequent complication is delayed or missed diagnosis.

### Proximal Radius Fracture

Less common in children. When it occurs, it more often involves the radial neck rather than the radial head.

- **Radial head fracture** — less common in children. Check for pain on pronation or supination, and neurovascular function. Look for other injuries.
- **Radial neck fracture** — more common in children. Check neurovascular function. Frequently associated with other elbow injuries (e.g., olecranon fracture).

## Management

Your doctor will assess and manage according to fracture type. Complications from elbow fractures can result in permanent disability, so specialist involvement is often required.

### General Measures

- Provide age-appropriate and fracture-appropriate pain relief
- Apply backslab splinting to relieve pain
- Encourage the child to move fingers, wrist, and shoulder through full range of motion 3–4 times per day
- If uncertain whether a fracture is present (negative X-ray, low-force mechanism, no nerve or arterial damage), your doctor may apply a backslab and reassess the next day

### By Fracture Type

**Lateral condylar fracture:**
Your doctor will seek urgent specialist paediatric orthopaedic assessment — this applies even if there is uncertainty about whether a significant injury has occurred.

**Medial epicondylar fracture:**
- If undisplaced: your doctor may treat with an above-elbow cast for 1–2 weeks
- If displaced: urgent specialist paediatric orthopaedic assessment is needed

**Olecranon fracture:**
- If isolated and undisplaced: your doctor may immobilise in a backslab with the elbow at 90° flexion in a sling, with fracture clinic follow-up in 1 week and a repeat X-ray
- If displaced or combined with another fracture: urgent specialist paediatric orthopaedic assessment is needed

**Proximal radius fracture:**
- *Radial head — undisplaced:* Your doctor may treat conservatively with backslab and early mobilisation in 2–3 days, with orthopaedic advice to confirm optimal treatment
- *Radial head — displaced:* Your doctor will seek paediatric orthopaedic advice
- *Radial head still stiff after 6+ weeks:* Non-urgent orthopaedic assessment
- *Radial neck — less than 20° angulation:* Your doctor may treat conservatively in an above-elbow plaster for 3–4 weeks
- *Radial neck — more than 20° angulation:* Your doctor will seek paediatric orthopaedic advice

**Supracondylar fracture:**
- *Grade 1 (not sent to hospital):* Your doctor may immobilise in backslab and arrange fracture clinic follow-up within a few days. Parents or caregivers should be advised to call the doctor or go to the emergency department immediately if there is increasing pain or swelling. X-ray at 7–10 days to exclude displacement; reassess union at 3–4 weeks. Recovery of full elbow extension may take 3 months.
- *Grade 2 or 3:* Urgent specialist paediatric orthopaedic assessment for consideration of surgery
