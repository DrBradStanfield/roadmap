# Incidental Renal Lesion

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers renal lesions found unexpectedly on imaging (such as ultrasound or CT) performed for another reason.

---

## Background

Both benign and malignant lesions may be found incidentally on imaging, and it can be difficult to distinguish between them. Up to 50% of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) cases are found this way. Most lesions have no associated symptoms.

**Possible malignant lesions include:**
- Renal cell carcinoma (RCC)
- Urothelial carcinoma
- Metastatic lesion
- Lymphoma

**Possible benign lesions include:**
- Simple cysts (including those from end-stage renal disease or polycystic kidney disease)
- Angiomyolipoma (AML)
- Renal adenoma
- Abscess
- Oncocytoma
- Vascular malformation

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Any renal or systemic symptoms (most lesions cause none)
- Past history of malignancy or kidney disease
- Family history of kidney disease
- Personal risk factors for renal malignancy, including:
  - Smoking
  - Hypertension
  - Workplace chemical exposures
  - Medications
  - Familial disorders (von Hippel-Lindau disease, tuberous sclerosis, sickle cell trait/disease)

Physical examination typically includes assessment for:

- Local, metastatic, or paraneoplastic features — including hypertension, anaemia, hypercalcaemia, fever, weight loss, polycythaemia, raised ESR, or abnormal liver enzymes
- The classic triad of flank pain, haematuria, and a palpable abdominal mass (when present, this suggests advanced renal malignancy)

## Management

Your doctor will manage the lesion according to its type:

### Renal Cysts — Bosniak Classification

Radiologists and urologists classify renal cysts using the Bosniak system:

| Class | Description | Next steps |
|-------|-------------|------------|
| **1** | Simple benign cyst — no septations or echoes | No further investigation or follow-up needed |
| **2** | Minimally complex — a few thin septa, thin calcification, or non-enhancing lesion <3 cm | No further imaging or follow-up needed |
| **2F** | More complex — increased/thicker septa, thicker calcifications, or non-enhancing lesion ≥3 cm | Your doctor may consider further imaging and urology referral for surveillance |
| **3** | Indeterminate — may be malignant; thickened septa or nodularity with measurable enhancement | Your doctor may consider further imaging and urology referral |
| **4** | Clearly malignant | Your doctor will arrange further imaging and urology referral |

Note: Ultrasound cannot always classify renal lesions using the Bosniak system.

### Solid Renal Mass

- **If malignancy is suspected** (indicated in the radiology report): Your doctor may consider urgent further imaging, urology referral noting high cancer suspicion, and blood tests including renal function (eGFR), midstream urine, and liver function tests.
- **If there is also a bladder mass suspicious for bladder cancer:** Your doctor may arrange urine cytology samples, but this will not delay the urology referral.

### Echogenic Renal Lesions

- ≤10 mm: No further imaging is usually required
- >10 mm: Your doctor may consider CT renal tract or MRI, but only if recommended in the radiology report

### Angiomyolipoma (AML) Surveillance

Your doctor may consider urology referral for a confirmed AML ≥10 mm if any of the following apply:

- Multiple echogenic lesions with suspected or known tuberous sclerosis
- Patient under 30 years of age
- Female patient of child-bearing age
- Symptomatic (e.g., pain or haematuria)

For all other patients, management is guided by AML size:

- ≤20 mm: No follow-up required
- 21–39 mm: Your doctor may arrange an ultrasound in 5 years, then manage based on size at that time
- ≥40 mm: Your doctor will refer to urology

### Before Further CT Imaging

If CT with intravenous contrast is needed, your doctor will arrange an eGFR (kidney function test) if one has not been done in the past 2 weeks.
