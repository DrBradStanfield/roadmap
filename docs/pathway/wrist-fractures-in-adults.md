# Wrist Fractures in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of wrist fractures in adults, including which fracture types need urgent specialist review.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. Take a history of the injury — mechanism, timing, and symptoms.
2. Examine the wrist:
   - Check neurovascular status (circulation, sensation, movement).
   - Check for tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox, which may indicate a scaphoid fracture.
3. Arrange wrist X-ray and/or scaphoid views.
   - If there is obvious deformity or signs of fracture, pain relief and immobilisation may be arranged before X-ray.
   - If there is concern about scapholunate widening or carpal misalignment, clenched fist views may be requested.
4. Look for an associated avulsion of the ulnar styloid process.

---

## Types of Wrist Fracture

Common wrist fractures include:

- **Colles' fracture** — distal radius with dorsal displacement of fragments. Most require manipulation.
- **Smith's fracture** — distal radius with volar displacement of fragments; often seen in younger patients after high-energy trauma.
- **Scaphoid fracture** — predominantly seen in teenagers and adults after a fall onto an outstretched hand (FOOSH). May present with relatively little pain. Complications of missed or poorly managed scaphoid fractures include:
  - Delayed union or non-union — most commonly due to delayed or inadequate stabilisation.
  - Avascular necrosis — more common when the fracture is more proximal due to reduced blood supply.
- **Barton's fracture** — fracture-dislocation of either the volar or dorsal lip of the radius; unstable and usually requires surgery.
- **Chauffeur's fracture** (radial styloid fracture) — may have an associated scapholunate or carpal ligament injury; usually not displaced.
- **Ulnar styloid fracture** — often associated with other wrist injuries.

## Management

Many wrist fractures will require manipulation and reduction under analgesia. Your healthcare provider will manage the fracture based on its type and stability.

### General Principles

- If the fracture is **unstable**, urgent orthopaedic assessment will be arranged. Pain relief will be provided for transport.
- If presentation is after hours, your doctor may apply a back slab and arrange orthopaedic review the following day.
- If the fracture occurred with **minimal trauma** (e.g., a fall from standing height), your doctor may consider whether osteoporosis is a contributing factor and discuss bone health assessment and fracture prevention.

### Colles' Fracture

- Most require manipulation — your doctor will discuss whether acute orthopaedic review is needed.
- If the fracture is in an acceptable position, it is placed in a Colles' plaster (below-elbow plaster of Paris with palmar flexion and ulnar deviation).
- Follow-up X-ray is arranged at 1–2 weeks.
- Plaster is typically removed at 6 weeks.

### Smith's Fracture

- If manipulation is required, orthopaedic review will be arranged.
- Immobilised in an above-elbow plaster of Paris with the wrist supinated for 3 weeks, then changed to a below-elbow plaster if stable.
- Plaster removed at 6 weeks.

### Scaphoid Fracture

- If a fracture is seen on initial X-ray, your doctor will immobilise the wrist in a split scaphoid cast or backslab (slight palmar flexion and radial deviation; thumb not included).
- **Even if X-ray is normal**, a fall onto an outstretched hand with scaphoid tenderness is treated as a possible scaphoid fracture and immobilised for 10 days.
- Your doctor may arrange an MRI if the initial X-ray is normal, as MRI is more sensitive for scaphoid fractures and can detect soft tissue or ligament injuries.
- If MRI is not done and pain and tenderness persist, a repeat X-ray at 10–14 days is arranged.
- **Proximal scaphoid fractures** or fractures displaced 1 mm or more are referred for orthopaedic assessment for possible surgery.

**By fracture location:**
- *Mid-waist or distal fracture:* Below-elbow cast; changed to fibreglass at 1 week if initially in plaster. At 6 weeks, cast removed and X-ray taken — if less than 50% healing or ongoing discomfort, orthopaedic review is arranged.
- *Scaphoid tubercle fracture:* Below-elbow plaster for 4–5 weeks; orthopaedic review for consideration of operative vs non-operative management.
- *Partial scapholunate ligament injury:* Managed with a Tubigrip bandage and referral to hand physiotherapy for a splint and rehabilitation programme.
- *Acute complete scapholunate ligament rupture* (gap ≥3 mm between scaphoid and lunate): Orthopaedic assessment arranged.

### Barton's Fracture

Most require surgery — urgent orthopaedic assessment will be arranged.

### Chauffeur's Fracture (Radial Styloid)

- If more than 2 mm intra-articular displacement, urgent orthopaedic assessment is arranged.
- If undisplaced, treated with a Colles' plaster.

---

## Rehabilitation

- **Early mobilisation** of fingers, elbow, and shoulder is encouraged from the time of injury.
- After plaster removal, your doctor will refer you for physiotherapy to begin an active exercise programme.
- Physiotherapy assessment may also be helpful if there are barriers to recovery, reduced strength or range of movement, or persistent pain.
- If everyday activities such as self-care, driving, or work are affected, occupational therapy assessment may be considered.
- If returning to work is a concern, work rehabilitation assessment can be arranged.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Review

Discuss with your doctor or seek urgent assessment if:

- The fracture is unstable or manipulation is required
- Barton's fracture is suspected
- Radial styloid fracture with more than 2 mm intra-articular displacement
- Proximal scaphoid fracture or scaphoid fracture displaced 1 mm or more
- Suspected acute complete scapholunate ligament rupture
- Less than 50% healing on X-ray at 6 weeks
- Possible scaphoid fracture remains symptomatic despite normal X-rays at 1 month
- Suspected scapholunate widening or carpal misalignment
