# Acute Abdominal Pain in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to acute abdominal pain in children under 15 years — assessing the likely cause and determining urgency.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance immediately if:

- Signs of shock, sepsis, or peritonitis
- Child significantly distressed by pain
- Bile-stained (green) vomiting — especially in a newborn
- Acute scrotal pain

Other warning signs that suggest a serious condition:

- Child looks acutely unwell
- Significant vomiting in an infant
- Unable to walk or move due to abdominal pain

---

## Background

The key consideration in acute abdominal pain is distinguishing between surgical and non-surgical causes.

- A wide range of abdominal and non-abdominal conditions can cause acute pain in children
- Non-specific, self-resolving pain is the most common presentation
- Functional abdominal pain is common but is a diagnosis of exclusion
- Newborns often present due to caregiver concern and require a thorough assessment

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

### Take a history

**Pain characteristics:**
- Onset, pattern, location, intensity, character, and what makes it better or worse
- How pain affects the child's activity, behaviour, oral intake, and sleep
- Appendicitis typically starts around the umbilicus and migrates to the right lower abdomen over time
- Episodic or colicky pain — consider intussusception, mesenteric adenitis, gastroenteritis, constipation
- Sudden severe pain — consider testicular or ovarian torsion, intussusception, or intestinal perforation
- Shoulder-tip pain — consider blood under the diaphragm after trauma

**Associated symptoms:**
- Fever — high fever without abdominal signs suggests an alternative cause such as pneumonia
- Vomiting:
  - Usually comes before abdominal pain in non-surgical conditions
  - Bile-stained vomiting suggests malrotation volvulus (especially newborns)
  - Recurrent projectile non-bilious vomiting in infants aged 2–6 weeks with weight loss suggests pyloric stenosis
- Bowel movements — frequency, consistency, blood or mucus; "redcurrant jelly stool" is a late sign of intussusception
- Urinary symptoms — frequent urination and pain suggest urinary tract infection; polyuria with thirst or weight loss suggests diabetic ketoacidosis
- Respiratory symptoms — cough or increased breathing effort suggests pneumonia

**Other history:**
- Food intake before and since the pain started
- Recent abdominal trauma
- Previous abdominal surgery
- Past episodes of constipation or recurrent abdominal pain
- Underlying conditions associated with rarer causes (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease, diabetes, cystic fibrosis, liver disease)
- In adolescent females: last menstrual period and gynaecological history — pregnancy-related and ovarian causes must be considered

### Perform examination

Your doctor will:
- Observe the child's gait, posture, and level of distress
- Check vital signs (temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, blood pressure)
- Assess hydration and general appearance (pallor, jaundice)
- Gently examine the abdomen, watching for guarding, rigidity, and tenderness on palpation or percussion
- Check for signs of peritonism — child won't want to move, can't walk or hop comfortably, pain on abdominal percussion
- Always examine testes and scrotum in boys — including cremasteric reflex (absent in testicular torsion)
- Check for hernia
- Perform cardiovascular, ENT, and respiratory examination

### Consider investigations

Many children do not need investigations. Your doctor may consider:
- Blood sugar level (to check for diabetic ketoacidosis)
- Urine dipstick (for infection, glucose, ketones)
- Urine pregnancy test if indicated
- Abdominal ultrasound or chest X-ray if a specific cause is suspected

## Possible Causes by Age Group

### Newborns (under 4 weeks)
- Volvulus due to malrotation — can cause bile-stained vomiting
- Hirschsprung's disease
- Incarcerated hernia

### Infants
- **Intussusception** — one section of bowel telescopes into another; most common ages 2 months to 2 years; may cause "redcurrant jelly stool"
- **Pyloric stenosis** — projectile vomiting at 2–6 weeks with poor weight gain
- Volvulus — vomiting with distended abdomen
- Incarcerated hernia — groin or scrotal swelling
- Meckel's diverticulum — usually presents with rectal bleeding

### Preschool age
- Appendicitis
- Intussusception
- Testicular torsion — acute severe pain, nausea, vomiting, inability to walk
- Trauma

### School age
- Appendicitis
- Testicular torsion
- Trauma (e.g., handlebar injury)
- Non-specific acute abdominal pain / mesenteric adenitis

### Adolescents
- Appendicitis
- Testicular or ovarian torsion
- Trauma
- Ovarian cyst rupture or torsion — acute severe pain, may resemble appendicitis
- Ectopic pregnancy (adolescent females)
- Biliary tract disorders (cholecystitis or gallstones) — more likely if obese or has haemolytic disease

### Common non-surgical causes (all ages)
- **Non-specific abdominal pain** — most common; often viral, post-gastroenteritis, or constipation
- **Mesenteric adenitis** — right lower quadrant pain associated with recent upper respiratory infection; loose stools, mild fever; no signs of peritonism
- Constipation
- Gastroenteritis
- Urinary tract infection
- Functional abdominal pain
- Pneumonia (basal) — consider if respiratory symptoms present
- Tonsillitis
- Diabetic ketoacidosis — weight loss, polyuria, polydipsia
- Pancreatitis (rare) — raised lipase
- Henoch-Schönlein purpura — palpable purpura on buttocks and lower legs, abdominal pain, joint pain; most common ages 2–8
- Inflammatory bowel disease — fever, bloody diarrhoea, weight loss
- Food allergy or intolerance
- Toxin exposure or overdose

## Appendicitis — What to Look For

Appendicitis is a common surgical cause in children. Features include:

**History:**
- Central, constant pain that moves to the right lower abdomen
- Pain worsens with movement, coughing, or sneezing
- Associated with loss of appetite, nausea, and possible fever
- May or may not have vomiting or diarrhoea

**Examination findings:**
- Child appears distressed and limits movement
- Tenderness in the right lower abdomen
- Guarding, rebound, and percussion tenderness
- Rovsing's sign — pain in the right lower abdomen when pressing the left lower abdomen
- Pain on right hip flexion or extension

Atypical presentations exist — retrocaecal appendix may have absent guarding; pelvic appendix may cause vague lower abdominal pain or diarrhoea. In adolescent females it can be difficult to distinguish from ovarian causes.

## Management

### If red flags are present

Your doctor will call an ambulance and arrange urgent hospital assessment. While waiting:
- The child will be kept nil by mouth
- Oxygen and IV fluids may be given if needed
- Pain relief (analgesia) can be safely given — there is no evidence that it should be withheld

### If mildly unwell but no red flags

Your doctor may:
- Observe the child in the clinic for a period to watch for evolving signs
- Arrange investigations based on findings
- Request specialist advice or hospital assessment if the diagnosis is unclear, another serious condition is suspected, or the child is not improving

Conditions your doctor may manage according to their own pathway:
- Gastroenteritis
- Constipation
- Urinary tract infection
- Tonsillitis or pneumonia
- Functional abdominal pain

### Non-specific abdominal pain or mesenteric adenitis — community management

Your doctor may:
- Reassure you that there is no acute surgical cause
- Advise clear fluids and regular pain relief
- Arrange a follow-up review
- Advise you to return promptly if pain worsens or new symptoms develop
- Provide a fact sheet on abdominal pain and after-hours care contact details
