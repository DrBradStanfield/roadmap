# Burn Injuries

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial assessment and management of burn injuries in adults and children, including first aid, wound care, and when specialist assessment is needed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance (111) if any of the following apply:

- Burns greater than 10% total body surface area (TBSA) in an adult, or greater than 5% TBSA in a child
- Full-thickness burns greater than 5% TBSA in any patient
- Burns involving the face, hands, feet, perineum, genitalia, or major joints
- Electrical or chemical burns
- Suspected inhalation injury
- Circumferential burns to limbs or chest
- Patient younger than 2 years or older than 70 years
- Burns associated with other trauma
- Suspected non-accidental injury
- Burns causing uncontrolled pain or toxic shock
- Patient with pre-existing medical conditions that could complicate recovery

**Also seek emergency assessment if at any point the patient develops:**

- Temperature of 38.9°C or higher
- Systolic blood pressure of 90 mmHg or lower (or below the 5th percentile by age in children)
- Diffuse macular erythroderma (widespread rash)
- Signs of dysfunction in 3 or more organ systems

---

## Background

Severe burns are among the most serious injuries. Shortly after a burn, fluid shifts from the circulation into the surrounding tissues due to altered capillary permeability. If more than 15% of the body surface is burned, this progressive fluid loss can lead to hypovolaemic shock.

Burns can take up to three days to fully declare their depth and extent — initial assessments may underestimate severity. Any burn mismanaged early can cause significant lifelong problems.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess:

1. **Airway, breathing, and circulation first** — before focusing on the burn itself
2. **History:**
   - Cause of burn (flame, scald, electricity, chemical — including chemical name if applicable)
   - Time and place of injury (burns in an enclosed space raise suspicion for inhalation injury)
   - First aid measures already taken
   - Any associated trauma
   - Past medical history and tetanus immunisation status
3. **Non-accidental injury** — your doctor will consider this if there is a delay in seeking help, inconsistent history, unusual burn patterns (e.g. glove-and-stocking immersion lines), or past history of inflicted injury

**Burn depth** is assessed by examining colour, blisters, capillary refill, and sensation:

| Depth | Appearance | Sensation | Expected Healing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Epidermal (e.g. sunburn) | Red, no blisters | Painful | 7–10 days, no scarring |
| Superficial dermal (e.g. scald splash) | Pale pink, small blisters | Painful | Within 2 weeks |
| Mid dermal (e.g. flame, oil) | Dark pink or blotchy, large blisters | Variable | 14–21 days, moderate scar risk |
| Deep dermal (e.g. flame, grease) | Blotchy red or fixed staining | Absent | Slow; usually needs grafting |
| Full thickness (e.g. immersion scald, flame, chemical, high-voltage electricity) | White, black, or brown/waxy/charred | Absent | Grafting needed if >1 cm |

**Total body surface area (TBSA):** Only de-epithelialised areas are counted — epidermal burns are excluded. A patient's palm represents approximately 1% TBSA.

## Management

### First Aid (Apply Immediately or Within 3 Hours)

- Apply cool running water (8–25°C, ideally 15°C) for at least 20 minutes — tap water is fine
- Do not use ice
- Remove stuck clothing under running water
- For chemical burns: irrigate copiously for at least one hour
- Remove jewellery early and elevate burnt limbs to reduce swelling
- Keep the patient warm to prevent hypothermia

**Your doctor may consider:**

- Intravenous morphine for pain relief in acute burns (preferred over intramuscular or oral routes)
- Tetanus-prone wound management if indicated
- Eye irrigation and chloramphenicol drops if the eyes are involved
- Elevating the head of the bed if the face, head, or neck is burned

---

### Wound Dressing — by Burn Depth

**Epidermal burns:**
- Apply a water-based, non-scented moisturiser
- Advise the patient to return if blisters or skin loss develop

**Superficial or mid-dermal burns:**
- Leave small blisters intact; debride blisters over joints or if they restrict movement; snip large tense blisters
- Apply a low- or non-adherent moisture-controlled dressing (e.g. Cuticerin, Adaptic, or Atrauman tulle with an absorbent pad)
- Silicone non-adherent options: Mepitel, Mepilex, Mepilex Border, Silflex
- **Do not use silver sulfadiazine cream (SSD/Flamazine)** — it is toxic to fibroblasts, delays healing, obscures wound depth assessment, and requires painful daily changes

**Deep dermal or full-thickness burns:**
- Your doctor will consider specialist plastic surgery assessment if the burn is more than 1–2 cm wide
- For smaller burns: apply an antimicrobial silver dressing (e.g. Acticoat or Mepilex Ag)
  - Acticoat: change every 3 days (Acticoat 7 every 7 days); moisten with water, not saline
  - Mepilex Ag: keep dry
- **Do not use silver sulfadiazine cream (SSD/Flamazine)**

**Mixed depth burns:**
- Your doctor may consider an antimicrobial silver dressing

---

### Follow-Up Review

**At 48–72 hours:**
- Monitor for signs of infection or depth progression
- Consider risk of toxic shock — even in small burns in children
- If wound is healing: continue current dressing
- If wound is deteriorating or infected: switch to antimicrobial dressing; consider oral antibiotics; review again in 48 hours
- If deep dermal or full thickness: your doctor may consider early specialist referral and will continue a silver dressing for 7 days

**At 7 days:**
- If healing well: continue dressings
- If not progressing: your doctor will seek plastic surgery advice

**Ongoing:**
- If not healed by 14 days: your doctor will seek specialist advice
- For the year after injury: use daily moisturiser; protect the burn area from sun with sunscreen
- Scar management: gently stretch and massage the scar area; use sunscreen; your doctor may refer to occupational therapy if scars become hypertrophic or develop a contracture
