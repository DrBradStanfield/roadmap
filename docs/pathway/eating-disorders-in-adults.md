# Eating Disorders in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway applies to adults aged 18 years and older. Eating disorders are psychological illnesses characterised by abnormal or disturbed eating habits, with significant physical and mental health consequences. Early diagnosis and treatment — ideally within the first 3 years of illness onset — are associated with better outcomes.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Attention

Tell your doctor immediately or seek emergency care if any of the following are present:

- Rapid or severe weight loss
- Acute medical instability (e.g., collapse, seizure, chest pain, irregular heartbeat)
- Suicidal ideation or intent
- Electrolyte abnormalities (low potassium, phosphate, or sodium)

---

## Background

Eating disorders affect both men and women. While diagnosed rates are higher in females, disordered eating behaviours (bingeing, purging, laxative misuse, fasting for weight loss) are nearly as common in men.

Patients with eating disorders may be difficult to engage — they often don't recognise the need for treatment, or deny the problem exists. This is part of the illness, not a character failing.

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you using the following approach.

### SCOFF Screening Questionnaire

Your doctor may use the SCOFF questionnaire. Score 1 point for every "yes". A score of 2 or more suggests a likely case of anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa.

1. Do you make yourself **S**ick because you feel uncomfortably full?
2. Do you worry you have lost **C**ontrol over how much you eat?
3. Have you recently lost more than **O**ne stone (6.4 kg) in a 3-month period?
4. Do you believe yourself to be **F**at when others say you are too thin?
5. Would you say that **F**ood dominates your life?

If the score is 2 or more, your doctor will ask follow-up questions about your diet, weight, binge eating, purging, laxative or diuretic use, compulsive exercise, guilt around food, and how these affect your day-to-day life.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

**Suggestive features:**
- Unexplained weight loss
- Purging, bingeing, or fasting
- Excessive exercise
- Misuse of laxatives or diuretics
- Distorted body image
- Personal or family history of eating disorders

**Weight:**
- Pre-illness weight and rate of weight loss
- Concerns about body shape or weight

**Physical activity and behaviours:**
- Meal patterns, dietary restrictions, fasting, ritualistic eating
- Binge eating (frequency, amount, triggers)
- Compensatory behaviours — self-induced vomiting, misuse of medications (laxatives, diuretics, insulin, thyroxine, weight-loss preparations)
- Exercise frequency, duration, and type
- Disruption to day-to-day living

**Physical symptoms:**
- Dizziness, faintness, collapse, increasing fatigue
- Feeling the cold
- Amenorrhoea or irregular menstrual cycle (note: regular withdrawal bleeds may occur with oral contraceptives)

**Psychiatric and medical history:**
- Prior treatment of an eating disorder
- Suicidal thoughts, mood disorder, or anxiety
- Increasing social isolation
- Metabolic conditions (e.g., type 1 diabetes, hypothyroidism) — starvation and medication misuse carry more serious consequences in these conditions

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will check:

**Baseline parameters:**
- Height, weight, and BMI
- Temperature
- Hydration status
- Lying and standing blood pressure (after 2 minutes standing)
- Lying and standing pulse and rhythm (after 2 minutes standing)
- Squat test (ability to rise unassisted from squatting)

**Signs of acute medical instability** (in adults aged 17+):

- Severe or sudden weight loss:
  - More than 1 kg per week for more than 2 weeks
  - 15–20% of body weight in 3 months
- Cardiac instability, including syncope:
  - Resting systolic blood pressure below 80 mmHg, or postural drop of more than 20 mmHg
  - Bradycardia (resting heart rate below 40 beats per minute)
  - Postural change in heart rate of more than 20 beats per minute (lying to standing)
  - Chest pain, significant oedema (risk of mitral valve prolapse and pericardial effusion)
  - ECG: prolonged QTc greater than 500 ms, or ventricular arrhythmia
- Neurological symptoms: seizures, confusion, ophthalmoplegia, ataxia, tetany
- Severe dehydration
- Muscle weakness or rapidly diminishing exercise tolerance
- Hypothermia (body temperature below 35°C)
- Biochemical abnormalities:
  - Blood glucose below 2.5 mmol/L with symptoms (sweating, palpitations, hunger, dizziness, confusion, seizure)
  - Potassium below 2.5 mmol/L
  - Phosphate below 0.7 mmol/L
  - Hyponatraemia
  - Severe neutropenia (neutrophils below 1.0 × 10⁹/L)
  - eGFR below 60

**Medical complications your doctor will check for:**

- *From dietary restriction:* weight loss, hypothermia, global weakness, cardiac instability (dehydration, arrhythmias, heart failure, mitral valve prolapse, pericardial effusion)
- *From purging:* hypokalaemia, hypophosphataemia, Mallory-Weiss syndrome (oesophageal tears), dental enamel erosion, parotid/salivary gland hypertrophy, calluses on dorsum of hand, GORD, constipation, diarrhoea and malabsorption, ECG changes (prolonged QTc, U waves)
- *From ongoing malnutrition:* osteoporosis, myocardial atrophy, mitral valve prolapse, cytopenias (especially neutropenia), gastroparesis, constipation

### Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

**Blood tests** (depending on degree of malnutrition and compensatory behaviours):
- Full blood count (FBC) — particularly neutropenia
- Electrolytes — particularly potassium
- Renal function
- Glucose
- Calcium, phosphate, magnesium
- Thyroid function (T3 is reduced with malnutrition)
- Creatinine kinase (elevated with excessive exercise)
- Bicarbonate (elevated with purging; reduced with laxatives)
- Ferritin, B12, folate
- AST, ALT, GGT
- Luteinising hormone, follicle-stimulating hormone, estradiol
- Protein, albumin

**Baseline ECG**, looking for:
- Bradycardia
- Signs of hypokalaemia
- Arrhythmias
- Prolonged QTc interval (some medications used in treatment may further prolong QT)

## Conditions

### Anorexia Nervosa

- Usually earlier onset than other eating disorders (average age 14–16 years)
- Significant weight loss, intense fear of weight gain, and extreme food restriction
- Body image distortion or over-evaluation of body shape and weight
- Two subtypes:
  - **Restrictive** — calorie restriction only
  - **Purging** — calorie restriction plus regular purging or binge eating in the past 3 months

### Bulimia Nervosa

- Weight may fluctuate slightly above and below a healthy BMI
- Recurrent episodes of binge eating (large amounts of food in a discrete time period, with a sense of loss of control) at least once a week for 3 months
- Inappropriate compensatory behaviours to prevent weight gain: self-induced vomiting, laxative/diuretic misuse, fasting, or excessive exercise
- Self-evaluation is heavily influenced by body shape and weight

### Binge Eating Disorder

- Rapidly ingesting very large volumes of food in discrete time frames, followed by guilt, remorse, and self-disgust
- Unable to control the length or frequency of binges
- Binges used to manage stress or psychological distress
- **No compensatory behaviours** (unlike bulimia), leading to weight gain
- High distress and secrecy about eating behaviours

### Avoidant / Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID)

- Avoids or restricts food to the extent of significant weight loss, nutritional deficiencies, dependence on supplements or feeding tubes, or marked disruption to psychosocial functioning
- Not motivated by weight or body image concerns
- May lack interest in food, dislike sensory characteristics, or fear choking, gagging, or vomiting

### Other Specified Feeding or Eating Disorder (OSFED)

- Pattern of disordered eating that causes significant distress but does not fully meet criteria for anorexia nervosa or bulimia nervosa
- Examples:
  - Atypical anorexia nervosa (criteria met but weight is within or above normal range)
  - Low-frequency or short-duration bulimia nervosa or binge eating disorder
  - Purging disorder (recurrent purging without binge eating)

## Management

Your doctor will manage your care based on the specific condition identified.

### Admission Criteria (Hospital)

Your doctor will arrange emergency assessment if any of the following are present:

| Situation | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Life-threatening weight loss | BMI below 13, or weight loss of 20% or more over 3 months with severe restriction |
| Acute medical complications | Syncope, seizures, cardiac failure |
| Severe dehydration | May require brief admission for rehydration |
| Hypoglycaemia | Blood glucose below 2.5 mmol/L with symptoms |
| Electrolyte/blood abnormality | Potassium below 2.5 mmol/L; Phosphate below 0.7 mmol/L; eGFR below 60; AST or ALT above 500 units/L; Neutrophils below 1.0 |
| Physiological instability | Heart rate below 40 bpm; Systolic BP below 80 mmHg; Postural tachycardia (HR rise >20 bpm on standing); Temperature below 35°C |
| Abnormal ECG | Arrhythmia; Prolonged QTc above 500 milliseconds |

If you are suicidal or there are concerns about acute psychiatric risk or self-harm, your doctor will arrange urgent mental health assessment.

### Anorexia Nervosa

Your doctor may consider:

1. Providing information and support; directing you to online cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) modules
2. Referring to mental health and eating disorders services for specialist assessment
3. Scheduling regular reviews — initially weekly, then monthly as clinically appropriate
4. Monitoring with physical examination and blood tests at each review
5. Offering nutritional supplements (multivitamins, thiamine, B-complex, fortified drinks) depending on BMI and intake
6. If BMI is below 16 or weight loss is rapid, weekly monitoring for medical complications of dietary restriction
7. Arranging emergency assessment if acute medical instability develops

### Bulimia Nervosa

Your doctor may consider:

1. Providing information and support; directing you to online CBT modules
2. Scheduling regular reviews — initially weekly, then monthly as clinically appropriate
3. Monitoring for purging-related complications (electrolytes, ECG changes)
4. Blood tests initially, then every 1–4 weeks depending on purging frequency
5. Treating electrolyte disturbances; considering regular potassium or phosphate supplementation if needed
6. **SSRI therapy** to reduce binge frequency — your doctor will seek specialist mental health advice before prescribing, as higher doses than usual are typically required
7. Arranging emergency assessment if acute medical instability develops

### Binge Eating Disorder

Your doctor may consider:

1. Providing information and support; directing you to online CBT modules
2. Referring to funded or private cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) or dialectical behavioural therapy (DBT) services — both have good evidence for binge eating disorder
3. Referring to a Health Improvement Practitioner (where available at your practice)

### Avoidant / Restrictive Food Intake Disorder (ARFID)

Your doctor may consider:

1. Providing information and support
2. Monitoring for complications of dietary restriction — weekly if rapid weight loss, up to every 3 months if stable
3. Physical examination and blood tests as above
4. Correcting nutritional deficiencies, introducing new foods, and encouraging weight restoration
5. Managing any underlying anxiety

### Other Specified Feeding or Eating Disorder (OSFED)

Your doctor may consider:

1. Providing information and directing to online CBT resources
2. Monitoring for complications of dietary restriction — weekly if rapid weight loss, up to every 3 months if stable
3. Physical examination and blood tests as above; repeating if initial results are abnormal

### Severe and Enduring Eating Disorders

For patients with long-standing illness, your doctor may:

1. Coordinate care with 3-monthly physical assessments and monitoring for medical complications
2. At every visit, assess: weight, hours of exercise, binge/purge episodes since last visit, vital signs, hydration, mood and suicidal ideation, and arrange FBC (especially neutrophils) every 3 months
3. Focus on quality of life and identifying stressors; consider counselling and support services
4. Monitor for long-term complications: osteoporosis, myocardial atrophy, mitral valve prolapse, cytopenias (especially neutropenia), gastroparesis, constipation
5. If not improving or worsening, refer to specialist mental health and eating disorders services
6. Encourage involvement of a family member or support person in the long-term management plan
