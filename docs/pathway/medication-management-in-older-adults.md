# Medication Management in Older Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the safe use of medicines in older adults — including how doctors review, reduce, and manage medications to reduce harm and improve quality of life.

---

## Background

- Medicine use in older adults is a complex balance between managing disease and avoiding medicine-related problems.
- Older patients experience a disproportionate number of medicine-related adverse events compared to the general population.
- The incidence of adverse drug reactions and interactions increases with the number of medications used.
- **Polypharmacy** (the use of 5 or more drugs) is associated with frailty, falls, cognitive impairment, and functional decline.
- Older patients are under-represented in most drug trials, raising doubts about applying trial results to older or frail individuals with multiple health conditions.
- Many patients are prescribed drugs intended only for a limited time but are never reviewed — SSRIs, proton pump inhibitors, and antipsychotics given for sleep or agitation are common examples.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. **Take a full medication history**, including:
   - All prescription and over-the-counter medications
   - Complementary, alternative, and supplement use
   - Whether every medication has a clear and current reason for use
   - Dose, frequency, and how consistently the medication is being taken
   - How many pharmacies are involved and who else is prescribing

2. **Assess overall health**, including:
   - Physical and cognitive function
   - Patient goals, function, and prognosis
   - Signs or symptoms that may be caused by a medication (see table below)

3. **Check kidney function** — creatinine clearance is used to guide dose adjustments (not laboratory eGFR alone, which can mislead dosing in smaller or larger patients).

### Signs and Symptoms That May Be Medication-Related

Consider current medicines as a possible cause of new symptoms before attributing them to a new illness or ageing.

| Symptom | Medicines that may contribute |
|---|---|
| Agitation and/or tremor | Metoclopramide, prochlorperazine, antipsychotics |
| Ankle swelling | Calcium channel blockers, steroids |
| Bleeding and/or bruising | Aspirin, clopidogrel, NSAIDs, anticoagulants (NOACs, warfarin) |
| Confusion | Anticholinergic medicines, antidepressants, benzodiazepines, opioids |
| Constipation | Calcium supplements, diltiazem, iron supplements, opioids, verapamil |
| Dizziness and/or fainting | Anticholinergics, antidepressants, benzodiazepines, antihypertensives, opioids |
| Impaired physical function | Cumulative anticholinergic and sedative medicine exposure |
| Muscle pain | Statins |
| Nausea | GLP-1 agonists, opioids, NSAIDs, SSRIs, vitamins, chemotherapy, cholinesterase inhibitors |
| Shortness of breath | Beta blockers |
| Urinary incontinence | Antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, diuretics, cholinesterase inhibitors |

Other common medicine-related symptoms to watch for: anxiety, hallucinations, confusion, changes in sexual function, depression, dizziness, tremor, weakness, breathlessness, falls, postural hypotension, bleeding, loss of appetite, rash, tiredness, or reduced alertness.

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following when reviewing your medications:

### Deprescribing

Deprescribing — the supervised reduction or stopping of long-term medications — can reduce adverse drug reactions, falls, and in some cases mortality. General approach:

1. **Prepare** — ideally discuss eventual deprescribing when a drug is first prescribed
2. **Recognise** — polypharmacy, adverse drug reactions, lack of efficacy, or a change in treatment goals (e.g., onset of frailty, dementia, or terminal illness)
3. **Prioritise** — stop one drug at a time; start with the one most likely causing problems; focus on anticholinergic and centrally-acting drugs
4. **Wean carefully** — some medicines must be tapered slowly rather than stopped abruptly:
   - Benzodiazepines
   - Opioids
   - Beta blockers
   - Corticosteroids (prednisone)
   - SSRIs
   - Proton pump inhibitors
   - Levodopa
   - Other CNS-active medicines
5. **Monitor** — watch for withdrawal or discontinuation symptoms (e.g., SSRIs and proton pump inhibitors can cause significant symptoms if stopped abruptly), as well as positive changes in cognition, falls, quality of life, and function

### Medicines to Stop or Substitute

Your doctor will consider whether each medication is still needed, weighing risks against benefits. Medicines that are often reviewed for reduction or stopping:

**Can often be stopped without tapering:**
- Statins (when life expectancy is limited or goals of care have changed)
- Blood sugar-lowering medicines or insulin (when tight control is no longer appropriate)
- Antibiotics prescribed for asymptomatic urinary tract infections

**Commonly require slow tapering:**
- Proton pump inhibitors
- Benzodiazepines and zopiclone
- SSRIs
- Prednisone
- Antipsychotics given for sleep or agitation
- Opioids for non-cancer pain

### Medicines That Should Almost Always Be Avoided in Older Adults

The following are generally considered inappropriate in older adults due to disproportionate risk:

- **Anticholinergics** — can cause confusion, constipation, raised eye pressure, cardiac arrhythmias, and falls
- **Antipsychotics** — can cause sedation, Parkinsonism, anticholinergic effects, falls, and increased risk of vascular events
- **Benzodiazepines** — can cause confusion, sedation, falls, tolerance, and physical dependence
- **NSAIDs** — can cause gastrointestinal bleeding, kidney impairment, fluid retention, and falls
- **Antihistamines** (sedating)
- Amitriptyline, doxepin, imipramine (older tricyclic antidepressants)
- Fluoxetine (long half-life increases fall risk)
- Amiodarone (complex toxicity profile)
- Methyldopa
- Indomethacin
- Nitrofurantoin (avoid for long-term use due to pulmonary and hepatic toxicity)
- Oxybutynin (anticholinergic burden)
- Dextropropoxyphene

### Potentially Harmful Drug Combinations

Your doctor will check for these:

| Drug combination | Risk |
|---|---|
| ACE inhibitor + angiotensin receptor blocker (ARB) | Low blood pressure, electrolyte disturbance, kidney failure |
| Verapamil or diltiazem + beta blocker | Low blood pressure, bradycardia |
| SSRIs + tramadol and/or other antidepressants | Serotonin syndrome |
| Anticoagulant + antiplatelet agents | Increased bleeding risk |
| NSAIDs + ACE inhibitors + diuretics | Kidney failure |

### General Prescribing Principles for Older Adults

- Treatment goals are identified in consultation with the patient and carers — quality of life may be more important than statistical reduction of a future event
- Non-drug alternatives are considered first where possible
- Start with a low dose and titrate slowly — the right dose is the lowest that achieves the desired effect
- Side effects and expected benefits are discussed before starting any new medication
- Prescribing is coordinated with all members of the patient's care team

### Improving Adherence

Your doctor may suggest:
- Simplifying the medication regimen
- Using dose administration aids (e.g., blister or compliance packaging)
- Involving carers to assist with medication management

### Medication Management Review

A pharmacist-led medication management review may be arranged to provide an independent assessment of your medicines.
