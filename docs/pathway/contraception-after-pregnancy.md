# Contraception After Pregnancy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers contraception after birth, miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, abortion, and gestational trophoblastic disease (GTD).

---

## Background

Contraception counselling should be a routine part of antenatal care. Most contraceptive methods (except the combined oral contraceptive pill) can be started immediately after birth if desired and the patient is medically eligible.

If pregnancy is not desired, contraception is required from:
- **21 days** after childbirth
- **5 days** after miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, abortion, or gestational trophoblastic disease

Emergency contraception should be offered if unprotected sexual intercourse (UPSI) occurs after these windows.

Long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs — intrauterine contraception and the contraceptive implant) are recommended as a first-line choice for all eligible patients due to their excellent efficacy and safety.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Ask about your plans for future pregnancy, including how you would feel about becoming pregnant again
- Consider personal and social factors that may influence contraceptive choice, including smoking, alcohol use, ability to remember a daily pill, preferences for "fit and forget" versus daily methods, and willingness to cope with irregular bleeding or amenorrhoea
- Ask about the recent pregnancy or birth, including whether it occurred while using contraception, any blood pressure problems during pregnancy, gestational diabetes, or caesarean section
- Ask about breastfeeding plans (as this affects method choice)
- Review previous contraception experience — what has worked, what hasn't, and any side effects
- Check gynaecological and obstetric history, including menstrual patterns, any known gynaecological conditions, and symptoms of sexually transmitted infections (STIs)
- Review related conditions such as acne, PCOS, abnormal uterine bleeding, dysmenorrhoea, endometriosis, or premenstrual syndrome, which some contraceptive methods can help manage
- Check for coexisting medical conditions that may affect method safety, including:
  - Obesity (BMI ≥35)
  - Personal or family history of venous thromboembolism (VTE)
  - Known thrombogenic mutations
  - Cardiovascular disease (hypertension, valvular heart disease, ischaemic heart disease)
  - Current or previous breast or endometrial cancer, or BRCA1/BRCA2 gene mutations
  - Unexplained vaginal bleeding
  - Stroke or TIA
  - Migraine with aura
  - Hepatobiliary disease
  - HIV
  - Diabetes with nephropathy, retinopathy, or neuropathy
  - Positive antiphospholipid antibodies or SLE
- Check current medications that may interfere with contraceptive efficacy, especially:
  - **Liver enzyme-inducing drugs** (e.g., carbamazepine, phenytoin, oxcarbazepine, rifampicin, rifabutin, St John's wort) — these reduce efficacy of the combined pill, progestogen-only pill, and contraceptive implant
  - **Lamotrigine** — combined pill increases metabolism of lamotrigine (seizure risk); progestogen-only pill may increase lamotrigine levels
  - **Griseofulvin** — effect on levonorgestrel emergency contraception is unclear; doubling the dose to 3 mg can be considered
- Exclude current pregnancy before starting any contraceptive method
- Check blood pressure and calculate BMI
- Ensure cervical screening is up to date
- Offer STI screening if indicated

## Management

### After Birth (Postpartum)

Your doctor may consider:

**Intrauterine devices (IUDs)** — both the levonorgestrel intrauterine system (LNG-IUS) and copper IUD (Cu-IUD) are considered safe in breastfeeding.
- Can be inserted within 48 hours of birth (vaginal or caesarean), or from 28 days postpartum if not pregnant and no contraindications (e.g., postpartum sepsis)
- There is a small risk of uterine perforation (1.4 per 1000); this risk is approximately 6 times higher in breastfeeding women and 3 times higher within 36 weeks postpartum, though the absolute risk remains low
- If the uterus is not fully involuted, insertion may need to be delayed

**Progestogen-only contraceptives** — include the levonorgestrel implant (Jadelle), depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) injection, and progestogen-only pill (POP). No adverse effects on breastfeeding or infant development have been found.
- All methods can be started without restriction at any time postpartum
- Note: a single DMPA injection may delay return to fertility for up to 12 months and cannot be reversed once given
- The demands of new parenthood may make meeting the strict timing requirements of POPs challenging

**Combined oral contraceptive pill (COC)** — estrogen-containing pills have restrictions postpartum due to VTE risk and potential effects on milk supply.
- Contraindicated from birth to end of 5th week postpartum
- After 6 weeks, benefits appear to outweigh risks for breastfeeding patients (UKMEC 2)
- No restrictions from 6 months postpartum (UKMEC 1)
- COCs carry additional VTE risk in the postpartum period — your doctor will assess individual risk factors

**Lactational amenorrhoea** — more than 98% effective at preventing pregnancy if:
- Exclusively breastfeeding on demand (at least 8–12 feeds per day, no more than 4 hours between daytime feeds and 6 hours between night feeds)
- Amenorrhoeic (no periods)
- Less than 6 months postpartum

Effectiveness is reduced when breastfeeding becomes less frequent, menstruation returns, or from 6 months postpartum.

**Barrier methods**
- Condoms can be used immediately after delivery
- Diaphragms can be used from 6 weeks postpartum once uterine involution is complete; must be refitted after pregnancy

**Fertility awareness methods** — can be used after pregnancy but may be difficult to use reliably until regular menstrual cycles are re-established

**Permanent contraception**
- Vasectomy: if planned as the couple's preferred method, ideally organised before delivery so sperm is undetectable by 21 days postpartum
- Female tubal ligation: a safe option after birth; some LARC methods are equally or more effective and may have additional non-contraceptive benefits. Your doctor will advise about the increased risk of regret with early postpartum permanent contraception

**Emergency contraception after birth**
- Oral levonorgestrel 1.5 mg — safe from 21 days after birth; no adverse effects on breastfeeding or infant
- Copper IUD (Cu-IUD) — safe from 28 days after birth; note higher relative risk of perforation in breastfeeding patients (absolute risk remains low)

An interval of less than 12 months between birth and conceiving again is associated with increased risk of pre-term birth, low birth weight, and small-for-gestational-age babies.

### After Abortion

Contraception should be discussed before the abortion procedure. Start contraception within 5 days of abortion if pregnancy is not desired, as ovulation and sexual activity can resume rapidly.

Your doctor may consider:

**IUDs** (LNG-IUS or Cu-IUD) — can be safely inserted after an uncomplicated abortion.
- For medical abortion: after confirmed expulsion of pregnancy (or beta-hCG drop >80% at 7–14 days, or negative low-sensitivity pregnancy test at 3–4 weeks)
- For surgical abortion: immediately after evacuation of the uterine cavity
- Small relative increased risk of expulsion with early insertion

**Progestogen-only contraceptives** (Jadelle implant, DMPA injection, POP) — all can be started any time, including immediately after medical or surgical abortion.
- Note: if DMPA is started at the time of mifepristone administration, there may be a slightly higher risk of failed abortion

**Combined oral contraceptive pill** — can be started at any time after an abortion

**Barrier methods** — condoms can be used immediately; diaphragms can be used immediately after first trimester abortion (once fitted), or from 6 weeks after a second trimester abortion

**Fertility awareness methods** — can be used but may be difficult until regular cycles are re-established

**Female permanent contraception** — can be used after abortion but should be performed some time after the procedure; your doctor will advise about increased regret rates and possible failure rates with early permanent contraception

**Emergency contraception** — indicated if UPSI occurs from 5 days after abortion; if the patient has had GTD with abnormal hCG levels, specialist advice should be sought before using a Cu-IUD

### After Ectopic Pregnancy or Miscarriage

Your doctor will discuss fertility intentions. Contraception should be started within 5 days if pregnancy is not desired — ovulation can resume rapidly.

- After ectopic pregnancy treated with **methotrexate**: use effective contraception for 3 months due to methotrexate's teratogenic effects
- In other situations: no need to delay conception after ectopic pregnancy or miscarriage — pregnancy outcomes after miscarriage are actually better when conception occurs within 6 months

Your doctor may consider the same range of methods as after abortion:

**IUDs** — can be safely inserted if there is no evidence of sepsis; small relative increased risk of expulsion with early insertion

**Progestogen-only contraceptives** (Jadelle, DMPA, POP) — all can be started immediately after miscarriage or ectopic pregnancy; can be started on the day of methotrexate administration or surgical management

**Combined oral contraceptive pill** — can be started at any time after miscarriage or ectopic pregnancy; exclude antiphospholipid syndrome (APS) before using in patients with recurrent miscarriage

**Barrier methods** — condoms immediately; diaphragms after fitting/refitting (immediately after ectopic pregnancy or first trimester miscarriage; from 6 weeks after second trimester miscarriage)

**Fertility awareness methods** — can be used but may be difficult until regular cycles re-establish

**Female permanent contraception** — can be used but your doctor will advise about increased regret rates with early permanent contraception after pregnancy loss

### After Gestational Trophoblastic Disease (GTD)

Effective contraception should be started as soon as possible (within 5 days of treatment) as ovulation can resume rapidly. Using contraception after GTD does not increase the risk of GTD in future pregnancies.

Your doctor will advise avoiding pregnancy until it is safe to conceive:
- At least **6 months** after a complete molar pregnancy
- Until **2 consecutive monthly hCG levels are normal** after a partial molar pregnancy
- For **1 year** after completing chemotherapy for GTD

Your doctor may consider:

**IUDs** — if hCG levels are not normal, specialist advice should be sought before inserting an IUD; if GTD is suspected but not confirmed, the treating specialist will discuss IUD insertion on a case-by-case basis

**Hormonal contraception** — all methods (Jadelle implant, DMPA injection, POP, and combined pill) can be started at any time, including immediately after uterine evacuation

**Barrier methods** — condoms immediately; diaphragms from 6 weeks after GTD when uterine involution is complete

**Fertility awareness methods** — can be used but may be difficult until regular cycles re-establish

**Female permanent contraception** — can be used after GTD but should be performed some time after surgical evacuation; your doctor will advise about increased regret rates

---

## When to Seek Specialist Advice

Your doctor will seek specialist (gynaecology) advice if:
- Emergency contraception is needed after a complicated abortion, miscarriage, or ectopic pregnancy
- IUD is being considered when hCG levels are abnormal (GTD)
- You have complex coexisting medical conditions that affect contraceptive safety
