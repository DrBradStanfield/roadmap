# Hypoglycaemia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the recognition and management of hypoglycaemia (low blood sugar) in adults and children — including those with diabetes and children without a diabetes diagnosis.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance immediately if:

- Loss of consciousness
- Slow to respond or unresponsive

---

## Background

Hypoglycaemia most commonly affects people taking **insulin or sulfonylurea medications** for diabetes. All patients on these medications are at increased risk.

### Symptomatic Hypoglycaemia

- Usually occurs when blood glucose (BG) falls below **3.9 mmol/L**
- Typical symptoms: sweating, hunger, tremor, or anxiety
- Nocturnal hypoglycaemia may cause sleep disruption or go unnoticed
- More common and more dangerous in older adults on insulin (increased risk of falls and fractures)

### Hypoglycaemic Unawareness

- Significant hypoglycaemia without typical warning symptoms
- More likely in long-standing Type 1 diabetes
- Repeated minor episodes erode the body's ability to detect and signal low glucose
- Patients may lose consciousness before they notice any symptoms
- Can also occur with sulfonylureas or insulin in MODY, post-pancreatitis, or post-pancreatectomy

### Severe Hypoglycaemia

Any hypoglycaemic episode that requires **outside assistance** to recognise or treat.

---

## Symptoms

- Sweating, fatigue, lethargy
- Palpitations, tremors, anxiety
- Hunger
- Dizziness and weakness
- Confusion or cognitive impairment
- In severe cases: seizure or coma

---

## Common Causes

- Missed or delayed meals
- Too little carbohydrate with meals
- Extra physical activity not offset by extra carbohydrate or a reduced insulin dose
- Insulin or antidiabetic medication dose too high
- Alcohol on an empty stomach or excessive alcohol intake
- Too much time between injection and eating
- Changing the timing of insulin administration
- Addison disease (rare — consider in Type 1 diabetes)
- Inherited metabolic or endocrine disorders (especially in children without diabetes)

---

## Acute Management — Adults

Your doctor will guide management, but for context:

### If Conscious and Able to Swallow

- Sit or lie down
- Check blood glucose — if < 4 mmol/L, take **30 g of fast-acting carbohydrate** immediately
- Examples of 30 g of rapid-acting carbohydrate:
  - 10 Dextro or vita glucose tablets (or 6 BD glucose tablets)
  - 350 mL fruit juice or non-diet soft drink
  - 18 jellybeans
  - 2 tablespoons of honey
  - 3 tablespoons of jam
  - 2 Hypofit gels
- Retest after 10 minutes:
  - Still < 4 mmol/L: repeat the carbohydrate dose
  - Above 4 mmol/L: have a snack or meal (e.g. toast, crackers with cheese), recheck in 30 minutes
- If blood glucose cannot be measured but symptoms suggest hypoglycaemia, take 30 g carbohydrate. If no improvement after 15 minutes, seek medical assistance.

### If Unconscious or Unable to Swallow — Emergency

- Call an ambulance immediately
- While waiting, a trained person may administer:
  - **Glucagon 1 mg** intramuscularly (upper outer thigh). Note: less effective in Type 2 diabetes.
  - **10% glucose IV** — 100 mL bolus (requires IV access, clinical setting)
- Repeat blood glucose every 10–15 minutes; aim to keep BGL > 4 mmol/L

## Acute Management — Children (< 15 years) with Diabetes

- If unresponsive: **call an ambulance immediately**

### If Conscious and Able to Swallow

- Check blood glucose — if < 4 mmol/L, give **10–15 g of fast-acting carbohydrate**
- Examples:
  - 125 mL pre-packed juice drink
  - 4–5 'dextro' glucose tablets
  - 3–5 Mentos
  - 2–3 teaspoons honey
  - 100–150 mL soft drink
  - Hypofit gels
- Do not delay treatment if clinical suspicion is high or the child has reduced consciousness
- Retest after 10 minutes:
  - Still < 4 mmol/L: repeat
  - Above 4 mmol/L: give a snack or meal, recheck in 30 minutes

### If Unconscious or Unable to Swallow — Emergency

Call an ambulance. While waiting, initiate one of:

**Mini-dose glucagon (subcutaneous):**
- Dose by age: 20 micrograms if ≤ 2 years, then add 10 micrograms per year of age (maximum 150 micrograms total)
- Recheck blood glucose in 15 minutes
- If no response: double the glucagon dose or initiate 10% glucose IV/IO if available

**10% glucose IV/IO:**
- 2 mL/kg bolus (200 mg/kg glucose)
- Recheck in 10 minutes; repeat if no response
- Aim to keep BGL > 4 mmol/L

---

## Acute Management — Children (< 15 years) without Diabetes

- If unresponsive: **call an ambulance immediately**
- Check blood glucose
- Treatment is required if:
  - Known inborn error of metabolism and BGL < 3.5 mmol/L
  - All other non-diabetic children and BGL < 3 mmol/L
- If possible, collect blood samples (glucose, B-hydroxybutyrate, insulin, cortisol; blood gas) before starting treatment — to help diagnose the underlying cause
- Treat with **10% glucose IV/IO**: 2 mL/kg bolus, then continuous infusion of 3–5 mL/kg/hour to maintain BGL while awaiting ambulance
- If IV/IO not available: 15 g oral buccal glucose gel; repeat every 10 minutes if no response; aim BGL > 3.5 mmol/L (metabolic disorder) or > 3 mmol/L (other children)

---

## Post-Episode Follow-Up (Adults)

Your doctor will typically:

1. **Identify and correct the cause** — review medication dose, timing, diet, and recent activity
2. **Consider reducing insulin dose** by 20%; if hypoglycaemia continues, reduce by a further 10%
3. **Review glucagon prescription** — provide repeats for patients with severe/recurrent episodes, those in isolated locations, or those who don't respond to oral treatment. (Note: glucagon has a short expiry date — check regularly.)
4. **Give driving advice:**
   - No driving for **1 hour** after mild hypoglycaemia
   - No driving for **24 hours** after severe hypoglycaemia
   - Check blood glucose before every drive; ensure BGL is > 5 mmol/L
5. **Night-time monitoring** if nocturnal hypoglycaemia is suspected (e.g. set alarm for 2–3 am and check BGL)
6. **Refer to diabetes specialist** if severe hypoglycaemia, especially in elderly or those with significant co-morbidities
7. **Recommend a medical alert card or bracelet** for patients on glucose-lowering therapy
8. **Refer for non-acute diabetes assessment** if recurrent hypoglycaemia or hypoglycaemic unawareness persists after initial adjustments — may include consideration of continuous glucose monitoring (CGM)
