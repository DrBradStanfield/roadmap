# Foreign Body in Eye

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to a foreign body in the eye — assessing for penetrating injury and managing conjunctival and corneal foreign bodies.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department immediately if:

- Severe impact to the eye
- History suggestive of a penetrating injury:
  - High velocity injury (e.g., metal on metal such as hammering)
  - Working without safety glasses or goggles
- Signs of possible penetrating injury:
  - Pupil distortion (e.g., tear-drop shaped pupil)
  - Reduced or changed vision

---

## Background

A foreign body in the eye is one of the most common eye complaints. The particle usually sticks to the surface of the cornea (front of the eye) or lodges under the upper eyelid on the inner surface (tarsal conjunctiva).

Conditions that can mimic a foreign body include:
- Conjunctivitis (eye infection)
- Corneal ulcers
- Iritis (inflammation inside the eye)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. **Take a careful history** — asking about events that could cause eye trauma, including hammering, high velocity injuries, or working without eye protection, even in the days before symptoms started. If the history suggests possible trauma, a penetrating injury is assumed until ruled out.

2. **Check visual acuity** — testing each eye separately with an eye chart. If vision appears blurred due to watering or pain, anaesthetic drops may be used first to allow a clear test.

3. **Examine the eye** — if no penetrating injury is suspected, your doctor will evert (flip) both eyelids and apply fluorescein dye to highlight any damage to the eye surface.

4. **Look for the foreign body** — examining all surfaces of the eye including behind both lids. The red reflex is checked; foreign bodies appear as dark defects. More than one foreign body may be present.

5. **Signs of a penetrating open globe injury** that your doctor watches for include:
   - Brown or black tissue protruding from the cornea or white of the eye
   - Full thickness cut through the cornea or sclera
   - Abnormal or tear-drop shaped pupil
   - Unusual red reflex
   - Obvious deformed or sunken eye globe
   - Floaters (may indicate bleeding inside the eye from ocular penetration)

   **Important:** If there is any possibility of a penetrating injury, your doctor will not touch the eye, evert the lid, or attempt to remove any material, as this could cause further injury.

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider:

### If Penetrating Injury Suspected

If there are any signs of corneal or scleral penetration (open globe injury), your doctor will not attempt removal and will arrange urgent ophthalmology (eye specialist) assessment.

### Conjunctival Foreign Body

Foreign bodies on the inner surface of the eyelid are removed using a sterile cotton bud after topical anaesthetic (numbing) drops are applied.

### Corneal Foreign Body

Removal is attempted only in cooperative adults when your doctor is confident it is safe:

1. Topical anaesthetic drops are applied
2. A cotton bud is used first with a sweeping motion
3. If the cotton bud does not work, a specially bent needle (spatulated needle) may be used tangentially along the corneal surface to gently lift the foreign body — the side of the needle is used, not the tip, to avoid perforation
4. After removal, antibiotic eye ointment — typically **chloramphenicol** 1% — is applied four times a day for 5 to 7 days

If the foreign body cannot be easily removed, your doctor will arrange ophthalmology assessment — urgently if there are signs of penetration, or within 24 to 48 hours for deeper or central foreign bodies. Chloramphenicol ointment is prescribed in the interim.

### Rust Rings

When a metallic foreign body has been on the eye, it can leave a rust ring. Your doctor may consider:

- Applying **chloramphenicol** 1% eye ointment four times over 24 hours after initial removal — this can make the rust ring easier to remove
- **Central rust rings:** Ophthalmology referral for specialist removal
- **Peripheral rust rings:** May be removed in general practice 24 hours after the metallic object has been removed, using a spatulated needle or sterile corneal burr. Small peripheral rings may be left alone
- If rust removal is not possible, private ophthalmology assessment may be arranged

### Analgesia and Eye Padding

- **Pain relief:** Oral paracetamol and NSAIDs are effective. Topical anaesthetic drops are not prescribed for take-home use — they mask symptoms and delay healing
- **Eye padding:** Your doctor may pad the eye shut for initial protection while the anaesthetic is still working. If the eye is padded, you cannot legally drive or operate machinery while patched

### Follow-Up and Corneal Abrasions

- Patients with minimal pain, good vision, and a small easily-removed foreign body do not always need a routine review. The pad (if used) is removed after 24 hours, and **chloramphenicol** ointment is used for 3 days
- All other patients are reviewed after 24 hours
- If a corneal abrasion (scratch) is present:
  - Treated with antibiotic ointment (e.g., chloramphenicol) for 3 to 4 days
  - Minor abrasions — review if symptoms worsen or do not resolve
  - Major abrasions or concerning history — review after 24 hours, then as needed
  - Large abrasions (e.g., from garden plants) — review in 48 hours to confirm healing
  - Minor symptoms may continue for up to 2 weeks
