# Constipation in Palliative Care and Oncology

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Always discuss your symptoms and management with your doctor or specialist palliative care team.*

This pathway covers adults with constipation in palliative care or oncology settings. Management should be tailored to the patient's stage of illness and goals of care.

---

## Background

Constipation may be defined as a change in a person's regular bowel habits, including:

- reduced frequency of bowel movements or passage of hard stool
- a lack of satisfaction with bowel movements
- the need to strain during bowel movements

Constipation is very common in palliative care, with multiple contributing factors including opioids, anticholinergic drugs, reduced oral intake, and impaired mobility.

---

## Assessment

### History

Ask about:

- Type and duration of change in bowel habits (Bristol Stool Chart is a useful reference)
- Previous and current laxative use
- Medications that cause constipation:
  - **Opioids** (very common cause)
  - Ondansetron
  - Calcium supplements
  - Iron supplements
  - Aluminium antacids
  - Calcium channel antagonists
  - Diuretics
  - Verapamil
  - **Anticholinergic drugs:** hyoscine butylbromide, cyclizine, tricyclic antidepressants, haloperidol (to a lesser extent)
- Reduced mobility or fluid/fibre intake
- Other symptoms that may suggest constipation: colicky abdominal pain, anorexia, nausea, vomiting, urinary retention, anxiety, confusion (especially in elderly patients)

### Physical Examination

- Assess hydration
- Examine abdomen for faecal or other masses
- Listen for bowel sounds
- Perform rectal examination (or inspect stoma if present) — looking for soft or hard faeces, or an empty rectum
- Neurological examination to exclude spinal cord compression or pathology

### Goals of Care

Consider the patient's stage of illness:

- If the patient has acutely declined from a highly functioning state, consider investigating and treating reversible causes
- If the patient's short-term prognosis is poor (likely to die soon), consider comfort care only

### Causes to Consider

- **General:** low fibre/fluid intake, weakness or immobility, depression
- **Mechanical:** intra- or extraluminal gastrointestinal masses, adhesions, ascites
- **Medications:** opioids, ondansetron, calcium supplements, iron supplements, antacids, calcium channel antagonists, diuretics, verapamil, anticholinergics
- **Metabolic:** hypothyroidism, hypercalcaemia, diabetes, hypopituitarism, hypokalaemia
- **Neurological:** Parkinson's disease, stroke, autonomic neuropathy, spinal lesion, multiple sclerosis

### Investigations (if clinically appropriate)

- FBC, creatinine, electrolytes, calcium
- Abdominal X-ray if bowel obstruction suspected, or to assess faecal loading
- Blood tests for reversible metabolic disturbances (renal failure, hypothyroidism, hypercalcaemia, diabetes)

## Management

> **Important:** All management decisions, including laxative choice and dose, should be made in partnership with your doctor or specialist palliative care team. In palliative care, doses, indications, and routes of administration are often unapproved and require clinical oversight.

### 1. Treat Contributing Causes

Where possible, address:

- Dehydration
- Malignant spinal cord compression
- Hypercalcaemia, hypothyroidism, diabetes, Parkinson's disease
- Bowel obstruction (constipation may itself be the cause)
- Medications — stop or reduce constipating medications if possible

If a rectal tumour is suspected, seek specialist palliative care advice before proceeding with rectal treatments.

### 2. General Measures

- Regular toileting, especially after meals
- Adequate privacy and comfort
- Increase fluid and fibre intake if possible (e.g. kiwifruit, prune juice)
- Gentle increase in activity if possible

### 3. Prophylactic Laxatives

If the patient is taking **opioids or anticholinergic drugs**, prophylactic laxatives should be prescribed routinely — do not wait for constipation to develop.

> **Note:** Lactulose and bulk-forming laxatives (e.g. Metamucil) are generally not appropriate in palliative care as they require high fluid intake.

**Preferred options:**

**Option A — Oral stimulant and softener combination**
- Docusate + senna (Laxsol): 1 to 2 tablets twice daily; maximum 3 tablets three times daily
- If this causes cramps, reduce dose or switch to an osmotic laxative

**Option B — Separate softener plus stimulant**

*Stimulants (choose one):*
- Bisacodyl: 5–10 mg at night, or 5 mg once to twice daily
- Sennoside B (Senokot – part subsidy): 15–30 mg (2–4 tablets) at night

*Softener:*
- Docusate sodium (Coloxyl): 100–480 mg daily in divided doses

**Option C — Macrogol (osmotic laxative)**
- For chronic constipation: 1–3 sachets (13 g each) per day, each dissolved in 125 mL water
- For faecal impaction: up to 8 sachets per day in 1 L water drunk over 6 hours, for up to 3 days — stop other oral laxatives during impaction treatment

### 4. Treatment Based on Rectal Examination Findings

Your doctor will guide treatment based on what they find on rectal examination:

**Empty rectum** → Macrogol (see dosing above)

**Full rectum** → Oral laxative plus rectal preparations:

*Suppositories (preferred first — better retained in palliative care):*
- Soft faeces: bisacodyl 10 mg suppository x 1–2, against the rectal wall
- Hard faeces: bisacodyl 10 mg suppository x 1–2 against the rectal wall, PLUS glycerol suppositories x 1–2 pushed into the faecal mass
- Administer with patient in left lateral position; push suppositories approximately 4 cm into rectum

*If suppositories are ineffective:*
- Sodium citrate micro-enema (Micolette) x 1–2 at room temperature, using gravity — do not force
- If still ineffective: docusate enema (softens hard stool) or Fleet sodium phosphate enema (use once or twice only — prolonged use risks electrolyte imbalance)

### 5. If Constipation Fails to Settle

Your doctor should:

- Exclude bowel obstruction
- Repeat rectal examination
- If empty rectum: increase macrogol to 8 sachets/day; if macrogol not tolerated, consider picosulfate sodium (not funded)
- If full rectum: repeat rectal preparations

### 6. Opioid-Induced Constipation — Methylnaltrexone

If opioid-induced constipation is suspected and oral/rectal treatments are ineffective or not tolerated, your doctor may consider **subcutaneous methylnaltrexone (Relistor)**:

- Peripheral opioid antagonist — fully funded via Special Authority in New Zealand
- Dose is weight-based; reduce in renal impairment
- Administered on alternate days by subcutaneous injection (can be given by patient or carer)
- Response usually within 4 hours (sometimes as fast as 30 minutes) — ensure toilet or bedpan is nearby

### 7. Ongoing Bowel Management

- Once bowels are open, continue prophylactic laxatives
- Aim for a soft, regular, easy-to-pass bowel motion (Bristol Stool Chart type 3–4)
- Assess bowel motions daily and adjust laxatives accordingly
- Maintain close communication with the palliative care team

---

## When to Involve the Specialist Palliative Care Team

Seek specialist palliative care support and advice for:

- Anticipatory planning or acute management needs
- Arranging a joint visit
- Considering admission to hospice
- Suspected rectal tumour
- Refractory constipation not responding to the above measures
