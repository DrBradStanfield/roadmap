# Urinary Incontinence in Women

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of urinary incontinence in women, including stress, urge, and mixed incontinence.

---

## Red Flags — Discuss with Your Doctor Promptly

See your doctor promptly if you have:

- Blood in the urine (haematuria — microscopic or visible)
- Suspected pelvic mass

---

## Assessment

Your healthcare provider will take a history including:

- Onset and duration of symptoms
- Frequency of incontinence episodes
- Quantities of leakage
- Symptoms suggesting urinary infections
- Types of activities that trigger incontinence (e.g., cough, sneeze, arriving home)
- Limitations of activities due to incontinence
- Obstetric history
- Impact on quality of life
- History of previous prolapse or incontinence surgery

Your doctor will also ask whether surgical mesh may have been inserted during any previous prolapse or incontinence surgery.

### Type of Incontinence

Your healthcare provider will assess the type of incontinence:

- **Stress incontinence** — leakage triggered by physical exertion or increased abdominal pressure
  - Mild: leaks when dancing, trampolining, or during sports
  - Moderate: leaks when coughing or sneezing
  - Severe: leaks when walking downhill or rising from a low chair
- **Urge incontinence** — a sudden strong need to urinate followed by involuntary leakage
- **Mixed incontinence** — a combination of stress and urge incontinence
- **Overactive bladder syndrome** — any combination of urgency, frequency, or nocturia, with or without urge incontinence

### Examination

Physical examination may include:

- Body mass index (BMI) measurement
- Abdominal examination to check for masses
- Speculum and bimanual pelvic examination — to check for vaginal atrophy and urine leakage with straining

### Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- Urinalysis
- Pelvic ultrasound if a pelvic mass is suspected

---

## Management

### General Measures (All Types)

Your healthcare provider may recommend:

- Keeping a **bladder diary** for at least 2 days (including both working and leisure days), recording fluid intake, voiding volumes, and leakage episodes
- **Fluid modification:**
  - Aim for 6–8 cups (1.5–2.0 L) of fluid per day, excluding alcohol
  - Limit caffeine to 2–3 cups per day; try decaffeinated alternatives
  - Space drinks throughout the day
  - If nocturia is a problem, aim for the last drink to be 2–3 hours before bedtime and empty your bladder before bed
- **Weight loss** if BMI is above 30 — your doctor may refer you to a dietitian or weight management programme
- **Treating any urinary tract infection (UTI)** if present
- **Managing exacerbating factors** such as chronic cough, asthma, constipation, or heavy lifting

---

### Stress Incontinence

Your healthcare provider may recommend:

- Improving general fitness
- A **6-month supervised trial of pelvic floor muscle exercises** — supervised physiotherapy (women's health physiotherapy) or a continence advisory service typically produces better results than unsupervised exercises
- If there is little improvement after 6 months of one-on-one physiotherapy, your doctor may refer you for specialist urology or gynaecology assessment

---

### Urge Incontinence

Your healthcare provider may recommend:

- **Minimising bladder irritants** (e.g., caffeine, alcohol, spicy foods, carbonated drinks)
- **Bladder retraining** — a structured programme to gradually extend the time between voids, usually over 6 weeks
- **Vaginal estrogens** for postmenopausal women — these are an effective treatment for genitourinary syndrome of menopause and can assist with recurrent UTIs or urinary incontinence:
  - Typically estriol vaginal cream, applied at bedtime nightly for two weeks, then twice weekly
  - Considered safe in women with previous venous thromboembolism, or survivors of gynaecological cancer (endometrial, cervical, or ovarian)
  - Contraindicated in active endometrial cancer or unexplained vaginal bleeding
  - For women with a history of estrogen-dependent breast cancer: low-dose vaginal estrogens may still be considered if genitourinary symptoms persist despite non-hormonal therapy — discuss with your doctor
  - Additional progesterone is not required for endometrial protection with vaginal estrogens
  - Initial side effects may include vaginal irritation or stinging; starting with small doses and increasing gradually can help
- **Anticholinergic medication** if bladder retraining and other measures are insufficient:
  - **Oxybutynin:** typically started at 5 mg twice daily, increasing to three times daily as needed. In older adults, start with 2.5 mg twice daily and increase slowly. Note: anticholinergic medications have been associated with an increased risk of dementia, particularly in older adults — discuss the risks and benefits with your doctor.
  - **Solifenacin (Vesicare):** 5 mg once daily, increasing to 10 mg daily if no response after 2 weeks. An alternative for those who cannot tolerate oxybutynin. Dose should not exceed 5 mg daily with moderate liver impairment, low kidney clearance, or certain other medications.
- If these treatments are unsuccessful, your doctor may refer you for specialist urology or gynaecology assessment. The specialist may consider **botulinum toxin (Botox) injections** into the detrusor muscle — an option when overactive bladder symptoms are refractory to other treatments (may need to be repeated every 6–9 months).

---

### Mixed Incontinence

Your healthcare provider will treat the most predominant symptoms first — following the approach for either stress or urge incontinence as appropriate.

---

### Neurological Conditions

Women with neurological conditions such as multiple sclerosis or Parkinson's disease are less likely to benefit from physiotherapy alone and may need earlier specialist referral.
