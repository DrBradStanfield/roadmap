# Pressure Injuries in Spinal Cord Impairment

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of pressure injuries (pressure sores/ulcers) in adults with spinal cord impairment (SCI). Even minor skin changes need careful attention — especially in areas without normal sensation. This information is a guide only. Always discuss with your doctor or wound care nurse.

---

## Important: Take Skin Concerns Seriously

Any skin concern in a person with SCI — even just a patch of redness — needs careful attention, especially where sensation is impaired. Do not wait for obvious breakdown before seeking assessment.

---

## Causative Factors

Your doctor or nurse will ask about factors that may have contributed to the pressure injury:

- Limited mobility or activity (including any new limitations)
- Poor transfer technique or decline in transfer ability
- Ageing
- Previous skin breakdown in that area or elsewhere
- Frequent falls
- Decline in neurological status
- Weight gain or loss
- Medications including sedatives
- Spasticity
- Bowel or bladder incontinence
- Other health conditions, especially diabetes
- Recent surgery
- Pregnancy
- Cognitive impairment
- Psychosocial factors (inconsistent carers, substance use, smoking, change in circumstances)
- Poor nutrition or inadequate hygiene
- Malfunctioning pressure relief equipment (wheelchair cushion, mattress)
- Poor posture in chair, wheelchair, or bed
- Pressure from clothing (e.g., shoe seams) or medical equipment

---

## Common Pressure Points

Areas to check include: posterior head, shoulders, scapula, thoracic spine, pelvic crests, sacrum, buttocks, ischial tuberosities, and heels.

---

## Staging Pressure Injuries

| Stage | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| Stage 1 | Non-blancheable erythema (redness that does not whiten with pressure) |
| Stage 2 | Partial thickness skin loss; red wound bed or serous-filled/ruptured blister |
| Stage 3 | Full thickness skin loss; may include subcutaneous fat but no exposed muscle, tendon, or bone |
| Stage 4 | Full thickness tissue loss with exposed muscle, tendon, cartilage, and/or bone |
| Suspected deep tissue injury | Purple or maroon discoloured intact skin or blood-filled blister from pressure to underlying tissue |
| Unstageable | Wound bed covered by eschar (dead tissue) |

---

## Wound Assessment

Your doctor or nurse will use the T.I.M.E.S. model:

- **Tissue** — Assessing for non-viable tissue (necrotic tissue, slough) and blood supply
- **Infection** — Signs include wound breakdown, increasing size, delayed healing, malodour, new or increased pain (excessive pain may indicate necrotising fasciitis), purulent discharge, discolouration, hypergranulation, pocketing, spreading erythema, induration, or undermining
- **Moisture balance** — Checking exudate levels; too much causes maceration, too little delays healing
- **Edge advancement** — Whether wound edges are migrating normally or appear abnormal (rolling, hypergranulating, undermining)
- **Surrounding skin** — Assessing turgor, warmth, perfusion, signs of infection, maceration, or excoriation

Photos of all affected areas with a ruler in view should be taken.

---

## Management

### If Septic (Signs of Serious Infection)

Seek emergency assessment immediately. Signs of spreading or systemic infection include severe increasing pain, erythema beyond 2 cm from the wound edge, fever, rigors, tachycardia, lymphangitis, or abscess formation.

### Off-Loading

The pressure injury must be off-loaded (pressure removed) immediately:

- **Stage 1:** Off-load for 2 weeks, then follow up until healed
- **Stage 2 or more:** Off-load for 2–4 weeks, then follow up until healed
- If the injury is on the ischial tuberosities, sacrum, or buttocks, off-loading requires bed rest of the injured site

During off-loading, your doctor will arrange:

- District nursing for wound management, nutritional advice, and monitoring
- Increased home support if needed

### Treating Infection

Antibiotics and appropriate wound care will be prescribed if infection is present.

### Addressing Causative Factors

Managing underlying factors — posture, nutrition, diabetes, psychosocial issues — is essential to healing and prevention of recurrence.

### Follow-Up

Your doctor will arrange early and regular follow-up. If you are on bed rest, home visits will be needed. Do not wait to be asked — contact your practice if you are concerned.

**If no healing is evident after 2–3 weeks** (regardless of stage), your doctor will seek specialist assessment and request serial photographs.

**Seek urgent advice or assessment if:**
- The wound is covered by eschar
- The wound is full thickness (stage 3 or 4) at any review

---

## Prevention

Once the immediate issue is addressed, preventing further pressure injuries is essential.

**SSKIN framework:**

- **Support surface** — Use appropriate support surfaces and equipment; check regularly for correct positioning
- **Skin assessment** — Visually check all areas without normal sensation twice daily
- **Keep moving** — Follow a repositioning and mobilisation regime
- **Incontinence and moisture** — Maintain effective bowel and bladder management; use appropriate clothing and dressings
- **Nutrition** — Maintain a healthy diet including adequate fluids, protein, vitamins, and good oral care

Involve your carers and whānau in prevention education. Learn to recognise early signs of pressure injury.

---

## When to Seek Help

Contact your doctor or seek urgent care if:

- You notice any new redness, skin breakdown, or wound — even minor
- A wound is not improving after 2–3 weeks of treatment
- You develop signs of infection (spreading redness, fever, increased pain)
- A wound appears deep or is covered by dark/black dead tissue
- You are unwell
