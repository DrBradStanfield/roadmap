# Osteoporosis in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: July 2026.*

Osteoporosis is a progressive condition in which bones become more fragile and the risk of fracture rises. It causes no symptoms until a fracture occurs.

> **Always discuss your bone health and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Symptoms to Report to Your Doctor

- If you take a bisphosphonate or denosumab, report any **thigh, hip, or groin pain**. This can be an early sign of a rare atypical thigh bone (femoral) fracture. Your doctor may arrange an X-ray, seek orthopaedic advice, and stop the bisphosphonate if the X-ray suggests a fracture.
- If you have broken a bone after a minor fall (from standing height or less), ask your doctor about a bone health assessment. Treatment is usually started without waiting for a scan.

---

## Background

- Osteoporosis can result from bone loss and changes in bone structure as people age, from an underlying condition, or from certain medications at any age.
- The WHO defines osteoporosis as a T-score less than −2.5. In practice, this has been superseded by fracture risk assessment.
- **Osteopenia** is a T-score between −1 and −2.5. Fracture risk is lower than with osteoporosis, but because many more people have osteopenia, most fractures occur in this group.
- Studies show higher bone density and fewer fractures in people of Polynesian descent, including Māori.

**Hip fractures** are the most serious fractures caused by osteoporosis:
- Fewer than 50% of people who survive a hip fracture walk unaided again.
- The one-year mortality rate after a hip fracture is about 20%.
- Of those admitted from their own home, about 20% are discharged to institutional care.
- There is a 2.5 times increased risk of future fractures.

Only 30% of hip fractures occur in men, but men have a higher mortality rate and are more likely to have a secondary cause, e.g. excessive alcohol, corticosteroids, smoking, or low testosterone (hypogonadism).

## Assessment

Many fragility fractures can be prevented with appropriate screening and management.

### Who should be assessed

Your doctor may consider an osteoporosis risk assessment if you have any of the following:

- **A previous fragility fracture:** a fracture from low-energy trauma, such as a fall from standing height or less. These are most common in the wrist, hip, spine, and upper arm. Fractures of the skull, hands, and feet are excluded.
- **Women older than 60 years.**
- **Men aged 75 years or older.**
- **Major risk factors**, including older age or a parent who had a hip fracture.

**Lifestyle factors:**
- Current smoking
- Low body weight (BMI)
- Low physical activity or immobility
- More than 2 alcoholic drinks daily
- A history of falls, poor balance, or low muscle mass
- Malnutrition
- Severe vitamin D deficiency (25-hydroxyvitamin D less than 30 nmol/L)

**Medical conditions:**
- Endocrine: Cushing syndrome, type 1 and type 2 diabetes, hypogonadism, hyperparathyroidism, untreated hyperthyroidism
- Loss of periods (amenorrhoea) for longer than 6 months in women younger than 45 years, not related to hormonal therapy or contraception. Causes include eating disorders, malnutrition, early menopause, and relative energy deficiency in sport.
- Disordered eating or inadequate calories
- Inflammatory conditions, e.g. rheumatoid arthritis
- Malabsorption: coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, cystic fibrosis
- Organ or bone marrow transplant, myeloma, and MGUS
- HIV
- Chronic liver disease or moderate to severe kidney disease
- COPD
- Spinal cord injuries
- Some cancers and their treatments, especially breast and prostate cancer and blood cancers

**Medications:**
- Large effect on bone density: glucocorticoids (steroids) over 5 mg per day for more than 3 months; androgen deprivation therapy (anti-androgens, gonadorelin analogues); ferric carboxymaltose.
- Modest effect on bone density: aromatase inhibitors, chemotherapy drugs, suppressive doses of thyroxine, long-term proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), methotrexate, pioglitazone, depo-medroxyprogesterone. The bone loss from depo-medroxyprogesterone normally reverses a year after stopping.
- Increased risk of falling: SSRIs, tricyclic antidepressants, older anticonvulsants (phenytoin, carbamazepine), lithium, antiparkinsonian medications, antipsychotics, anxiolytics, and sedatives including zopiclone.

### Fracture risk calculators

Your doctor may estimate your fracture risk with a calculator. **FRAX** and the **Garvan Fracture Risk Calculator** are the most widely used. They use different risk factors and may give different results:
- At very high or very low risk, both give similar results. For borderline cases, it may be worth using both and following whichever gives the higher risk.
- Garvan includes the number of past fractures and falls in the last year. FRAX includes ethnicity, smoking, alcohol, and glucocorticoid use.
- Garvan may be more appropriate for very old patients, as FRAX can underestimate their risk.

| Risk level | 10-year hip fracture risk | 10-year major osteoporotic fracture risk | Usual approach |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Low | Less than 1.5% | Less than 10% | Reassurance |
| Moderate | 1.5 to 3% | 10 to 20% | DXA scan if not already done, and consider treatment |
| High | More than 3% | More than 20% | Offer treatment, but consider a DXA scan and any secondary causes |

Hip fracture risk is low in women younger than 70 years, so major osteoporotic fracture risk may be more useful in this age group. With high risk, treatment should start straight away if a DXA scan would be significantly delayed or cannot be accessed.

### Bone density (DXA) scan

A DXA scan measures bone mineral density using a very low level of radiation. Your doctor may consider one if your risk is moderate or high and you have not had one. DXA scans are funded only when certain criteria are met; you may also choose to pay for one yourself.

Results are reported as:
- **T-score:** how far your bone density is from that of healthy young adults.
- **Z-score:** how far it is from people of your age and sex.

| T-score | Result |
| --- | --- |
| More than −1 | Normal |
| −1 to −2.5 | Osteopenia (low bone mass) |
| Less than −2.5 | Osteoporosis |

- Bone density is only one part of the picture. The result is used to recalculate your fracture risk, which predicts who needs medication better than bone density alone.
- Wear-and-tear changes in the spine can give falsely reassuring spine results. In older adults, the hip is the preferred site.
- A Z-score less than −2 suggests a **secondary cause** of low bone density.

### Blood tests

For anyone with osteopenia or osteoporosis, your doctor may arrange:
- **First-line:** FBC, calcium and phosphate, liver function, kidney function.
- **Second-line** (only if a secondary cause is suspected or the Z-score is less than −2): TSH, parathyroid hormone (PTH) if calcium is high, coeliac antibodies, serum protein electrophoresis and free light chains, testosterone and SHBG if low testosterone is suspected, and a 24 hour urine cortisol or 1 mg overnight dexamethasone test if Cushing syndrome is suspected. Together with first-line tests, these exclude 90% of other causes.

**Secondary causes** include malabsorption (e.g. coeliac disease), inflammatory conditions (rheumatoid arthritis, IBD, COPD, connective tissue disorders), prolonged loss of periods or early menopause, low body weight from any cause, hypogonadism, chronic liver or kidney disease, long-standing untreated hyperthyroidism, Cushing syndrome, primary hyperparathyroidism, monoclonal gammopathy and multiple myeloma, and medications such as steroids.

## Management

The goal is to prevent fractures. However, many people do not keep taking their osteoporosis treatment.

### Treatment by fracture risk

**High risk:** 10-year hip fracture risk of 3% or more, major osteoporotic fracture risk of 20% or more, a hip or spine T-score of −2.5 or less, or a T-score of −1.5 or less while on long-term glucocorticoids (5 mg or more of prednisone equivalent per day for 3 months or longer).
- Your doctor may offer medication for osteoporosis.
- If you are younger than 50 years, your doctor may consider an endocrinology referral.

**Moderate risk:** hip fracture risk of 1.5 to 3%, major osteoporotic fracture risk of 10 to 20%, or a T-score of −1 to −2.5 (osteopenia).
- Whether to treat depends on your preferences, other fracture risk factors, other health conditions (including falls risk), and safety. Bisphosphonate treatment has been shown to be cost-effective in this group.
- Without treatment, fracture risk doubles every 5 to 6 years.
- Fracture risk is usually re-evaluated every 5 years, with a follow-up DXA scan after 5 to 10 years depending on the expected rate of bone loss.

**Low risk:** hip fracture risk less than 1.5%, major osteoporotic fracture risk less than 10%, or a normal DXA scan.
- Your doctor may reassure you, plan a later reassessment, and encourage you to reduce risk factors. Involving whānau in these conversations is likely to lead to better results.

### After a fragility fracture

- If a Fracture Liaison Service has assessed you, your doctor will usually follow its recommendations.
- Medication for osteoporosis is usually started without waiting for DXA results.

If a secondary cause is found, your doctor will manage it and may refer you to endocrinology if your conditions are complex.

### Medications

Your doctor may consider:
- **Oral bisphosphonates** (e.g. risedronate): usually given for about 4 to 5 years, followed by a drug holiday of 1 to 2 years if fracture risk remains high. Risedronate acts for a shorter time, so a 1-year holiday may be considered.
- **Zoledronic acid infusion:** optimal initial treatment is 5 mg every 18 months for 4 doses. The interval can then usually be extended to 2 or 3 years. Drug holidays do not apply.
- **Denosumab:** should be considered life-long treatment, because bone loss speeds up rapidly after stopping. If stopping cannot be avoided, most people are switched over to a bisphosphonate.
- **Teriparatide:** given as a limited course. Your doctor will plan ongoing treatment, usually with a bisphosphonate, before the course ends.
- **Teriparatide or denosumab** may be considered if you keep having fractures on adequate treatment, or your T-score is less than −3.

## Lifestyle Advice

Supervised, high-intensity resistance and impact training safely improves bone density, bone structure, and physical function in postmenopausal women and older men with low bone mass, and can reverse losses. The benefits depend on the dose.

**Exercise:** an osteoporosis programme should ideally include four parts:
- Balance exercises to help prevent falls, e.g. t'ai chi or yoga.
- Professionally prescribed, supervised muscle-strengthening exercise (free weights, machines, resistance bands, or body weight), twice a week, getting harder over time.
- Weight-bearing exercise (walking, dancing, climbing stairs) is good for overall health but does not specifically improve bone density.
- Flexibility exercises, e.g. yoga and stretching.

Activities that involve rapid, repetitive, sustained, weighted, or end-of-range twisting or bending of the spine may need to be modified, especially if your fracture risk is high. Both too little and too much exercise affect bone health. If you do less than 2.5 hours of moderate exercise a week, your doctor may help you increase it. If you show signs of over-exercising, your doctor may advise on nutrition and safer training and consider a dietitian or sports physician.

**Falls prevention:** your doctor may address falls risk and consider a formal assessment. Risk factors include:
- A fall in the last 12 months (the biggest risk factor)
- Being visibly frail, slow, or unsteady
- Taking more than 4 regular medications, or at-risk medications (especially sedatives)
- Low blood pressure on standing
- Cognitive impairment
- Conditions such as stroke, arthritis, Parkinson's disease, osteoporosis, urinary urgency or incontinence, nerve damage in the legs, or recent surgery
- Vision problems, including multifocal or bifocal glasses
- Living alone
- Being nutritionally at risk

**Diet and other habits:**
- Eat whole foods daily from the four food groups. Some calcium-rich foods in the diet are preferable. Calcium supplements are no longer routinely recommended.
- Maintain a healthy weight. A low BMI or losing weight are both linked to lower bone density and a much higher fracture risk.
- Limit alcohol and stop smoking.
- Get sufficient vitamin D. There are no internationally agreed levels for sufficient vitamin D. The evidence that vitamin D deficiency contributes to osteoporosis is very weak unless the deficiency is severe. Sunlight is the best source.

## Ongoing Review

Osteoporosis is generally a life-long condition. Lifestyle, exercise, and falls prevention continue indefinitely.

- **Checking oral treatment:** long-term adherence to, and absorption of, oral bisphosphonates is often poor. Your doctor may check a blood marker called P1NP 6 months after starting if there is concern. With effective treatment, P1NP falls to less than 35 micrograms/L. A level that stays at 35 or more micrograms/L may suggest the tablets are not being taken or absorbed well, and switching to zoledronic acid may be considered. P1NP is not needed with intravenous bisphosphonates or denosumab.
- **Drug holidays:** atypical thigh bone fractures are a rare association with more than 5 years of oral bisphosphonates. After 5 years of treatment, there are 8 atypical femoral fractures for every 859 fractures prevented. A drug holiday cuts this risk by about two-thirds, and the risk drops off dramatically within one to two years. It is very uncommon with zoledronic acid.
- **Repeat DXA scans** are used to check whether treatment is working, adherence, or bone density before a planned medication change. They are generally done a minimum of 2 years apart, and less often in lower-risk people, ideally on the same machine.
- If bone density keeps falling despite treatment, your doctor may look again for a secondary cause.

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may seek specialist advice if:
- An atypical thigh bone fracture is suspected (orthopaedic advice).
- A DXA scan cannot be accessed.
- There are challenges with assessment or management after a recent fragility fracture.
- You fracture again while on treatment.
- You are at high fracture risk and younger than 50 years, or have complex medical conditions (endocrinology).
- There is concern about long-term management, or mainly about falls risk and frailty (older adults' health).

## Key Concepts for Patients

- Osteoporosis causes no symptoms until a bone breaks.
- Your fracture risk, not your bone density alone, guides whether treatment is needed.
- A broken bone after a minor fall is a signal to check your bone health.
- Strength and balance training can improve bone density and help prevent falls.
- Report any thigh, hip, or groin pain while on a bisphosphonate or denosumab.
- Always discuss treatment changes, including stopping or pausing medication, with your doctor.
