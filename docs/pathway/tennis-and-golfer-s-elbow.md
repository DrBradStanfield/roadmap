# Tennis and Golfer's Elbow

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of lateral epicondylitis (tennis elbow) and medial epicondylitis (golfer's elbow).

---

## Background

Tennis elbow (lateral epicondylitis) and golfer's elbow (medial epicondylitis):

- Are usually overuse injuries, occurring after minor trauma to the proximal insertion of the extensor (tennis elbow) or flexor (golfer's elbow) tendons at the elbow
- Usually occur after repetitive use of the forearm during sports, work, or hobbies, but can occur after an acute injury
- Are most common in people aged 30 to 50 years
- Are self-limiting — about 80% of patients recover within 1 year

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Pain near the medial or lateral epicondyle, which may radiate into the forearm
- Whether the pain came on gradually or after an acute injury
- What makes the pain worse (typically use of extensor or flexor muscles)

Physical examination typically includes:

- **Tennis elbow:** Tenderness over the lateral epicondyle with the elbow bent at 90°; pain on resisted middle finger or wrist extension with the elbow fully extended
- **Golfer's elbow:** Tenderness over the medial epicondyle with the elbow bent at 90°; pain on resisted pronation of the forearm

If there is a history of injury, your doctor may arrange an X-ray to check for a fracture.

---

## Management

### Reassurance

Your doctor will reassure you that most people recover within 1 year regardless of the treatment given.

### Activity Modification

Your doctor may advise:

- Ergonomic modifications to your workplace or sports technique
- Rest and ice after activities
- For tennis elbow: using the wrist in a supinated (palm-up) position as much as possible

### Physical Therapy

Your doctor may consider referral to physiotherapy or hand therapy for:

- Graduated eccentric strengthening exercise programmes
- Assessment and modification of occupational or recreational activities
- Bracing (counterforce brace for epicondylitis)

### Splints

- Support or night splints may be helpful for both short-term and long-term symptom relief
- There is no strong evidence to prefer over-the-counter versus custom splints

### Medications

Your doctor may consider:

- **Topical or oral NSAIDs** (anti-inflammatory medicines such as ibuprofen or diclofenac) — for short-term use only

### Corticosteroid Injection

Your doctor may consider an injection around the epicondyle:

- Injections may provide short-term symptom relief for approximately 6 to 12 weeks
- However, current evidence questions their benefit beyond the short term — people with tennis elbow may have worse outcomes at 1 year with corticosteroid injections compared to exercise alone
- The injection typically contains a corticosteroid (10 mg) combined with a local anaesthetic (lidocaine)
- A rehabilitation, exercise, and physiotherapy programme remains critical to management alongside any injection

> Note: Autologous blood injections and botulinum toxin type A have insufficient evidence to support their use.

### When to Consider Specialist Review

If symptoms are severe or have not improved after 6 months of treatment, your doctor may consider referral for an orthopaedic elbow assessment.
