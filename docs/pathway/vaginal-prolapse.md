# Vaginal Prolapse

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of vaginal (pelvic organ) prolapse in women.

---

## Background

Pelvic floor dysfunction can result in prolapse of the female pelvic organs — including the urethra, bladder, uterus, small bowel, and large bowel.

Symptoms are varied and may involve any pelvic organ. The most common presentations are:

- A sensation of fullness, pressure, or a bulge in the vagina ("something coming down")
- Bladder or bowel incontinence
- Recurrent urinary tract infections or difficulty passing urine
- Obstructed defecation or incomplete bowel emptying
- Sexual dysfunction

Many women with pelvic floor dysfunction are asymptomatic, and prolapse may be found on routine examination.

---

## Assessment

Your healthcare provider will take a detailed history, including:

**Vaginal symptoms:**
- Sensation of fullness, pressure, or bulge
- Something feeling like it is "coming down"
- Difficulty with intercourse

**Urinary symptoms:**
- Frequency, urgency, or incontinence
- Difficulty initiating stream or feeling of incomplete bladder emptying

**Bowel symptoms:**
- Constipation, urgency, or incontinence
- Incomplete emptying

Your doctor will also ask about:
- Effect on daily lifestyle and activities
- Smoking status
- Previous pelvic or incontinence surgery — particularly whether surgical mesh was used (if so, a different specialist pathway applies)

**Physical examination** typically includes:
- Body mass index (BMI) calculation
- Bimanual examination to exclude a pelvic mass
- Assessment of prolapse extent — your doctor may ask you to bear down (valsalva or cough) during examination
- Pelvic floor muscle strength assessment — you may be asked to squeeze as if stopping the flow of urine
- Speculum examination assessing each vaginal wall individually at rest and with bearing down

If urinary symptoms are present, your doctor will check for urinary tract infection.

### Prolapse Grading

Prolapses are graded as follows (assessed with a cough or valsalva):

| Stage | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| Stage 0 | No prolapse |
| Stage 1 | More than 1 cm above the hymen |
| Stage 2 | Within 1 cm proximal or distal to the plane of the hymen |
| Stage 3 | More than 1 cm below the hymen but not complete eversion |
| Stage 4 | Complete eversion of the vagina |

Each stage can apply to:
- Anterior vaginal wall (cystocele and/or urethrocele)
- Posterior vaginal wall (rectocele or enterocele)
- Cervix or vaginal vault

## Management

### Lifestyle and Education

Your healthcare provider may advise:

- **Weight management** — a healthy BMI reduces the risk of prolapse recurrence if surgery is ever needed
- **Bowel health** — avoiding straining and constipation; maintaining a high-fibre diet and good hydration
- **Lifestyle modification** — regular exercise; smoking cessation to reduce chronic cough
- **Avoiding heavy lifting**
- **Pelvic floor exercises** — particularly important if pelvic floor weakness is present. Ask your doctor or a physiotherapist for guidance on correct technique

### Conservative Treatment (Symptomatic Prolapse)

Your healthcare provider may recommend:

1. **Treat urinary tract infection** if present
2. **Manage urinary incontinence** if present
3. **Vaginal estrogen** — for postmenopausal women, topical estrogen (e.g., Ovestin cream) can help with vaginal tissue quality. This is contraindicated in women with a history of endometrial cancer, and requires specialist advice before use in women with a history of breast cancer
4. **Pelvic floor physiotherapy** — referral to a women's health physiotherapist for supervised pelvic floor rehabilitation
5. **Ring pessary** — a device inserted into the vagina to support the prolapsed tissue. Your doctor can discuss whether this is appropriate for you

### When Specialist Referral Is Indicated

Your doctor may refer you to a gynaecologist if:

- You have had previous incontinence or prolapse surgery, or have mesh in situ
- There is no improvement after at least 6 months of conservative management
- You experience severe pain
- There is urinary or faecal incontinence
- There is incomplete voiding or defecating difficulty
- The prolapse is protruding outside the hymen
- You have recurrent urinary tract infections

For asymptomatic prolapse, your doctor may consider a referral to women's health physiotherapy as a first step.
