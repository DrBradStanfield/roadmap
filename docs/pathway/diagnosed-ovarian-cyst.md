# Diagnosed Ovarian Cyst

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the management of ovarian cysts identified on ultrasound in post-pubertal patients aged 15 years or older.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

Contact your doctor urgently or go to the emergency department if you have:

- **Acute severe pelvic pain** associated with a known cyst — this may indicate ovarian torsion or acute haemorrhage
- **Features suggesting ovarian cancer** on imaging or examination

---

## Background

- Ovarian cysts may be found incidentally or when investigating gynaecological symptoms or non-specific gastrointestinal symptoms.
- They are common: occurring in 6.6% of premenopausal women and 5–17% of postmenopausal women.
- **Common types include:**
  - Functional cysts (follicle or corpus luteum) — typically shrink over 4–6 weeks
  - Paratubal and paraovarian cysts — almost always asymptomatic
  - Hydrosalpinx — blocked fallopian tube full of fluid
  - Endometriomas — deposits of endometriosis on the ovary
  - Dermoid cysts — may contain hair, skin, teeth, bone, and other tissues
  - Cystadenomas — mucinous or serous
- **Uncommon types include:** ovarian cancers and borderline ovarian tumours.
- Polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS) involves multiple ovarian follicles, not true cysts.
- Routine population screening for ovarian cancer is not currently recommended.

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider the following when evaluating an ovarian cyst:

### Differential Diagnosis

Possible diagnoses include:
- Benign cystadenoma
- Dermoid cyst
- Endometrioma
- Functional or physiological cyst
- Haemorrhagic cyst
- Ovarian malignancy

### Cyst Classification

**Simple cyst:**
- Unilateral
- Usually associated with a normal regular menstrual cycle
- May be heterogenic or haemorrhagic
- Typically smaller than 5 cm in diameter — a simple cyst larger than 5 cm warrants further investigation or surveillance

**Complex cyst:**
- Usually larger than 8 cm, but may be any size
- Multiple cysts, bilateral, loculated, or septated
- Solid components or papillary areas
- Associated ascites or metastases
- Common benign examples:
  - Dermoids — tend to be mobile and float high in the pelvis; torsion is possible
  - Endometriomas — often associated with moderately raised CA125 levels (35–80 kU/L), tenderness, and dyspareunia

### Other Considerations

Your doctor will also ask about:
- Family history of breast or ovarian cancer
- Menopausal status — **postmenopausal** is defined as:
  - Aged over 45 years, not using hormonal contraception, and last period more than 12 months ago, or
  - Has had a hysterectomy and is either aged over 50 years or is experiencing vasomotor symptoms (e.g., hot flushes)

## Management

Your doctor will manage the cyst based on its type and your menopausal status. The following is a summary of what your doctor may consider:

### Premenopausal — Simple Cysts (including physiological and haemorrhagic cysts)

- **Smaller than 5 cm** — no follow-up required
- **5 to 7 cm** — your doctor may arrange annual clinical review and ultrasound
  - If no changes after 3 annual reviews, gynaecology referral is considered
  - If new symptoms or suspicious ultrasound findings appear, gynaecology referral is considered
- **Larger than 7 cm** — gynaecology referral is considered

Suspicious ultrasound findings that may prompt referral include: abnormal blood flow, multiloculation, solid areas, papillary areas, irregular cyst, increasing size, bilateral lesions, ascites, or metastases.

### Premenopausal — Complex Cysts

- **No obvious ultrasound diagnosis** — gynaecology referral is considered
- **Suspected dermoid cyst:**
  - If symptomatic or larger than 5 cm — gynaecology referral is considered
  - If asymptomatic and 5 cm or less — your doctor may arrange annual ultrasound and clinical review
  - Referral is considered if symptoms change, suspicious findings develop, or no change after 2 annual reviews
- **Suspected endometrioma:**
  - Your doctor may request a follow-up ultrasound after 3 months to exclude haemorrhagic cyst
  - If cyst resolves — no further follow-up required
  - If cyst persists and there are symptoms, fertility issues, or cyst is larger than 3 cm — gynaecology referral is considered
  - If no symptoms and cyst is 3 cm or less — your doctor may arrange annual ultrasound and clinical review; referral is considered if symptoms change or no improvement after 2 annual reviews

**Investigations your doctor may arrange before referral:** Ultrasound report, CA125, CEA, CA19-9, Beta HCG. If you are under 40: also LDH and AFP.

### Postmenopausal — Simple Cysts

- **Smaller than 1.5 cm** — no follow-up required
- **1.5 cm to 5 cm:**
  - If unilateral, asymptomatic, no ascites or metastases, and normal CA125 — your doctor may arrange 6-monthly clinical review, CA125, and ultrasound for 1 year
    - If cyst resolves — no further follow-up required
    - If no change after 1 year — gynaecology referral may be considered, taking into account your age, other health conditions, and preferences
    - If any changes — gynaecology referral is considered
  - If bilateral, symptomatic, ascites, metastases, or CA125 abnormal — gynaecology referral is considered
- **Larger than 5 cm** — gynaecology referral is considered

**Investigations your doctor may arrange before referral:** Ultrasound report, CA125, CEA, CA19-9.

### Postmenopausal — Complex Cysts

Gynaecology referral is considered for all postmenopausal complex cysts. Investigations before referral: Ultrasound report, CA125, CEA, CA19-9, Beta HCG (if premenopausal), LDH and AFP (if under 40).
