# Skin Infections in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: April 2022.*

This pathway covers skin infections in children aged 3 months to 14 years. It includes cellulitis, boils and abscesses, infected eczema, and other common skin infections.

> **Always discuss your child's symptoms and any treatment with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department if your child:**
- Seems very unwell with a skin infection, or you are very concerned about them. This may be **sepsis**, which is life-threatening and often recognised late. Early recognition and treatment are vital.
- Is unwell in themselves, including a fever of more than 38ºC or a fast heart rate.
- Is under 3 months old and has a skin infection.
- Has a weakened immune system (is immunocompromised).
- Has swelling and redness around a joint or around the eyes. This may be a joint infection or periorbital cellulitis.
- Has marked pain that seems out of proportion to how the skin looks, or an infected area that is spreading fast. This may be **necrotising fasciitis**, a medical and surgical emergency.
- May have **eczema herpeticum** (rapidly worsening, painful eczema with fever and clustered blisters; see below).

Your doctor will arrange same-day hospital assessment for any of these.

**See a doctor or nurse the same day if a sore or red area:**
- Is the size of a 10 cent piece or bigger.
- Has pus.
- Is getting bigger.
- Has red streaks coming from it.
- Is not getting better within 2 days.
- Is near the eye.

---

## Background

- New Zealand has some of the highest rates of serious skin infections, such as impetigo, particularly among children.
- Studies have shown that at-risk groups have a slightly increased risk of developing rheumatic fever or post-streptococcal glomerulonephritis.
- Māori and Pacific peoples and people from low socio-economic areas are disproportionately affected. This is likely due to a range of factors, including overcrowding and reduced access to primary healthcare.
- Targeted education, prevention, and control measures are needed to reduce this burden of disease.

## Assessment

### History

Your doctor may ask about:
- Symptoms such as pain, itch, weeping, crusting, colour, and swelling, and whether they limit normal activity.
- Whole-body symptoms such as fever, vomiting, chills and rigors, lethargy, or being unable to drink.
- How long the symptoms have lasted and how they are changing.
- Recent breaks in the skin, such as insect bites, grazes, cuts, or bite wounds.
- Immunisation history. *Haemophilus influenzae* is an important cause of infection in unimmunised children.
- Any creams or medicines already being used on the skin.
- Previous skin infections.
- Eczema or other skin problems in the child or family.
- Anyone else in the household with current or recent signs of infection.
- Allergies.
- Social circumstances and supports.

**Recent immunisation:** Swelling and redness of a limb is common after an immunisation and can look like cellulitis. It usually starts within 24 hours of the immunisation, in a child who is otherwise well, with relatively little pain.

### Examination

- Temperature and heart rate.
- The skin is checked for rash, redness, warmth, tenderness, swelling, and discharge.

**Signs that suggest a severe skin infection:**
- Swelling, redness, tenderness, or warmth that is getting worse quickly.
- Pain.
- Loss of function, such as of a joint.
- Extensive sores with pus or crusts.
- Signs of infection spreading through the body, such as a fast heart rate.

### Investigations

**Your doctor may consider:**
- **A wound swab or pus sample** if there is an open wound or a pustule. If there is enough pus to collect, this is sent instead of a swab, as it is a better sample. A swab helps guide antibiotic choice for a wound that is clinically infected. It cannot diagnose infection, which relies on clinical judgement.
- **A swab for MRSA** (methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus*) if there are risk factors:
  - Frequent courses of antibiotics.
  - Infection not responding to usual oral antibiotics.
  - Family history of MRSA.
  - Time in a healthcare setting with high MRSA risk, such as a North Island hospital or an overseas hospital.
  - Crowded living conditions.
  - Open or broken skin.
- **Imaging** (usually X-ray or ultrasound) after paediatric advice, if an injury raises concern about a broken bone or something stuck under the skin.

Blood tests and X-rays are not usually needed for uncomplicated skin infections.

### Working Out the Type of Infection

Your doctor will consider the most likely diagnosis:

- **Cellulitis:** localised redness, warmth, tenderness, and swelling, especially on a limb. It is a common bacterial skin infection that can affect people of all ages and can occur anywhere on the body. The most common causes are *Streptococcus pyogenes*, *Staphylococcus aureus*, and group C or group G streptococci. Severe or rapidly progressive cellulitis may lead to septicaemia, bone infection (osteomyelitis), joint infection (septic arthritis), endocarditis, or necrotising fasciitis.
- **Necrotising fasciitis:** early signs may be non-specific and similar to cellulitis. They include marked pain out of proportion to the examination, rapidly advancing soft tissue involvement, signs of sepsis, blistering with dead-looking skin (necrotic bullous changes), and crepitus (a crackling feel under the skin). It needs prompt surgical assessment.
- **Infected eczema:** itchy skin with eczema features plus signs of infection:
  - Pustules, weeping, or crusting.
  - Blisters or erosions (punched-out lesions).
  - A sudden widespread eczema flare with increased itch.
- **Eczema herpeticum:** a severe, sudden-onset herpes simplex infection, most often seen as a complication of eczema. It often needs hospital admission. Features include:
  - Areas of rapidly worsening, painful eczema.
  - Fever, lethargy, and distress.
  - Clustered blisters like early cold sores, and punched-out lesions.
  - Failure to respond to treatment.
- **Boils and abscesses (purulent infections):** a swelling containing pus or fluid, draining pus, or with a yellow or white centre. The most common cause is *Staphylococcus aureus*. A boil (furuncle) is a deep infection of a hair follicle and appears as one or more tender red spots, lumps, or pustules. A boil with multiple heads is called a carbuncle. Large boils can form abscesses (a collection of pus in a cavity). Infection of the surrounding skin (cellulitis) can also occur and may cause fever and illness.
- **Itchy spots (papules):** may be scabies or other insect bites or stings, a viral rash (viral exanthem), or impetigo.
- **Fungal infection:** round or oval flat scaly patches, which are often itchy.
- **Wound infection:** a localised infection around a wound. Your doctor will also keep in mind a healing burn or non-accidental injury.

## Management

- If sepsis is likely, your child will be treated for sepsis in hospital.
- Your doctor will arrange hospital assessment for any red flag, including suspected periorbital cellulitis or joint infection, suspected necrotising fasciitis, or suspected eczema herpeticum.
- For children at high risk of acute rheumatic fever, skin infections are treated according to specific guidelines.
- Treatment then depends on the diagnosis: abscess, bite wound, cellulitis, impetigo, infected eczema, recurrent staphylococcal infection, scabies, or wound infection.
- Your doctor may also offer a referral to the Healthy Homes Initiative.

### Advice for Parents and Caregivers

**School and early childhood centre:** If there are open sores that cannot be covered, keep your child away until the sores have dried up, or until at least 24 hours after oral antibiotics have been started.

**Healthy skin:**
- Good food and nutrition are important for healthy skin.
- Clean hands with soap and water often.
- Cut and file fingernails.
- Cover sores and cuts with plasters.
- Keep skin clean and wear clean clothes.
- Keep the house clean inside and out.
- Wash sheets and towels regularly in hot water, or dry them in a dryer.
- Treat animals for fleas regularly.

**Caring for minor cuts, sores, and other skin conditions:**
- Clean and cover cuts and sores with plasters.
- Check cuts and sores every day.
- Care for other skin conditions such as eczema, using creams and lotions.
- Use your own sheets and towels.
- If you need help, ask the nurse or another health worker.

**If your child is prescribed antibiotics:**
- Take the full course as prescribed.
- Do not share medicines with others.
- Supervise children taking medicine.
- If the infection keeps getting worse or does not clear up, go back to your doctor.
