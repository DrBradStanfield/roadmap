# Acitretin Shared Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Acitretin is an oral retinoid used for moderate-to-severe skin conditions. It is always started by a specialist; ongoing prescribing can then be shared with your GP.

---

## What Is Acitretin Used For?

Acitretin is used for:

- Moderate-to-severe plaque or pustular psoriasis resistant to topical treatments
- Severe congenital ichthyosis
- Severe Darier disease
- Skin cancer prevention in immunosuppressed patients

---

## Who Should Not Take Acitretin?

Acitretin is relatively contraindicated in:

- Women of child-bearing potential or those who are breastfeeding
- People with liver or kidney impairment
- People with poorly controlled high lipids (dyslipidaemia)
- People taking tetracycline antibiotics

Before starting, your specialist will have checked your liver function tests (LFTs) and fasting lipids.

---

## How to Take It

- Usual dose: 10 to 50 mg daily
- Take with or after food
- Requires special authority (funded prescription)
- Response may take up to 4 weeks

**Important advice from your specialist:**

- Avoid alcohol — it can increase liver toxicity and raise acitretin levels even after stopping the drug
- Protect your skin and eyes from sunlight; avoid sunbeds
- Do **not** donate blood while taking acitretin

---

## Monitoring — What Your GP Will Check

Your GP will arrange blood tests at:

- **1 month** after starting, then
- Every **3 to 6 months** ongoing

Tests include:

- AST and ALT (liver enzymes)
- Lipids (cholesterol and triglycerides)
- Pregnancy test (if applicable)

---

## Possible Side Effects

### Common (General)

| Side Effect | What to Do |
|---|---|
| Dry lips and skin; skin peeling | Adjust dose (consult specialist) |
| Hair loss (reversible when drug is stopped) | Usually resolves; discuss with doctor |
| Skin redness, itch, worsening psoriasis | Consult specialist |
| Increased sensitivity to sunlight | Use sunscreen and protective clothing |
| Runny nose (rhinitis) | Symptomatic treatment |

### Eyes

| Side Effect | What to Do |
|---|---|
| Dry eyes (xerophthalmia) | Artificial tear drops |
| Reduced night vision | Discuss with doctor |

### Gut

| Side Effect | What to Do |
|---|---|
| Nausea; abdominal discomfort | Take with food |

### Liver — Stop and Contact Your Doctor

Stop acitretin and seek medical review if:

- ALT or AST rises to more than 2–3 times the upper limit of normal
- Abnormal LFTs do not return to normal or continue to worsen

### Metabolic

| Side Effect | What to Do |
|---|---|
| Elevated lipids not responding to diet | Your doctor may consider lipid-lowering therapy; may need to stop acitretin |

### Neurological — Stop and Contact Your Doctor

Stop acitretin and seek medical review if you develop:

- Severe headache, visual disturbance, nausea, or vomiting (may indicate benign intracranial hypertension)
- Peripheral neuropathy or depression

### Blood

- Agranulocytosis (very low white cells) — very rare. Stop drug and seek urgent medical review.

### Muscles

- Arthralgia (joint pain) or myalgia (muscle pain) — pain relief may be used; discuss with your doctor.

---

## Drug Interactions

Tell your doctor and pharmacist about all medications. Key interactions:

- **Tetracycline antibiotics** — Do not use together. Risk of benign intracranial hypertension.
- **Other retinoids (e.g. vitamin A supplements)** — Avoid. Combining may cause a vitamin A toxicity-like condition.
- **Methotrexate** — Increases risk of liver toxicity. If both must be used, more frequent LFT monitoring is required.
- **Alcohol** — Increases acitretin blood levels, including for some time after stopping the drug. Avoid alcohol.

For a full interaction check, a pharmacist can help, or contact a medicines information service.

---

## Pregnancy

Acitretin is highly teratogenic (causes birth defects):

- It is relatively contraindicated in women who could become pregnant
- **Conception should be avoided for at least 3 years after stopping acitretin**
- Regular pregnancy testing is part of monitoring

Discuss contraception and family planning with your doctor before and during treatment.

---

## Cervical Screening

Acitretin is **not** classified as an immunosuppressant. The usual cervical screening interval applies — no changes required because of this medication.

---

## Vaccinations

Acitretin does not suppress the immune system. Vaccinations can be given as normal. Your GP may recommend:

- Annual flu vaccine
- Pneumococcal vaccination
- Shingles vaccination (consider before starting treatment)
- Hepatitis B vaccine if you are non-immune and at risk
