# Sub-fertility

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and initial management of sub-fertility — difficulty conceiving after a period of regular, unprotected intercourse.

---

## Background

1 in 4 couples in New Zealand experience fertility problems.

Age is the most important determinant of female fertility. In couples where the female partner is aged 25 years, 80% will conceive within a year of trying. This percentage declines with age.

Other causes of sub-fertility include:

- Disorders of ovulation
- Disorders of the fallopian tubes and/or endometriosis
- Seminal abnormalities (sperm problems)

In 30–40% of cases there is more than one cause, and in 20–30% of cases the sub-fertility remains unexplained. Māori and Pacific people are less likely to seek medical help for sub-fertility.

---

## When to See a Doctor

Investigations are usually arranged when:

- The couple has been trying to conceive for **more than 6 months**, and the female partner is **older than 35 years**, or
- The couple has been trying to conceive for **more than 12 months**, and the female partner is **35 years or younger**

Your doctor may investigate earlier if there are predisposing factors, such as irregular periods, symptoms suggesting endometriosis, undescended testes, or known semen abnormalities.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

**Both partners:**
- Ages
- How long you have been trying to conceive (off all contraception, without a resulting live birth)
- Previous pregnancies and children for either partner
- Frequency and timing of intercourse
- Sexually transmitted infections
- Medical history and medications (especially medications that can affect fertility)
- Smoking, vaping, alcohol, cannabis, and other substance use, including anabolic steroids
- Social factors (e.g. shift work, chemical exposure)
- Family history of premature ovarian insufficiency, fertility issues, or chromosomal abnormalities (e.g. cystic fibrosis, Klinefelter's syndrome)

**Female partner specifically:**
- Obstetric history: pregnancies, miscarriages, terminations
- Menstrual cycle regularity and pattern, age of menarche
- Heavy or painful periods, bleeding between periods
- Symptoms of PCOS (polycystic ovarian syndrome):
  - Irregular cycles (fewer than 8 cycles per year, or cycles outside the 22–34 day range from 3 years after menarche)
  - Signs of androgen excess: hirsutism (excess hair), acne, hair thinning
  - Acanthosis nigricans (darkened skin patches) or skin tags
- Symptoms of endometriosis:
  - Period pain that limits daily activities and quality of life
  - Non-cyclical pelvic pain
  - Deep pain with intercourse, often persisting 2–3 days after
  - Fatigue alongside any of the above
  - Abdominal bloating, unexplained bowel or bladder symptoms especially if cyclical
- Previous uterine or ovarian problems
- Libido concerns or vaginismus

**Male partner specifically:**
- Erection or ejaculation problems
- Testicular problems (infection, trauma, tumour, surgery)
- Congenital anomalies
- Androgen (steroid) use

**Physical examination** your doctor may perform:
- BMI and blood pressure (both partners)
- Pelvic examination if there are symptoms suggesting abnormal bleeding or endometriosis
- Signs of PCOS or thyroid disease (acne, hair changes, metabolic features)
- Genital examination in the male partner if there is a relevant history

## Investigations

Your doctor may consider the following investigations. These are typically arranged once you meet the timing criteria above, or earlier if there are predisposing factors.

**For the female partner:**

*Standard investigations:*
- FSH (follicle-stimulating hormone) and estradiol (E2) — ideally on day 2–4 of the menstrual cycle (day 1 = first day of bleeding)
- Full blood count (FBC), blood group and antibody screen, ferritin
- Syphilis serology
- Rubella immunity
- HbA1c
- Hepatitis B antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C antibodies, HIV status
- Prolactin — if there is menstrual disturbance alongside sub-fertility
- TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) — if irregular cycles are present

*Additional investigations where relevant:*
- If an ovulation disorder is suspected (irregular or absent periods):
  - Day 2–4: LH, free androgen index, free testosterone, SHBG
  - Day 21: Progesterone (may need repeating every 7 days until menstruation if cycle is prolonged; a result above 20 nmol/L suggests ovulation has occurred)
  - For patients with absent periods (amenorrhoea), your doctor may prescribe a short course of **norethisterone** (10 mg twice daily for 5 days) to trigger a period, allowing blood tests to be timed correctly
- Cervical screening if not up to date
- STI screen if indicated

*Optional (unfunded):*
- **AMH (anti-Müllerian hormone)** — a test of ovarian reserve. It does not predict your natural chance of conception, but gives an indication of how quickly your egg reserve may decline and can help predict response to IVF. AMH peaks in the mid-20s and declines with age. It is not useful in PCOS. This test is self-funded.

**For the male partner:**

- **Semen analysis** — the provision of a sample can feel embarrassing, but your doctor will handle this sensitively. The sample must be kept warm and delivered to the laboratory within 1 hour of collection. Semen analysis is a crude measure of male fertility and results can vary significantly between samples — if the first result is abnormal, a repeat is arranged 4–6 weeks later.
- Hepatitis B antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis C antibodies, HIV status, syphilis screen

## Management

### Permanent Causes of Infertility

If one or both partners has a known permanent cause of infertility (such as azoospermia, bilateral tubal blockage, bilateral removal of ovaries, premature ovarian failure, or previous sterilisation), your doctor will refer directly to a fertility specialist.

### Optimising Fertility

Your doctor may consider the following advice to improve your chances of conception:

**Timing of intercourse:**
- The fertile window is from approximately 5 days before until 1 day after ovulation
- Alternate-day intercourse from the end of your period until at least one day after expected ovulation is as effective as daily intercourse and may reduce pressure
- Ovulation tracking tools can help:
  - Smartphone apps that combine temperature, urine, or salivary data
  - Urinary LH kits (commercially available) — helpful if cycles are regular. Note these can give false positives in PCOS, primary ovarian insufficiency, or perimenopause

**Lifestyle factors:**

*For both partners:*
- **Smoking and vaping:** Smoking reduces monthly conception chances by approximately 50%. Vaping may also impair sperm health and its effect on egg quality is unknown. Both partners need to be non-smokers for at least 3 months to be eligible for publicly funded fertility treatment.
- **Weight:** A healthy BMI improves fertility and eligibility for funded treatment. Women need a BMI under 35 for referral, and under 32 for publicly funded treatment. Men need a BMI under 40.
- **Alcohol:** Associated with reduced fertility in both men and women. Abstaining is recommended. There is no known safe level of alcohol in pregnancy.
- **Cannabis and other substances:** Cannabis has a detrimental effect on both male and female fertility. Abstaining is advised.
- **Caffeine:** High intake may reduce fertility, though study quality is low.
- **Chemical exposure:** Some chemicals (bisphenols, phthalates, parabens, pesticides) can affect reproductive health. Minimising exposure is sensible.

*For male partners additionally:*
- Avoid medications that affect sperm (e.g. **finasteride**, anabolic steroids)
- Wear loose-fitting underwear; avoid saunas and hot tubs
- Aim for ejaculation every 48–72 hours
- **Oral antioxidant supplements** (e.g. Menevit, Vitamenz) may improve sperm health — these are not funded. Sperm production cycles take 74–78 days, so ideally supplementing for at least 3 months before testing

### Supplements for the Female Partner

Your doctor may recommend:

- **Folic acid** — recommended from at least 4 weeks before conception until 12 weeks of pregnancy, to reduce the risk of neural tube defects
  - Standard dose: 0.8 mg daily
  - Higher dose (5 mg daily) if: personal or family history of neural tube defect, BMI above 30, diabetes, coeliac disease or malabsorption, sickle cell anaemia, epilepsy or anti-seizure medication, or taking folate antagonists (e.g. sulfasalazine)
- **Iodine (potassium iodate 150 micrograms daily)** — recommended when pregnant or breastfeeding; ideally started when planning pregnancy, especially for vegetarians or vegans. Seaweed and kelp tablets are not recommended (iodine content is variable and can be toxic). Not all over-the-counter antenatal vitamins contain the recommended dose.
- **Vitamin D** — if risk factors for deficiency are present (dark skin, limited sun exposure, malabsorption, obesity, certain medications)

### PCOS and Ovulation Problems

If ovulatory dysfunction is related to PCOS, your doctor will manage this alongside the sub-fertility pathway. Ovulation induction is not initiated in primary care — it requires a fertility specialist. Where specialist access is limited, **letrozole** may be considered as an oral ovulation induction agent (note: this is not a licensed indication in New Zealand).

### Vaccinations

Your doctor may offer vaccination for measles, mumps and rubella, varicella (chickenpox), and COVID-19 if you are non-immune. Avoid pregnancy for 1 month after these live vaccines. Influenza and pertussis vaccines are best given during pregnancy.

### Travel

If planning overseas travel while trying to conceive, discuss infectious disease risks (e.g. Zika, malaria) with your doctor, as these may affect fertility planning.

### Other Fertility Options

If conception does not occur or is not possible, your doctor may sensitively discuss:
- Fertility preservation (freezing eggs, sperm, embryos, or tissue for later use)
- Donor egg or donor sperm
- Surrogacy (traditional or gestational)
- Adoption and fostering

LGBTQIA+ individuals and couples can find specific support and treatment information at [Fertility NZ — LGBTQIA+](https://www.fertilitynz.org.nz/lgbtqi).

## Specialist Referral

If you have been trying to conceive for the required period and meet eligibility criteria, your doctor may refer you for a fertility specialist assessment. This can be publicly funded or private, depending on your eligibility.

**Publicly funded eligibility** generally requires:
- New Zealand citizenship, residency, or a work permit for at least 2 years
- At least 1 year of trying to conceive (or a known severe cause)
- Female partner's BMI under 35 (treatment only starts when under 32); male partner's BMI under 40
- Both partners non-smokers (or ex-smokers for more than 3 months)
- No documented drug or alcohol use disorder in the previous 12 months
- Female partner under 40 years; male partner under 55 years
- 0 or 1 existing child to the current relationship

A specialist will calculate a CPAC (Clinical Priority Assessment Criteria) score at your first appointment — a score of 65 or above is required to access publicly funded IVF treatment.

Single women and same-sex couples may be eligible if clear biological causes of sub-fertility are present (e.g. ovulatory dysfunction, known tubal sub-fertility, severe endometriosis). Otherwise, eligibility requires completion of 12 cycles of donor insemination (at least 6 in an accredited fertility clinic).

If you do not meet eligibility criteria or prefer not to wait, your doctor can refer you for a **private fertility assessment**. Private assessment does not preclude access to publicly funded treatment if you later meet criteria.
