# Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: July 2017.*

Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) includes Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis, and indeterminate colitis. All cause inflammation of the gut lining, with diarrhoea, rectal bleeding, abdominal pain, and weight loss.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department if you have suspected or known IBD and you have:**
- Fever.
- A fast heart rate.
- Low blood pressure.
- Abdominal pain.

**Also call 111 or go to the emergency department if you are acutely unwell with a flare-up.** In ulcerative colitis, doctors treat a flare as acute when there are more than 6 bloody bowel motions per day, plus one or more of:
- Temperature more than 37.8°C.
- Heart rate more than 90.
- Haemoglobin (Hb) less than 105 on a blood test.
- CRP more than 30 on a blood test.

Serious causes your doctor may consider in a flare include megacolon or a bowel perforation in ulcerative colitis, and bowel obstruction or an abscess in Crohn's disease.

---

## Background

- The type and location of inflammation tell Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis apart:
  - **Ulcerative colitis** affects only the colon. The inflammation of the bowel lining is continuous and extends up from the anus.
  - **Crohn's disease** can affect the end of the small bowel (terminal ileum) and/or the colon. The inflammation goes through the full thickness of the bowel wall and is often patchy.
  - **Indeterminate colitis** (also called IBD-U, IBD-type unspecified) is when the bowel is inflamed but there are no features to diagnose ulcerative colitis or Crohn's disease for certain.
- New cases of IBD peak between 15 and 35 years of age, but IBD may occur at any age.
- Diagnosis is made by colonoscopy and biopsy, with further imaging as required.

### Smoking and IBD

Smoking and IBD have a paradoxical relationship:
- Smoking increases the risk of developing Crohn's disease.
- Smoking reduces the risk of ulcerative colitis. Stopping smoking can trigger ulcerative colitis.
- Smoking worsens the course of Crohn's disease.

### Effects outside the gut

IBD can also affect other parts of the body (extra-intestinal manifestations):
- Skin, such as erythema nodosum or pyoderma gangrenosum.
- Arthritis.
- Eyes, such as episcleritis or iritis.
- Mouth ulcers.
- Night sweats.
- Primary sclerosing cholangitis (a liver and bile duct condition).

## Assessment

### Symptoms that suggest IBD

- IBD may be suspected with diarrhoea with urgency, rectal bleeding, abdominal pain, and weight loss of more than 4.5 kg.
- Symptoms at night, such as diarrhoea or abdominal pain that wakes you, suggest a serious cause such as IBD or colorectal cancer. Functional diarrhoea, such as irritable bowel syndrome (IBS), usually stops at night.

### History

Your doctor may ask about:
- Family history of IBD, colorectal cancer, coeliac disease, or autoimmune disease.
- Medicines, especially NSAIDs (which can damage the bowel), antibiotics, and laxatives.
- Smoking, and when any change in smoking happened relative to your bowel symptoms.
- Recent travel.
- Symptoms in the skin, eyes, or joints.

### Examination

- Temperature, pulse, and blood pressure.
- Examination of the abdomen and rectum, looking for bleeding from the back passage and for disease around the anus such as abscesses, fistulas, or fissures.

### Investigations

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Blood tests: FBC, CRP, LFTs, electrolytes, and coeliac markers.
- Stool tests: faecal culture (including ova and parasites if appropriate) and *Clostridioides difficile* toxin.

## Management

### First presentation

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Reviewing you once blood results return:
  - Anaemia, raised white cells or platelets, or a raised CRP or ESR suggest IBD. In that case a faecal calprotectin test is not needed.
  - Otherwise, a faecal calprotectin test. A negative result (less than 50 micrograms per L) makes IBD extremely unlikely.
- Arranging a colonoscopy, ideally done by a gastroenterologist when IBD is likely. Colorectal cancer is also considered as a cause of the symptoms.
- If IBD is diagnosed, referral to a gastroenterologist for treatment.

If IBD is highly likely and you are unwell, your doctor may seek specialist advice. People in this situation can become acutely unwell with potentially severe disease. The specialist may advise:
- Starting a 5-aminosalicylate (5-ASA), such as mesalazine, olsalazine, or sulfasalazine, while you wait for an appointment, or other treatments such as steroids.
- An urgent gastroenterology appointment.

Treatment by a gastroenterologist involves bringing the disease into remission and then keeping it in remission, either with medicines or surgery.

### Ongoing management

Your doctor may refer you to a gastroenterologist, depending on how severe your IBD is and the medicines you take. **Your doctor may consider:**
- Help to stop smoking.
- Checking that you take your medicines as prescribed, any side effects, and drug monitoring.
- Warning you about the risk of opportunistic infections, such as chickenpox (varicella), because of a weakened immune system. Immunomodulators, long-term steroids, and biologics weaken it, especially if you take more than one of these. See your doctor early if you are unwell.
- Avoiding long-term prednisone or repeated courses of prednisone, and referral for steroid-sparing treatments.
- Setting up recalls, such as for flu vaccination, and adding alerts to your record if you take immune-suppressing drugs or biologics.
- Your confidence and self-image. Depression is seen more often in IBD.

#### Nutrition

- In active Crohn's disease, undernutrition is common: weight loss, protein deficiency, and deficiencies in vitamins, minerals, and trace elements.
- Undernutrition has a negative impact on the course of the disease, the rate of complications after surgery, and mortality.
- A healthy balanced diet is encouraged.
- Your doctor may check for and treat nutritional deficiencies, such as iron, vitamin B12, and vitamin D. Vitamin K, zinc, and folate deficiencies are less common.
- Unintentional weight loss or nutrient deficiencies are a reason for referral to a gastroenterologist.

#### Bone health

Your doctor may check your bone density, because repeated courses of prednisone or low vitamin D levels raise the risk of osteoporosis.

#### Contraception

Young women may want to discuss contraception, fertility, and pregnancy with their doctor.
- The combined oral contraceptive pill may be absorbed less well if Crohn's disease affects the small bowel. Large bowel involvement does not affect absorption.
- The combined pill should not be used if you are prone to severe flares needing hospital care, because the risk of blood clots (venous thromboembolism) is increased.
- IBD increases the risk of osteoporosis. The effect of the contraceptive injection medroxyprogesterone acetate (Depo Provera) on bone density may add to this. Other progestogen-only contraceptives may be better, as they do not affect bone density.

#### Pregnancy and breastfeeding

- Before conception, good control of IBD is important. All medicines except methotrexate are continued before and during pregnancy.
- Fertility:
  - In ulcerative colitis, fertility is no different unless you have had "pouch surgery".
  - In Crohn's disease, the main factor is good disease control. The better the control, the more likely you are to get pregnant.
- The main issues are preterm birth (ulcerative colitis and Crohn's disease) and low birth weight (Crohn's disease). Caesarean section is more common.
- Your doctor may refer you to a gastroenterologist if you are planning a pregnancy or are newly pregnant.
- A higher dose of folic acid (5 mg) is used for women taking sulfasalazine, or with malabsorption after small bowel surgery (resection).

**Medicines in pregnancy and breastfeeding:**
- It is safe to continue 5-ASA during pregnancy and breastfeeding.
- Methotrexate must not be used in pregnancy. Both women and men should delay a pregnancy for at least 3 to 6 months after stopping it.
- Steroids may be associated with cleft palate in the first trimester, but are used if needed to control the disease. Your doctor may seek specialist advice if unsure.
- Thiopurines, such as azathioprine and 6-mercaptopurine (6-MP): the New Zealand Formulary classes mercaptopurine as pregnancy category D and advises avoiding it when breastfeeding. Specialists often continue thiopurines in pregnancy. Local specialist advice is that they are safe to use for conception and pregnancy, and that mercaptopurine is widely used in pregnancy. Decide with your specialist.
- Biologics have shown no significant abnormalities to date. They may need to stop at 32 weeks, as they cross the placenta. Specialist input is required. Babies should have no live vaccines for the first 6 months.
- Apart from methotrexate, and the formulary advice on mercaptopurine above, these medicines are considered safe in breastfeeding.

### Colon cancer surveillance

- Both ulcerative colitis and Crohn's colitis increase the risk of colon cancer.
- The main risk relates to how long you have had the disease, how much of the bowel it affects, and how active it is, rather than which type of IBD you have.
- A baseline colonoscopy is offered at about 10 years after the disease starts.
- Further surveillance colonoscopies are usually 3 yearly, but this will change if other risk factors are present.

### Flare-ups

If you have any red flags or are acutely unwell, call 111 or go to the emergency department (see above).

**Tests your doctor may consider:**
- Faecal culture and *Clostridioides difficile* toxin. Relapses are often linked to infections, or to *C. difficile* after antibiotics.
- Blood tests: FBC, CRP, LFTs, and electrolytes.

**In ulcerative colitis, your doctor may consider optimising 5-ASA:**
- Increasing oral 5-ASA, such as mesalazine 4 g per day, which can be taken as a once-daily dose.
- Starting rectal 5-ASA, such as mesalazine enemas, if the disease is on the left side of the colon. These can be difficult to hold, but it is worth persisting.
- Suppositories for proctitis (inflammation of the rectum).
- If you are on the maximal 5-ASA dose, or the response is limited after 1 week, starting prednisone and referral to a gastroenterologist. Options include starting an immunomodulator or changing current medicines.

**In Crohn's disease, your doctor may consider:**
- Starting prednisone and referral to a gastroenterologist. It is important to consider starting an immunomodulator or a biologic, or changing current medicines.

If there are concerns about the best medicine to use, your doctor may seek specialist advice or referral, especially if you already take an immunomodulator or a biologic.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you to a gastroenterologist if:
- IBD is diagnosed at first presentation or on colonoscopy.
- You have flare-ups, especially if steroids are used.
- There are medicine problems, or steroid-sparing treatments are needed.
- You are on the maximal 5-ASA dose, or the response is limited after 1 week.
- A pregnancy is planned or has been diagnosed.
- You have unintentional weight loss or nutrient deficiencies, shown by blood tests or diet history.
