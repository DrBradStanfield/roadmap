# Diving Accidents and Decompression Illness

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and emergency management of decompression illness (DCI, also known as "the bends") and related diving injuries.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Call an ambulance if any of the following are present after diving:

- Symptoms within 24 hours of diving
- Co-existing near-drowning
- Rapid ascent when diving
- Ascent over 300 m after diving (e.g. flying)

If red flags are present, your doctor will arrange urgent transfer to a hyperbaric unit.

---

## What Is Decompression Illness?

Decompression illness (DCI) is a condition affecting primarily divers (and less frequently aviators) following decompression or exposure to reduced ambient pressure. It has a spectrum of severity and may require urgent treatment.

It may be caused by:

- Bubbles developing in tissues due to inadequate ascent time and decompression
- Cerebral gas embolism — air trapped from the lungs entering the arterial circulation
- Arterialisation of venous bubbles through a patent foramen ovale (PFO) or other right-to-left shunt

Symptoms usually develop within 2 hours and almost always within 24 hours of decompression. Symptoms appearing soon after surfacing suggest greater severity.

**Barotrauma** is damage to body tissues from a sudden change in pressure. Commonly affected areas are the middle ear, sinuses, and teeth. Anyone with sensorineural hearing loss and/or vertigo following a pressure exposure requires specialist review to exclude inner ear injury.

---

## Symptoms of DCI

The following symptoms after diving should be taken seriously:

- Joint or muscle pain around a joint or tendon (the most common presenting symptom)
- Numbness or tingling
- Itching, redness, or mottled skin
- Significant malaise, fatigue, or persistent headache
- Dizziness or vertigo
- Weakness or paralysis
- Chest pain or shortness of breath
- Ataxia (loss of coordination)
- Confusion, memory loss, or unusual behaviour
- Visual disturbance
- Unconsciousness

---

## Risk Factors for DCI

- Previous decompression illness
- Rapid ascent when diving
- Heavy exertion during or soon after a dive
- Multiple ascents during a dive
- Multiple days of diving
- Deep (over 30 m) or long dives
- Equipment failure during a dive
- Running out of air while diving
- Cold water temperatures
- Flying within 24 hours of diving
- Ascending to over 300 m within 24 hours of diving

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Confirm the airway, breathing, and circulation are stable
- Take a full history of the dive profile, symptoms, and timeline
- Establish diving experience, medications, and past medical history (dive computer should travel with the patient if available)
- Check for symptoms of DCI (see above)
- Assess for barotrauma — checking the middle ear, sinuses, teeth; looking for vertigo and sensorineural hearing loss
- Perform an ENT, cardiovascular, and neurological examination
  - Note: neurological findings are often patchy — loss of sensation in non-dermatomal patterns is common

**Key clinical note:** If symptoms are stable 24 hours after diving and there have been no CNS signs, the patient is very unlikely to deteriorate.

### Differential Diagnoses

Your doctor will consider other causes but will arrange transfer to a hyperbaric unit unless an alternative diagnosis can be confirmed:

- Non-diving causes (e.g. stroke, musculoskeletal conditions)
- Other diving-related causes: pneumothorax, other barotrauma, animal envenomation, air contamination, CO2 retention, hyperventilation, panic

### Investigations

If they do not delay transfer, your doctor may arrange:

- Chest X-ray (lying down — to check for barotrauma and aspiration)
- ECG
- Full blood count (FBC)
- Creatinine and electrolytes
- Blood gases (to assess respiratory failure or acidosis)

---

## Management

### Emergency: Suspected DCI

If DCI symptoms or any red flags are present, your doctor will arrange urgent transfer to a hyperbaric unit for hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT).

**Transport principles:**
- Keep the patient horizontal initially — at least the first hour on 100% oxygen before sitting or standing
- Transport must remain below 300 m altitude (or in a pressurised aircraft with cabin pressure below 300 m)
- If the dive buddy had a similar dive profile, they should be transferred too

### Late Presentations

- Up to 24 hours: urgent transfer
- Up to 2 weeks: prompt transfer (full medical assessment required)
- Over 2 weeks: rarely treated with hyperbaric oxygen

### Oxygen Therapy

High-flow oxygen is the critical first-line treatment:

- Best option: specialised oxygen resuscitation system with a demand valve regulator and tight-fitting mask or mouthpiece
- Alternative: non-rebreather mask at more than 15 L/min
- Do **not** use nasal prongs — they do not deliver sufficient oxygen concentration
- Continuous high-flow oxygen should be maintained. Air breaks are not advised for divers with less than 12 hours' equivalent sea-level oxygen exposure

### Intravenous Fluids

Once under medical care:

- If no clinical shock or dehydration: 1000 mL crystalloid rapidly, then 1000 mL per 8 hours
- Watch for urinary retention

### Missed Decompression (No Symptoms)

If a diver has missed decompression stops but has no symptoms of DCI:

- Advise rest
- Start oxygen, give oral fluids, and observe
- Seek hyperbaric advice
- Transfer at the first sign of any DCI symptoms

### Ongoing Monitoring

- Daily review with full examination including neurological assessment
- If deterioration occurs, seek urgent hyperbaric advice for consideration of re-treatment
- If sensorineural hearing loss or vertigo develops, urgent ENT assessment is needed to exclude inner ear injury

---

## When to Refer

Your doctor will arrange **urgent hyperbaric assessment** if:

- Any symptoms of DCI are present
- Any red flags are present
- Uncertain about oxygen therapy
- Any neurological deterioration occurs
- Any symptoms after diving, regardless of suspected diagnosis
- Missed decompression has occurred (even without symptoms)

**Acute ORL (ENT) assessment** is needed for: sensorineural hearing loss or vertigo after diving.

---

*Always discuss symptoms after diving with your doctor promptly — even if symptoms seem mild, they can worsen. This pathway is for clinical reference; individual management should be guided by your treating doctor.*
