# Acute Kidney Injury

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of acute kidney injury (AKI) in adults — identifying who needs emergency care and how to manage and monitor recovery.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department immediately if:

- New severe AKI with creatinine > 354 micromole/L
- Significant fluid overload or potassium > 6.5 mmol/L
- Clinical suspicion of urinary obstruction
- Solitary kidney
- Suspected acute glomerulonephritis (new hypertension with blood in the urine, reduced urine output, and swelling)

---

## What Is AKI?

AKI is a common condition — a sudden fall in kidney function that can arise from many causes including circulatory shock, sepsis, and nephrotoxic drugs. Early detection and treatment may improve outcomes.

**AKI is defined as a creatinine change known or presumed to have occurred within the past 7 days:**

- **Stage 1** — creatinine rises > 1.5–1.9 times baseline, or > 26 micromole/L within 48 hours
- **Stage 2** — creatinine rises > 2–2.9 times baseline
- **Stage 3** — creatinine rises > 3 times baseline, or creatinine > 354 micromole/L

If the change in creatinine does not fit this timeframe, your doctor will work to distinguish AKI from subacute kidney injury or worsening chronic kidney disease (CKD).

---

## Who Is at Risk?

Your doctor will consider AKI if you are acutely unwell and have any of the following risk factors:

- Chronic kidney disease (eGFR < 60)
- Heart failure
- Anaemia
- Liver disease
- Diabetes
- Taking an ACE inhibitor or ARB with a diuretic — especially if also taking an NSAID (the "triple whammy" combination) — particularly if dehydrated
- Previous episode of AKI
- Low blood volume (e.g. burns, vomiting, ileostomy)
- Low blood pressure
- Recent surgery
- Nephrotoxic drugs in the last week (e.g. NSAIDs, aminoglycosides)
- Recent iodinated contrast or angiogram
- Symptoms or risk factors for urinary obstruction
- Sepsis
- Age > 65 years
- Neurological or cognitive impairment with limited access to fluids

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will examine you, checking for:

- Heart rate and blood pressure (including lying and standing)
- Fluid status (swelling, raised jugular venous pressure)
- Signs of low blood pressure or shock
- Signs of infection or sepsis
- Signs of liver disease
- Signs of urinary blockage
- Confusion (a sign of uraemia)
- Blood or protein in the urine (without signs of urinary tract infection — suggests kidney inflammation)

**Sepsis** is life-threatening and often under-recognised. Your doctor will consider this if you have a confirmed or suspected infection and appear very unwell, particularly if your heart rate and breathing are significantly elevated.

---

## Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- **Urine tests** — urinalysis, urine albumin/creatinine ratio, red cell morphology, microscopy, and culture
- **Blood tests** — creatinine, eGFR, electrolytes, full blood count, calcium, phosphate, bicarbonate, liver function. Note: eGFR values are not reliable when creatinine is changing rapidly
- **Kidney ultrasound** — if the cause is unknown, to look for obstruction

---

## Management

### Important note about your medications

If you have an acute illness, your doctor will review all your medications and may temporarily stop ACE inhibitors, ARBs, diuretics, and NSAIDs to protect your kidneys.

### AKI Stage 1

- Your doctor will arrange a review within 72 hours
- They will treat any identifiable cause
- Medications that stress the kidneys will be reviewed or paused
- Clinical monitoring (blood pressure, fluid status, kidney function) every 2–3 days
- If kidney function continues to worsen, specialist assessment will be arranged
- If the cause is corrected, creatinine usually returns to baseline within 1–2 weeks, though full recovery may take up to 8 weeks

### AKI Stage 2

- Your doctor will arrange a review within 24 hours
- Same medication review and monitoring as Stage 1 (every 2–3 days)
- Specialist assessment if kidney function continues to worsen

### AKI Stage 3

- Your doctor will arrange urgent hospital assessment

---

## Medications to Avoid with AKI

The following drugs are nephrotoxic (harmful to kidneys) and should be avoided or used with great caution:

- NSAIDs and COX-2 inhibitors (e.g. ibuprofen, diclofenac, celecoxib)
- The "triple whammy" combination: ACE inhibitor or ARB + diuretic + NSAID — avoid during any acute illness
- Aminoglycosides (e.g. gentamicin)
- Amphotericin
- Lithium
- Calcineurin inhibitors (ciclosporin, tacrolimus)
- Methotrexate

Drugs that can worsen low blood pressure (and therefore kidney blood flow):
- ACE inhibitors, ARBs, diuretics

Drugs that can accumulate when kidneys are not working well:
- Opiates, digoxin, lithium, metformin

---

## Long-Term Monitoring After AKI

An episode of AKI increases the likelihood of developing CKD later. Your doctor will:

- Arrange an annual kidney health check for 3 years after AKI, including:
  - Blood pressure measurement
  - Urine albumin/creatinine ratio (microalbuminuria)
  - Creatinine, eGFR, and electrolytes
- Monitor blood pressure, targeting:
  - < 140/80 in most people
  - < 130/70 if you have diabetes, urine ACR > 30, or long-standing hypertension with ACR > 3.5
  - < 150/90 may be reasonable if aged > 75 years
- Monitor kidney function regularly
- Avoid high-risk medications during any future acute illness
