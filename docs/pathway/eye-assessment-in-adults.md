# Eye Assessment in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway describes how your doctor approaches an eye assessment in adults — what history they will take, how they examine the eye, and what findings may be significant.

For eye problems in children, a separate assessment pathway applies.

---

## Symptoms Your Doctor Will Ask About

Your doctor will ask about:

- **Visual disturbance** — decreased vision, floaters, flashes of light, distortion, or double vision (diplopia)
- **Discomfort** — foreign body sensation, sensitivity to light (photophobia), pain, or itch
- **Appearance** — redness or changes in how the eye looks
- **Discharge** — whether it is watery, mucoid (clear/stringy), or purulent (yellow/green pus)

They will also ask about:

- Any history of eye trauma, surgery, contact lens use, or a lazy eye (amblyopia)
- Eye drops you currently use
- Relevant medical conditions and medications
- Family history of blindness, glaucoma, or macular degeneration
- Your occupation and driving status

## How Your Doctor Examines the Eye

Your doctor will perform a systematic examination, which may include some or all of the following steps depending on your symptoms.

### 1. General Appearance

Your doctor will examine:

- Your face and eyelids, including the undersurface of the lids
- The front of the eye — looking for redness or discharge in the conjunctiva (the white of the eye and inside the lids), sclera, iris, and pupil

Redness is assessed by how severe it is and where it is greatest — near the cornea, in the fornix (fold of the eye), or in a localised sector.

Notable findings may include:

- **Episcleritis or scleritis** — deeper vessels with a violet hue; a painful ache suggests scleritis
- **Iris obscuration** — may indicate corneal clouding, blood, or pus in the anterior chamber
- **Irregular or tear-shaped pupil** — may be seen in penetrating eye injury or iritis

### 2. Visual Acuity

Your doctor will test your vision in each eye separately using a Snellen chart at the correct distance, with your usual glasses if worn.

- If your vision is still reduced, they may recheck using a pinhole (which helps distinguish refractive errors from other causes)
- If vision improves with a pinhole and there is no cataract, you may be referred to an optometrist to update your glasses prescription
- If you cannot read any letters, your doctor will check finger counting, hand movement perception, and light perception in order

Near vision is also tested with reading glasses.

### 3. Visual Fields

Your doctor will test your peripheral vision by asking you to cover one eye and count or detect finger movements presented in different quadrants of your vision. This helps detect field defects that may indicate neurological or optic nerve problems.

### 4. Eye Movements

Your doctor will check for reduced eye movements, double vision (diplopia), and nystagmus (involuntary eye movement), and ask about pain on eye movement.

### 5. Anterior Chamber and Pupil Reactions

Using a light, your doctor will:

- Shine a light from the side to look for blood (hyphaema) or pus in the front chamber of the eye
- Check direct and consensual pupil reactions
- If there is visual loss, they will perform a swinging flashlight test to look for a relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD), which can indicate optic nerve damage

### 6. Ophthalmoscope Examination

Your doctor may use an ophthalmoscope to examine:

- **Cornea with fluorescein staining** — a blue light reveals green staining of any surface defects, such as a corneal abrasion or herpetic (dendritic) ulcer
- **Red reflex, cornea, and lens** — from arm's length, the red/orange glow inside the pupil is assessed. A poor or absent red reflex may suggest inflammation, blood, cataract, vitreous opacity, or retinal detachment
- **Fundus** — the optic disc, blood vessels, peripheral retina, and macula are examined. Your doctor will not dilate the pupil if there is a suspected head injury or iris trauma

Dilating eye drops (mydriatics) may be used to widen the pupil for a better view of the fundus. Local anaesthetic drops may be used to aid examination — if pain resolves completely with these, the problem is likely on the surface of the eye.

### 7. Slit Lamp Examination

If a slit lamp is available, your doctor may use it to examine the eyelids, conjunctiva, sclera, cornea, anterior chamber, iris, pupil, and lens in detail. This is particularly useful for corneal problems.

### 8. Intraocular Pressure

If appropriate equipment is available, your doctor may measure intraocular pressure (normal is 10 to 21 mmHg). This will not be done if there is a suspected globe injury.

### 9. Colour Vision

If relevant to your symptoms, colour vision may be tested using Ishihara test plates, with each eye tested separately.

## Key Examinations for Vision Loss or Disturbance

If you present with vision loss or visual disturbance, your doctor will ensure all of the following are completed:

- Visual acuity with usual glasses and pinhole
- Red reflex and assessment for cataract
- Swinging flashlight test (RAPD)
- Visual field testing
- Fundus examination — optic discs, vessels, peripheral retina, and macula
- Amsler Grid test for central visual field (tested with reading glasses, each eye separately)

---

## Management

Your doctor will determine management based on the findings of the assessment. This may include:

- Referral to an ophthalmologist for urgent or specialist review
- Treatment of surface eye conditions such as corneal abrasions or conjunctivitis
- Referral to an optometrist if the problem is related to an outdated glasses prescription
- Further investigation of systemic or neurological conditions identified during the assessment
