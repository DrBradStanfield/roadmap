# Dysmorphic Child — Genetics

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach when a child has facial or physical features that are unusual compared to family features — which may be associated with developmental delay, reduced growth, or an underlying genetic condition.

---

## Background

Dysmorphic children have facial or physical features that are unusual in the context of family features. This may be associated with developmental delay, reduced growth, or other structural abnormalities, and may reflect an underlying genetic diagnosis.

Broaching possible dysmorphism can be difficult. If the family are unconcerned and the child is well, there is rarely a rush.

> **Important:** Changing facial features over time may reflect a serious underlying diagnosis — for example, coarse features may be associated with metabolic storage disorders. Always discuss concerns with your doctor.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will typically:

1. **Sketch a 3-generation family tree**, noting:
   - Family history of children or adults with similar features, developmental delay, intellectual disability, or possible consanguinity
   - Pregnancy history — possible exposure to teratogens or infections; results of ultrasound, amniocentesis, or chorionic villus sampling (CVS) if known
   - Delivery complications or neonatal problems

2. **Complete a developmental assessment:**
   - Assess developmental milestones
   - Ask about emotional and behavioural problems, and autistic-like features

3. **Perform a physical examination:**
   - Describe facial features and take a facial photograph
   - Features examined may include: head shape, eye spacing and slant, ear location and structure, size or shape of nose, mouth and chin, palate shape
   - Plot height, weight, and head circumference on percentile charts
   - Assess general muscle tone, movement, and posture
   - Examine the cardiovascular system — many genetic conditions have associated heart defects and murmurs

4. **Consider common conditions** that may present with dysmorphic features:

### Down Syndrome

A chromosomal condition (usually trisomy 21) associated with characteristic facial features, intellectual disability, and a range of associated health conditions.

### Fetal Alcohol Spectrum Disorder (FASD)

A disorder caused by maternal consumption of alcohol during pregnancy. There is a wide range of multisystem presentations, including a variety of neurodevelopmental issues.

### Fragile X Syndrome

The most frequent form of inherited intellectual disability (ID). Affected males have a moderate to severe intellectual disability; females are usually more mildly affected. The genetics are complex.

Potential medical issues include:
- Major reproductive implications for females
- Visual impairment and strabismus
- Recurrent ear infections, hearing impairment
- Speech and language issues, delayed milestones
- ADHD, social difficulties
- Epilepsy (approximately 20%), autism-like features
- Aortic dilatation, mitral valve prolapse
- Connective tissue dysplasia, scoliosis, congenital hip dislocation
- Premature ovarian failure (approximately 20%) in females with pre-mutation
- Fragile X-tremor/ataxia syndrome

### Prader-Willi Syndrome

Historically overdiagnosed based on clinical findings alone. Genetic confirmation is important.

Potential medical issues include:
- Type 2 diabetes, severe obesity, sleep apnoea
- Osteoporosis
- Dental abnormalities
- Strabismus, myopia
- High tolerance to pain
- Hyperphagia, impulse control difficulties, self-injury, skin picking
- Hypotonia, undescended testes, delayed puberty
- Decreased ability to vomit

Ongoing screening typically includes: regular height, weight, and BMI; HbA1c; annual thyroid-stimulating hormone.

5. **Ask about associated medical problems** such as epilepsy, heart problems, recurrent ear infections, or eye problems.

## Management

Your doctor will typically:

1. **Provide information to the family** about how making a diagnosis may be helpful, including:
   - Management options — for example, early intervention to help improve learning outcomes
   - Prognosis
   - Family recurrence risk
   - Access to appropriate supports and services

2. **If dysmorphic features are present, shared features exist in other family members, and an underlying genetic condition is suspected in an otherwise well child:** your doctor may consider referring for a non-acute genetic health assessment. The referral typically includes a 3-generation family tree and a facial photograph.

3. **If dysmorphic features are present alongside other concerning features** (e.g. developmental delay, examination or growth abnormalities): your doctor may consider referring for a non-acute paediatric medicine assessment. The referral typically includes a facial photograph.
