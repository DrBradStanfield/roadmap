# Vitamin D Supplementation

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: February 2025.*

This pathway covers who needs vitamin D supplementation, when blood testing is warranted, and how to dose colecalciferol for prevention versus confirmed deficiency.

> This is general information, not medical advice. Your doctor will decide whether you need testing or supplementation, and at what dose.

For vitamin D deficiency in children, see the separate Vitamin D Deficiency in Children pathway.

---

## Background

- Vitamin D maintains calcium and phosphate homeostasis, and supports bone health and muscle function.
- Low vitamin D is linked to osteoporosis and osteomalacia in adults, and rickets in children. Deficiency may result from poor nutrition, malabsorption, or lack of sun exposure.
- Treatment of **mild** vitamin D deficiency does not appear to have significant benefits for fracture risk or bone mineral density.
- Supplementation may reduce falls in older people in residential care, though evidence is not strong.
- There is little evidence that supplementation prevents falls and osteoporosis in the general population, or that it prevents cancer or cardiovascular disease.

---

## Sources of Vitamin D

### Sunlight

- UVB from sunlight is the main source of vitamin D for most people.
- A healthy person can synthesise all their vitamin D needs from sufficient sun exposure.
- Adults older than 65 convert vitamin D less efficiently in their skin.
- Adequate synthesis can occur despite recommended sunscreen use, but **not** through glass.
- Sun exposure needs balancing: too much raises skin cancer risk, too little lowers vitamin D.
- People with dark skin require 3 to 4 times more exposure to gain the same benefit.

### Diet

Adequate intake through diet alone is hard to achieve. The adequate intake for adults aged 51+ is 400–600 IU (10–15 micrograms) per day.

- Small amounts: fatty fish (salmon, herring, mackerel)
- Very small amounts: liver, eggs, and fortified foods such as margarine and some low-fat dairy products (milk, yoghurt)

## Who Is at Risk of Deficiency?

Your doctor will assess for risk factors, which include:

- Older, frail, or disabled people who rarely go outside
- People with darkly pigmented skin
- People who reduce sun exposure for cultural or medical reasons (e.g. veils and full body coverage clothing, previous skin cancer, photosensitivity)
- **Pregnant people** with any of: naturally dark skin; living south of Nelson-Marlborough (New Zealand) during winter or spring; or limited time outdoors or minimal sun exposure for religious, cultural, personal, or medical reasons
- People with malabsorption conditions (coeliac disease, IBD, previous bariatric surgery)
- People with obesity, or chronic liver or kidney disease
- People on enzyme-inducing medications (anticonvulsants, antiretrovirals, TB treatment, long-term glucocorticoids)

---

## Should You Get a Blood Test?

Your doctor will advise whether testing is appropriate. In general:

- **If you have risk factors for deficiency, testing is not recommended.** Supplementation is recommended instead. Testing is considerably more expensive than supplementation, and is not usually necessary before or after starting it.
- **Testing is only indicated in limited circumstances:**
  - No previous level over 50 nmol/L in the last 2 years, AND one of: unexplained low serum calcium, unexplained raised ALP (bone origin), or a specialist has specifically recommended it
  - Follow-up after a previous result below 50 nmol/L, endorsed by a specialist

Speak with your doctor about whether testing adds value in your situation.

---

## When Is Supplementation Recommended?

Your doctor will recommend supplementation if you have:

- **Any ongoing risk factors for deficiency:** long-term supplementation
- **Osteopenia or osteoporosis:** long-term supplementation
- **Starting or receiving bisphosphonate therapy:** long-term supplementation
- **Pregnancy with any risk factors:** throughout pregnancy

---

## How Is Vitamin D Supplemented?

Your doctor will choose the right formulation and dose for your situation.

### Pregnant or Breastfeeding (with risk factors)

**During pregnancy:**
- Preferred: colecalciferol oral drops (400 units/drop), 1 to 2 drops (400–800 units) once a day throughout pregnancy
- Daily dosing is recommended in pregnancy; larger monthly doses raise concerns and have insufficient safety evidence

**While breastfeeding:**
- Monthly supplementation is safe and is given for maternal benefit
- Breastfeeding supplementation does little to raise vitamin D in breast milk. Infants who are exclusively breastfed, or who receive less than 500 mL of formula a day, are recommended to start vitamin D supplementation before 4 weeks of age (see your doctor)

### Prevention (risk factors present, no confirmed deficiency)

Your doctor may prescribe one of the following:

- **Colecalciferol 1.25 mg (50,000 units) capsule, one per month**
  - Does not need to be taken with food
  - Contains soya oil (not suitable if allergic to soya or peanut) and gelatin, an animal product that may be unsuitable for some people. The currently funded capsule's gelatin is derived from beef, not pork, so may be acceptable to some.
- **Colecalciferol drops:** 1 drop (400 units) daily is usually sufficient
  - Occasionally, people with known malabsorption (e.g. bariatric surgery, gastrectomy) need higher doses
- **Alendronic acid + colecalciferol tablet:** contains 140 micrograms of vitamin D per tablet, which is sufficient when taken weekly
- **Multivitamin preparations:** 400–800 units (10–20 micrograms) per day is adequate for most people. Many multivitamins do not provide enough vitamin D unless excessive doses of other vitamins (e.g. vitamin A) are taken. Over-the-counter vitamin D on its own, without other vitamins and minerals, is available.

**Note:** Calcitriol is no longer recommended for general vitamin D supplementation, only in significant kidney impairment.

### Confirmed Vitamin D Deficiency

Your doctor will prescribe a higher dose. Options include:

- **Colecalciferol 1.25 mg (50,000 units) capsule monthly**
  - If your level was below 30 nmol/L, your doctor may give 2 capsules for the first dose, then 1 monthly, though there is no consensus on whether a loading dose is necessary
- **Colecalciferol drops at higher dose:**
  - 3 to 4 drops (1,200–1,600 units) daily, OR
  - The contents of one full bottle as a single dose once a month
  - Daily dosing is recommended if pregnant

Your doctor will also advise on ensuring adequate dietary calcium intake alongside vitamin D.

## A Note on Calcium

Vitamin D helps maintain calcium balance and bone health. Your doctor may provide information about adequate dietary calcium intake alongside any vitamin D supplementation. The resources below include a calcium calculator.
