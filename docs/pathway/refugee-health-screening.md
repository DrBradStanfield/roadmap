# Refugee Health Screening

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial health screening for former refugees and people seeking asylum after arriving in New Zealand. A comprehensive assessment is recommended to identify physical, psychological, and social needs and to plan catch-up healthcare.

---

## Background

People of refugee background are often affected by complex health inequalities arising from pre- and post-migration experiences. They may have:

- Physical and psychological conditions resulting from pre-migration trauma, torture, or prolonged detention
- Challenges navigating an unfamiliar health system, language barriers, housing and employment difficulties, and social isolation

Former refugees have often missed opportunities for preventive care and will need catch-up vaccinations and a sensitive, supportive approach to address physical and psychological problems before they become chronic.

All former refugees and people seeking asylum are eligible to access publicly funded health services in New Zealand.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will usually need an extended appointment — or several appointments — to complete screening, address identified needs, and allow for interpreting time.

### Preparation

- An interpreter should be arranged if needed. Family members and friends should not be used as interpreters. Language, dialect, and patient preferences for interpreter gender and ethnicity should be checked.
- Offshore health records (clinical notes, specialist reports, imaging, vaccination records) should be brought to the appointment.
- The limits of confidentiality should be explained: information is kept within the treating team unless there are safety concerns or the patient consents to sharing.

### History

Your doctor will take a comprehensive history, with particular attention to:

**General medical history**
- Current health concerns, including dental problems, vision and eye symptoms, hearing, medications (including herbal and traditional medications), and allergies
- Past medical history: chronic diseases, injuries, hospitalisations, and prior infectious diseases (tuberculosis, malaria, hepatitis, parasites)
- Social history: travel history, countries of transit, date of arrival, languages spoken, household composition, education, work history, religion, and supports in New Zealand

**Immunisation history**
- Vaccination records if available; many patients will not have documentation

**Lifestyle history**
- Diet, exercise, smoking, alcohol, and substance use (including betel nut, khat, sheesha)
- Risk of micronutrient deficiencies (vitamin D, B12, iron, thiamine — thiamine deficiency is more common in those from rice-based diets, e.g., Myanmar, Thailand, Nepal, Bhutan; pregnant and lactating women are at higher risk)

**Family violence**
- Routine screening for safety in the home is part of the assessment

**Mental health**
- Sleep (including nightmares), appetite, energy, mood, memory, concentration, and plans for the future
- Screening for depression, anxiety, and PTSD
- If trauma is disclosed, a trauma-informed care approach is used

### Specific population groups

**Women:** Obstetric and gynaecological history; consideration of female genital mutilation (FGM)

**Pregnant women:** Initial antenatal assessment; screening for vitamin D deficiency, thiamine deficiency, and iron deficiency

**Children:** Pregnancy and birth history, developmental milestones, dietary history, FGM (girls), psychosocial issues, and schooling

**Adolescents:** Full youth health assessment including HEeADSSS assessment; family structure and role changes with migration

**Older adults:** Health and frailty assessment; medication review; cognitive assessment using a culturally appropriate tool (e.g., RUDAS scale); sources of social support

**LGBTQIA+ people:** Awareness of challenges including discrimination, family rejection, and increased mental health distress

**People living with disabilities:** Activities of daily living assessment; cultural concerns around home help and residential care

**Asylum seekers:** Increased risk of mental distress, PTSD, and suicide; awareness of available services

### Physical examination

Your doctor will perform a focused examination that may include:

- Measurements and vital signs (fever may suggest serious infection such as malaria)
- Visual acuity and eye examination (trachoma, glaucoma, cataracts)
- Ear examination
- Dental check (decay, gum disease, oral cancer, vitamin B and C deficiency signs)
- Neck, thyroid, and lymph node examination (lymphadenopathy may indicate tuberculosis or HIV)
- Cardiovascular examination (signs of rheumatic heart disease)
- Respiratory examination
- Abdominal examination (hepatosplenomegaly — consider malaria, hepatitis B, schistosomiasis, tuberculosis, HIV)
- Skin, neurological, musculoskeletal, and genitourinary examinations as indicated

### Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

**Tuberculosis (TB) screening** (if not tested within the last 6 months):
- Under 2 years: tuberculin skin test (TST)
- 2–10 years: IGRA/Quantiferon Gold
- 11–15 years: IGRA/Quantiferon Gold and chest X-ray
- 16 years or older (not pregnant): chest X-ray
- If pregnant: chest X-ray deferred until after delivery

**Parasite screening:**
- No screening needed if the patient has received albendazole and ivermectin, is immunocompetent and asymptomatic, and is not from a schistosomiasis-endemic country
- If no presumptive treatment, or if immunocompromised or symptomatic: 3 stool samples (for ova, cysts, and parasites) plus schistosomiasis and Strongyloides serology
- If treated with albendazole and ivermectin and from a schistosomiasis-endemic country: schistosomiasis serology
- If albendazole given but not ivermectin: schistosomiasis and Strongyloides serology

**Blood tests** (if not previously tested or at-risk with normal results):
- Infection serology: HIV, syphilis, hepatitis B surface antigen, hepatitis C antibody
- Routine: FBC, ferritin, renal function, liver function, calcium, vitamin D
- Cardiovascular risk: HbA1c and lipids if criteria met
- Thalassaemia screening: if low MCV/MCH or family history of haemoglobinopathy
- B12/folate: if macrocytosis or history of significantly restricted diet

**H. pylori stool antigen testing:** if aged 18 years or older with dyspepsia or signs of peptic ulcer disease (proton pump inhibitors should be stopped 2 weeks before, and antibiotics or bismuth 4 weeks before)

**Screening programmes:** cardiovascular risk assessment, sexual health screening, cervical screening, breast cancer screening, bowel screening (as indicated by age and criteria)

## Management

Management is guided by the results of the assessment. Your doctor may consider:

### Infections

**Hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV, syphilis:** Managed according to the relevant clinical pathway for each condition.

**TB screening results:**
- Abnormal TST or IGRA in children: your doctor will seek paediatric advice
- Abnormal IGRA in adults with normal chest X-ray: your doctor will request an infectious diseases assessment
- Abnormal chest X-ray: your doctor will seek respiratory advice (adults) or paediatric advice (children)

**Intestinal parasites (stool positive):**

- *Threadworm/pinworm (Enterobius vermicularis):* mebendazole 100 mg single dose, repeated in 2 weeks (if aged 6 months or older and not pregnant). Infectious disease advice is sought for pregnant patients, those under 6 months, or if treatment fails.
- *Roundworm, whipworm, hookworm:* mebendazole 100 mg twice daily for 3 days (if aged 12 months or older and not pregnant). For ages 6–11 months, albendazole (dose by weight). Infectious disease advice for pregnant patients, those under 6 months, or treatment failure.
- *Tapeworm (Taenia solium):* infectious disease advice is always sought. Risk of cysticercosis is assessed (history of seizures, soft tissue cysts, household contacts).
- *Tapeworm (Taenia saginata) or dwarf tapeworm (Hymenolepis nana):* praziquantel (if aged 4 years or older and not pregnant); dose varies by species. Taken with food to reduce nausea. Infectious disease advice for pregnant patients, those under 4 years, or treatment failure.
- *Entamoeba histolytica:* your doctor will assess for extraintestinal amoebiasis (amoebic liver abscess — fever and right upper quadrant pain). Treatment with metronidazole followed by paromomycin for intestinal carriage. Infectious disease advice if pregnant, under 1 month, or suspected extraintestinal disease.
- *Giardia:* notifiable condition. Treatment with ornidazole or metronidazole for patient and symptomatic contacts. Patients can remain infectious for months; swimming in pools is avoided until 2 weeks after symptoms resolve. Temporary lactose avoidance may help persistent diarrhoea.
- Non-pathogenic organisms (including Entamoeba coli, Blastocystis hominis, Dientamoeba fragilis, and others) do not require treatment.

**Schistosomiasis serology positive or equivocal:**
- Urine dipstick for haematuria; if present, urine microscopy for ova
- Stool microscopy arranged
- Your doctor may consider praziquantel 20 mg/kg three doses 4–6 hours apart (total 60 mg/kg), taken with food, if aged 4 years or older and not pregnant. Infectious disease advice is sought for pregnant patients or those under 4 years, and before treatment if acute schistosomiasis is suspected.

**Strongyloides serology positive or equivocal:**
- Stool microscopy arranged
- If from a Loa loa-endemic country: blood film for Loa loa filariasis between 10 am–2 pm after 2 weeks in New Zealand. If positive, infectious disease advice is sought. If negative, ivermectin is considered.
- If not from a Loa loa-endemic country: ivermectin 0.2 mg/kg daily for 2 days (if weighing 15 kg or more and not pregnant), taken on an empty stomach. Infectious disease advice for pregnant patients or those under 15 kg.

**H. pylori:** Managed according to the dyspepsia/GORD pathway.

**Sexual health screening results:** Managed according to the relevant condition (e.g., chlamydia, gonorrhoea).

### Nutritional deficiencies

**Iron deficiency:** Your doctor will assess for causes and may treat with oral iron or IV iron infusion.

**B12 deficiency:** Your doctor may arrange H. pylori stool testing and manage according to the suspected B12 deficiency pathway.

**Vitamin D deficiency:** Your doctor may prescribe colecalciferol supplementation. Dose depends on risk factors and whether the patient is pregnant. Sun-smart behaviour advice is provided.

**Thiamine deficiency:** Managed based on risk factors and clinical presentation (testing is not always reliable). At-risk pregnant and lactating women may be prescribed vitamin B complex. Symptomatic patients may receive oral thiamine.

### Thalassaemia

- If positive thalassaemia screen: partner testing is arranged; family members of child-bearing potential may be tested. Patient information is provided on alpha thalassaemia, beta thalassaemia, haemoglobin E, or sickle cell disease.
- Your doctor will seek obstetric advice if the patient is pregnant with beta thalassaemia, or if both patient and partner have positive screens (risk of cardiomyopathy from iron overload and fetal growth restriction).
- If iron-deficient with a negative screen: thalassaemia screening is repeated once iron-replete, as iron deficiency can mask beta thalassaemia trait.

### Preventive care and support

- Immunisation catch-up to the New Zealand schedule is planned
- Children under 5 years are registered with a Well Child provider
- Patients of African descent aged 40+ and other patients of refugee background aged 50+ are advised to see an optometrist for glaucoma screening
- Dental enrolment is encouraged; funding is available for patients under 18 and some adults
- If pregnant, your doctor will support early entry into antenatal care
- If mental health or wellbeing concerns are identified, support through migrant and refugee services is arranged
- Healthy living advice (diet, exercise) is provided as appropriate
- Translated patient information is provided where available
