# Responding to Drug-Seeking Behaviour

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers how doctors assess and manage situations where a patient may be seeking controlled medications inappropriately.

---

## Background

General practice is often the first point of contact for a patient who is dependent on drugs, or who seeks to obtain controlled drugs for supply to others. Your doctor's role is to try and reach agreement on ways you can seek appropriate help.

Doctors have a legal and ethical responsibility to prescribe controlled drugs appropriately — guarding against misuse while ensuring people have medication available when they genuinely need it.

---

## Possible Presentations

Your doctor may be concerned if a combination of the following are present. Note that these features can have many explanations — your doctor will not automatically assume drug-seeking is occurring.

A patient may:

- Present near closing time without an appointment
- Exhibit assertive or demanding behaviour
- Have recently moved to the area, making verification with a previous doctor difficult
- Report symptoms that are inconsistent with examination findings
- Show signs of intoxication or withdrawal
- Use manipulating behaviour, such as comparing different doctors' opinions, making threats, or reporting lost prescriptions
- Be reluctant to provide their address or name of their regular doctor
- Have unusually detailed knowledge of specific medications and refuse all alternatives
- Use multiple doctors ("doctor shopping")
- Have a history showing escalating use of a medicine over time

---

## Drugs Commonly Sought

- **Opioids** — morphine, oxycodone, codeine, tramadol
- **Benzodiazepines and Z-drugs** — clonazepam, zopiclone
- **Methylphenidate**
- **Cyclizine**
- **Anticholinergics** — benztropine
- **Promethazine**
- **Quetiapine**
- **Gabapentinoids** — gabapentin, pregabalin

---

## Management

Your doctor will not prescribe controlled drugs to someone unknown to the practice if there is concern that drugs are being sought inappropriately.

### General Approach

Your doctor may:

- Listen empathetically and try to identify goals you can both agree on
- Offer help for the underlying problem, even if not the specific solution requested
- Refer you to a Community Alcohol and Drug Service (CADS) for specialist support
- Verify your prescription or medication history with a previous doctor, pharmacist, or hospital
- Ask for photo identification
- Check dispensing history records
- Check whether there is a restriction notice in place (a formal legal notice limiting your ability to receive certain controlled drugs)

### If Your Request Cannot Be Met

Your doctor may explain that they are unable to prescribe certain controlled drugs if they believe doing so would treat drug dependency without appropriate authorisation.

Under New Zealand law (Misuse of Drugs Act 1975), prescribing controlled drugs for addiction treatment is only permitted through specifically authorised services.

### Ways Your Doctor May Manage Uncertainty

If your doctor is uncertain about the legitimacy of a request, they may consider:

- Prescribing for a short period only (e.g. 2–3 days)
- Specifying a nominated pharmacy
- Requesting supervised daily dosing
- Prescribing a different medication that is appropriate for your reported symptoms
- Seeking a second opinion from a colleague

### Telehealth Consultations

If you are seeing a doctor via phone or video:

- They may ask you to come in person if they are not your usual doctor or cannot verify your identity
- A limited prescription may be issued and an in-person appointment requested for any further supply
- If you phone for a repeat prescription for a controlled drug and there are concerns, you will be asked to make an in-person appointment

---

## Safety

If anyone threatens or intimidates practice staff, the immediate priority is staff safety. The police (111) will be called if there is any threat or danger.
