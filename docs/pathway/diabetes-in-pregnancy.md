# Diabetes in Pregnancy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers diabetes in pregnancy — both pre-existing diabetes and gestational diabetes that develops during pregnancy. Always discuss your individual situation with your doctor or midwife, who will guide you through the appropriate next steps.

---

## Red Flags — Speak to Your Doctor Promptly

- Poor blood glucose control during pregnancy

---

## Background

Diabetes in pregnancy can be due to pre-existing (diagnosed or undiagnosed) diabetes, or gestational diabetes that develops during the pregnancy.

Diabetes in pregnancy is associated with an increased risk of miscarriage, congenital malformations, and complications for both mother and baby. The risk is proportionate to the level of blood glucose control, particularly around the time of conception. Good blood glucose control — before conception if possible, or as early as possible after diagnosis — is important to reduce these risks.

---

## Screening — Who Gets Tested and When

All pregnant women are screened for diabetes as part of routine antenatal booking blood tests. Up to one-third of women with diabetes in pregnancy have no risk factors, so universal screening is recommended.

### Risk Factors for Gestational Diabetes

Your doctor will assess whether you have risk factors, which include:

- Increasing maternal age (particularly age > 40 years)
- Family history of diabetes in a first-degree relative
- High-risk ethnic group (Indo-Asian, Māori, Pasifika, Middle Eastern)
- Elevated BMI (≥ 27 kg/m² in Indo-Asian; ≥ 30 kg/m² in other ethnicities)
- Previous large baby (birthweight > 4000 g)
- Previous gestational diabetes
- Previous impaired fasting glucose or impaired glucose tolerance
- Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
- Cardiovascular disease, persistent high blood pressure (> 135/80 mmHg), elevated triglycerides or cholesterol
- Advancing age: ≥ 35 years for Māori, Indo-Asian, and Pasifika; ≥ 45 years for other ethnicities
- Acanthosis nigricans (dark skin patches, usually at the neck or armpits)
- Long-term use of steroids or antipsychotic medication
- Physical inactivity or sedentary lifestyle

## Booking Blood Test — HbA1c Interpretation

Your doctor will arrange an HbA1c as part of your first antenatal blood tests. If you have two or more risk factors, this should be done as early as possible.

| HbA1c result | What it means | Next step |
|---|---|---|
| ≤ 40 mmol/mol | Unlikely to have pre-existing glucose intolerance or diabetes | Routine gestational diabetes screening at 24–28 weeks |
| 41–49 mmol/mol | May reflect pre-diabetes or pre-gestational diabetes | Arrange OGTT immediately |
| ≥ 50 mmol/mol | Consistent with pre-existing or gestational diabetes | Prompt obstetric assessment |

**Note:** From 1 July 2026, national HbA1c thresholds are changing. Diabetes will be defined as HbA1c ≥ 48 mmol/mol (down from 50), and prediabetes as 42–47 mmol/mol. Your doctor will apply the relevant thresholds based on when your test was taken.

---

## Screening at 24–28 Weeks

If you do not have gestational diabetes based on booking bloods, your midwife or doctor will offer testing between 24 and 28 weeks of pregnancy. This is important — testing, diagnosis, and referral should ideally occur by 28 weeks for optimal management.

### Glucose Challenge Test (GCT) — 50 g, non-fasting

Used if you have no risk factors and your booking HbA1c was < 41 mmol/mol.

| Result at 1 hour | Next step |
|---|---|
| ≥ 11.1 mmol/L | Prompt obstetric assessment |
| 7.7–11.0 mmol/L | Proceed to 75 g OGTT as soon as possible |
| ≤ 7.6 mmol/L | No further screening required (unless clinically indicated) |

### Oral Glucose Tolerance Test (OGTT) — 75 g, fasting

Used if you have risk factors, or booking HbA1c was 41–49 mmol/mol.

| Result | Next step |
|---|---|
| Fasting BGL ≥ 5.5 mmol/L, or 2-hour BGL ≥ 9.0 mmol/L | Prompt obstetric assessment |
| Fasting BGL ≤ 5.4 mmol/L and 2-hour BGL ≤ 8.9 mmol/L | No further screening required (unless clinically indicated) |

## Management During Pregnancy

Your doctor will refer you for obstetric assessment if you have gestational diabetes, pre-existing diabetes, an abnormal glucose test, or HbA1c ≥ 50 mmol/mol. Most of the specialist management is coordinated by the obstetric team. Your GP or midwife may assist with the following:

### Monitoring

Blood glucose levels (BGLs) are the best monitoring tool during pregnancy — HbA1c becomes less reliable due to increased red cell turnover. Your doctor will advise you to check your BGLs at fasting, before eating, and 1–2 hours after eating.

**Blood glucose targets during pregnancy** (lower than for non-pregnant people):

| Time of measurement | Target |
|---|---|
| Fasting | < 5.0 mmol/L |
| 1 hour after eating | < 7.4 mmol/L |
| 2 hours after eating | < 6.7 mmol/L |

### Medications

Medications are titrated based on fasting and postprandial glucose levels, not HbA1c. **Metformin and insulin are the only currently recommended medications for diabetes in pregnancy.** Other diabetes medications (e.g., SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 RAs, sulfonylureas) should not be used.

If you have pre-existing diabetes and your pregnancy is unplanned, your doctor will:
- Start folic acid 5 mg/day and iodine 150 micrograms/day
- Review all current medications and stop teratogenic ones (statins, ACE inhibitors, ARBs)
- Arrange any diabetes investigations not done in the past 3–6 months (HbA1c, urine albumin:creatinine ratio, eGFR, LFTs, lipids)
- Refer for retinal (eye) screening in the first trimester — this is always required with pre-existing diabetes

### Additional Considerations

- **Aspirin 100 mg daily** may be recommended for pre-eclampsia prevention in pre-existing diabetes if started before 20 weeks gestation (ideally 12–16 weeks), continued to 36 weeks. Not started if > 20 weeks.
- **Calcium supplementation** (e.g., 2.5 g calcium carbonate per day) may be considered for pre-eclampsia prevention if you have a low-calcium diet.
- **Vitamin D supplementation** may be offered if you have risk factors for deficiency (darkly pigmented skin; limited sun exposure for cultural, religious, or medical reasons; malabsorption conditions; obesity; chronic liver or renal disease; or enzyme-inducing medications). If recommended, colecalciferol oral drops (400–800 units/day) are preferred throughout pregnancy.

## Postpartum Management

After the birth, your doctor will:

1. **Review insulin.** Insulin requirements fall rapidly after delivery. Women with type 2 diabetes who were not on insulin before pregnancy can usually stop it. Blood glucose will be monitored closely to prevent hypoglycaemia.

2. **Review medications if breastfeeding.** Sulfonylureas, vildagliptin, SGLT2 inhibitors, and GLP-1 RAs are not recommended while breastfeeding. Metformin passes into breast milk in small amounts — the decision to restart it is made jointly with you and a specialist.

3. **Check HbA1c at 3 months**, then annually. The postpartum OGTT is no longer routinely used — HbA1c has replaced it.

4. **Lifestyle advice** — the goal is to return to 90% of your pre-pregnancy weight. This significantly reduces the risk of developing type 2 diabetes.

5. **Consider metformin** to support weight loss and reduce the risk of progressing to type 2 diabetes.

6. **Discuss future planning:**
   - Contraception, and the importance of pre-pregnancy planning if you have diabetes (including stopping teratogenic medications before conception)
   - Your ongoing risk of type 2 diabetes: the cumulative risk following gestational diabetes is estimated to be as high as 50% within 5 years postpartum. Lifestyle and pharmacological interventions (e.g., metformin) can reduce this risk.
