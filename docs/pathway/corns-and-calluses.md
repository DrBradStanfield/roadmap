# Corns and Calluses

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of corns and calluses, including when specialist referral is needed.

---

## Background

Both corns and calluses are areas of thickened skin that result from repetitive friction and pressure, often overlying a bony prominence. They most commonly occur on the feet, but can occur on the hands from the pressure of holding a pen or using a hammer or racquet.

**Corns** are pinpoint areas of friction and are painful. **Calluses** involve a wider area of friction and are usually painless. Corns can become soft and macerated between the toes, can be very painful, and are at risk of secondary infection (bacterial and fungal).

---

## Assessment

### Distinguishing corns and calluses from warts

Your doctor will examine the lesion and may pare it back to distinguish corns and calluses from warts:

| Feature | Corns and calluses | Warts |
|---|---|---|
| Location | More common on weight-bearing areas or over bony prominences | Anywhere |
| Pain with pressure | Direct (vertical) pressure | Lateral (squeeze) pressure |
| Bleeding on paring | No punctate bleeding | Multiple pinprick bleeding points |
| Skin lines | Pass through the lesion | Are interrupted by the wart |
| Appearance | May have a central core | May have overlying callus if on weight-bearing area |

### Identifying the cause

Your doctor will look for:

- Poorly-fitting footwear (most common cause)
- Abnormal foot mechanics, e.g. excessive foot pronation or supination
- Deformities of the feet, e.g. hammer toe, claw toe
- High levels of activity, e.g. long-distance running, repetitive use of tools

### Risk factors your doctor will consider

Certain conditions increase the risk of chronic ulceration or infection and require more careful management:

- Diabetes
- Peripheral vascular disease
- Peripheral neuropathy

---

## Management

### Conservative management

Your doctor may consider the following:

- **Calluses:** Soak the skin in warm water, then file or pumice the thickened skin, or use a scalpel to pare back the thickened area.
- **Corns:** Pare down the skin surface with a scalpel blade and remove the central plug. Referral to a podiatrist may be recommended for treatment.
- **Keratolytic cream:** Your doctor may apply or prescribe a keratolytic such as 10% urea cream or a salicylic acid preparation (15–27%). Note: salicylic acid should not be used in patients with diabetes or peripheral vascular disease due to risk of chemical burns.

### Patient self-care advice

Your doctor may advise:

- Maintain good foot hygiene to help prevent infection.
- Use a non-medicated self-adhesive felt pad or simple cotton wool padding between the toes to relieve pressure.
- Soften and file down the corn or callus between treatments, then apply an antiseptic such as Betadine.
- **Avoid over-the-counter corn removal products** (corn plasters or pads) — these can damage healthy skin and cause ulcers. This is particularly important for older adults and people with diabetes or peripheral vascular disease.
- Discuss well-fitting footwear; consider orthotics or toe separators to prevent recurrence. Corns may settle on their own if footwear is changed or the source of friction is removed.

## High-Risk Patients: Diabetes and Vascular Disease

Patients with diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, or a history of foot ulceration or amputation need more careful management.

### Active diabetic foot disease — urgent review needed

If a patient with diabetes has any of the following, your doctor will refer to appropriate specialist services promptly:

- Foot ulcer
- Severe or spreading infection (rapid deterioration of ulcer, deep abscess, cellulitis, systemically unwell)
- Postoperative open wound
- Critical limb ischaemia: rest or night pain, pale or mottled feet, dependent rubor, ischaemic ulceration, or gangrene

### Acute Charcot foot

Charcot foot is uncommon (affecting 1–2% of people with diabetes) and is often misdiagnosed. Your doctor will be alert to:

- A hot, swollen, initially painless foot — though it may become mildly or even intensely painful, making weight-bearing impossible
- Foot pulses usually present
- Associated peripheral neuropathy
- Swelling that may involve the calf or present as a swollen leg
- Commonly mistaken for infection, gout, sprain, or deep vein thrombosis (DVT)
- Risk of osteomyelitis, especially if any ulceration is present
- Delayed diagnosis can result in permanent disability; in later stages the foot becomes deformed with a "rocker bottom" appearance due to stress fractures and unstable joints

### High-risk diabetic foot features

Your doctor will assess whether a patient with diabetes is at high risk, which includes those in remission with any of:

- Previous amputation
- Previous ulcer
- Consolidated Charcot foot

Or those with 2 or more of the following risk factors:

- Loss of sensation
- Peripheral arterial disease
- Significant callus
- Significant deformity
- Pre-ulcerative lesion
- eGFR less than 15 (CKD stage 5)

Additional follow-up (e.g. every 4 weeks until well managed) and caution with home treatment devices (battery-operated foot files, foot baths) and beauty salon pedicures will be recommended for high-risk patients.

---

## When Specialist Referral May Be Needed

Your doctor may refer to a podiatrist or orthopaedic specialist if:

- Conservative management is unsuccessful
- Orthotics are required to manage corns
- There is a bony prominence or hammer toe that does not respond to conservative treatment — surgical options may be discussed
- The patient is at high risk of chronic ulceration or infection
