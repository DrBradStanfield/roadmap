# Colorectal Cancer Follow-up

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway applies to people with colorectal cancer who have completed curative treatment — including surgery, chemotherapy, and radiotherapy — and are currently disease-free based on imaging. Initial surveillance is usually organised by the specialist team; this covers what happens during and after that phase.

---

## About Follow-up After Colorectal Cancer

Your doctor will explain the purpose and schedule of follow-up:

- The aim is to detect any recurrence early, so that further treatment can be offered if suitable. Most colorectal cancer recurrences are asymptomatic until advanced.
- Follow-up targets potentially treatable recurrences, most commonly in the liver or lung.
- Follow-up is usually for 5 years. Around 80% of recurrences occur within 5 years, most within the first 2 years.
- The risk of recurrence depends on the stage of cancer and other tumour factors.
- Intensive follow-up has not been shown to improve overall survival, but does detect recurrences earlier.

---

## Surveillance Schedule

Your specialist team sets the follow-up plan. Your GP may be asked to help coordinate some of this. A typical schedule is:

**CEA blood test** (carcinoembryonic antigen):
- Every 3 to 6 months for 5 years
- May be extended to 7 years if you were diagnosed before age 55

**Colonoscopy:**
- First colonoscopy: within 6 to 12 months of surgery (if a complete colonoscopy wasn't done at diagnosis), otherwise at 1 year
- Then at 3 years if no abnormality found at 1 year
- Then every 5 years until age 75, unless you are unfit for the procedure or decline
- Beyond age 75 depends on your health and preferences
- More frequent if there is a high polyp burden or if recommended by the Familial Gastrointestinal Cancer Service

**CT scan:**
- Usually at 18 and 36 months after surgery
- May be more frequent if you are considered higher risk

## Symptoms to Watch For

Contact your doctor promptly if you notice any of the following — these can be signs of cancer coming back:

- Rectal bleeding
- Change in bowel habit (look for changes from your established post-treatment baseline — bowel function often changes after surgery or radiotherapy)
- Unexplained weight loss
- Unexplained abdominal pain or bloating
- Fatigue or symptoms of unexplained iron deficiency or anaemia
- Tenesmus (a persistent feeling of needing to empty the bowel)
- Mucus in faeces
- Night sweats

Your doctor will assess these symptoms and arrange investigation or specialist review as needed.

---

## Blood Tests

If not already arranged by your specialist, your GP may arrange:

- CEA (carcinoembryonic antigen) — routinely every 3 to 6 months
- If recurrence is suspected: CEA, full blood count (FBC), liver function tests (LFT)

If the CEA is significantly elevated (above 3.0 micrograms/L) or there are abnormal liver function tests or unexplained iron deficiency anaemia, your doctor will arrange urgent specialist review.

If CEA is 3.0 or below, or similar to previous results, your doctor will typically repeat it in 3 months.

## Managing Side Effects of Treatment

Cancer treatment can cause lasting side effects. Your doctor can help manage these.

### Low Anterior Resection Syndrome (LARS)

LARS is a group of bowel symptoms that can occur after rectal surgery where the anal sphincter is preserved. It can significantly affect quality of life.

Symptoms include:
- Unpredictable bowel function
- Altered stool consistency
- Increased stool frequency, repeated painful stools
- Difficulty emptying
- Urgency, incontinence, soiling

**Management (your doctor will guide this):**
- Rule out other causes and check for medication side-effects
- Lifestyle: regular exercise, reduce alcohol, caffeine, and fat; establish a daily routine; lose weight if obese
- Diet: trial of soluble fibre (oats, legumes, vegetables, fruit, psyllium husk) — though this can worsen symptoms in some people
- Loperamide: low doses (2 to 6 mg daily) are often helpful and safe long-term
- Strategies: avoid large meals before bed, long trips, or long meetings; try a glycerine suppository or Microlax enema to clear the rectum; take loperamide 30 minutes before your most symptomatic time
- Skin care: barrier cream (e.g. zinc oxide), warm sitz baths, avoid soap near the area; Proctosedyl or Ultraproct for inflamed skin tags; glyceryl trinitrate ointment or diltiazem paste for anal fissures — your doctor will prescribe
- If symptoms are debilitating and not improving, your doctor will refer you to a surgeon to discuss options such as trans-anal irrigation, sacral nerve stimulation, or stoma formation

### Frequent Bowel Motions

It can take up to 12 months for bowel motions to settle after surgery or completion of chemotherapy.

Helpful measures (your doctor or dietitian can advise):
- Avoid excessive straining
- Eat small, frequent meals; chew thoroughly; avoid caffeine, alcohol, and sorbitol
- Fibre supplements (psyllium husk, Fybogel, or Benefiber) may help
- Loperamide 2 mg, one to four times daily as needed

If you have had a colon J pouch after ileostomy reversal, frequent or loose motions are normal — try spending longer on the toilet to fully empty the pouch.

### Rectal Bleeding

- **Sinister bleeding** (blood coating or mixed with the stool, dark blood, or clots): see your doctor promptly — specialist review will be arranged.
- **Outlet bleeding** (bright red blood after the motion or on the paper): discuss with your doctor; this may relate to haemorrhoids or other benign causes.

### Rectal Discharge After Loop Stoma

A mucus or bowel motion discharge from the rectum is normal after a loop stoma. It varies in amount and should gradually settle. Sit on the toilet when you feel the urge but avoid straining. If you notice blood or pus in the discharge, contact your doctor — stoma nursing advice will be sought.

### Sexual Dysfunction

- **Erectile dysfunction** can occur after rectal resection, particularly if radiotherapy was also used. See your doctor — the Erectile Dysfunction pathway covers management options.
- **Vaginal dryness and tightening** can occur after radiotherapy. Lubricating gels or creams and vaginal dilators may help. Your doctor can refer you to a pelvic floor physiotherapist or gynaecologist.

### Side Effects of Chemotherapy

- Peripheral neuropathy (numbness/tingling in hands and feet)
- Fatigue

These may improve gradually over time. Discuss ongoing symptoms with your doctor.

### Side Effects of Pelvic Radiotherapy

- Osteoporosis or sacral insufficiency fracture
- Menopausal symptoms
- Sexuality and fertility issues
- Sacral plexopathy (nerve pain)

Your doctor can arrange appropriate assessment and management.

### Incisional Hernia

An incisional hernia is a bulge at the site of a surgical incision. Treatment options exist. A support belt or garment (such as a Corsinel appliance) may help. Your doctor can advise on ACC eligibility for treatment.

## Lowering the Risk of Recurrence

Your doctor may discuss lifestyle measures to reduce recurrence risk:

- **Diet:** A Mediterranean diet is a reasonable, safe approach. Increasing fibre and reducing sugar, high-glycaemic-load foods, and red meat are supported by evidence.
- **Exercise:** Regular physical activity reduces colorectal cancer risk.

---

## Psychosocial Support

Cancer affects more than physical health. Your doctor can ask about and help with:

- Anxiety or depression
- Body image and sexual wellbeing
- Social engagement, employment, financial stresses
- Physical capacity and daily living

Useful support organisations:
- Cancer Society of New Zealand
- Bowel Cancer New Zealand (Living Beyond Bowel Cancer)
- Work and Income, Green Prescription

---

## When to See Your Doctor

Go to your doctor or seek urgent review if:
- You develop any symptoms suggesting recurrence (see list above)
- Your CEA result is significantly elevated
- You notice sinister rectal bleeding (blood mixed with or coating stool)
- Your known chronic symptoms change character
- You are diagnosed with a new non-colorectal cancer
- You think you may have lost contact with your specialist follow-up

Your doctor will coordinate with the specialist team and arrange investigations or referral as appropriate. Always discuss any new or changing symptoms with your doctor rather than waiting for the next scheduled appointment.
