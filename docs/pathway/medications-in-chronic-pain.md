# Medications in Chronic Pain

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the medications used to manage chronic non-cancer pain, including which drug classes to consider for different pain types, dosing principles, and important safety information.

---

## Key Principles

Medications for chronic pain are used **alongside** non-drug approaches — physical and functional therapy, psychological management, and pain education. The goal is to improve physical and emotional functioning, not necessarily to eliminate pain entirely.

Your doctor will discuss realistic expectations before starting any medication:
- Pain may be reduced or altered, but may not be removed completely
- All medications have side-effects; these often decrease with time
- Interactions increase with the number of drugs taken
- Benefits take time — a full trial of a new medication may take up to 3 months
- The best available treatments average roughly 30% pain reduction in about half of treated patients

If a medication is not providing significant benefit, it should be stopped and another option explored.

## Matching Medication to Pain Type

### Nociceptive and Inflammatory Pain

This is pain from tissue damage or inflammation (e.g. arthritis, musculoskeletal).

**Paracetamol:**
- First-line option; lower gastrointestinal risk than NSAIDs
- Long-term dose: maintain at 3 g daily (reduced maximum — monitor liver function at intervals)
- Be aware of medication-overuse headache risk with regular use

**NSAIDs (e.g. ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, celecoxib):**
- Effective for inflammatory pain but carry risks to the gastrointestinal tract, kidneys, and heart
- Contraindicated in patients taking anticoagulants
- GI toxicity ranking (lowest to highest): ibuprofen → aspirin → diclofenac → naproxen → ketoprofen
- COX-2 inhibitors (e.g. celecoxib) have lower GI risk but this is lost when combined with aspirin
- For patients with cardiovascular disease or cardiovascular risk, naproxen or celecoxib are preferred if an NSAID is necessary
- Use at the lowest effective dose for the shortest necessary time
- Add a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) if at higher GI bleeding risk (e.g. age >65, long-term use, prior GI bleed, concurrent corticosteroids/SSRIs/antiplatelets)
- Check renal function within 10–14 days if co-prescribing with ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or diuretics
- Be aware of medication-overuse headache risk with regular use

**Topical capsaicin:**
- Works by depleting substance P in nerve endings (not directly anti-inflammatory)
- 0.025% for osteoarthritis; 0.075% for postherpetic neuralgia and diabetic neuropathy (funded with appropriate endorsement)
- Takes 1 week for initial response, 4–6 weeks for full effect
- Apply 3–4 times a day; do not massage in
- Initial burning sensation is normal and reduces with continued use
- Do not apply to broken skin; wash hands after application

### Neuropathic Pain

This is pain from nerve damage or dysfunction (e.g. postherpetic neuralgia, diabetic neuropathy, peripheral neuropathy).

**Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs):**

Your doctor may prescribe *nortriptyline* or *amitriptyline* at low doses for pain, not depression.

- Nortriptyline: start 10–25 mg (sometimes 5 mg) as a single early evening dose, 3–4 hours before bed. Typical effective analgesic dose up to 50 mg. Trial at least 3 weeks.
- Amitriptyline is more sedating — may be preferred if poor sleep is also a problem
- There is significant individual variation in response due to differences in the CYP2D6 enzyme
- Blood levels can be measured to confirm therapeutic range (best at steady state: 10+ days on stable dose, 15 hours after last dose)

**Gabapentinoids (gabapentin and pregabalin):**

These carry special risks and require careful prescribing.

*Important safety note:* Gabapentinoids enhance the effects of opioids, alcohol, and recreational drugs — increasing the risk of accidental overdose. Special precautions apply if you are taking opioids or have a history of substance or alcohol use disorder. Your doctor may arrange more frequent dispensing (weekly rather than 3-monthly) in these situations.

*Gabapentin:*
- Start 300 mg at night (100 mg if elderly or frail), increasing every 3–7 days
- Maximum dose 1800–3600 mg per day in divided doses (3–4 times daily)
- Dose reduction required in renal impairment or haemodialysis
- Does not follow linear pharmacokinetics — higher doses don't always mean higher blood levels
- Available without Special Authority

*Pregabalin:*
- Start 75 mg at night (25 mg if elderly or frail), increasing every 3–7 days
- Maximum dose 300 mg twice daily
- More easily titrated than gabapentin; may have greater misuse potential at high doses
- Funded without Special Authority restriction
- **Do not stop abruptly** — withdrawal can cause anxiety, nausea, irritability, sweating, tremors, and return of pain. Gradual tapering is required.

Note: The combination of gabapentinoids with tricyclic antidepressants is no longer recommended — it does not provide additional pain relief but significantly increases side-effects.

**Topical lidocaine:**
- May help painful neuropathy; off-label and unfunded
- Prescribe as lidocaine 2% gel applied to unbroken skin, up to 4 times a day
- Dose reduction needed in renal or hepatic impairment

**Topical capsaicin** (same guidance as under nociceptive pain above)

### Nociplastic Pain

This is pain from altered pain processing without clear tissue damage — examples include fibromyalgia, irritable bowel syndrome, and non-specific low back pain. It is less likely to respond to pain medications.

**Tricyclic antidepressants** (same guidance as under neuropathic pain above)

**Noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors (NRIs) — off-label:**

*Venlafaxine* (SNRI):
- Start 37.5 mg in the morning
- Analgesic effect requires at least 150 mg daily to achieve noradrenergic effect (individual variation in metabolism)
- Take in the morning due to alerting effect; about one quarter of patients respond better to twice-daily dosing

*Alternative NRIs (all off-label):*
- Mirtazapine — particularly useful if poor sleep or decreased appetite are also present
- Bupropion

**Important:** Risk of serotonin syndrome when combining serotonergic drugs (e.g. any of the above with TCAs, tramadol, or opioids). Your doctor will assess this risk carefully.

**Clonidine (off-label):**
- Used as a transdermal patch: start 100 microgram/24 hours, replace weekly, increase in 100 microgram increments at minimum 1-week intervals; maximum 300 microgram/24 hours weekly
- Generally well tolerated; side-effects include GI disturbance, headache, postural hypotension, dizziness
- Particularly useful for patients withdrawing from opioids

## Medications Currently Under Evaluation (Non-Funded)

These options may be considered if pain remains inadequately controlled despite standard treatments, and the patient is able to fund them:

**Medicinal cannabis:**
- The Faculty of Pain Medicine does not endorse its use in chronic non-cancer pain
- Evidence is limited, with a significantly worse number-needed-to-treat (NNT) compared to other pain medications

**Low-dose naltrexone:**
- Standard naltrexone (50 mg) is used for alcohol and opioid dependence; at very low doses (3–4.5 mg) it may have anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects
- Some small studies suggest possible benefit in fibromyalgia and multiple sclerosis; no large clinical trials confirm efficacy in chronic pain; not in any chronic pain guideline
- If trialled: start 0.5–1 mg daily, increase by 0.5–1 mg every two weeks; typical dose 3–5 mg; maximum 5 mg
- Must be obtained from a compounding pharmacy (standard tablets are 50 mg)
- Cannot be used concurrently with opioids
- Uncommon side-effects: transient nausea/GI upset, insomnia, vivid dreams

## Opioids in Chronic Non-Cancer Pain

**Starting opioid analgesia for chronic non-cancer pain is not recommended.**

Risks include:
- Opioid-induced hyperalgesia (worsened pain)
- Poorer functional outcomes
- Increased risk of death, especially in combination with gabapentinoids
- Tolerance, dependence, endocrine effects (e.g. hypogonadism), immunosuppression

**Codeine and pethidine have no role in pain management** — more harm than benefit.

**Tramadol:**
- Not a traditional opioid but causes opioid-like dependence and withdrawal
- Classified as a Class C opioid from October 2023 (NZ)
- Modified release: 100–200 mg twice daily; maximum 400 mg/day
- Do not use if there is a risk of seizures
- Side-effects: nausea, vomiting, drowsiness, dizziness; can cause confusion and seizures on withdrawal
- Risk of serotonin toxicity with SSRIs, TCAs, or venlafaxine
- Risks of misuse, abuse, and dependence

**For patients already on long-term opioids** (e.g. prescribed by a previous doctor), your doctor may consider a managed withdrawal programme. Continuing may be appropriate only if the patient has minimal pain and impairment, remains employed, and is on an oral morphine equivalent of ≤40 mg/day. In this case:
- The opioid risk tool is used to assess abuse risk before prescribing
- The lowest effective dose is used
- Side-effects are discussed openly: constipation (always use prophylactic laxatives), nausea/vomiting, respiratory impairment, mood disturbances, dependence, addiction, neuroendocrine effects, opioid-induced hyperalgesia, immunosuppression, falls risk

## When to Refer

Your doctor will consider referring to a **specialist pain clinic** if:
- Chronic pain is inadequately controlled despite recommended non-pharmacological and medication trials
