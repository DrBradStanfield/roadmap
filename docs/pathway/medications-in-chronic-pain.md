# Medications in Chronic Pain

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: May 2024.*

This pathway covers the medications used to manage chronic non-cancer pain, including which drug classes to consider for different pain types, dosing principles, and safety information.

> This is general information from a pathway written for doctors. Do not start, stop, or change a pain medicine without talking to your doctor.

---

## Key Principles

Medications for chronic pain are used **alongside** non-drug approaches: physical and functional therapy, psychological management, and pain education. The goal is to improve physical and emotional functioning.

Your doctor will discuss realistic expectations before starting any medication:
- Pain may be reduced or altered, but may not be removed completely
- All medications have side-effects that affect most patients, but side-effects may decrease with time
- Interactions increase with the number of drugs taken
- Benefits take time to begin, and the dose needs to be increased slowly under supervision. A full trial of a new medication may take up to 3 months.
- Current treatments give modest improvements at best. The best available evidence averages roughly 30% pain reduction in about half of treated patients.

Your doctor may check your pain, activity level, and side-effects before and after a trial. A [pain diary](https://aci.health.nsw.gov.au/__data/assets/pdf_file/0011/592085/Pain-diary-HM-tool.pdf) can help with this.

If a medication is not providing significant benefit, it should be stopped and another option explored.

## Matching Medication to Pain Type

### Nociceptive and Inflammatory Pain

**Paracetamol:**
- First-line option. It is not as effective as NSAIDs, but it has a lower risk of side-effects, particularly gastrointestinal ones
- Long term, the dose is kept at 3 g daily (a reduced maximum) and other medications are added. Your doctor may consider checking liver function at intervals.
- Be aware of medication-overuse headache risk with regular use

**NSAIDs (e.g. ibuprofen, naproxen, diclofenac, celecoxib):**
- Effective for inflammatory pain but carry risks to the gastrointestinal tract, kidneys, and heart
- Contraindicated in patients taking anticoagulants
- GI toxicity ranking for non-selective NSAIDs (lowest to highest): ibuprofen → aspirin → diclofenac → naproxen → ketoprofen. Ibuprofen loses its GI advantage at full doses.
- COX-2 selective NSAIDs still cause GI toxicity, but the risk is lower than for non-selective NSAIDs. This protective effect is lost when combined with aspirin.
- Low-dose aspirin affects the stomach lining, even when enteric-coated. The GI side-effects of NSAIDs and aspirin add together.
- NSAIDs increase the risk of cardiovascular events and can worsen existing cardiovascular disease. If there is no alternative and an NSAID must be used by someone with cardiovascular disease or at risk of it, naproxen or celecoxib are preferred
- Use at the lowest effective dose for the shortest necessary time
- Your doctor may add a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) if you are at higher risk of GI bleeding, e.g. NSAID use for longer than 1 week, a higher NSAID dose, age older than 65 years, past GI bleeding from NSAIDs or peptic ulcer disease, or also taking corticosteroids, bisphosphonates, SSRIs, SNRIs, or antiplatelets
- Check renal function within 10–14 days if co-prescribing with ACE inhibitors, ARBs, or diuretics
- The ongoing need is reviewed 2 to 4 weeks after starting
- Be aware of medication-overuse headache risk with regular use

**Topical capsaicin:**
- Works by depleting substance P in nerve endings (not directly anti-inflammatory)
- 0.025% strength is recommended for osteoarthritis. 0.075% is used for postherpetic neuralgia and diabetic neuropathy.
- Takes 1 week for initial response, 4–6 weeks for full effect
- Apply regularly 3–4 times a day; do not massage in
- Initial burning sensation is normal and reduces with continued use
- Do not apply to broken skin or unintended areas. Wash hands after application, unless the hands are being treated.

### Neuropathic Pain

**Tricyclic antidepressants (TCAs):**

Your doctor may consider *nortriptyline* or *amitriptyline*.

- Nortriptyline: start 10–25 mg (sometimes 5 mg) as a single early evening dose, 3–4 hours before bed. Typical effective analgesic dose up to 50 mg. Trial at least 3 weeks.
- Amitriptyline is more sedating and may be more useful where sleep is a problem
- There is significant individual variation in response due to differences in the CYP2D6 enzyme
- Blood levels can be measured to confirm therapeutic range (best at steady state: 10+ days on stable dose, 15 hours after last dose)

**Gabapentinoids (gabapentin and pregabalin):**

These carry special risks and require careful prescribing.

*Important safety note:* Gabapentinoids enhance the effects of opioids, alcohol, and recreational drugs. This raises the risk of side-effects and accidental overdose. They also carry a risk of dependence and misuse. Special precautions apply if you are taking opioids or have a history of substance or alcohol use disorder. Your doctor may consider:
- naloxone (an opioid antagonist) if you are at risk of opioid overdose
- monthly dispensing instead of every 3 months in general
- weekly dispensing if you are on opioid substitution treatment, benzodiazepines, or opioids, or have a history of substance or alcohol use disorder

Gabapentinoids are started at a low dose and increased if needed. They are stopped if there is no improvement after a trial of one month.

*Gabapentin:*
- Start 300 mg at night (100 mg at night if elderly or if side-effects are a concern), increasing every 3–7 days
- Maximum dose 1800–3600 mg per day, usually in divided doses (3–4 times daily)
- Lower doses are used in renal failure. On haemodialysis, dosing follows the renal specialist's advice (may be every other day or after dialysis).
- Does not follow linear pharmacokinetics; higher doses don't always mean higher blood levels

*Pregabalin:*
- Funded
- Works the same way as gabapentin but is more easily titrated
- Start 75 mg at night (25 mg if elderly or frail), increasing if needed every 3–7 days to the lowest effective dose
- Maximum dose 300 mg twice daily
- May have a greater potential for misuse than gabapentin, because more of it is absorbed at high doses
- **Withdrawal can be severe.** Stopping suddenly can cause anxiety, nausea, irritability, sweating, tremors, and return of pain. Your doctor may consider a gradual taper if planning to stop.

Note: The combination of gabapentinoids with tricyclic antidepressants is no longer recommended. It generally does not provide more pain relief but has significantly more side-effects.

**Topical lidocaine:**
- May help painful neuropathy; off-label and unfunded
- Your doctor may prescribe lidocaine 2% gel, applied to the painful area of unbroken skin up to 4 times a day
- Dose reduction needed in renal or hepatic impairment

**Topical capsaicin** (same guidance as under nociceptive pain above)

### Nociplastic Pain

Nociplastic pain comes from altered pain processing (central sensitisation) with no clear evidence of tissue damage or disease. Normal-looking tissue becomes hypersensitive. Examples include fibromyalgia, irritable bowel syndrome, and non-specific low back pain. It is less likely to respond to pain medications.

**Tricyclic antidepressants** (same guidance as under neuropathic pain above)

**Noradrenaline reuptake inhibitors (NRIs), off-label:**

*Venlafaxine* (SNRI):
- Start 37.5 mg in the morning
- Analgesic effect requires at least 150 mg daily to achieve noradrenergic effect (individual variation in metabolism)
- Take in the morning due to alerting effect; about one quarter of patients respond better to twice-daily dosing

*Alternative NRIs (all off-label):*
- Mirtazapine: useful if sleep problems or decreased appetite are also present
- Bupropion

**Important:** Risk of serotonin syndrome when combining serotonergic drugs (e.g. any of the above with TCAs, tramadol, or opioids). Your doctor will assess this risk carefully.

**Clonidine (off-label):**
- Used as a transdermal patch: start 100 microgram/24 hours, replace weekly, increase in 100 microgram increments at minimum 1-week intervals; maximum 300 microgram/24 hours weekly
- Generally well tolerated; side-effects include GI disturbance, headache, postural hypotension, dizziness
- Useful for patients withdrawing from opioids

## Medications Currently Under Evaluation (Non-Funded)

These options may be considered if pain remains inadequately controlled despite standard treatments, and the patient is able to fund them:

**Medicinal cannabis:**
- The Faculty of Pain Medicine does not endorse its use in chronic non-cancer pain
- Evidence is limited, with a significantly worse number-needed-to-treat (NNT) compared to other pain medications

**Low-dose naltrexone:**
- Standard naltrexone (50 mg) is used for alcohol and opioid dependence; at very low doses (3–4.5 mg) it may have anti-inflammatory and immunomodulatory effects
- Some studies suggest possible benefit in chronic pain such as fibromyalgia and multiple sclerosis. No large clinical trials have evaluated it for chronic pain, and it is not in any chronic pain guideline.
- This is an off-label use
- If trialled: start 0.5–1 mg daily, increase by 0.5–1 mg every two weeks; typical dose 3–5 mg; maximum 5 mg
- Must be obtained from a compounding pharmacy (standard tablets are 50 mg)
- Cannot be used concurrently with opioids
- Uncommon side-effects: transient GI upset (nausea, constipation, or loose motions), insomnia, vivid dreams

## Opioids in Chronic Non-Cancer Pain

**Starting opioid analgesia for chronic non-cancer pain is not recommended.**

Risks include:
- Opioid-induced hyperalgesia (worsened pain)
- Poorer functional outcomes
- Increased risk of death, especially in combination with gabapentinoids
- Tolerance, dependence, endocrine effects (e.g. hypogonadism), immunosuppression

Immediate-release opioids are appropriate only as a time-limited trial for acute nociceptive and inflammatory pain.

**Codeine and pethidine have no role in pain management** because they cause more harm than benefit.

**Tramadol:**
- Not a traditional opioid but causes opioid-like dependence and withdrawal
- Classified as a Class C opioid from October 2023 (NZ)
- Modified release: 100–200 mg twice daily; maximum 400 mg/day
- Do not use if there is a risk of seizures
- Side-effects: nausea, vomiting, drowsiness, dizziness; can cause confusion and seizures on withdrawal
- Risk of serotonin toxicity with SSRIs, TCAs, or venlafaxine
- Risks of misuse, abuse, and dependence

**For patients already on long-term opioids** (e.g. prescribed by a previous doctor), your doctor may consider a managed withdrawal programme. Chronic pain very rarely responds to opioids. Continuing may be appropriate only if the patient has minimal pain and impairment, remains employed, and is on an oral morphine equivalent of ≤40 mg/day. In this case:
- The opioid risk tool is used to assess abuse risk before prescribing
- The lowest effective dose is used
- Side-effects are discussed openly: constipation (always use prophylactic laxatives), nausea/vomiting, respiratory impairment, mood disturbances, dependence, addiction, neuroendocrine effects (e.g. hypogonadism, hyperprolactinaemia, adrenal insufficiency), opioid-induced hyperalgesia
- Other risks weighed: substance use or mental health conditions, COPD, obstructive sleep apnoea, liver or kidney impairment, frailty and falls, immunosuppression, and harm to others through diversion

## When to Refer

Your doctor will consider referring to a **specialist pain clinic** if:
- Chronic pain is inadequately controlled despite recommended non-pharmacological and medication trials
