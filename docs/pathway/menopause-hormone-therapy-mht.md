# Menopause Hormone Therapy (MHT)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of women considering or already using menopause hormone therapy (MHT) — previously known as hormone replacement therapy (HRT).

---

## Background

MHT is the most effective treatment for troublesome vasomotor symptoms (hot flushes, night sweats) that affect quality of life and have not responded to lifestyle changes. It also preserves bone density and reduces fracture risk.

**When MHT is generally safe:**
- Women younger than 60 years with no contraindications who are within 10 years of their final menstrual period (FMP) have a favourable benefit-risk ratio
- There is no increased mortality for women who start MHT younger than 60 years or within 10 years of their FMP

**Premature ovarian insufficiency (POI) and early menopause:**
- MHT is recommended for the majority of women with POI (younger than 40 years) or early menopause (younger than 45 years) to manage symptoms and preserve bone density, typically until age 50 or longer if needed

**Starting MHT after age 60:**
- The benefit-risk ratio is less favourable when initiating MHT after 60, due to greater baseline risk of coronary heart disease, stroke, venous thromboembolism (VTE), and dementia
- However, if MHT was started at a younger age, it may be continued beyond 60 after individual risk-benefit assessment
- Risk mitigation includes using lower doses and a non-oral (transdermal) route

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess whether MHT is appropriate for you by:

1. **Determining whether MHT is indicated:**
   - Moderate-to-severe perimenopause or menopause symptoms in women younger than 60 years
   - POI or early menopause (to manage symptoms and protect bone health)

2. **Reviewing contraindications** — MHT cannot be used if you have:
   - Pregnancy
   - Undiagnosed abnormal vaginal bleeding
   - Suspected or active breast, endometrial, or other hormone-dependent cancer
   - Past history of breast cancer
   - Active thromboembolic disorder (e.g. blood clot)
   - Acute-phase (first 6 weeks) myocardial infarction or stroke
   - Uncontrolled or untreated cardiovascular disease
   - Severe active liver disease
   - Porphyria

   *Relative contraindication:* Past history of gynaecological cancer (e.g. ovarian or endometrial) — specialist advice required.

   *Conditions where transdermal therapy may still be used with care:*
   - Previous ischaemic heart disease or cerebrovascular disease
   - Previous venous thromboembolism
   - Diabetes mellitus
   - Hepatobiliary disease
   - Migraine with aura
   - Uterine fibroids (low-dose transdermal estrogen is unlikely to accelerate growth)
   - Hypertriglyceridaemia

3. **Midlife health checks** your doctor will complete or review:
   - Cervical screening and mammogram (up to date)
   - Blood pressure, weight, lipid profile, and HbA1c (cardiovascular risk)
   - Bone density assessment if relevant

4. **Symptom assessment:** A symptom score sheet helps establish a baseline and track response to treatment.

5. **History to guide regimen choice:**
   - Whether periods have stopped, and how long ago (menopause = 12 months since last period)
   - Whether you still need contraception (MHT is **not** contraceptive)
   - Whether your uterus is intact or has been removed (hysterectomy)

## Contraception Alongside MHT

MHT does not prevent pregnancy. Women at risk of unplanned pregnancy during the menopause transition need contraception:

- If younger than 50 years: contraception for **2 years** after final menstrual period
- If 50 years or older: contraception for **1 year** after final menstrual period
- All women can stop contraception at age 55 years regardless of bleeding pattern

Your doctor may consider:
- Combined oral contraceptive pill (COC) — for women under 50 only; **not** used together with MHT
- Mirena (levonorgestrel intrauterine system) — also serves as the progestogen component of MHT for endometrial protection; provides contraception for up to 8 years if fitted at age 45 or older (replace at age 55)
- Copper IUD — if fitted aged 40 or older with 300 mm² or more of copper, can be retained until 1–2 years after last period
- Progestogen-only pill or implant — can be continued to age 50 and beyond
- Barrier methods

## Benefits and Risks — What the Evidence Shows

Your doctor will discuss the following before starting MHT:

### Breast Cancer
- Combined oral MHT for more than 5 years may be associated with a small increased risk — approximately 15 extra cases per 10,000 women over the period of use
- Risk appears related to the type of progestogen; micronised progesterone (Utrogestan) appears safer than synthetic progestogens
- Risk decreases when MHT is stopped
- Estrogen-only MHT does not appear to increase breast cancer risk in the first 7 years, and some studies suggest a reduced risk
- The chance of dying from breast cancer is not increased in women using MHT

### Coronary Heart Disease
- MHT does not increase coronary heart disease risk in women younger than 60 or within 10 years of menopause; some data suggests a reduction in risk
- Risk increases as age and time since menopause increase

### Stroke
- MHT does not increase stroke risk for women younger than 60 or within 10 years of menopause
- There may be an increased risk if oral MHT is started after age 60 or more than 10 years from menopause
- Low-dose transdermal estrogen may not increase stroke risk

### Venous Thromboembolism (VTE / Blood Clots)
- Oral MHT increases VTE risk, but the absolute risk is rare in women aged 60 or younger (approximately 2–3 per 1,000 women-years compared to 1 per 1,000 in non-users)
- There is no evidence of increased VTE risk with transdermal estrogen

### Fractures
- MHT reduces the risk of osteoporotic fractures: approximately 44–53 fewer fractures per 10,000 women-years compared to placebo

### Weight
- MHT does not cause significant weight gain. Average midlife weight gain is 0.7 kg/year regardless of MHT use
- MHT may reduce the increase in waist circumference in the first 3 years of use

### Mood and Depression
- There is evidence that estrogen therapy has comparable antidepressant effects to antidepressants in depressed perimenopausal women
- It is less effective as a treatment for postmenopausal depression
- Screening for depression is important; psychological therapy or antidepressants may be needed alongside or instead of MHT

## Management

### Choosing a Regimen — Key Principle: Uterus Intact vs Hysterectomy

- **Uterus intact:** Estrogen must always be combined with progestogen (to protect the endometrium)
  - **Cyclical** combined regimen if still having periods, or within 12 months of FMP, or younger than 50
  - **Continuous** combined regimen if more than 12 months from FMP and older than 50
- **Total hysterectomy:** Estrogen alone is suitable
- **Subtotal hysterectomy (cervix remaining):** Endometrial tissue may still be present; progestogen may still be required — your doctor will determine this

---

### Estrogen Options

Your doctor may consider the following estrogen preparations:

**Transdermal estrogen (patch or gel) — generally preferred:**
- Advantages: avoids liver metabolism, no effect on VTE risk, preferred for migraines, lower total dose
- Disadvantages: skin irritation, patches may fall off with sweating

*Estradiol transdermal patch:*
- Low dose: 25 micrograms (twice weekly)
- Medium dose: 50 micrograms
- High dose: 75–100 micrograms

*Estrogel pump (estradiol hemihydrate gel):*
- Low dose: 1 actuation (0.75 mg)
- Medium dose: 2 actuations (1.5 mg)
- High dose: 3–4 actuations (2.25–3 mg)
- Apply once daily to clean, dry skin on arms or inner thigh; avoid breasts and face; let dry 5 minutes; avoid skin contact with others for at least 1 hour

**Oral estrogen:**
- Progynova (oral estradiol valerate): starting dose 1–2 mg daily

Your doctor will typically start at a low dose and titrate every 3 months to the lowest dose that controls your symptoms. Side effects to watch for include nausea, bloating, breast tenderness, and headache.

---

### Progestogen Options (for women with an intact uterus)

Your doctor may consider:

**Natural micronised progesterone — Utrogestan:**
- Cyclical use: 200 mg daily for 12 days per month
- Continuous use: 100 mg daily
- Taken at night (sedative effect); can be inserted vaginally if oral is not tolerated
- Research suggests it has a more neutral effect on cardiovascular and breast cancer risk than synthetic progestogens

**Mirena (levonorgestrel intrauterine system):**
- Provides endometrial protection for up to 5 years from insertion
- Also provides contraception
- Avoids systemic progestogen side effects

**Norethisterone (Primolut N):**
- Cyclical: 2.5 mg daily for 10–14 days per month
- Continuous: 1.25 mg daily

**Medroxyprogesterone acetate (Provera):**
- Cyclical: 10 mg daily for 10–14 days per month
- Continuous: 2.5–5 mg daily

Note: Low-dose progestogen-only pills (e.g. Microlut, Noriday) are **not** recommended — insufficient evidence for endometrial protection. Progesterone cream is **not** recommended for systemic MHT use.

---

### Regimens by Menopausal Status

**Premature ovarian insufficiency or early menopause (under 45 years):**
- Your doctor may consider medium to high dose estrogen, with cyclical or continuous progestogen
- Combined oral contraceptives (COC) are an alternative for women under 50 who also need contraception (COC and MHT should not be used together)

**Perimenopause (still having periods, or within 12 months of FMP):**
- Cyclical combined regimen (estrogen daily + progestogen for 10–14 days per month)
- Or continuous estrogen + Mirena
- Or COC for women under 50 (not combined with MHT)
- Irregular bleeding is common in the first 3–6 months

**Postmenopause — uterus intact (12+ months after FMP):**
- Continuous combined regimen (estrogen + progestogen daily)
- Alternative: Tibolone (see below)
- Women on continuous combined MHT should not bleed after the first 6 months

**Postmenopause — hysterectomy (12+ months after FMP):**
- Estrogen only (oral or transdermal)

---

### Tibolone

Your doctor may consider tibolone (Livial) as an alternative for postmenopausal women, particularly where improvement in libido is desired:
- Has estrogenic, progestogenic, and androgenic effects
- Alleviates vasomotor symptoms, may help mood, treats urogenital atrophy, preserves bone density
- May help sexual dysfunction
- Side effects: headache, dizziness, nausea, abdominal pain
- Contraindicated with history of breast cancer, VTE, active cardiovascular disease, or stroke
- Not funded (approximately $110/month as of 2024)

---

### Compounded Bioidentical Hormones

Your doctor will advise caution with custom compounded bioidenticals:
- Not regulated — concerns over consistency, purity, safety, and efficacy
- No adequate safety data on progesterone creams and breast cancer risk
- No evidence of advantage over conventional MHT

## Monitoring and Follow-Up

Your doctor will review you at **12 weeks** after starting MHT to assess symptom relief and adjust the regimen if needed, then **every 12 months**.

At each annual review, your doctor will:
- Assess dose adequacy and consider reducing to the lowest effective dose
- Check for new contraindications
- Check blood pressure, HbA1c, and lipid profile
- Confirm breast and cervical screening is up to date
- For women over 60: perform a detailed risk-benefit assessment before continuing

**Managing side effects:**

*Breakthrough or irregular bleeding:*
- Common in the first 3–6 months; further investigation is needed if it persists beyond 6 months
- Pelvic ultrasound may be arranged; endometrial thickness should be 8 mm or less for perimenopausal women
- Your doctor may adjust progestogen dose or type

*Progesterone symptoms (bloating, irritability, depression, headaches, breast tenderness):*
- Your doctor may change the route or type of progestogen, reduce the dose, or consider switching to tibolone

*Nausea:*
- Your doctor may reduce the estrogen dose or switch to transdermal therapy

---

## Stopping MHT

Your doctor will discuss the timing of stopping MHT with you based on your individual risks and benefits. A trial of withdrawal may be considered for women aged 60 or older, or women who had POI/early menopause and are now over 50.

Some women choose to continue MHT after reviewing risks and benefits. The lowest effective dose and a non-oral route reduce risk.

If withdrawing gradually, your doctor may:
- Reduce the dose every 3–6 months (some women need a slower taper)
- Reduce to the lowest dose first, then cut further (e.g. halving a patch)
- Stop progestogen once estrogen is stopped

Expect some return of vasomotor symptoms during withdrawal.

---

## When Your Doctor May Refer

- Past history of gynaecological cancer (e.g. ovarian or endometrial) — specialist advice to develop a shared care plan
- History of hysterectomy for severe endometriosis — gynaecology advice on continuing progestogen
- Persistent irregular bleeding at 6 months with endometrial thickness >8 mm on ultrasound — non-acute gynaecology assessment
- Severe symptoms not responding to MHT — endocrinology or gynaecology advice, or menopause specialist assessment
