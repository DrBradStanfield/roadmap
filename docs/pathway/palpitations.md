# Palpitations

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: August 2022.*

The most common cause of palpitations is an increased awareness of the normal heartbeat, including extra (ectopic) beats. Most episodes seen in general practice are not linked to heart disease. Only a minority of people need advanced tests or a specialist.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department if you feel unwell with palpitations and any of:**
- Chest pain
- Breathlessness
- Loss of consciousness (fainting)
- A fast heart rhythm that keeps going

Your doctor will also send you to the emergency department if a fast rhythm called supraventricular tachycardia (SVT) cannot be safely treated in the clinic, or if treatment there has not worked.

---

## Background

- A diagnosis can usually be made from a detailed history, an examination, and basic tests.
- Evidence suggests normal amounts of caffeine are unlikely to contribute to abnormal heart rhythms, in people with or without heart disease. Caffeine briefly raises heart rate and blood pressure. Some people may notice more symptoms, and there are case reports of serious rhythm problems after excessive caffeine.

---

## Assessment

### History

Your doctor will ask you to describe your symptoms fully. How they feel can point to the cause:

- **Sinus tachycardia** (a fast but normal rhythm): comes on and settles gradually; basically regular, though the rate may vary slightly; you can count the rate (less than 160 beats per minute); no major drop in blood pressure or circulation.
- **Ectopic beats:** often described as missed, skipped, or strong beats, or weak extra beats. Particularly noticed at rest.
- **Atrial fibrillation:** irregularly irregular; usually faster than a normal rate; may start or stop suddenly (paroxysmal atrial fibrillation); may come with signs of an unstable circulation.
- **Supraventricular or ventricular tachycardia:** starts and stops suddenly; generally very rapid, and can feel "too fast to count"; often comes with signs of an unstable circulation.

Your doctor may also ask about:
- How often episodes happen and how long they last.
- Triggers, especially any link with exercise.
- Whether symptoms start and stop suddenly or gradually.
- Your estimated heart rate, and whether the beats are regular or irregular.
- Other symptoms, such as breathlessness, chest pain, fainting, or nearly fainting.
- Any previous heart disease.
- Medicines that can affect heart rhythm: anti-arrhythmic drugs, beta-2 agonists such as salbutamol, decongestants, digoxin, phenothiazines, theophylline, and tricyclic antidepressants.
- Alcohol and drug use, smoking, and caffeine intake (including energy drinks).
- Family history, especially sudden unexplained death. If a relative had sudden unexplained death or cardiac arrest aged less than 30 years, your doctor may consider an inherited condition causing rhythm problems or heart muscle disease. If suspected, you and all your first-degree relatives may need further tests.
- Possible underlying conditions:
  - Structural heart disease, such as cardiomyopathy or valve disease
  - Anxiety
  - A high-output state, such as infection, anaemia, or pregnancy
  - Hormone conditions, such as an overactive thyroid or phaeochromocytoma
  - Electrolyte (salt) imbalances

### Examination

- Pulse over the heart and at a peripheral site, such as the wrist.
- Temperature and blood pressure.
- Signs of heart failure, anaemia, or thyroid disease.
- Any recordings or reports from your smart watch or other personal ECG device.

### Investigations

**Resting ECG.** An ECG is still useful even if symptoms have settled by the time you are seen. It may show problems such as reduced blood flow to the heart (ischaemia), atrial fibrillation, ectopic beats, or heart block. Features of concern include:
- An abnormal baseline ECG
- A short PR interval and delta wave, which may suggest Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome
- A long QT interval, greater than:
  - 0.48 seconds in females
  - 0.47 seconds in males
- Significant thickening of the left or right side of the heart
- Pathological Q waves or T wave inversion

If the ECG does not help, go back during an episode to your general practice, the nearest after-hours centre, or the emergency department, so an ECG can be taken while you have symptoms. Matching symptoms to the ECG rhythm is the gold standard for diagnosis. This may not be possible, and an ECG diagnosis is not needed to rule out serious causes.

**Blood tests, if needed:** FBC, creatinine, sodium, potassium, CRP, and thyroid function.

**Holter monitoring** (a 24-hour heart recording) may be arranged if there are significant concerns and the diagnosis is unclear, or if anxiety is very high. It has limits:
- Less than 10% of 24-hour Holter recordings are diagnostic.
- It may be considered if symptoms occur more than 3 times per week, although the pickup rate is likely to be low.
- If symptoms are less frequent, longer monitoring with an event monitor is more likely to capture an episode.

## Management

Your doctor may seek help reading the ECG from a hospital or cardiology team if needed. For most people, management depends on the ECG findings or the most likely diagnosis.

### Sinus tachycardia

- Your doctor will treat any underlying cause.
- If a fast sinus rhythm persists without explanation, your doctor may refer you to cardiology or seek cardiology advice.

### Ectopic beats

- Your doctor will address underlying causes, such as alcohol or anxiety.
- If the extra beats are isolated and there are no other heart symptoms, your doctor may reassure you.
- If they are frequent and bothersome, your doctor may consider a trial of a beta blocker such as metoprolol or atenolol.

### Atrial fibrillation or flutter

These are managed as atrial fibrillation. See the atrial fibrillation pathway.

### Supraventricular tachycardia (SVT)

Your doctor will confirm the diagnosis with an ECG. On ECG, SVT is a regular, narrow complex fast rhythm. If the heart rate is less than 150 beats per minute, other diagnoses are generally more likely. Your doctor will also consider ventricular tachycardia, SVT with a bundle branch block, or Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome.

**Treatment in the clinic is not suitable, and you will be sent to the emergency department, if you have:**
- Systolic blood pressure less than 90
- Sudden confusion or a change in mental state
- Signs of shock
- Chest pain from reduced blood flow to the heart
- Acute heart failure or a heart attack

**If SVT is happening now**, your doctor may try vagal manoeuvres, with heart monitoring. These work in about a third of people:
- **Modified Valsalva manoeuvre:** lying propped up, you breathe out hard against a closed throat for over 15 seconds, then lie flat with your legs lifted to 45 degrees for 15 seconds.
- **Diving reflex:** an ice-cold wet towel on the face, if the first step does not work.
- **Carotid sinus massage:** may have more complications and be less effective. Your doctor will check your neck arteries first and always use heart monitoring.

Vagal manoeuvres are not used in people with aortic stenosis, carotid artery narrowing, glaucoma, or retinopathy.

If vagal manoeuvres do not work, your doctor will send you to the emergency department. If that is not readily available and your doctor is trained and equipped, they may give **adenosine** into a vein, with continuous ECG monitoring and a defibrillator nearby:
- The first dose is usually 6 mg. It is reduced to 3 mg if you take carbamazepine or dipyridamole, have had a heart transplant, or it is given through a central line.
- If it has not worked within 1 to 2 minutes, 12 mg may be given (the maximum single dose), and repeated if needed.
- Possible side effects include nausea, rhythm disturbances, dizziness, breathlessness, and a feeling of apprehension.
- Adenosine is not used with second or third-degree heart block or sick sinus syndrome (unless a pacemaker is fitted), long QT syndrome, severe low blood pressure, decompensated heart failure, or chronic obstructive lung disease (including asthma).
- If it works, you will have another ECG and be observed for 30 minutes, until your doctor is satisfied you are well.
- If it does not work, you will be sent to the emergency department.
- After a first episode of SVT, your doctor may seek hospital advice on whether you need admission.

**If SVT is not happening now**, usually no treatment is needed. If there are no concerning findings (for example, stable blood pressure and circulation, and normal heart sounds and ECG), your doctor may reassure you.

### If no concerning features are found

- If you are well and have none of the concerning features listed below, your doctor may reassure you that advanced tests or a specialist are not needed.
- Your doctor may discuss alcohol and drug use, smoking, and caffeine intake (including energy drinks).
- If you have a smart watch or other personal ECG device, use it to record symptoms if they come back.
- Medicines for benign palpitations are only considered if symptoms are distressing and reassurance has not helped. Your doctor may consider low-dose metoprolol or atenolol, or a calcium channel blocker as second line. These may not stop the extra beats, but usually ease the symptoms.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you to cardiology, or seek cardiology advice, if you are well but have any of these concerning features:
- Episodes with fainting, chest pain, or shortness of breath.
- Palpitations brought on by exercise.
- Sustained rapid palpitations.
- A family history of sudden cardiac death in a young relative.
- Major structural heart disease.
- A significant ECG abnormality, such as long QT syndrome or Wolff-Parkinson-White syndrome.
- Suspected paroxysmal atrial fibrillation lasting more than 10 minutes, or with heart symptoms.

Your doctor may also refer you if you have:
- A fast sinus rhythm that persists without explanation.
- Recurrent episodes of SVT, if you are not already seeing cardiology.
- A first episode of SVT that did not need hospital admission.
