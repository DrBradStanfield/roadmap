# Sexuality in Spinal Cord Impairment (SCI)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers sexual function, intimacy, fertility, and contraception in adults with spinal cord impairment (SCI). Changes in sexual function are common after SCI and can cause significant emotional distress for patients and their partners. This information is a guide only — always discuss your concerns with your doctor.

---

## Background

SCI disrupts motor, sensory, and autonomic pathways, which may affect sexual functioning. Patients and their partners often experience significant grief about these changes.

### For Women

- May experience diminished vaginal lubrication, arousal, and ability to experience orgasm
- Orgasm can occur if the sacral reflex arc is intact
- **Fertility is not affected by SCI** — contraception is essential if pregnancy is not desired
- Amenorrhoea (loss of periods) can occur after SCI due to physical or psychological trauma and may last on average 4–5 months

### For Men

- Alterations in ability to orgasm, erectile quality, and ejaculatory function are common
- **Reflexogenic erections** (mediated by touch, S2–S4) and **psychogenic erections** (mentally induced, T11–L2) are often unreliable, difficult to sustain, and may not be firm enough for penetration
- Ejaculation fails in approximately 90% of men with SCI
- Sperm count deteriorates from 3–6 weeks following injury — but pregnancy can still occur despite reduced sperm count

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Issues affecting intimacy: bladder/bowel concerns, hip adductor spasms, positioning difficulties, emotional impact (anger, grief)
- In both sexes: pre-existing sexual function, mood, self-image, level and completeness of injury
- In men: ability to gain and maintain erection, medications, co-morbidities (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia), age
- Fertility concerns and desire for pregnancy
- Skin care concerns related to sex
- Symptoms of UTI or sexually transmitted infections
- Contraception needs

**Note:** People with SCI at or above T6 are at risk of autonomic dysreflexia (AD) triggered by sexual activity (see below).

---

## Management

### Erectile Dysfunction

**First-line treatment: Phosphodiesterase 5 inhibitors (PDE5i)**

Options include sildenafil, tadalafil, and vardenafil. These medications:

- Improve erectile function in men with SCI, particularly those with preserved reflexogenic function
- Do not affect libido, ejaculation, or orgasm
- Differ in strength and time frames for achieving erections
- Have associated risks — your doctor will discuss these

If a PDE5i trial has been optimised and is only partially or not successful, your doctor may refer for additional options:

- **Vacuum devices or penile rings** — can be effective but must be used correctly; risks include bruising, skin injury, and priapism (prolonged erection lasting 4+ hours — this requires urgent medical care)
- **Intracavernosal injection therapy** — injecting alprostadil (or similar) into the penis; dose must be determined under urologist supervision; risks include penile swelling/tenderness and priapism (urgent medical care if this occurs)

### Fertility Concerns

If you want to father children with SCI, ejaculation methods include:

- **Vibrator assistance:** A low-amplitude vibrator held at the penile tip allows ejaculation in 30–40% of men with SCI. A high-amplitude vibrator (prescription required) achieves ejaculation in 55–85% of men. Risks include skin irritation and autonomic dysreflexia in men with SCI at or above T6.
- Rectal probe electroejaculation, performed by an SCI specialist urologist or fertility specialist.

Your doctor can refer to a fertility specialist if needed.

### Skin Care During Sex

To avoid skin injury or worsening pressure injuries:

- Inspect insensate areas for excessive friction or tears after sexual activity, particularly around the genitalia and buttocks
- Do not fall asleep on wet sheets
- If irritation is caused by decreased lubrication, use an artificial water-soluble lubricant

### Bladder and Bowel Preparation

- Restrict fluid intake for 3–4 hours before sexual activity and empty the bladder
- Men with an indwelling catheter: fold the catheter back along the penis (do not kink the tube) and hold with a condom
- Women with an indwelling catheter: fold up and tape securely to abdomen or groin (do not kink)
- If you have a flaccid bowel, empty it before sexual activity to reduce leakage risk

### Autonomic Dysreflexia Risk (SCI at or Above T6)

Sexual activity can trigger autonomic dysreflexia. If you develop AD symptoms during sexual activity:

- Stop immediately and sit upright
- Follow your autonomic dysreflexia management plan and seek treatment
- **Important:** Do not use glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) spray for AD if you have taken sildenafil in the last 24 hours, or tadalafil in the previous 4 days — this combination can cause a dangerous drop in blood pressure

### Hip Adductor Spasms

Hip adductor spasms can interfere with sexual activity. Your doctor will manage these according to the Spasticity in SCI pathway (positioning to break the reflex pattern, warm showers, medications, passive movements).

### Positioning and Aids

Your doctor or therapist may discuss:

- Using pillows and experimenting with positioning
- Stimulation of areas above the SCI level (e.g., nipples, ears) to enhance arousal
- Adaptive equipment and sexual aids (e.g., vibrator wands, assistive seating devices)
- Open communication and rebuilding intimacy with your partner

### Orgasm

- Men with complete injuries have less chance of achieving a conventional orgasm; those with incomplete injuries may or may not achieve orgasm
- Women with an intact sacral reflex arc can experience orgasm
- Sensitivity in new body areas can be developed and may lead to sexual pleasure

### STIs and Contraception

SCI does not protect against sexually transmitted infections or unplanned pregnancy. Discuss contraception options with your doctor — taking into account DVT risk and other medical factors. Intrauterine contraception can be considered in women not at risk of autonomic dysreflexia.

### Pregnancy in SCI

Pregnancy is possible with SCI. It increases the risk of:

- Pressure injuries
- Musculoskeletal discomfort
- Seating changes needs in later pregnancy
- Urinary tract infections
- Falls (changed centre of gravity)
- Deep vein thrombosis/pulmonary embolism
- Autonomic dysreflexia (especially during delivery and occasionally when breastfeeding) if SCI is at T6 or above

**If you are pregnant or planning pregnancy with SCI, discuss this with your doctor early.** Specialist SCI assessment and obstetric review should be arranged promptly.

---

## When to Seek Help

Contact your doctor if:

- You have concerns about sexual function or fertility related to your SCI
- You experience priapism (prolonged erection lasting 4+ hours) — this is a medical emergency
- You are pregnant or planning to become pregnant
- You have signs of UTI or sexually transmitted infection
- You need specialist referral for erectile dysfunction, fertility, sex therapy, or psychological support
