# Skin Lesion Excision

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers excision biopsies and full skin lesion excision — the surgical removal of a skin lesion under local anaesthetic with stitches to close the wound. Your doctor performs this procedure and will discuss whether it is appropriate for your specific lesion.

> **Always discuss with your doctor.** This information is a general overview. Your doctor will assess your individual situation and guide you on the best approach.

---

## When Is Excision Appropriate?

Your doctor will decide whether excision is the right approach based on the type of lesion. Excision may be performed in general practice, or you may be referred to a specialist if:

- The lesion cannot be closed with sufficient margins in a general practice setting
- Melanoma is suspected and requires wider surgical margins
- The lesion is in a difficult location (e.g., near the eye, nose, or eyelid margin)
- A more complex surgical technique (e.g., Mohs surgery) is needed

---

## Excision Margins

Different lesions require different surgical margins (the rim of normal skin removed around the lesion):

| Type of lesion | Margin |
|---|---|
| Suspected melanoma (excision biopsy) | 2 mm |
| Benign lesions | 2 mm (less if clearly benign, e.g. sebaceous cyst) |
| BCC less than 2 cm with well-defined border | 4 mm |
| Aggressive or unclear BCC, or BCC near sensitive structures | Specialist assessment required |
| Well-differentiated SCC less than 2 cm, or SCC on lip/ears | 4 mm |
| SCC larger than 2 cm | Specialist assessment required |

---

## Blood Thinners (Anticoagulants)

If you are taking aspirin, warfarin, a DOAC (e.g., rivaroxaban, apixaban), or clopidogrel, you can usually continue these medications before minor skin surgery. The risks of stopping them typically outweigh the small risk of extra bleeding from minor skin surgery.

If you take warfarin, your doctor will check your INR is within the therapeutic range the day before the procedure.

Tell your doctor about all medications you take, including blood thinners.

---

## The Procedure

Your doctor will:

1. **Discuss and document consent** — including the risks: bleeding, infection, incomplete removal, nerve damage, scarring, wound breakdown, or adverse reactions to anaesthetic or dressings.

2. **Mark the lesion** — under bright light, the edges of the lesion and the planned excision area are marked. The incision is planned as an ellipse (oval shape) aligned with the natural tension lines of your skin to minimise scarring. The ellipse is typically about 3 times longer than it is wide.

3. **Inject local anaesthetic** — the area is numbed with lidocaine (lignocaine), usually with adrenaline to reduce bleeding and extend the anaesthetic effect. You will be awake but should feel no pain. Local anaesthetic is not used in fingers, toes, ears, nose, or penis (different technique required), and is not used in infected or inflamed skin.

4. **Remove the lesion** — the ellipse of skin is cut out down to the layer of subcutaneous fat (the fatty tissue under the skin). The specimen is sent to the laboratory for analysis.

5. **Close the wound with stitches** — the wound is closed in layers if needed, and the outer stitches are placed. Timing for stitch removal depends on the location:

   | Location | Stitch removal |
   |---|---|
   | Face | 3 to 5 days |
   | Arms, scalp | 7 to 10 days |
   | Hands, feet, legs, trunk | 10 to 14 days |
   | Palms, soles | 14 to 21 days |

6. **Apply a dressing** — pressure is applied to stop any bleeding, the wound is cleaned, and a protective dressing is applied for at least 24 hours.

7. **Provide wound care instructions** — your doctor will explain how to look after the wound at home while it heals.

8. **Send the specimen to the laboratory** — the removed tissue is analysed by a pathologist (histology). Results typically take around 2 weeks.

---

## After the Procedure — Wound Care

Your doctor will give you specific instructions, but general advice after skin excision includes:

- Keep the wound clean and dry initially
- Avoid soaking the wound (baths, swimming) until healed
- Watch for signs of infection: increasing redness, swelling, warmth, discharge, or fever
- Return to have stitches removed at the timing advised for your body site
- Avoid strenuous activity that may stress the wound until it has healed

Contact your doctor if you are concerned about your wound.

---

## Getting Your Results

Your doctor will check the histology (laboratory) results at approximately 2 weeks. They will then contact you to:

- Confirm the diagnosis
- Advise whether the lesion was fully removed (clear margins)
- Discuss any further treatment needed

If the laboratory report suggests incomplete removal or recommends specialist review, your doctor will arrange a referral.

---

## Follow-up After Results

Depending on the histology finding:

- **Clear margins, benign lesion:** No further treatment usually needed.
- **Incomplete excision (deep or lateral):** The lesion was not fully removed. Your doctor will discuss re-excision or specialist referral.
- **Melanoma:** Always requires further surgery with wider margins. Specialist referral will be arranged.
- **Basal cell carcinoma (BCC) or squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) with incomplete margins:** Further excision or specialist assessment (e.g., Mohs surgery) may be needed.

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor may refer you to a dermatologist or plastic surgeon if:

- Melanoma is suspected and cannot be managed in general practice with appropriate margins
- The histology result recommends specialist review
- The lesion requires Mohs surgery or complex reconstruction

You can also choose to see a specialist privately.
