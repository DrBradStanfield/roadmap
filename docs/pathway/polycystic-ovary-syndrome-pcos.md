# Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of PCOS — the most common endocrine disorder in young women, affecting 10–13% globally.

---

## Background

PCOS is characterised by a varied and often complex array of metabolic and endocrine abnormalities. The exact cause is unknown, though environmental and genetic factors can affect its presentation. Prevalence is similar across ethnicities, although ethnic variation occurs in presenting features and clinical manifestations.

Women with PCOS are at increased risk of:

- Metabolic syndrome, insulin resistance, gestational diabetes, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidaemia, and cardiovascular disease
- Endometrial cancer (2 to 6-fold increased risk)
- Fertility problems
- Depression and anxiety
- Eating disorders and disordered eating
- Obstructive sleep apnoea

Obesity contributes to these risks, but not all women with PCOS are overweight — hyperinsulinaemia and other metabolic changes are also present in lean women with PCOS.

Diagnosis is important: lifestyle changes and medical management can minimise metabolic complications and improve fertility.

## Assessment

Your doctor will use age-appropriate diagnostic criteria. A clinical diagnosis of PCOS can often be made without ultrasound or abnormal blood test results.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- **Irregular menstrual cycles or amenorrhoea**
  - Irregular cycles are defined as:
    - In the first year after first period: normal variation during puberty
    - 1–3 years after first period: shorter than 21 days or longer than 45 days
    - More than 3 years after first period to perimenopause: shorter than 21 days or longer than 35 days, or fewer than 8 cycles per year; or any single cycle longer than 90 days
  - Amenorrhoea:
    - Primary: absence of first period by age 15, or more than 3 years after breast development
    - Secondary: no periods for 6 months in a woman who was previously menstruating
- **Symptoms of androgen excess** (hirsutism, acne, hair thinning/loss)
- **Subfertility** (trying to conceive for more than 6 months if older than 35, or more than 12 months if 35 or younger)

### Examination

Your doctor will examine for:

- Signs of androgen excess: hirsutism (using a standardised scoring scale such as the modified Ferriman-Gallwey score), acne, androgenetic alopecia
- Signs of insulin resistance (e.g. darkening of skin in skin folds — acanthosis nigricans, skin tags)
- Blood pressure, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI)

### Diagnostic Criteria

**Adults** — Rotterdam criteria require 2 of the following 3, after excluding other causes:
1. Irregular menstrual cycles or ovulatory dysfunction
2. Clinical hyperandrogenism (hirsutism, acne, female pattern hair loss) and/or biochemical hyperandrogenism (elevated total or free testosterone)
3. Polycystic ovaries on ultrasound (20 or more follicles of 2–9 mm diameter and/or ovarian volume greater than 10 mL)

**Adolescents** — both of the following are required, after excluding other causes:
1. Irregular menstrual cycles or ovulatory dysfunction
2. Clinical hyperandrogenism (hirsutism and/or severe acne) and/or biochemical hyperandrogenism

Note: Ultrasound is not used to diagnose PCOS in adolescents less than 8 years after their first period, as multi-follicular ovaries are common in normal adolescents.

### Investigations

If a clinical diagnosis cannot be made, your doctor may arrange:

- **Total testosterone and sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG)** — to check for biochemical hyperandrogenism. Note: reliable assessment is not possible in women currently on hormonal contraception; a gap of 3 months or more is needed after stopping
- **Beta-hCG** — to exclude pregnancy
- **TSH** — if thyroid dysfunction is suspected
- **Prolactin** — if irregular periods or galactorrhoea are present; raised prolactin (confirmed on a second test) may warrant referral to endocrinology
- **FSH** — if premature ovarian insufficiency is suspected (more than 4 months of amenorrhoea, particularly with typical symptoms)
- **24-hour urinary cortisol or overnight dexamethasone suppression test** — if Cushing's syndrome is suspected (central obesity, moon face, purple striae, hypertension)
- **Pelvic ultrasound (adults only)** — may be arranged if there is high clinical suspicion and only one diagnostic criterion is met; not used in patients currently on hormonal medications or in adolescents

## Management

Management of PCOS is lifelong. Your doctor will discuss your goals, preferences, and the benefits and potential side effects of any treatment.

### Lifestyle

- Healthy eating and regular physical activity
- Preventing weight gain; if BMI is 25 or higher, symptoms are likely to improve with weight loss of 5–10%

### Psychological and Wellbeing

Your doctor will routinely screen for depression and anxiety (both more common in PCOS) and ask about the psychological impact of symptoms such as hirsutism. Be cautious with medications that may worsen PCOS symptoms (e.g. those causing weight gain). Women with PCOS are also at higher risk of eating disorders and disordered eating.

Your doctor may consider referral for psychosexual counselling if symptoms are affecting sexual wellbeing.

### Obstructive Sleep Apnoea

Your doctor will screen for symptoms such as loud snoring or waking unrefreshed.

### Contraception

If sexually active and not wishing to conceive, contraception is important — approximately 60% of women with PCOS will conceive without medical help.

### Hyperandrogenism (Acne, Hirsutism, Hair Loss)

Your doctor may consider one or more of the following:

- **Combined oral contraceptive pill (COC)** — first-line. All COCs increase SHBG and reduce free androgens, and provide contraception, endometrial protection, and cycle regulation. Anti-androgenic COC options include cyproterone acetate/ethinylestradiol (Ginet 84 — subsidised) and drospirenone/ethinylestradiol (Yasmin, Yaz — not subsidised, higher VTE risk).
- **Spironolactone** 100–200 mg daily — second-line. Starting dose 25 mg twice daily, maximum 100 mg twice daily. Requires contraception. Potassium checked at 1 month. Reassessed after 6 months.
- **Cyproterone acetate** — second-line (usually initiated in secondary care). Associated with meningioma risk; no more effective than COC for acne and hirsutism.
- **Acne management** — as per standard acne guidelines
- **Cosmetic treatments** — including laser therapy for hirsutism

If there is no improvement after 6–9 months of pharmacological management, your doctor will refer to endocrinology.

### Irregular Periods

Your doctor may consider:

- **Lifestyle changes** if BMI is 25 or higher
- **Endometrial protection** — if fewer than 4 periods per year, there is an increased risk of endometrial hyperplasia and cancer. Options include:
  - Combined oral contraceptive pill (COC) — first-line
  - Cyclical progestogen, e.g. medroxyprogesterone (Provera) 5–10 mg for 10 days every 3 months, or norethisterone
  - Levonorgestrel intrauterine system (Mirena) — fully funded; replaced every 5 years when used for endometrial protection
- **Metformin** — off-label use; may increase frequency of periods in women with oligomenorrhoea but is unlikely to produce a regular cycle and has not been proven to protect the endometrium

If there is no improvement after 3–6 months, your doctor will seek gynaecology advice.

### Fertility

Your doctor will:

- Encourage lifestyle management if BMI is 25 or higher
- Consider metformin — may improve ovulation rates; off-label use
- Not initiate ovulation induction medications (e.g. clomifene) in primary care — this is managed in specialist settings

### Metabolic Risks (Diabetes, Cardiovascular Disease)

Your doctor will:

- Schedule annual blood pressure and BMI check
- Screen for high cholesterol and diabetes at diagnosis and every 1–2 years thereafter
- Manage any cardiovascular risk factors
- Consider metformin — recommended if type 2 diabetes or impaired glucose tolerance and lifestyle changes have not been sufficient; also considered for weight and metabolic outcomes in adults with BMI 25 or higher (off-label use)

**Metformin dosing** (when used):
- Week 1: 500 mg with breakfast
- Week 2: 500 mg with breakfast and 500 mg with evening meal
- Maintenance: 500 mg with breakfast and 1000 mg with evening meal, or 850 mg twice daily
- Start with food to reduce gastrointestinal side effects
- Can be associated with low vitamin B12 — monitoring may be needed
- Appears safe for long-term use

## When Your Doctor Will Refer

- Suspected androgen-producing tumour (testosterone > 5 nmol/L or virilising features) — specialist endorsement required before additional testing (DHEA-S, androstenedione)
- Suspected Cushing's syndrome — specialist endorsement required before 24-hour cortisol testing
- Hyperandrogenism symptoms not improving after 6–9 months of pharmacological treatment — non-acute endocrinology referral
- Irregular periods not improving after 3–6 months of medical management — gynaecology advice
- Fertility concerns — subfertility pathway and possible specialist referral
