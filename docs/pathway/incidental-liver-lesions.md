# Incidental Liver Lesions

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers liver lesions found incidentally on imaging — for example, on an ultrasound or CT scan performed for another reason. Not all liver lesions require further investigation or ongoing monitoring.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Abdominal pain — masses occasionally present this way
- **Risk factors for malignancy:**
  - Previous malignancy
  - History of cirrhosis or advanced liver fibrosis
  - Chronic hepatitis B or C infection
  - Metabolic syndrome
  - NAFLD (fatty liver disease)
  - Anabolic steroid use
  - Family history of liver cancer
  - Enlarging liver lesion on serial scans
- **Risk factors for chronic liver disease:**
  - Heavy alcohol use (past or current)
  - Chronic hepatitis B or C infection
  - Metabolic syndrome
  - Haemochromatosis
  - Chronic use of hepatotoxic medication (e.g., methotrexate)
- **Symptoms that may suggest liver cancer** (tumours usually only become symptomatic when locally advanced):
  - Upper abdominal discomfort
  - Abdominal swelling or hard lump/mass
  - Right shoulder pain (may indicate diaphragmatic referred pain)
  - Back pain
  - Jaundice, dark urine, pale stools, or itching
  - Bruising
  - Reduced appetite, nausea, or weight loss
  - Unusual tiredness or confusion
- Family history of liver cancer or liver disease
- Use of oral contraception (associated with increased risk of hepatic adenoma)
- Pregnancy

Physical examination typically looks for signs of chronic liver disease:

- Jaundice
- Liver tenderness, enlarged liver or spleen, irregular contour, or palpable masses
- Ascites (fluid in the abdomen)
- Peripheral oedema
- Spider naevi, gynaecomastia, palmar erythema
- Flapping tremor or confusion
- Muscle wasting (sarcopenia)

Your doctor may consider blood tests based on the radiology report, history, and examination — for example:

- **Liver function tests:** FBC, LFTs, albumin, INR, renal function
- **Tumour markers** (alpha fetoprotein / AFP, CA19-9, CEA) — only if there is a past malignancy or liver disease. Normal results do not always exclude malignancy.
- Creatinine and beta-HCG may be needed before contrast CT or MRI imaging.

## Lesion Types and Management

Management depends on the type of liver lesion identified on imaging:

### Hepatic Haemangioma

A benign liver lesion made up of clusters of blood-filled cavities. Present in around 7% of people — usually less than 3 cm in size. Oral contraceptive pills and pregnancy are not contraindicated.

Your doctor may consider specialist referral if:
- The lesion is causing symptoms (e.g., pain or fullness)
- Size is greater than 10 cm (giant haemangioma), even without symptoms
- Imaging features are atypical or the diagnosis is uncertain

### Focal Nodular Hyperplasia (FNH)

A benign lesion more common in women. Oral contraceptive pills and menopause hormone therapy (MHT) are not contraindicated.

Your doctor may consider specialist referral if:
- The lesion is causing symptoms (e.g., pain or fullness)
- Imaging features are atypical or the diagnosis is uncertain
- The lesion is increasing in size
- A biopsy or surgical assessment is needed (e.g., if greater than 5 cm or difficult to distinguish from other lesions)

### Hepatocellular Adenoma (HCA)

An uncommon benign liver lesion, usually found in young women associated with oral contraception or anabolic steroid use. There is a potential for haemorrhage and malignant transformation, so specialist referral is recommended for all patients.

Your doctor may consider:
- Discontinuing estrogen-containing oral contraception or anabolic steroids (this can sometimes lead to regression)
- Ongoing surveillance with imaging (6 or 12 monthly)
- Weight management support if overweight
- If found during pregnancy, urgent surgical specialist assessment

### Liver Cysts

Asymptomatic simple (benign) liver cysts generally do not require specific follow-up or treatment.

Your doctor may consider specialist referral if:
- The cyst is causing symptoms (e.g., pain or fullness)
- Imaging features are atypical or the diagnosis is uncertain
- Polycystic liver disease (more than 10–20 fluid-filled cysts) with symptoms

### Suspected Metastatic Liver Disease

Metastatic liver cancer is more common than primary liver cancer.

Your doctor will consider:
- If there is a history of malignancy — referral back to the patient's oncologist or surgeon
- If no previously diagnosed malignancy — specialist assessment to investigate for a primary lesion
- Urgent referral if a metastatic lesion is suspected

### Suspected Hepatocellular Carcinoma (HCC)

If a primary liver malignancy is suspected, your doctor will arrange urgent specialist assessment (gastroenterology).

## Indeterminate or Atypical Lesions

Your doctor may consider specialist (gastroenterology) assessment if:

- Imaging features are atypical or the diagnosis is uncertain
- There are associated symptoms such as pain, compression, obstructive symptoms, or unexplained abnormal liver function tests

For other incidental findings, further imaging is only arranged if recommended by the radiologist's report.
