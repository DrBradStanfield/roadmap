# Acute Foot Injuries

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of acute foot injuries in adults, including fractures and dislocations.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history of the mechanism of injury to help determine the likely diagnosis:

- **Fall onto feet** — look for talus or calcaneal fractures (especially heel strikes); assess for associated injuries (Achilles tendon, spine, pelvis, hips, knees)
- **Violent twisting injury** — look for hind-foot and mid-foot dislocations, especially Lisfranc injuries
- **Direct trauma** (falling objects, crush injuries) — look for metatarsal fractures and dislocations
- **Inversion injury / "sprained ankle"** — look for avulsion fractures at the base of the fifth metatarsal
- **Dorsiflexion and inversion of the ankle** — look for lateral talus fracture (snowboarder's fracture)
- **Prolonged or repetitive exercise-associated pain** — look for metatarsal stress fractures

### Examination

Your doctor will examine the foot and ankle with you standing and lying down, assessing:

- Foot and ankle alignment, arch height, and gait
- Skin, nails, toe alignment, joint swelling, and calluses
- Foot temperature, pulses, and sensation
- Range of motion: inversion, eversion, dorsiflexion, and plantarflexion
- Midtarsal joint movement separately from the ankle

### Imaging

Your doctor will arrange foot and ankle x-rays. Additional imaging may be needed depending on what is suspected:

- **CT scan** — if lateral talus or calcaneal fracture is suspected despite a normal x-ray (requires specialist referral)
- **Radionuclide bone scan** — if a stress fracture is suspected
- **Weight-bearing x-rays** — if a Lisfranc injury is suspected

## Injury Types

### Talus Fracture

- Can be confused with a severe lateral ankle sprain
- Complications include non-union, malunion, and subtalar arthrosis
- All types (avulsion, talar dome, talar neck, lateral/snowboarder's) require acute orthopaedic assessment

### Calcaneal (Heel Bone) Fracture

- Comminuted, displaced, intra-articular, or Achilles-insertion fractures require emergency orthopaedic assessment
- A tongue-type fracture displacing the tuberosity is a surgical emergency — the overlying skin is at risk of breakdown within hours
- Extra-articular calcaneal fractures are managed initially with a backslab (plaster splint) and non-weight bearing for 1 week, then followed up with a specialist

### Metatarsal Fractures

Several distinct types, each managed differently:

**Lisfranc injury**
- Involves the bones and joints connecting the mid-foot to the forefoot (first, second, and third metatarsals + cuneiforms)
- Usually caused by high impact or a twisting fall
- Signs: midfoot swelling, inability to bear weight, plantar bruising
- A fracture at the base of the second metatarsal strongly suggests this diagnosis
- Can be difficult to diagnose and is often missed — causes significant disability if untreated
- Requires acute orthopaedic assessment for surgical consideration

**Isolated non-displaced metatarsal shaft fracture**
- Your doctor may apply a below-knee plaster backslab for 1 week
- Non-weight bearing for the first 2 weeks
- Follow-up x-rays at 1 week and 4 weeks after injury

**Fifth metatarsal proximal avulsion fracture (base, non-displaced)**
- Commonly follows an inversion ankle injury
- Your doctor may apply Tubigrip and provide crutches, with weight bearing as tolerated
- If pain is significant, a weight-bearing plaster or moon boot may be used
- Follow-up at 8 weeks if not healing on x-ray

**Jones fracture (fifth metatarsal proximal shaft fracture)**
- A transverse fracture of the proximal shaft of the fifth metatarsal, extra-articular to the tarso-metatarsal joint
- Your doctor may apply a below-knee plaster of Paris and provide crutches
- Follow-up orthopaedic assessment is arranged

**Metatarsal stress fractures**
- Common in runners and people doing prolonged or unusual exercise
- Most often in the second metatarsal shaft, also third; less common elsewhere
- Pain typically starts during exercise and progresses to pain at rest
- Swelling over the forefoot and localised tenderness over the metatarsal shaft
- Your doctor may advise elevation, rest, and reduced activity; analgesia; plaster cast if pain is severe
- Full recovery expected within 6–8 weeks

## Management

Your doctor will manage the injury based on its type. General principles include:

- Analgesia for pain relief
- Plaster backslab or cast, and crutches, for fractures requiring immobilisation
- Non-weight bearing instructions where appropriate
- Referral to orthopaedics for fractures requiring surgical assessment
- Follow-up x-rays at defined intervals to confirm healing

### Allied Health Support

Your doctor may consider referral to:

- **Physiotherapy** — if there are barriers to recovery, or strength, range of motion, proprioceptive, or functional deficits
- **Podiatry** — if uncomfortable weight-bearing, where foot posture control (padding, strapping, orthotics) would reduce load on the injured area
- **Occupational therapy** — if difficulty with everyday activities (self-care, household tasks, driving, work, or study)
- **Work rehabilitation** — if experiencing difficulties returning to or sustaining work tasks

---

## When to Seek Urgent Assessment

Your doctor will arrange acute orthopaedic assessment if:

- Talus fracture (any type)
- Calcaneal fracture that is comminuted, displaced, involves the Achilles tendon insertion, or is intra-articular
- Suspected Lisfranc injury

Non-urgent orthopaedic follow-up is arranged for extra-articular calcaneal fractures, Jones fractures, and suspected non-union of fifth metatarsal avulsion fractures at 8 weeks.
