# Angina

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of angina — chest pain caused by reduced blood flow to the heart muscle.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Help

**Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately if:**

- You have acute chest pain that is severe, prolonged, or new
- You have symptoms of unstable angina:
  - Angina that has come on within the last month and is severe or frequent
  - Angina that is getting worse — more frequent or more severe than your usual pattern
  - Angina that occurs at rest or with minimal physical activity
- Pain is associated with sweating or nausea

Unstable angina is a medical emergency. It can be difficult to distinguish from a heart attack on initial assessment.

---

## Background

Angina is characterised by pain or discomfort in the chest, neck, shoulders, jaw, or arms caused by reduced blood flow to the heart.

- Triggers may include physical exertion, emotional upset, or cold weather
- Symptoms are typically relieved within about 5 minutes by rest or glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) spray

**Stable angina** follows a predictable pattern — brought on by known triggers and relieved by rest or GTN.

**Unstable angina** is less predictable and more dangerous. It includes:
- Angina of recent onset (less than 1 month) that is severe or frequent
- Worsening angina superimposed on a previously stable pattern
- Angina at rest or with minimal exertion

The pain of unstable angina is similar to stable angina but is often more intense and longer-lasting. It can also cause sweating and nausea.

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. **Take a detailed history of symptoms** — this is the most important step in diagnosis:
   - Location, radiation, severity, duration, and frequency of pain
   - What provokes it and what relieves it
   - Relationship to physical exertion
   - Associated symptoms such as breathlessness
   - Exercise tolerance (e.g., how far you can walk)
   - Note: some people — especially those with diabetes — may not experience pain but may have other reproducible exercise-induced symptoms

2. **Assess your cardiovascular risk:**
   - Previous history of cardiovascular disease or coronary artery disease (e.g., heart attacks, stents, bypass surgery)
   - In Māori or Pacific patients, cardiovascular disease is more common and tends to present at a younger age

3. **Consider other causes of chest pain:**
   - Gastrointestinal (e.g., reflux, oesophageal spasm, biliary colic — these can be difficult to distinguish from cardiac pain)
   - Musculoskeletal
   - Pericarditis (inflammation around the heart)
   - Anxiety or panic disorder
   - Respiratory causes
   - Breast pain
   - Shingles
   - Viral causes (e.g., Bornholm disease)

4. **Perform a physical examination:**
   - Blood pressure
   - Pulse rate and rhythm
   - Heart sounds (including listening for murmurs — severe aortic stenosis can cause angina-like symptoms)
   - BMI
   - Assessment to exclude musculoskeletal, gastrointestinal, and respiratory conditions

5. **Arrange initial investigations:**

   Blood tests typically include:
   - Full blood count (FBC)
   - Creatinine, sodium, potassium
   - Cholesterol and lipids
   - HbA1c (blood sugar marker)

   An ECG (heart tracing) will usually be performed. Note: a resting ECG can be normal in over 90% of people with recent angina symptoms — a normal ECG does not rule out angina.

6. **Determine whether there is a high or low probability of angina**, and manage accordingly.

## Management

### High Probability of Angina

Your doctor may consider:

- **Referral for cardiology assessment** for diagnostic investigations, assessment of severity, and consideration of procedures (e.g., angiography)
- **Medication** to reduce the risk of a heart attack and relieve symptoms while awaiting specialist assessment:
  - Aspirin 100 mg daily (enteric coated)
  - A statin (e.g., atorvastatin 40 mg daily) to lower cholesterol
  - A beta blocker (e.g., bisoprolol 2.5 mg daily) or calcium channel blocker to prevent angina episodes
  - If beta blockers and calcium channel blockers are not suitable, a long-acting nitrate may be used instead
- **Glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) spray** for acute symptom relief, with instructions on how to use it
  - Important: do not use GTN spray if you are also taking erectile dysfunction medications (e.g., sildenafil, tadalafil) — this combination can cause a dangerous drop in blood pressure
- **An angina action plan** (from the Heart Foundation) to know what to do if symptoms change
- **Addressing all modifiable cardiovascular risk factors** — including smoking, blood pressure, cholesterol, diabetes, and weight

### Low Probability of Angina

Your doctor may:

- Monitor your symptoms over time
- If chest pain persists and concern about angina remains, refer for cardiology assessment and consider starting the medications listed above

### Existing (Established) Angina

If you already have a diagnosis of angina, your doctor will regularly check:

- Frequency and severity of episodes
- Whether you are experiencing angina at night
- Any associated symptoms (e.g., breathlessness)
- How often you are using your GTN spray
- Whether you are taking your medications (aspirin, statin, etc.) as prescribed
- Quality of life, social factors, and physical activity levels

If symptoms are worsening or poorly controlled, your doctor may:

- Increase the dose of your beta blocker or calcium channel blocker
- Add a second medication (beta blocker or calcium channel blocker)
- Add a third medication (long-acting nitrate)
- Note: beta blockers should not be combined with verapamil (risk of dangerous low blood pressure and cardiac arrest); use caution combining beta blockers with diltiazem (risk of heart block and slow heart rate)
- Refer to cardiology if symptoms remain poorly controlled despite maximum tolerated medications

**If you develop recurrent or worsening symptoms — even if you have had previous heart surgery or stents — seek urgent medical attention.** Further ischaemia (reduced blood flow) is possible and should be assumed until proven otherwise.
