# Low FODMAP Diet

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers when a low FODMAP diet may be appropriate, what it involves, and important safety considerations.

---

## Red Flags — See Your Doctor First

Do not start a low FODMAP diet without medical review if you have any of the following. These symptoms may indicate a more serious underlying condition that needs investigation:

- Unexplained weight loss
- Rectal bleeding
- Unexplained anaemia
- Abdominal or rectal mass
- Strong family history of colon cancer
- Family history of inflammatory bowel disease
- Symptoms that started after age 50
- Persistent daily diarrhoea
- Fever
- Recurrent vomiting
- Nocturnal bowel movements (waking at night to pass stool)
- Persistent, progressive, or severe symptoms
- Family history of breast or ovarian cancer

Your doctor will need to rule out conditions such as diverticulitis, coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, endometriosis, ovarian cancer, and colorectal cancer before a low FODMAP diet is considered.

---

## Background

**FODMAP** stands for:

- **F**ermentable
- **O**ligosaccharides
- **D**isaccharides
- **M**onosaccharides
- **A**nd
- **P**olyols

These are short-chain carbohydrates that are not completely digested or absorbed in the intestines.

Poor absorption of FODMAP molecules can cause:

- Abdominal bloating and distension
- Abdominal pain
- Excessive wind
- Changes in bowel habits (diarrhoea, constipation, or both)
- Other gastrointestinal symptoms

A low FODMAP diet focuses on reducing intake of foods high in these molecules. It is one of the most effective dietary therapies for people with a confirmed diagnosis of irritable bowel syndrome (IBS). It may also benefit people with fructose malabsorption or similar conditions.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess whether a low FODMAP diet is appropriate for you. This includes:

- Checking for red flag symptoms (listed above) to rule out serious underlying conditions
- Asking about potential symptom triggers such as caffeine, medications, stress, and alcohol
- Considering your history of disordered eating — a low FODMAP diet is **not recommended** for people with a history of disordered eating patterns, as it is severely restrictive

If you have a history of disordered eating, your doctor may consider alternatives such as:

- Pharmacotherapy (e.g., antispasmodics, laxatives)
- Herbal therapies (e.g., peppermint oil)
- Probiotics
- Psychological therapies (e.g., gut-directed hypnotherapy, cognitive behavioural therapy, stress management)

---

## Management

**Important:** A low FODMAP diet should only be undertaken under the supervision of a dietitian. Self-administering the diet is not recommended — a heavily restricted food intake can lead to deficiencies in calcium, fibre, iron, and natural prebiotics. It also carries a risk of further weight loss in people who are already losing weight unintentionally.

Your doctor may consider the following approach:

1. **Address symptom triggers** — your doctor will advise you to reduce or avoid caffeine, alcohol, stress, and any medications that may contribute to your symptoms.

2. **Dietitian referral** — a low FODMAP diet is only recommended with dietitian support, for both adults and children. Your doctor may refer you to a dietitian if your symptoms are consistent with IBS or related conditions (such as fructose malabsorption, gluten intolerance, or lactose intolerance). Note that publicly funded dietitian assessment for IBS has limited availability.

3. **Elimination phase** — if the dietitian assesses you as eligible, they will guide you through a period of restricting high FODMAP foods (typically several weeks). This phase is carefully supervised to ensure your nutritional needs for energy, macronutrients, and micronutrients are met.

4. **Reintroduction phase** — under dietitian guidance, foods are gradually reintroduced to identify your individual triggers and develop a personalised long-term eating plan.
