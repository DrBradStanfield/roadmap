# Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms in Men (LUTS)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) in men — including storage and voiding difficulties often related to benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) or other urological conditions.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

**Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately if:**

- Suspected acute spinal cord compression or cauda equina syndrome (see signs below)

**Signs of cauda equina syndrome include:**
- Saddle anaesthesia (numbness in the inner thighs and perineum)
- Back pain
- Motor weakness or abnormal gait
- Altered bladder and bowel function, particularly faecal incontinence and urinary retention
- Loss of reflexes (Achilles and anal)
- Sexual dysfunction

**Do not wait if PSA is ≥ 50 nanograms/mL, or ≥ 10 micrograms/mL with severe back pain and acute neurological symptoms** — these require immediate management, not a repeat test.

---

## Background

Lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) in men are often multifactorial, arising from a combination of storage and voiding problems. Symptoms may be obstructive, irritative, or both.

Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH) is a common cause, but it is important to assess carefully to rule out other serious conditions including prostate cancer, neurological disease, infection, and bladder cancer.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history, looking for:

**Obstructive symptoms:**
- Poor flow
- Hesitancy
- Terminal or post-micturition dribbling
- Overflow incontinence

**Irritative symptoms:**
- Frequency
- Dysuria
- Nocturia
- Urgency

**Other relevant features:**
- Haematuria (blood in urine)
- Recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs)

Your doctor will also ask about:

- Loin or pelvic pain
- History of urological stones, UTI, or prostatitis
- History of stroke, complicated diabetes, or neurological disease
- History of pelvic or spine trauma, surgery, or radiation treatment
- Family history of prostate cancer (one first-degree relative under 70 doubles the risk; two or more increases it 4–5 times; BRCA gene also increases risk)
- Erectile dysfunction
- Constipation
- Anxiety
- Systemic symptoms that could suggest malignancy: fatigue, bone pain or fracture, unintentional weight loss, neurological symptoms
- Medications that may contribute — including diuretics, sympathomimetics (e.g., pseudoephedrine), anticholinergics (antihistamines, tricyclic antidepressants, SSRIs, oxybutynin, phenothiazines), opiates, benzodiazepines, and some muscle relaxants (e.g., orphenadrine)

**High-risk features your doctor will screen for:**
- Visible haematuria
- Recurrent or persistent UTI with haematuria in men aged over 40
- Unexplained persistent microscopic haematuria in men aged over 40

**Symptom severity scoring:**

Your doctor may use the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS) to assess severity:
- Mild: score less than 8
- Moderate: score 8 to 19
- Severe: score 20 to 35

**Physical examination typically includes:**
- Abdomen — check for palpable bladder, abdominal masses, peripheral oedema
- External genitalia — foreskin (phimosis, balanitis xerotica obliterans), urethral orifice, testes
- Digital rectal examination (DRE) — smooth, firm, symmetrical prostate enlargement suggests BPH; asymmetrical hard areas or nodules raise concern for prostate cancer
- Neurological — lower limb power, tone, reflexes, anal tone, and saddle sensation

**Investigations your doctor may arrange:**
- Urine dipstick and mid-stream urine (MSU)
- Prostate-specific antigen (PSA) blood test
- STI screen if aged under 30
- Creatinine (kidney function)
- Renal ultrasound if there is a palpable bladder, abdominal or pelvic mass, reduced kidney function from suspected chronic urinary retention, or associated haematuria or recurrent UTIs
- Bladder diary (recording fluid intake and urine volumes over 2 days)

**Understanding PSA results:**

PSA above 4 may be abnormal at any age, and carries approximately 30% positive predictive value for cancer. However, elevated PSA can have non-malignant causes including UTI, prostatitis, BPH, recent prostate biopsy, ejaculation within 48 hours of testing, or intense exercise (including cycling) within 48 hours.

If PSA is abnormal, your doctor will usually repeat it after 6–12 weeks (12 weeks if there is a reversible cause to treat, 6 weeks if not).

**PSA referral thresholds (Ministry of Health guidelines):**
- Age 50–70 with normal DRE: PSA ≥ 4 nanograms/mL
- Age 71–75 with normal DRE: PSA ≥ 10 nanograms/mL
- Age ≥ 76 with normal DRE: PSA ≥ 20 nanograms/mL
- Palpable prostate abnormality on DRE at any age
- A significant PSA rise from a previously low level

A suspicious DRE should be investigated even if PSA is normal — not all prostate cancers raise PSA.

## Management

Your doctor will manage LUTS based on the underlying cause and symptom severity.

**Emergency situations:**

If acute spinal cord compression or cauda equina syndrome is suspected, your doctor will arrange urgent ambulance transport to hospital.

**Urinary tract infection:**

Your doctor will treat any identified UTI with appropriate antibiotics.

**Haematuria:**

Your doctor will manage haematuria according to the haematuria pathway.

**Overactive bladder:**

Your doctor may consider the following medications:
- Oxybutynin 2.5 mg twice daily with food, which may be increased to 5 mg three times daily
- Imipramine 10 mg at night, which may be increased to 30 mg
- Solifenacin up to 10 mg once daily

These medications reduce bladder contractility and must be used carefully in patients with elevated post-void residual urine, as they can cause urinary retention. If symptoms improve, treatment is usually continued long term — it may take several weeks to notice benefit. If treatment is ineffective, a urology referral will be arranged.

**Terminal or post-micturition dribbling:**

Post-micturition dribble as an isolated symptom is usually due to dilatation of the bulbar urethra that occurs with middle age. Urine pools in the urethra and leaks out after leaving the toilet. Your doctor can reassure you that referral is not needed. A practical technique is to massage urine out by hand, starting at the perineum beneath the scrotum — this stimulates pelvic floor muscle contraction and removes residual urine.

**Nocturnal polyuria:**

Your doctor may recommend:
- Keeping a bladder diary for 2 days, including volume of the first morning void
- If one-third or more of total 24-hour urine output is produced at night: reduce caffeine and alcohol intake, restrict fluids after 6.00 pm, consider taking a diuretic mid-afternoon to shift urine output earlier in the day, and rest with legs elevated after lunch

**Benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH):**

If other causes have been excluded, the diagnosis is likely BPH. Your doctor will advise on any underlying risks and manage according to symptom severity and quality of life impact.

**Prostate cancer concern:**

If PSA or DRE findings are significantly abnormal, your doctor will follow the prostate cancer diagnosis pathway and may refer to urology.

**Acute urinary retention:**

If you cannot pass urine at all, this requires urgent management — your doctor will arrange catheterisation.

---

## When Referral to Urology Is Needed

Your doctor may refer you to a urologist if:
- There are high-risk features (visible haematuria, recurrent UTI with haematuria over age 40, persistent microscopic haematuria over age 40) or an abdominal mass
- PSA or DRE findings suggest high suspicion of prostate cancer
- There is a known neurological cause for the symptoms (such as multiple sclerosis, spinal injury, Parkinson's disease, or stroke)
- Medical therapy has failed or is not tolerated
- Complications are present, including suspected chronic urinary retention, elevated creatinine, recurrent UTIs, incontinence unresponsive to treatment, hydronephrosis, bladder stones, or persistent haematuria
