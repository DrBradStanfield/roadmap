# Acute Respiratory Illness in Children Under 2 Years

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway helps identify the most likely cause of acute respiratory illness in children under 2 years and guides assessment and management.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately if the child has:

- Suspected epiglottitis
- Cyanosis (bluish discolouration of lips or skin)
- Age under 3 months
- Existing co-morbidity (e.g., respiratory or cardiac condition, born before 32 weeks gestation)
- History of apnoea (stopping breathing)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Length and type of symptoms, including the type of cough (e.g., spasmodic, harsh barking, or associated with stridor)
- Feeding, number of wet nappies, and alertness
- Personal risk factors:
  - Premature birth
  - Poor nutrition
  - Breastfed for less than 2 months
  - Underlying conditions (e.g., chronic lung disease, cystic fibrosis, primary immunodeficiency, chronic aspiration, or airway malformation)
- Environmental risk factors:
  - Tobacco smoke exposure
  - Cold, damp, or overcrowded housing
  - Not immunised
- Social risk factors (transport, phone access, housing stability, ability to attend follow-up)

Physical examination typically includes:

- General appearance, alertness, and hydration
- Vital signs (fever, heart rate, respiratory rate, oxygen saturation — particularly if below 93% on room air)
- Signs of significant underlying pathology:
  - Cyanosis or apnoea
  - Absent breath sounds or dull percussion note (suggests pleural effusion)
  - Added sounds: stridor, wheeze, crackles, crepitation
  - Reduced air entry
  - Respiratory rate above 60 per minute
  - Increased respiratory effort: chest wall indrawing, retractions, grunting, nasal flaring

### Normal Vital Sign Ranges in Children

| Age | Approx. weight (kg) | Heart rate (bpm) | Respiratory rate (breaths/min) |
|-----|---------------------|------------------|-------------------------------|
| Term newborn | 3.5 | 120–170 | 25–60 |
| 3 months | 6 | 115–170 | 25–60 |

### Severity Assessment

Your doctor will assess severity across several areas:

| | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|-|------|----------|--------|
| Behaviour | Normal | Some/intermittent irritability | Increasing irritability, lethargy, or fatigue |
| Respiratory rate | Normal to mildly elevated | Increased | Markedly increased or decreased |
| Accessory muscle use | None to mild chest wall retraction | Moderate retractions, tracheal tug, nasal flaring | Marked retractions, tracheal tug, nasal flaring |
| Oxygen saturation (room air) | >92% | 90–92% | <90% (may not correct with oxygen) |
| Apnoea | None | Brief episodes possible | Frequent or prolonged episodes |
| Feeding | Normal | Difficulty or reduced feeding | Reluctant or unable to feed |

*Reproduced with permission from Australasian Bronchiolitis Guideline, Journal of Paediatrics and Child Health.*

## Possible Causes

### Epiglottitis

A potentially life-threatening bacterial infection (most commonly *Haemophilus influenzae*) causing swelling of the epiglottis. Rapid onset over hours. Distinguished from croup by:

| Feature | Croup | Epiglottitis |
|---------|-------|--------------|
| Onset | Days | Hours |
| Fever | Moderate | High |
| Cough | Prominent | Absent |
| Drooling | Mild | Prominent |
| Activity | Upset | Lethargic |
| Stridor | Inspiratory, high-pitched | Soft, expiratory snore |

**If epiglottitis is suspected:** Keep the child calm, do not perform any interventions or investigations. Call an ambulance immediately and phone the emergency department to advise the child is on the way.

### Croup

Harsh barking cough, with or without stridor or respiratory difficulty. Slower onset than epiglottitis, usually with a viral prodrome.

### Pertussis (Whooping Cough)

Spasmodic cough episodes, sometimes followed by an inspiratory whoop. Your doctor may consider this diagnosis if the cough pattern is paroxysmal.

### Bacterial Pneumonia

Unusually rapid breathing with crackles on examination and fever above 39°C.

### Viral Bronchiolitis

Respiratory distress with wheeze, typically in infants under 12 months, often caused by RSV. Most common cause of wheeze in this age group.

### Asthma

Recurrent wheeze with respiratory distress. Less common under 2 years but possible, particularly with a personal or family history of atopy.

### Infrequent Preschool Wheeze

- 80% of infants who start wheezing in the first 2 years of life do not go on to develop asthma.
- Acute wheezing episodes triggered by viral upper respiratory tract infections are common in infants with naturally smaller airways.
- These infants often appear well between episodes and do not have increased atopy.
- Wheezing often resolves by age 3–5 years.
- Exacerbating factors include environmental tobacco smoke, damp housing, and inadequate heating.

### Acute Viral Upper Respiratory Tract Infection (URTI)

If there are no concerning features or red flags, simple viral URTI is the most likely diagnosis. Cough and cold medicines have no proven benefit in children and carry safety risks. Simple home remedies may help:

- Water or honey sips (honey is safe for children 12 months and older only — do not give honey to infants under 12 months)
- **Plunket's cough mixture** (for children aged 12 months to 6 years with mild sore throat or cough, no signs of chest infection or breathing difficulty):
  - Fresh orange or lemon juice — 2 tablespoons
  - Honey — 2 tablespoons
  - Glycerine — 1 tablespoon
  - Olive oil — 1 teaspoon
  - Mix together; give half to 1 teaspoon, 2–3 times daily. Store in an airtight container in the fridge. Omit honey for children under 12 months.

## Management

Your doctor may consider:

- **Any red flag or severely unwell child:** Emergency assessment. Suspected epiglottitis requires an ambulance immediately.
- **Harsh barking cough (croup suspected):** Assessment and treatment for croup; rule out epiglottitis and foreign body inhalation.
- **Spasmodic cough:** Evaluation for pertussis.
- **Rapid breathing, crackles, high fever:** Evaluation and treatment for bacterial pneumonia.
- **Wheeze with respiratory distress:** Assessment for bronchiolitis, asthma, or preschool wheeze.
- **No concerning features:** Supportive management for viral URTI.

For all presentations, your doctor may also consider:

- Annual influenza vaccination (fully funded until age 5)
- Regular follow-up if social risk factors are present and the child frequently presents with respiratory illness
- Smoking cessation advice for all household members
- Checking eligibility for the Healthy Homes Initiative (housing support)
- Frequent hand washing by all family members during respiratory viral season
- Alternatives to childcare centres for very young children with severe or recurrent illness

If the diagnosis is unclear, or there is uncertainty about whether hospital admission is needed — especially in a very unwell or very young child — your doctor will seek specialist paediatric advice.
