# Post-operative Care of Cardiac Patients

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the most common complications and management issues presenting to general practice following cardiac surgery.

---

## Background

Patients are typically discharged 5 to 7 days after cardiothoracic surgery. They are advised to see their general practitioner 2 to 3 days after discharge, then as required.

Carer support for the first 14 days after discharge is recommended — ideally someone staying overnight and available by phone throughout.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will review you initially 2 to 3 days after discharge, then as required.

The review typically covers:

- Physical and psychological wellbeing
- Symptoms since discharge
- Mobilisation progress
- Medications
- Wounds and sternal healing
- Cardiovascular and chest examination

Bloods and an ECG may be arranged where appropriate (for example, a full blood count if anaemia is suspected, or an ECG if atrial fibrillation is suspected).

### Common post-operative issues your doctor will look for

**Pleural effusions and lung complications**

Postoperative pleural effusions are common (30–40% of patients) and can occur up to 6 weeks after surgery. They are more common on the left side. Your doctor will check for:

- Increased shortness of breath, cough, or new chest pain (especially if worse on breathing in)
- Signs on chest examination: dullness at the base, asymmetrical chest expansion, decreased breath sounds, pleural rub

Lung infections can also occur, particularly at the left lung base.

**Atrial fibrillation (AF)**

New AF occurs in up to 40% of cardiac surgery patients, usually within 3 days of the operation. Your doctor will ask about symptoms and check for this.

**Weight**

Patients should lose 4 to 5 kg in the post-operative period. Weight gain is abnormal and usually indicates fluid retention. Your doctor may recommend daily weighing and asking you to report any weight gain.

**Vision**

Temporary visual changes (blurred vision or flickering) are common after cardiac surgery. The cause is not fully understood.

**Cognitive function**

Neurocognitive dysfunction — ranging from mild difficulty concentrating to short-term memory loss — is common after cardiac surgery.

## Management

### Wound and sternal healing

Your doctor will check your wounds and may:

- Remove dressings if wounds are healing well with no discharge
- Remove sutures from the skin, drain sites, and wound ends as appropriate

**Wound care advice:**

- Shower daily to wash and dry wounds — soap and gentle rubbing are fine
- Report any increased redness, discharge, or pain (with or without fever) — these may indicate infection

**When to seek further assessment:**

- If a deeper knot or wire is causing lumps, granulomas, or sinuses — your doctor will refer for specialist review
- If sternal clicking or mobility persists beyond 3 months — this may indicate non-union and warrants specialist review

### Mobilisation

Following a graded mobilisation programme from the day of discharge helps prevent cardiovascular and respiratory complications.

If you are not reaching mobilisation targets, your doctor will assess for underlying causes including:

- Inadequate pain relief
- Infection
- Shortness of breath
- Atrial fibrillation
- Pleural or pericardial collections
- Depression

### Pleural effusions and lung complications

**Pleural effusions:**

- Small effusions with mild symptoms may be managed with a short course of furosemide (a fluid tablet) and encouraged mobilisation, confirmed with a chest X-ray
- Larger effusions or moderate to severe symptoms require specialist cardiothoracic assessment, as drainage may be needed

**Lung complications:**

- General mobilisation and any physiotherapy exercises from hospital help prevent lung infections
- A productive cough without fever may be treated with oral antibiotics
- Fever or systemic symptoms require cardiothoracic surgery advice

### Anticoagulation

**Mechanical heart valves** require both warfarin and aspirin:

- Warfarin is dosed to a target INR based on valve position and individual risk:

| Patient risk | Mechanical aortic valve (target INR) | Mechanical mitral valve (target INR) |
|---|---|---|
| No other thrombotic risk factors | 2.5 | 3.0 |
| Other thrombotic risk factors present | 3.0 | 3.0 |

- Aspirin 75–100 mg per day (if tolerated), dose determined by the specialist before discharge

If INR is subtherapeutic in a patient with a mechanical valve, your doctor may seek cardiology advice about bridging anticoagulation.

**Tissue valves and bypass grafts (CABG):** anticoagulation may be needed for other reasons (e.g., atrial fibrillation).

### Postoperative atrial fibrillation (AF)

New AF occurs in up to 40% of cardiac surgery patients, usually within 3 days. It is typically treated in hospital with amiodarone, which returns 80–90% of patients to normal rhythm.

- Amiodarone is continued at a maintenance dose for about 2 weeks after discharge in patients who leave hospital in normal rhythm; it may be continued longer for resistant AF until cardiology review at 6 weeks
- Beta-blockers are commonly prescribed for rate control as long-term medications; digoxin and calcium channel blockers are second-line
- Anticoagulation is usually started for patients who remain in AF
- Warfarin is preferred for the first 6 weeks post-surgery (easier to reverse if complications arise); switching to newer anticoagulants can be considered after 6 weeks if long-term anticoagulation is needed

If AF is new onset or difficult to control, your doctor will seek cardiology advice.

### Neurocognitive and visual disturbances

These are common after cardiac surgery and generally resolve without specific treatment.

- Neurocognitive dysfunction typically resolves over 1 to 2 months (though this is variable)
- Visual disturbance usually settles within days, though it may take up to 2 weeks
- You should not change your glasses in the first 3 months

**Your doctor will arrange ophthalmology review if:**

- Loss of vision
- Visual field defect
- Visual disturbances are not improving 1 week after discharge

### Anaemia

It is common to be discharged with a low haemoglobin that was tolerated during the admission. Blood transfusion around the time of surgery is associated with increased risk, so transfusion is generally reserved for significant bleeding or highly symptomatic anaemia.

Your doctor will consider specialist advice if haemoglobin falls below 75 g/L.

### Shortness of breath

Some shortness of breath on exertion is normal in the early post-operative period.

Shortness of breath at rest is not normal and may indicate:

- Pleural effusion
- Lung infection
- Congestive heart failure
- Pulmonary embolism

Your doctor will assess and investigate accordingly.

### Medication changes

After cardiac surgery, some pre-operative medications may have been changed or stopped. Your doctor will:

- Reconcile your medication list
- Monitor blood pressure and reintroduce blood pressure medications when appropriate (blood pressure is often lower post-operatively, and may not return to normal for 2 to 3 weeks)
- Review medications that may need adjustment, such as insulin, blood pressure tablets, and calcium channel blockers
- Review short-term post-operative medications including diuretics, potassium supplements, gastrointestinal protection, laxatives, and amiodarone

### Driving and flying

**Driving:** You must not drive for at least 4 weeks after cardiac surgery. The stand-down period is longer if you have sternal wound problems, had a stroke, or have ongoing memory or visual impairment. If you plan to return to passenger transport or heavy vehicle driving, a specialist cardiology assessment is required. Your doctor can advise on NZTA fitness-to-drive requirements.

**Flying:** If you have a flight booked shortly after surgery, contact the specific airline for their guidance.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Help

Contact your doctor or seek emergency care if you experience:

- Chest pain, especially if severe or at rest
- Significant shortness of breath at rest
- Fever or signs of wound infection (increased redness, discharge, or pain)
- Palpitations or a fast, irregular heart rate
- Sudden changes in vision or neurological symptoms

These may indicate complications requiring urgent assessment.

*Always discuss your recovery, symptoms, and any medications with your doctor. Do not adjust anticoagulation or other post-operative medications without medical advice.*
