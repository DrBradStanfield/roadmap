# COPD Diagnosis and Initial Management

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

COPD (chronic obstructive pulmonary disease) is a common, treatable lung condition where airflow becomes progressively blocked and cannot be fully reversed. This page explains how your doctor diagnoses COPD and the evidence-based approach to managing it.

> **Note (December 2025):** Asthma + Respiratory Foundation NZ released updated 2025 New Zealand COPD Guidelines. COPD pathways are currently under review in line with these guidelines.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if you have:

- Blue lips or fingertips (cyanosis)
- Sudden or severe worsening of breathlessness
- Confusion or drowsiness

---

## Who Is at Risk of COPD?

Your doctor will consider COPD if you are over 40 and have any of the following:

- A history of smoking (usually more than 10 pack years) or prolonged exposure to other lung irritants (e.g. occupational dust or gas)
- Persistent or recurring cough, shortness of breath, wheeze, or sputum production

**Note for Māori and Pacific peoples:** Rates of hospitalisation from COPD are higher in Māori and Pacific populations. Your doctor will aim to diagnose and treat COPD early, address risk factors, and arrange follow-up that works for you.

---

## How COPD Is Diagnosed

### Symptoms Your Doctor Will Ask About

**Cardinal symptoms:**
- Shortness of breath — early on, this may only occur during exertion or with chest infections
- Persistent or recurring cough
- Sputum (phlegm) production

**Other symptoms:**
- Wheeze, chest tightness
- Tiredness or fatigue
- Poor appetite and weight loss (in advanced stages)
- Drowsiness or confusion (in severe exacerbations)

Symptoms typically have an insidious (gradual) onset and are often worse in the mornings.

### History Your Doctor Will Gather

- Smoking history (pack years)
- Occupational or environmental exposure to fumes, dust, or passive smoke
- Recurrent chest infections (including in childhood)
- History of allergies, asthma, or atopy
- Previous hospitalisations for respiratory problems
- Other smoking-related diseases
- Family history of COPD or alpha-1 antitrypsin (A1AT) deficiency
- Current medications and other medical conditions
- Social and family support

### Physical Examination

Physical signs may be absent in early COPD. Your doctor will check:

- Vital signs: pulse, blood pressure, and oxygen saturation
- Height, weight, and BMI
- Signs of COPD: chest hyperinflation, use of accessory muscles, pursed lip breathing, cyanosis
- Signs of related conditions: heart failure, cor pulmonale

### Breathlessness Grading — MMRC Scale

Your doctor may grade your breathlessness using the Modified Medical Research Council (MMRC) scale:

| Grade | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 0 | Only breathless with strenuous exercise |
| 1 | Short of breath when hurrying on level ground or walking up a slight hill |
| 2 | Walk slower than others on level ground because of breathlessness, or must stop to catch breath at own pace |
| 3 | Must stop for breath after walking about 100 metres or after a few minutes on level ground |
| 4 | Too breathless to leave the house, or breathless when dressing |

### Spirometry — The Key Diagnostic Test

COPD is confirmed by spirometry with bronchodilator testing. Your doctor will look for:

- **FEV1/FVC ratio below 0.7** (after bronchodilator), AND
- **FEV1 below 80% predicted**

This shows airflow obstruction that is not fully reversible — the hallmark of COPD. Up to 50% of lung function can be lost before symptoms appear.

Your doctor will also consider whether you have features of **asthma-COPD overlap (ACO)** — see the separate Differentiating Asthma from COPD pathway.

### COPD Severity

| Severity | Spirometry (FEV1) | Typical Symptoms |
|----------|------------------|-----------------|
| Mild | 60–80% predicted | Few symptoms; breathlessness on moderate exertion; recurrent chest infections; little impact on daily life |
| Moderate | 40–59% predicted | Increasing breathlessness; limited on level walking; cough and sputum production |
| Severe | Less than 40% predicted | Breathless on minimal exertion; daily activities significantly curtailed; chronic cough and sputum |

### Other Investigations

**Blood tests** your doctor may arrange:
- Full blood count (FBC) — particularly eosinophil levels, which influence inhaler choice
- Electrolytes, kidney function, blood glucose, iron studies, thyroid function (TSH)
- D-dimer (with Wells' criteria) if pulmonary embolism is being considered
- **NT-proBNP** if heart failure is suspected:
  - Less than 35 pmol/L makes heart failure unlikely
  - Higher levels (age-adjusted thresholds) prompt further assessment for heart failure

**Imaging** — not required to diagnose COPD, but may be arranged to assess for:
- Complications (e.g. pneumonia, pneumothorax)
- Alternative diagnoses (e.g. lung cancer)
- A normal chest X-ray does not exclude COPD

**ECG** — to assess for co-existing cardiac disease.

## Management

### When to Seek Urgent Assessment

Your doctor will arrange urgent hospital assessment if you have:
- Rapid deterioration or severe breathlessness
- Cyanosis (blue lips/fingertips)
- Poor response to treatment
- Acute confusion or drowsiness
- Exacerbation complicated by heart failure or other decompensating conditions

If your symptoms worsen acutely beyond your usual day-to-day variation (breathlessness, cough, wheeze, sputum), see the separate **Acute Exacerbation of COPD** pathway.

---

### Lifestyle Advice

#### Smoking Cessation
The single most important intervention to slow COPD progression. Your doctor will raise this at every visit.

#### Physical Activity
Aim for at least 30 minutes of activity 5 days per week. A practical approach: walk until too breathless to continue, rest briefly, then resume.

#### Nutrition
- If underweight: smaller, more frequent high-calorie meals; simple food fortification (e.g. adding milk powder)
- If overweight: weight reduction advice
- Dietitian referral if there are ongoing nutritional concerns

#### Home Environment
A warm, dry, smoke-free home can improve COPD symptoms.

#### Occupational Factors
Reduce ongoing exposure to dust, fumes, or second-hand smoke where possible.

---

### Breathing and Symptom Strategies

- **Breathing exercises:** Diaphragmatic breathing and pursed-lip breathing can improve the disordered breathing pattern caused by COPD
- **Hand-held fan therapy:** Can reduce the sensation of breathlessness
- **Sputum management:** Patients with chronic sputum production may benefit from airway clearance techniques

---

### Vaccinations

For all patients with COPD:
- Annual influenza vaccination
- Pneumococcal vaccination (discuss timing with your doctor)
- COVID-19 vaccination

---

### Pulmonary Rehabilitation

Pulmonary rehabilitation is recommended for all patients with COPD. It reduces breathlessness and improves quality of life. Your doctor can refer you for an assessment.

---

### Mental Health and Other Co-existing Issues

Untreated anxiety and depression are common in COPD and significantly worsen outcomes. Treatment of these conditions improves quality of life. Your doctor will also consider:
- Cognitive impairment
- Alcohol dependence

---

### Inhaler Therapy — Step-by-Step

Your doctor will prescribe inhalers based on your severity and response to treatment. Correct inhaler technique is essential before escalating therapy.

**If you have asthma-COPD overlap (ACO) or an eosinophilic pattern** (high eosinophil count on blood test):
- Start with a **combined ICS/LABA inhaler** (inhaled corticosteroid + long-acting beta-2 agonist)
- Add reliever inhalers (SABA/SAMA) for short-term breathlessness relief
- A long-acting muscarinic antagonist (LAMA) may be added if breathlessness continues
- Do not stop inhaled steroids in ACO without specialist advice

**Standard COPD stepwise therapy:**

1. **As-needed reliever:** Short-acting beta2-agonist (SABA, e.g. salbutamol) and/or short-acting muscarinic antagonist (SAMA, e.g. ipratropium) for quick relief
2. **Add regular LAMA** (e.g. tiotropium, glycopyrronium, or umeclidinium) if breathlessness persists — do not continue SAMA alongside a LAMA (except in emergencies)
3. **If LAMA is not suitable:** Add a long-acting beta-2 agonist (LABA, e.g. salmeterol, formoterol, or indacaterol)
4. **If still breathless on single long-acting bronchodilator:** Dual **LAMA + LABA** combination
5. **If frequent or severe exacerbations:** Consider escalating directly to dual or triple therapy
6. **Triple therapy (LABA + LAMA + ICS):** For those who continue to have two or more exacerbations per year despite dual therapy and optimal inhaler technique

Your doctor will reassess symptoms 6 weeks after any medication change and optimise your inhaler technique at every review. An action plan will be developed with you and your family or carers.

---

### Complications and Co-existing Conditions

Your doctor will monitor for and manage related conditions:

**Complications of COPD:**
- Pulmonary hypertension and cor pulmonale (right heart strain)
- Secondary polycythaemia (thickened blood from chronic low oxygen)
- Pneumothorax (collapsed lung)
- Respiratory failure
- Osteoporosis

**Common co-existing conditions:**
- Obstructive sleep apnoea (OSA)
- Heart failure
- Atrial fibrillation (AF)
- Diabetes
- Anaemia
- Chronic kidney disease

---

### Home Oxygen

If your resting oxygen saturation is below 90% on more than one occasion when you are stable, your doctor will consider referring you for a home oxygen assessment. See also the **Advanced or End-Stage COPD** pathway.

---

### Follow-up

After diagnosis, your doctor will arrange follow-up to:
- Review your medications and adjust as needed
- Check and optimise your inhaler technique
- Provide education and develop a complete management plan (this often takes multiple visits)

---

## When Your Doctor May Refer You to a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you to a respiratory specialist (non-urgently) if you have moderate or severe COPD (FEV1 less than 59% predicted) and any of the following:
- You are under 60 years old
- Your spirometry shows a restrictive pattern (as well as obstruction)
- You have significant co-existing conditions or frequent exacerbations

For all patients: a **pulmonary rehabilitation** assessment is recommended.

---

## What to Discuss with Your Doctor

- What is my COPD severity based on my spirometry?
- Do I have any features of asthma-COPD overlap?
- What inhaler(s) am I starting and how do I use them correctly?
- Have I had my influenza, pneumococcal, and COVID-19 vaccinations?
- Can I be referred for pulmonary rehabilitation?
- Are there any co-existing conditions (heart failure, sleep apnoea, depression) that need treatment?
- What is my oxygen saturation, and do I need a home oxygen assessment?
- What should I do if my symptoms suddenly worsen?

Your doctor will tailor management to your individual circumstances. No single test or finding makes the diagnosis in isolation — your full history, examination, and spirometry are all considered together.
