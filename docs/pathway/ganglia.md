# Ganglia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of ganglia — fluid-filled cysts arising from joints or tendons.

---

## Background

Ganglia are small cysts containing a thick jelly-like material (synovial fluid):

- Usually attached to a joint or tendon
- Most commonly found on the dorsal or volar wrist, the DIP joints of the fingers (mucous cysts), the digital flexor tendon sheaths, and the top of the foot near small joints
- Generally harmless but may cause pressure symptoms
- Mostly slow-growing and minimally tender
- May resolve spontaneously but commonly recur
- Occur mostly in middle-aged women

The exact cause is unclear, but ganglia may be associated with repetitive movements, injuries, arthritis, and synovitis.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- How long it has been present
- Location (single versus multiple)
- Associated symptoms (e.g., pain, muscle weakness)
- Aggravating factors
- Effect on daily life

Physical examination typically includes:

- Checking for signs of infection
- Size and consistency — ganglia are generally uniformly rounded, firm, and rubbery
- Pain with movement or pressure
- Whether the lump is mobile or fixed to deep tissue
- Transillumination to differentiate a cyst from a solid lump (a cyst glows red when a light is pressed against it in a dark room; a solid lump does not)

### Differential Diagnosis

Your doctor will consider other possible causes, including:

- Lipoma
- Haematoma
- Abscess, rheumatoid nodules, or tophus
- Reactive lymphadenopathy
- Epidermoid cysts of the skin
- Sarcoma or other malignancy (very rare)
- Accessory muscles

### Concerning Features

Your doctor will check for features that require further investigation:

- Hard or irregular consistency
- Painful
- Rapidly enlarging
- Firmly attached to underlying structures
- Reduced joint range of movement
- Distal neurological deficit (weakness or altered sensation beyond the lump)

Asymptomatic ganglia without concerning features do not require further investigation.

If concerning features are present, or the diagnosis is uncertain, your doctor may arrange:

- A plain X-ray
- An ultrasound (to clarify whether the lesion is a benign cyst)

## Management

### Recurrence Rates

Your doctor will explain that ganglia commonly recur after treatment:

- ~90% recur after forcible rupture (the "bible bump" technique — not recommended)
- ~50% recur after aspiration
- ~30% recur after surgery
- ~12.5% recur after suture compression technique (small studies)

### Conservative Options

Your doctor may consider:

- **Watch and wait** — Up to 50% of ganglia resolve spontaneously within 6 years. Asymptomatic ganglia are generally best left alone.
- **Activity modification** — Avoiding weight-bearing and loading the wrist in end-range positions. A splint or soft wrist support may help with pain.
- **Forcible rupture is not recommended** due to the very high recurrence rate.
- **Needle aspiration** — Your doctor may insert a needle into the cyst to drain the fluid, then review in 7 days. If still present, aspiration may be repeated. Volar (palmar) ganglia are not aspirated without ultrasound guidance due to risk of neurovascular injury. Steroid injection is not used alongside aspiration — there is no added benefit and it carries risk of fat atrophy and skin pigmentation changes.

### Surgical Options

If the ganglion is painful, pressing on significant adjacent structures, or the diagnosis is unclear, your doctor may refer you to a specialist for assessment and possible surgery.

Benefits of surgery:

- Histological diagnosis can be confirmed
- Complex cysts can be treated

Disadvantages of surgery:

- Relatively high recurrence rates (~30%)
- Risk of scarring and other complications
- A postoperative splint is required for at least 3 weeks, which may delay return to work and other activities

Note: public hospitals do not accept referrals for ganglion removal for purely cosmetic reasons.
