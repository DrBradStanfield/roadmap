# Breast Engorgement and Oversupply

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of breast engorgement and oversupply (hyperlactation) in breastfeeding mothers.

---

## Background

- The feeling of breast fullness as milk comes in is **normal and physiological**.
- Engorgement is typically seen between days 3 and 5 after starting breastfeeding, and is less common in mothers whose infants feed more frequently in the first 48 hours.
- It is usually diffuse and bilateral.
- **Primary engorgement** occurs in the early days of lactation due to increased vascular flow, tissue oedema, and arrival of milk that may exceed the infant's ability to extract milk.
- **Secondary engorgement** occurs later when lactation is established, due to an imbalance between milk production and milk removal.
- Engorged breasts are hard, swollen, and tender — milk does not flow easily, and nipples may flatten, making attachment difficult.
- **Oversupply (hyperlactation)** is the production of breast milk beyond the volume required for healthy infant growth.
- Lack of interstitial oedema distinguishes oversupply from engorgement.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Care

See your doctor or midwife promptly if you develop signs of **mastitis or breast abscess**:

- Tender, hot, swollen wedge-shaped area of breast
- Temperature of 38°C or greater
- Systemically unwell: malaise, chills, or rigors
- Flu-like symptoms, headache, muscle and joint pain
- Painful swollen lymph nodes under the arm

---

## Assessment

Your doctor or midwife will consider predisposing factors, including:

**For engorgement:**
- Delayed start to breastfeeding
- Separation of mother and infant
- Large amounts of IV fluids during labour
- Poor attachment or latch
- Missing the infant's early feeding cues
- Scheduled (timed) feeding
- Feeds too short or infrequent
- Formula supplementation
- Infant illness affecting their ability to remove milk
- Excessive pumping
- Poorly fitted bra
- Inverted or flat nipples
- Breast implants or previous breast surgery

**For oversupply:**
- Naturally high rate of milk synthesis
- Excessive use of breast pump in addition to breastfeeding
- Iatrogenic hyperlactation (e.g., galactagogues, or advice to express in addition to direct breastfeeding without proper guidance)

### Signs and Symptoms in the Mother

**Engorgement:**
- Breasts swollen, distended, hard, and shiny due to tightly stretched skin
- Areola full and hard, making latching difficult
- Warmth and tenderness
- Breasts may throb
- Engorgement may extend into the armpit with painful lumps
- Blocked ducts or mastitis may be present

**Oversupply:**
- Persistent fullness; breasts do not feel comfortable or completely drained
- Breast or nipple pain
- Pain with first milk ejection (let-down)
- Forceful milk ejection
- Copious milk leakage during and between feeds
- Firm, tender, lumpy areas in the breast
- Blocked ducts or mastitis

### Signs and Symptoms in the Infant

**Engorgement:**
- Difficulty attaching at the breast
- Fussing at the breast
- Not satisfied after feeds
- Unsettled infant

**Oversupply:**
- Gagging or gulping, particularly at the start of a feed, or difficulty coping with milk flow
- Short, frequent feeds
- Fussiness at the breast; baby refusing the second breast
- Frequent heavy, wet nappies
- Frequent loose stools (often green and frothy, possibly explosive)
- Possetting and/or vomiting after feeds
- Extremely unsettled
- Minimal weight loss after birth followed by large weight gains

### Milk Flow Assessment

Your doctor or midwife may ask:

**Engorgement:**
- Is let-down difficult to elicit?
- Is milk flow restricted?
- Does the breast drain adequately and soften after feeds?
- Does the baby have a suck-and-swallow rhythm and seem satisfied?

**Oversupply:**
- Are the breasts persistently full?
- Do they not feel comfortable or drained?
- Is there pain with the first let-down?
- Is there forceful milk ejection?
- Do the breasts quickly refill?
- Is there copious leakage between feeds?

## Management

Your doctor or midwife may recommend the following:

### 1. Empathy and Reassurance

Normal fullness (breasts feel full, heavy, slightly tender) is part of establishing lactation and resolves on its own within a few days — it is distinct from engorgement (hard, red, shiny, painful breasts where milk does not flow).

### 2. Manage Predisposing Factors

- Offer frequent and unrestricted breastfeeds
- Avoid over-expressing breastmilk
- Ensure bras are well-fitting and supportive; remove restrictive clothing before breastfeeding
- Ensure correct [position and attachment](https://www.kidshealth.org.nz/good-latch-key-successful-breastfeeding)
- Try changing positions to allow good drainage
- Consider nipple shields if inverted or flat nipples (seek lactation consultant advice)

### 3. Feeding Technique Advice

**For babies under 4 weeks:**
- If breasts are full and areola firm, try [reverse pressure softening](https://www.breastfeeding.asn.au/resources/engorgement) first (5–10 minutes), then hand-express to soften the areola before latching if needed
- The baby may need breaks during the feed and to be positioned upright, especially if milk is fast-flowing
- Encourage the baby to drain the first breast well before offering the second
- Feed according to infant cues — aim for no longer than 3 hours between feeds, but feed sooner if the baby is hungry
- Avoid unnecessary expressing (stimulates more supply)
- Feed in an upright, laid-back, or straddle position
- Do not force-feed
- If the baby detaches before let-down, allow milk flow to settle before re-offering

**For babies over 4 weeks:** Similar advice applies — hand-express to soften areola if needed before latching, optimise positioning and attachment, and follow the same feeding frequency guidance.

### 4. Managing Breast Discomfort

**Non-pharmacological:**
- Apply cold or ice compresses for up to 20 minutes before and between breastfeeds (while reclined or lying down)
- A clean disposable nappy filled with water and frozen works as a reusable cold pack
- After cold compresses, use [lymphatic massage techniques](https://eadn-wc01-5994650.nxedge.io/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/ScreenShot2021-07-21at8.55.04PM-1024x769.png) for a few minutes
- Avoid heat, as this may increase inflammation

**Pharmacological:**
- Anti-inflammatory medications (e.g., ibuprofen) to alleviate discomfort

### 5. Reverse Pressure Softening

If engorgement is not responding to the above, your doctor or midwife may demonstrate **reverse pressure softening** — a technique to push fluid in the areola tissue backwards into the breast, making it easier to latch:

- Apply pressure with two or three fingers of each hand placed flat at the sides of and close to the nipple, and hold for 1–3 minutes
- Alternatively, use all fingertips of one hand around the nipple and push in, holding for 1–3 minutes, until the tissue softens
- [Patient information leaflet](https://www.healthunit.com/uploads/reverse-pressure-softening-nov-2018.pdf)

### 6. Recognise Mastitis

Your doctor or midwife will advise you to watch for signs of mastitis (wedge-shaped hard, hot, red area; fever 38°C+; flu-like symptoms) and seek assessment promptly if these develop.

### 7. Lactation Consultant Referral

Your doctor or midwife may refer you to a lactation consultant if:
- Nipple shields have been recommended
- You are having difficulty latching your baby
- Symptoms do not settle with the above measures

### Special Situation: Recent Perinatal Loss

Women who have experienced a perinatal loss are still at risk of breast engorgement and mastitis — colostrum is produced from around 16–18 weeks' gestation, and it may take 2–3 weeks for milk production to cease after a loss.

Your doctor or midwife will discuss all options, which may include:
- **Lactation suppression:** firm bra day and night, cold packs, minimal expressing (only to relieve discomfort), anti-inflammatories. **Cabergoline** is only effective if used within 24 hours of the birth.
- Continued expression
- Milk donation — see [Donated Breast Milk](https://info.health.nz/pregnancy-maternity/breastfeeding/donated-breast-milk)
- Using milk for other purposes (e.g., memory-making)
