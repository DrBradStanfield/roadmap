# Benzodiazepine and Zopiclone Withdrawal

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers managing withdrawal from benzodiazepines or zopiclone in general practice. It applies to patients who have developed dependence as a result of being prescribed these medications.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance immediately if the patient has:

- Withdrawal seizures

Also seek urgent assessment if the patient is acutely psychotic, agitated, confused, hallucinating, or at risk of self-harm or harm to others.

---

## Background

There are two main types of benzodiazepine or zopiclone use seen in general practice:

- **Low-dose use** (daily dose 30 mg diazepam equivalent or less) — Most common. Results from chronic use of prescribed medications, seen most often in women and older people. May be complicated by intermittent high-dose binges. These patients do not usually exhibit features of physical dependence or addiction.
- **High-dose use** (daily dose greater than 30 mg diazepam equivalent) — Usually occurs with features of dependence and addiction, often in association with polysubstance use and other co-morbidities. Patients may be obtaining medication from a variety of sources, both licit and illicit.

Some patients may not be physically dependent but have binges.

**High-dose use and novel benzodiazepines should not be managed in primary care without specialist addiction service (CADS) advice.**

---

## Assessment

### 1. Risk Factors for Physical Dependence

Consider whether the patient has:

- Alcohol use disorder or other substance use disorder
- Anxiety
- Chronic pain
- Depression
- Personality disorder

### 2. History of Use

Your doctor will ask about current and past benzodiazepine or zopiclone use:

- Age of initiation and reason for starting
- Route of administration (oral or injected)
- Types of benzodiazepine used (prescribed or non-prescribed, including novel benzodiazepines)
- Current use: total dose, frequency, timing of last dose
- Periods of abstinence and reasons for any relapse
- Previous interventions and treatments tried
- Problems related to use (e.g., personal injury, unemployment, car accidents)

**Note on novel benzodiazepines:** These are new psychoactive substances designed to mimic prescribed benzodiazepines but have not been tested or approved as prescription medications. They are often sold illicitly, can have higher potency and unknown half-lives, and carry an increased risk of overdose. Examples include flualprazolam, bromazolam, and etizolam. No known dose equivalents exist for novel benzodiazepines.

### 3. Signs of Dependence or Addiction

**Dependence** refers to physical symptoms of withdrawal and tolerance. Patients may report that they:

- Feel they cannot cope without the medication
- Experience anxiety, insomnia, or feel unwell after reducing the dose
- Feel the medication has less effect over time (tolerance)
- Take an extra dose during stressful times
- Have increased their dose
- Make sure they always have access to the medication

**Addiction** is defined as a problematic pattern of use with clinically significant impairment or distress, and at least 2 of the following in the past year:

- Using larger amounts or over a longer period than intended
- Using multiple sources (e.g., different doctors) to maintain supply
- Persistent desire to cut down
- A great deal of time spent obtaining or recovering from use
- Strong desire or craving to use
- Failure to fulfil major work, home, or educational roles
- Continued use despite interpersonal problems
- Important activities given up or reduced
- Continued use despite a persistent physical or psychological problem caused or worsened by use

It is possible to have physical dependence without being addicted.

### 4. Withdrawal Symptoms

Symptoms of withdrawal may be vague and can include:

- Delirium, confusion, hallucinations (auditory, visual, tactile)
- Seizures
- Anxiety, insomnia
- Sensory disturbances, paraesthesia
- Palpitations, hyperventilation
- Tremor, headache
- Irritable bowel
- Excitability, poor memory and concentration, feelings of unreality

**Onset and duration:** Withdrawal symptoms may occur after as little as 6 weeks of use. Onset depends on the half-life of the benzodiazepine used — typically 1 to 2 days for short-acting benzodiazepines and zopiclone, and 3 to 14 days for long-acting benzodiazepines. Symptoms may not peak for up to a month after cessation and can continue for several months.

| Length of action | Half-life | Drug |
|---|---|---|
| Very short | < 6 hours | Midazolam* |
| Short | 6 to 12 hours | Alprazolam*, oxazepam, temazepam*, triazolam, zopiclone |
| Medium | 12 to 24 hours | Bromazepam, lorazepam |
| Long | > 24 hours | Clobazam, clonazepam, diazepam*, flunitrazepam*, nitrazepam |

\* = rapid onset (< 1 hour after oral administration)

### 5. Overdose History

Your doctor will ask whether the patient has a history of:

- Benzodiazepine or zopiclone overdose
- Poor short-term memory, confusion, delirium, paradoxical agitation, sedation, slurred speech, or ataxia
- Presentations to the emergency department due to benzodiazepine or zopiclone use
- Intentional overdose

### 6. Psychosocial Issues

- Mental health history and treatment
- Comorbid physical or sexual health issues
- Living arrangements (especially if aged under 18)
- Relationships and support networks
- Legal issues
- Triggers and barriers that may influence withdrawal management

### 7. Alcohol and Other Drug Use

Assess alcohol use. Consider using the ASSIST-LITE screening tool for alcohol, tobacco, cannabis, opioid, and other drug use.

Ask specifically about **previous withdrawal seizures.**

### 8. Physical and Mental State Examination

Physical examination includes:

- Pulse, blood pressure, temperature, and weight
- Signs of withdrawal (confusion, hallucinations, sweating, tremor, poor memory)
- Signs of acute intoxication (sedation, confusion, slurred speech, drooling, ataxia)
- Head injury assessment if the patient is incoherent, disorientated, or drowsy
- Consider whether underlying illness (e.g., concussion, subdural haematoma, infection, diabetes, electrolyte disturbance) may explain apparent intoxication

Mental state assessment checks for:

- Misperceptions, hallucinations, delusions
- Suspiciousness, paranoia, psychosis
- Insomnia
- Symptoms of depression and anxiety

### 9. Investigations

Your doctor may consider:

- LFT, FBC
- Screening for blood-borne viruses (hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV) if the patient has used benzodiazepines or zopiclone by non-oral routes

### 10. Equivalent Oral Dose (Diazepam Equivalents)

Your doctor will calculate the equivalent oral dose of diazepam to assess the total daily dose. Note: exact equivalents are difficult to establish due to widely varying half-lives and receptor-binding characteristics.

| Drug | Approximate equivalent to diazepam 5 mg |
|---|---|
| Alprazolam | 0.5 mg |
| Clonazepam | 0.25 to 0.5 mg |
| Diazepam | 5 mg |
| Flunitrazepam | 0.5 mg |
| Lorazepam | 0.5 mg |
| Nitrazepam | 5 mg |
| Oxazepam | 15 mg |
| Temazepam | 10 mg |
| Triazolam | 0.25 mg |
| Zopiclone | 7.5 mg |

### 11. Risk of Potentially Fatal Withdrawal

Risk is higher if the patient:

- Has been taking benzodiazepines or zopiclone for more than 4 weeks
- Has been taking high doses
- Has been using more than one benzodiazepine, or benzodiazepine plus zopiclone
- Has a history of seizures due to alcohol, previous withdrawal, or other causes

## Management

Your doctor will manage this based on the patient's motivation and clinical situation.

### Key Practice Point

High doses, longer use, and using more than one benzodiazepine (or benzodiazepine plus zopiclone) are associated with more prolonged and severe withdrawal, which can include seizures, delirium, and suicidality. **Gradual dose reduction is strongly recommended** to prevent prolonged, severe withdrawal.

### Patient Not Motivated to Change (Stable Low Prescribed Dose)

Your doctor may:

1. Educate about the risks of continued use (see below)
2. Arrange regular follow-up to monitor motivation and general health
3. Use motivational interviewing to encourage dose reduction over time

**Risks of continued benzodiazepine or zopiclone use:**
- Addiction and tolerance
- Falls (especially in older people)
- Interactions with other drugs or alcohol
- Personal problems (relationship breakdown, financial difficulties)
- Functional problems (memory impairment, insomnia)
- Mental health disorders (anxiety, depression, psychosis)

### Patient Motivated to Change (Stable Low Prescribed Dose)

#### Assessing Suitability for General Practice Withdrawal Management

**Suitable patients** have:
- A supportive home environment
- Engagement, motivation, and insight
- Co-operative attitude
- Minimal co-morbidities

**Unsuitable patients** include those who:
- Developed dependence to benzodiazepines obtained without a prescription
- Have a medical condition that may deteriorate with withdrawal (e.g., psychosis, brain injury, pre-existing epilepsy)
- Have a history of withdrawal seizures
- Have co-existing drug misuse problems

If unsuitable, specialist addiction service advice is required before proceeding.

If suitable, the patient must understand and accept that there will be:
- No dose increases
- No replacements for lost prescriptions
- Only one prescriber

#### What to Expect from Withdrawal

Your doctor will explain that:

- Some withdrawal symptoms are inevitable, particularly anxiety and insomnia
- Coming off benzodiazepines or zopiclone can cause withdrawal seizures, which can be fatal — gradual dose reduction significantly reduces this risk
- Withdrawal can be complex and lengthy. Unlike most other drug reductions, symptoms can be worse towards the end of the process
- It can take up to a year to be drug-free, and in very severe cases up to 2 years
- Best results happen when the patient has a sense of ownership over the rate of withdrawal

#### Non-Pharmacological Interventions

Your doctor may suggest:

- Ongoing support and consultation at your practice
- Counselling via an addiction support service
- Coping strategies for withdrawal, including:
  - Benzodiazepine withdrawal fact sheets ([Reconnexion](https://www.reconnexion.org.au/resources))
  - Online support communities (e.g., [BenzoBuddies](http://www.benzobuddies.org/))
  - Anxiety management
  - Sleep hygiene guidance
  - Stress management
  - Healthy lifestyle choices (relaxation, nutrition, rest, exercise)
  - Digital mental health tools (e.g., [Just a Thought](https://www.justathought.co.nz/), [Smiling Mind](https://www.smilingmind.com.au/))

#### Supervised Gradual Dose Reduction

Your doctor may carry out the following:

1. **Check for fatal withdrawal risk** before starting — if significant risk, specialist addiction service assessment is required first.

2. **Stabilise intake** before beginning any reduction.

3. **Transfer short-acting benzodiazepines or zopiclone to diazepam** using equivalent dose conversion. For example: 0.5 mg lorazepam three times a day → 5 mg diazepam three times a day. Review 3 to 4 days after transfer to check for sedation.

   *Diazepam is not suitable for patients aged over 65, those unable to tolerate it, or those with severe hepatic impairment — specialist advice is required in these cases.*

   Example transfer from short-acting benzodiazepine to diazepam (time between each step may be one to several weeks depending on tolerability):

   |  | Morning | Lunch | Evening |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | Previous dose | 0.5 mg lorazepam | 0.5 mg lorazepam | 0.5 mg lorazepam |
   | Transfer 1 | 5 mg diazepam | 0.5 mg lorazepam | 0.5 mg lorazepam |
   | Transfer 2 | 5 mg diazepam | 0.5 mg lorazepam | 5 mg diazepam |
   | Transfer 3 | 5 mg diazepam | 5 mg diazepam | 5 mg diazepam |

4. **Begin dose reduction.** A reduction rate of 10 to 15% every 1 to 2 weeks is recommended but should be individualised based on symptoms. Some patients need slower reductions with longer intervals between steps.

5. **Adjust the plan as needed** — reduce the amount by which the dose is decreased, or increase the interval between reductions. Do not prescribe supplementary doses.

6. **Be aware that the final dose is often the hardest** — both psychological and chemical dependence can make the last reduction particularly challenging. Specialist addiction service advice may be helpful at this stage.

7. **Provide written documentation** of the treatment plan so both patient and doctor can track progress.

8. For a step-by-step deprescribing algorithm, see: [Benzodiazepine and Z-Drug (BZRA) Deprescribing Algorithm](https://deprescribing.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/BZRA-deprescribing-algorithms-2019-English.pdf)

#### Other Agents to Consider During Withdrawal

**Antidepressants** do not decrease or eliminate withdrawal symptoms, but may be useful if the patient is experiencing severe depression or anxiety during withdrawal. The patient may have heightened sensitivity to psychotropic medications during withdrawal.

**Analgesics** — Benzodiazepine or zopiclone withdrawal can cause acute physical pain. Paracetamol or NSAIDs can be used for relief. Non-pharmacological options include rest, hot or cold packs, massage, and relaxation or meditation.

#### Relapse Prevention Support

Your doctor may:

- Arrange regular follow-up (e.g., weekly or fortnightly)
- Manage co-morbid mental health conditions such as depression and anxiety
- Refer for ongoing counselling for relapse prevention or underlying mental health issues
- Encourage use of non-pharmacological strategies for managing distress episodes
- Provide relapse prevention information
