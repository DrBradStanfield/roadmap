# Postmenopausal Bleeding

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of vaginal bleeding after menopause — a symptom that always requires investigation to exclude serious causes, including endometrial cancer.

---

## Background

Postmenopausal bleeding (PMB) is vaginal bleeding occurring after 12 months or more of menopausal amenorrhoea in a woman aged over 40 years, or where premature menopause has been previously diagnosed.

**Recurrent PMB** refers to a new episode of bleeding following a 4-month interval with no vaginal bleeding after a previous PMB episode — and also requires investigation.

---

## Possible Causes

| Cause | Approximate frequency |
|---|---|
| Vaginal, endometrial, or urogenital atrophy | ~60% |
| Exogenous oestrogen (e.g. MHT) | ~15% |
| Endometrial hyperplasia | ~10% |
| Endometrial cancer | ~10% |
| Endometrial or cervical polyps | ~4% |
| Cervical cancer | <1% |
| Other (vaginal trauma, other genital tract malignancy, non-gynaecological bleeding) | rare |

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a thorough history, including:

- Pattern of bleeding
- Other pelvic symptoms: pain (including pain with intercourse), vaginal discharge, vaginal dryness, bladder or bowel symptoms
- Unexplained weight loss or loss of appetite
- Current medications — particularly menopause hormone therapy (MHT) or tamoxifen

**If you are taking MHT:** Erratic bleeding is common in the first 3–6 months after starting continuous combined MHT and does not necessarily need investigation. Unscheduled bleeding beyond 6 months, or bleeding outside the expected progestin-withdrawal window on cyclic regimens, requires investigation. Your doctor may also ask about possible drug interactions (carbamazepine, phenytoin, rifampicin, St John's wort, grapefruit juice).

**If you are taking tamoxifen:** Tamoxifen is associated with an increased risk of endometrial cancer. Investigation is generally more involved — a hysteroscopy and endometrial biopsy are usually needed rather than ultrasound alone, because ultrasound is less reliable in women on tamoxifen.

Physical examination typically includes:

- BMI measurement
- Abdominal examination
- Speculum examination (vulva, vagina, and cervix), including swabs if relevant
- HPV and cervical cytology co-test if not done in the past 3 months
- Bimanual pelvic examination

---

## Investigations

Your doctor will arrange a **pelvic ultrasound** (transvaginal ultrasound, TVUS) within 2 weeks in most cases. This measures the thickness of the endometrium (lining of the uterus), which guides further management.

---

## Management

Management depends on the ultrasound findings, and your doctor will guide you through each step. The general approach is outlined below.

### Urgent gynaecology referral

Your doctor will seek urgent gynaecology input if:

- Bleeding is severe and requires immediate intervention
- You are taking tamoxifen (endometrial cancer risk is elevated)
- You have had recurrent PMB
- There is any cervical or vaginal abnormality (other than atrophic vaginitis) on examination

### Based on ultrasound results

**Endometrial polyps seen on scan:** Your doctor may consider referral to gynaecology for assessment.

**Fluid in the endometrial cavity or cystic changes in the endometrium:** Your doctor may consider a pipelle biopsy regardless of endometrial thickness.

**Endometrial thickness less than 5 mm:** This is reassuring. Your doctor may consider treatment for vaginal atrophy with vaginal oestrogen (see below) and will review you in 2 months. If further bleeding occurs, gynaecology referral may be arranged.

**Endometrial thickness 5–8 mm:** Your doctor may consider a pipelle biopsy. Depending on results and ongoing symptoms, gynaecology referral may be arranged.

**Endometrial thickness greater than 8 mm:** Your doctor will likely arrange a pipelle biopsy and consider gynaecology referral, as the risk of endometrial cancer or polyp is higher at this thickness.

**Any other ultrasound abnormality:** Your doctor may consider gynaecology referral.

### Pipelle biopsy

A pipelle biopsy is a sampling of the endometrial lining, usually performed in a clinic setting. If performed, further management depends on the histology result:

- **Normal histology with endometrium less than 5 mm (single episode, normal examination and cervical test):** Your doctor may consider treatment for atrophic vaginitis with vaginal oestrogen, with 2-month review.
- **Normal histology with endometrium 5–8 mm:** Your doctor may consider gynaecology referral if further bleeding occurs despite treatment.
- **Normal histology with endometrium greater than 8 mm:** Your doctor will likely consider gynaecology referral given the higher risk of underlying pathology.
- **Abnormal histology** (carcinoma, or simple or complex hyperplasia with or without atypia): Gynaecology referral is arranged.
- **Inadequate, insufficient, or limited sample:** Your doctor will likely consider gynaecology referral.

### Vaginal oestrogen (for atrophic vaginitis)

If the cause of bleeding appears to be vaginal or endometrial atrophy, your doctor may consider prescribing vaginal oestrogen (estriol). This is an effective treatment for vaginal atrophy and is considered safe for most women, including those with a history of venous thromboembolism and most gynaecological cancer survivors.

**Vaginal oestrogen is not appropriate if you have:**
- Active breast cancer
- Active endometrial cancer or undiagnosed vaginal bleeding (until investigation is complete)

If you have a history of oestrogen-dependent breast cancer, your doctor will seek advice from an oncologist or breast specialist before prescribing.

**Estriol cream (Ovestin, 1 mg/g):** One applicator inserted vaginally at bedtime every night for two weeks, then twice a week thereafter. If the cream is irritating, your doctor may advise starting with smaller amounts applied with a finger and gradually increasing. Systemic absorption is low. Additional progesterone is not needed for endometrial protection.

Initial side-effects can include mild vaginal irritation or stinging, and rarely mild breast pain or light bleeding.
