# UTIs and Asymptomatic Bacteriuria in Pregnancy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of urinary tract infections and asymptomatic bacteriuria (ASB) in pregnancy.

---

## Background

Pregnant women with asymptomatic bacteriuria or lower urinary tract infections have a higher risk of developing pyelonephritis compared to non-pregnant women. This is due to physiological changes in pregnancy:

- Pressure on the bladder from the enlarging uterus
- Increased size of the ureters due to smooth muscle relaxation
- Dextro-rotation of the uterus compressing the right ureter
- Immunological changes throughout pregnancy

Pyelonephritis in pregnancy can have serious consequences including maternal sepsis, preterm labour, and premature delivery — it requires prompt treatment.

Following a risk-benefit discussion, low-risk women with ASB may wish to avoid antibiotics and prefer a back-pocket prescription to use if symptoms develop.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

Pyelonephritis in pregnancy requires urgent hospital assessment. Your healthcare provider will arrange this if you have:

- Flank pain
- Nausea or vomiting
- Fever greater than 38°C
- With or without cystitis symptoms

Signs of **sepsis** are a medical emergency. Seek emergency care if there are features of severe tachycardia or tachypnoea, or signs of significant hypoperfusion alongside a confirmed or suspected infection.

---

## Screening

Your healthcare provider or lead maternity carer (LMC) will typically:

- Screen all pregnant women for asymptomatic bacteriuria by arranging a **urine culture at 12 to 16 weeks**

**What bacteria are significant?**

A urine culture showing greater than 10⁸/L of a single uropathogen may be significant in pregnancy. Common uropathogens include:

- *Escherichia coli*
- *Klebsiella pneumoniae*
- *Proteus mirabilis*

Enterococci are less pathogenic and seldom cause symptomatic UTIs in people with normal urinary tracts.

---

## Assessment

### If You Have No Symptoms

- If your first urine culture shows no bacteriuria, no repeat culture is needed unless symptoms develop.
- If bacteriuria is found, your healthcare provider may arrange a **second urine culture** to exclude contamination — treatment is only recommended if the same organism is found on the second culture.
- If **group B streptococcus (GBS)** is found, your lead maternity carer will be informed, as you will require antibiotics during labour.

### If You Have Symptoms

Symptoms of **acute cystitis** include:

- Dysuria (pain on urination)
- Frequency and/or nocturia
- Haematuria (blood in urine)
- Suprapubic pain or pressure

Symptoms of **pyelonephritis** include:

- Flank pain
- Nausea or vomiting
- Fever greater than 38°C
- With or without cystitis symptoms

Your healthcare provider will:

- Check vital signs including temperature and heart rate
- Examine for loin tenderness and abdominal pain
- Ask about fetal movements if appropriate gestation
- Send a urine sample for culture — it is important to indicate that you are pregnant and your gestation

---

## Antibiotic Safety in Pregnancy

Your healthcare provider will consider antibiotic safety carefully. Antibiotics considered safe for UTI in pregnancy include:

- **Cefalexin**
- **Nitrofurantoin** — avoid from 36 weeks' gestation (or sooner if early delivery is planned), due to risk of haemolytic anaemia in the newborn
- **Amoxicillin + clavulanic acid (Augmentin)** — use with caution in the third trimester; some studies suggest an increased risk of necrotising enterocolitis in the newborn when used close to preterm birth
- **Amoxicillin** — not recommended for empirical treatment due to high resistance rates, but appropriate if indicated by culture results
- **Trimethoprim** — avoid in the first trimester due to theoretical interaction with folic acid metabolism
- **Fosfomycin** — may be used under special authority for multi-resistant *E. coli* cystitis

**Not recommended in pregnancy:**

- **Fluoroquinolones** (e.g. ciprofloxacin, norfloxacin) — should not be used at any time in pregnancy

## Management by Diagnosis

### Pyelonephritis

Pyelonephritis in pregnancy requires prompt and aggressive inpatient treatment. Your healthcare provider will arrange urgent hospital assessment:

- Less than 20 weeks gestation: acute general medicine assessment
- 20 weeks or more: acute obstetric assessment

Hospital treatment typically involves intravenous antibiotics initially, followed by oral antibiotics for 7 to 10 days.

After treatment, your healthcare provider may:

- Arrange a urine culture for test of cure 1 week after completing antibiotics, then repeat monthly until delivery
- Start low-dose prophylactic antibiotics if bacteriuria persists:
  - Nitrofurantoin (immediate release) 100 mg at night (avoid from 36 weeks)
  - Trimethoprim 300 mg at night (avoid in first trimester)
  - Cefalexin 500 mg at night
  - Prophylaxis continued for 4 weeks, then urine culture 1 week later
- If you have significant risk factors (pyelonephritis with sepsis, hydronephrosis, significant kidney stones, neurogenic bladder, or immune compromise), prophylaxis for the remainder of pregnancy may be recommended
- Your lead maternity carer will be informed of the diagnosis

### Symptomatic Acute Cystitis

Treatment is initiated for all pregnant women with cystitis symptoms — your healthcare provider will not wait for lab results before starting antibiotics.

Typical empirical treatment for 7 days:

1. **Nitrofurantoin** modified release 100 mg twice daily — avoid from 36 weeks onward
2. **Cefalexin** 500 mg twice daily
3. **Amoxicillin + clavulanic acid** 625 mg three times daily (use with caution in the third trimester)

Not recommended for empiric therapy:
- Trimethoprim — resistance rates exceed 20%; use only if confirmed susceptible, and avoid in the first trimester
- Amoxicillin — use only if confirmed susceptible by culture

Routine urine culture after resolution of symptoms is not recommended.

Your lead maternity carer will be informed of the diagnosis. If you have 3 or more separate episodes of cystitis, discuss this with your healthcare provider as management may change.

### Recurrent Symptomatic Cystitis (3 or more episodes)

Your healthcare provider may:

- Treat an acute episode as above
- Discuss a safety plan including a back-pocket prescription for early self-treatment at first signs of symptoms
- Advise you to seek medical attention if symptoms fail to improve quickly, especially with fever, nausea, or vomiting
- Advise contacting your LMC or delivery suite if infection is suspected in late pregnancy
- Consider antibiotic prophylaxis using an agent to which your isolate is susceptible:
  - Nitrofurantoin (immediate release) 100 mg at night (avoid from 36 weeks)
  - Trimethoprim 300 mg at night (avoid in first trimester)
  - Cefalexin 500 mg at night
  - After acute treatment, prophylaxis given for 2 further weeks, then urine culture 1 week later
- Consider investigation to exclude renal tract abnormalities if risk factors are present

### Group B Streptococcus (GBS) Bacteriuria

GBS bacteriuria at any stage of pregnancy means you will require **antibiotics at the time of labour** to prevent GBS disease in the newborn, even if treated earlier. Further GBS screening during pregnancy is not required.

**If you have symptoms (e.g. acute cystitis):**

Your healthcare provider will treat with antibiotics for 7 days:
- Amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily (first choice)
- Cefalexin 500 mg twice daily

**If you have no symptoms (asymptomatic GBS):**

The benefit of treating asymptomatic GBS is unclear. After a shared decision with your healthcare provider:

- Low-risk women (no history of premature labour, diabetes, recurrent UTIs, polycystic kidneys, congenital renal anomalies, or sickle cell disease) may choose to avoid antibiotics and instead have a back-pocket prescription ready
- If the decision is made to treat: 3 days of amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily (first choice) or cefalexin 500 mg twice daily

Your lead maternity carer will be informed of all positive GBS results including light growth.

### Asymptomatic Bacteriuria (Confirmed on Second Culture)

If the same bacterial species is found on a second urine culture, your healthcare provider may consider treating with antibiotics for 3 days, guided by culture and sensitivity results:

1. Nitrofurantoin modified release 100 mg twice daily (avoid from 36 weeks)
2. Trimethoprim 300 mg at night (avoid in first trimester)
3. Amoxicillin 500 mg three times daily
4. Cefalexin 500 mg twice daily
5. Amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 625 mg three times daily (use with caution in third trimester)

After shared decision-making, low-risk women may choose to avoid antibiotics and have a back-pocket prescription instead.

Important: Routine repeat urinalysis after treatment is not recommended — ASB often recurs and may represent colonisation rather than true infection, especially with GBS or enterococci. Suppressive treatment of ASB is not recommended.

### Sterile Pyuria (White Cells in Urine but No Bacteria)

If your urine shows white blood cells but no bacteria, your healthcare provider may:

- Repeat the urine sample with careful attention to collection technique
- Consider causes of vaginitis, including vaginal thrush or sexually transmitted infections (STIs)
- Arrange STI screening and treatment as appropriate

---

## High-Risk Group: History of Preterm Birth + UTI in Pregnancy

If you have a history of both preterm birth and UTI in a previous pregnancy, discuss this with your healthcare provider. After 14 weeks' gestation, obstetric assessment may be recommended as you are at higher risk of further preterm delivery and may need cervical monitoring.
