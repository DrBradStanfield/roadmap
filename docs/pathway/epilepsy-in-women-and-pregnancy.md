# Epilepsy in Women and Pregnancy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers contraception, pre-conception, pregnancy, and postpartum care for women with epilepsy who are taking antiepileptic drugs (AEDs). Always discuss your individual situation with your doctor or specialist.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Attention

Contact your doctor urgently or call an ambulance (111) if:

- Epilepsy is poorly controlled
- You have a history of eclampsia
- You are currently taking sodium valproate or multiple antiepileptic drugs and are pregnant or planning pregnancy
- You experience a seizure during pregnancy, especially in the third trimester with high blood pressure or protein in the urine (possible eclampsia)

---

## Important Note on Topiramate

Topiramate taken during pregnancy increases the risk of birth defects, developmental and learning problems, and babies being small for their gestational age. Your doctor will:

- Test for pregnancy before starting topiramate in anyone who could become pregnant
- Emphasise the need for effective contraception (e.g., contraceptive injection, IUD, combined oral contraceptive pill plus condoms)
- Seek prompt specialist advice if pregnancy is confirmed while you are taking topiramate

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask all women of childbearing age who take antiepileptic drugs about their contraceptive use and pregnancy plans.

---

## Management

### General Principles

Your doctor may recommend:

- Using **2 effective forms of contraception** (including condoms) if you are on antiepileptic drugs and not planning a pregnancy
- Taking **folic acid 5 mg daily** — this applies to all women of childbearing age on AEDs, not just those actively trying to conceive. More than half of pregnancies are unplanned
- Starting folic acid at least 3 months before trying to conceive, and continuing throughout pregnancy
- Discussing pre-conception planning with your GP or neurologist at least **6 to 12 months before** trying for a pregnancy
- Documenting your discussion and management plan

---

### Before Menarche or Not Yet Sexually Active

Your doctor may:

- Begin discussing the implications of antiepileptic drugs and pregnancy with you (and your parents or guardian if relevant), if you are likely to continue these medications into adulthood
- Ensure effective long-term contraception is in place if you are taking sodium valproate, while discussing alternative medications
- Review medications annually; arrange annual trough blood levels if you are taking lamotrigine or levetiracetam
- Advise that you can seek contraception without a parent or guardian present once you reach menarche or become sexually active

---

### Using Contraception

Antiepileptic drugs fall into two groups based on how they interact with contraception:

**Non-enzyme-inducing antiseizure medications (do not affect most hormonal contraceptives):**
Clobazam, Clonazepam, Ethosuximide, Gabapentin, Lacosamide, Lamotrigine, Levetiracetam, Pregabalin, Retigabine, Sodium valproate, Vigabatrin

**Enzyme-inducing antiseizure medications (can reduce the effectiveness of some hormonal contraceptives):**
Carbamazepine, Oxcarbazepine, Phenobarbital, Phenytoin, Primidone, Rufinamide, Topiramate

Your doctor may advise:

- Using 2 effective forms of contraception including condoms, regardless of which AED you are on
- Caution with progesterone-only pills (POPs) if you take lamotrigine — POPs may raise lamotrigine levels and cause side effects
- Starting folic acid 5 mg daily

**Emergency contraception:** Your doctor will discuss options with you:
- If you are on enzyme-inducing AEDs: the copper intrauterine device (Cu-IUD) is the only emergency contraceptive method not affected by these drugs. If a Cu-IUD is not suitable or available, a double dose of levonorgestrel (3 mg as a single dose) may be considered — this is an unapproved dose and effectiveness is uncertain
- If you are on non-enzyme-inducing AEDs: standard emergency contraception (1.5 mg levonorgestrel or copper IUD) can be used

---

### Pre-Conception

Your doctor may:

- Offer pre-conception advice and printed information
- Start or continue folic acid 5 mg daily at least 3 months before trying to conceive
- Review seizure control over the past 12 months (the best predictor of seizure control during pregnancy)
- Review your antiepileptic drugs — particularly to avoid sodium valproate if possible in women of childbearing age
- Refer you to a neurologist if you are on sodium valproate, have poorly controlled epilepsy, or are considering stopping an AED

**Sodium valproate risks:**
Sodium valproate carries a higher risk of fetal malformations than other AEDs, and this risk is dose-related. Malformations include neural tube defects, cardiac defects, cleft palate, and hypospadias. There is also strong evidence of harm to a child's brain development. If you are on valproate and cannot switch:
- Under a named neurologist's guidance, the dose should ideally be reduced to under 800 mg/day before conception
- The dose should be split into 3 to 4 doses per day to reduce peak blood levels

**General teratogenicity:**
- The general population rate of birth defects is approximately 2.4%. In women with epilepsy this rises to between 3 and 7%
- The more AEDs used, the higher the risk
- Your doctor will aim for the lowest effective dose, monotherapy where possible, and consider switching to a less teratogenic drug (e.g., lamotrigine or carbamazepine)
- AEDs should never be stopped without neurology advice
- If AEDs are withdrawn, withdrawal should be completed at least 6 months before planned conception

**Driving:** If AEDs are reduced or stopped, New Zealand Transport Agency (NZTA) requires a stand-down during withdrawal and for 6 months after withdrawal is complete. If a seizure occurs during or after withdrawal, the standard stand-down rules apply.

**Monitoring:** If you take lamotrigine or levetiracetam, your doctor will check a baseline blood level before conception and repeat every 2 months. Doses often need adjustment during pregnancy.

**Other considerations:**
- Antenatal screening will be discussed
- The risk of passing epilepsy to a child is generally low (under 4%), but slightly higher if the mother (rather than the father) has epilepsy, if epilepsy started early, or if more than one close relative has epilepsy

### During Pregnancy

Your doctor will reassure you that more than 90% of women taking AEDs have a normal pregnancy.

**Do not stop or alter your AEDs once pregnant** — it is too late to reduce teratogenicity risk, and stopping increases the risk of seizures. Most major malformations occur before a woman knows she is pregnant. If you are on sodium valproate or phenobarbitone, your doctor will seek urgent neurology advice.

Your doctor may:

- Start folic acid 5 mg daily if not already taking it
- Refer you to both an obstetrician (high-risk maternity) and a neurologist

**Monitoring lamotrigine or levetiracetam in pregnancy:**
Your doctor will monitor blood levels every 1 to 3 months and adjust the dose as needed — levels often rise throughout pregnancy. After delivery, doses should be reduced back toward pre-pregnancy levels over 2 to 4 weeks. Sudden dose reductions in the early postpartum period should be avoided, particularly if you are sleep-deprived.

**SUDEP (Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy):**
Pregnancy and the 6 months after birth are higher-risk periods. Your doctor may discuss strategies to reduce risk including:

- Optimising medication adherence
- Not sleeping, bathing, or caring for young children alone
- Ensuring family members know how to respond to a seizure

**Seizures during pregnancy:**

*Seizure in late pregnancy with high blood pressure or protein in urine — possible eclampsia:*
- Call an ambulance (111) immediately
- This is a medical emergency — your doctor will provide immediate airway management and seizure treatment while awaiting the ambulance
- Medications used may include intramuscular magnesium sulphate, diazepam, or midazolam
- Blood pressure may be urgently lowered with nifedipine (oral), labetalol (IV), or hydralazine (IV)

*Single seizure in late pregnancy without suspected eclampsia:*
- If you have epilepsy: your doctor will review your AED dose and blood levels, and consider causes such as missed doses, nausea affecting absorption, or altered drug metabolism. Urgent obstetric review will be arranged
- If you have no history of epilepsy: a structural cause (e.g., brain bleed, venous thrombosis) must be considered. Emergency assessment may be needed

*Recurrent seizures in pregnancy:*
- Associated with increased risk to the baby and increased maternal SUDEP risk
- Your doctor will arrange urgent neurology review and, if eclampsia is suspected, urgent obstetric review

---

### Delivery and Postpartum

**Delivery:**
Your doctor will advise:

- All women with epilepsy should deliver in hospital
- Water births are discouraged
- The baby should receive vitamin K at birth — AEDs can interfere with vitamin K metabolism and increase the risk of newborn bleeding. Maternal vitamin K supplementation in the third trimester is no longer recommended

**Postpartum precautions your doctor may discuss:**

- Stay in hospital under observation for 24 to 48 hours after delivery — seizure risk is highest immediately postpartum
- Feed and change your baby on a mat on the floor, not on a changing table or while in bed
- Bathe your baby in minimal water and only when someone else is present
- Carry your baby in a car seat (buckled), especially on stairs
- Shower rather than bathe; avoid swimming alone
- Take short naps while the baby sleeps — sleep deprivation can trigger seizures. Consider asking your partner or family to help with night feeds if sleep deprivation is severe
- Your family should know how to manage a seizure

**Medications:** Be cautious with pain medications that can lower the seizure threshold — for example pethidine, tramadol, and amitriptyline.

**Breastfeeding:** Breastfeeding while on AEDs is safe and encouraged if you wish to do so.

**Contraception and folic acid:** Your doctor will discuss restarting contraception and folic acid after delivery.

**AED doses:** If epilepsy is well controlled, your specialist team will guide a gradual reduction of AED doses back toward pre-pregnancy levels.
