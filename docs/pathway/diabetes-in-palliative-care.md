# Diabetes in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers management of diabetes in adults with a life-limiting illness. See also the general [Diabetes](type-2-diabetes.md) pathway.

---

## Background

When someone with diabetes has a life-limiting condition, the goals of diabetes care change fundamentally:

- The focus shifts to **preventing hypoglycaemia (low blood sugar) and symptomatic hyperglycaemia (high blood sugar)** while minimising the burden of blood glucose testing and insulin injections.
- **Preventing long-term complications is no longer a priority** — the timeframe is too short for this to matter, and strict control can cause unnecessary harm.

This shift in goals is an important conversation your doctor will have with you and your family.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will review:
- Symptoms of deterioration (pain, nausea/vomiting, thirst, infection, delirium, poor appetite, breathlessness)
- Current medications
- Recent blood glucose readings
- Symptoms of hypoglycaemia (sweating, fatigue, palpitations, tremor, hunger, dizziness, confusion, seizure)
- If on metformin: nausea and weight loss

If new-onset diabetes is suspected (e.g. from steroid treatment), your doctor will check blood glucose and confirm diagnosis with an HbA1c if appropriate.

---

## Management by Clinical Stage

### Type 1 Diabetes

Type 1 patients usually have specialist diabetes team involvement — your doctor will liaise with that team if there are concerns.

**Treatment goals:**
- Maintain quality of life
- Avoid hypoglycaemia; minimise hyperglycaemia
- Reduce short-term complications (infections, polyuria, nocturia, lethargy)
- Manage nutrition and hydration
- Support patient and family through the goal shift away from tight glycaemic control

Blood glucose target is relaxed to **6 to 15 mmol/L**. HbA1c testing is stopped.

**If clinically stable:**
- Continue blood glucose monitoring with relaxed targets
- Simplify insulin regimen if possible — do not stop insulin completely
- If appetite decreases: omit short-acting insulin first (can give after meals based on intake); reduce basal insulin if weight is dropping despite maintained appetite
- Reduce insulin by 10–20% increments; aim for morning blood glucose of 6–15 mmol/L
- If blood glucose > 15 mmol/L and the patient is symptomatic, a correction dose of rapid-acting insulin may be appropriate

**If clinically unstable or deteriorating:**
- Do not stop insulin — even without eating, stopping insulin in type 1 causes diabetic ketoacidosis. A basal insulin alone may be sufficient.
- Monitor blood glucose more frequently until condition stabilises or continues to decline
- If poor oral intake or vomiting: reduce background insulin by 10–20% increments; be very cautious with short-acting insulin (Novorapid, Humalog, Apidra); reduce or omit doses if eating very little
- Seek diabetes advice

**If actively dying:**
- Stop blood glucose testing and stop insulin

---

### Type 2 Diabetes

**Treatment goals** are the same as type 1 (see above): avoid hypoglycaemia, minimise symptomatic hyperglycaemia, maintain quality of life.

**If clinically stable:**
- Simplify medication regimen
- Consider stopping oral hypoglycaemic medications (e.g. metformin, glipizide) — especially if the patient has had rapid weight loss

**If clinically unstable or deteriorating:**
- Monitor blood glucose twice daily until condition stabilises or declines
- **Oral hypoglycaemics:**
  - If reduced oral intake or intermittent vomiting: reduce dose by 50%
  - If no oral intake: stop oral hypoglycaemics
- **Insulin:**
  - If oral intake is reduced or the patient is vomiting: reduce the dose
  - It may be possible to stop insulin entirely as clinical condition declines
- If persistent hyperglycaemia > 15 mmol/L with symptoms: consider starting intermediate or long-acting insulin (e.g. isophane/Protaphane, or glargine) once or twice daily

**If actively dying:**
- Stop blood glucose testing
- Reduce or stop insulin

---

### Corticosteroid-Induced Hyperglycaemia

Steroids are commonly used in palliative care and raise blood glucose — particularly in the afternoon and evening after a morning dose.

Your doctor may advise:
- Capillary glucose testing 2–4 times daily while on steroids
- If highest reading > 15 mmol/L: start intermediate-acting insulin (Protaphane or Humulin NPH) in the morning
- If highest reading ≤ 15 mmol/L: consider a sulphonylurea or increase the existing sulphonylurea dose
- If the steroid dose changes, insulin may need adjustment

---

## When to Involve a Specialist

Your doctor will seek diabetes advice or adult palliative care support and advice for:
- Medication questions
- Anticipatory planning
- Arranging a joint specialist visit
- Considering hospice admission

Hospital admission may be appropriate if the patient is acutely unwell.
