# Anorectal Abscess

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of anorectal abscess in adults.

---

## Background

A perianal abscess, or anorectal abscess, is a collection of pus around the perianal region. It typically arises from infected mucus-producing glands in the anal canal.

Types include:

- **Ischiorectal** – extends into the fat around the anal canal; can be large. Often presents with marked cellulitis and significant pain.
- **Intersphincteric** – between the internal and external sphincters. May have little to see externally, but causes extreme pain.
- **Horseshoe abscess** – tracts from one side of the anal canal around to the other; may require two drainage sites.
- **Superficial perianal abscess** – close to the anal verge; small but very painful lump.

Anorectal abscesses are more common in men and most commonly occur between ages 20 and 50.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Painful lump and sensation of fullness around the anal area
- Difficulty sitting down
- Any previous abscess or fistula
- Discharge of pus
- Systemic symptoms — fever, malaise, loss of appetite, sweating, rigors

Your doctor will also ask about:

- Crohn's disease (including family history) — this can predispose to anorectal abscess
- Immunosuppression or diabetes — these can make infection more severe

An anorectal abscess can often be diagnosed on history alone. Patients typically describe severe perianal pain and not wanting to sit down.

Physical examination typically includes:

- Inspection of the perianal area for a tender lump
- Assessment for associated cellulitis
- Palpation of inguinal lymph nodes

Note: if pain is severe, digital rectal examination may not be possible.

Investigations are not usually needed. Your doctor may consider sexually transmitted infection (STI) screening or diabetes screening if indicated.

---

## Management

Anorectal abscesses require **surgical drainage** — this is done under general anaesthetic and is the definitive treatment.

Your doctor may consider:

- **Analgesia** for pain relief while awaiting surgery
- **Antibiotics** — only if there will be a delay in reaching surgical assessment. Antibiotics are not a substitute for drainage, but they may be continued after surgery to reduce the risk of developing a fistula.

Important points to know:

- Anorectal abscesses are common and are not a sign of unhygienic behaviour. They result from blocked mucus-producing glands in the anal canal.
- They are extremely painful but can be resolved quickly with a drainage procedure under general anaesthetic.
- After surgery, wound care (including packing if needed) will be provided by district nurses until healed.
- A surgical review at 6 weeks may be arranged if there is suspicion of an underlying fistula or Crohn's disease.
