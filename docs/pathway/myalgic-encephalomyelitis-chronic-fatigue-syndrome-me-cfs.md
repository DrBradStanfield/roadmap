# Myalgic Encephalomyelitis/Chronic Fatigue Syndrome (ME/CFS)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of ME/CFS — a debilitating, chronic multisystem disease with a wide range of symptoms. Diagnosis is based on clinical criteria in the absence of alternative diagnoses. If you think you may have ME/CFS, discuss this with your doctor; they will guide diagnosis and management.

---

## Red Flag — Seek Medical Attention

- **Marked unintentional weight loss** — this needs prompt medical review to exclude other causes.

---

## Background

- ME/CFS is estimated to affect 2 to 4 out of 1,000 New Zealanders, with women affected approximately three times more often than men. Many people never receive a formal diagnosis.
- The exact cause is unknown, but research shows changes in the immune system, cellular metabolism, neuroendocrine system, and cardiovascular regulation.
- People with ME/CFS have often experienced disbelief from health services. This is well recognised — your experience is valid.
- Recovery is more common in the first 2 years; beyond this, prognosis varies widely. Early diagnosis and appropriate management improve outcomes.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you over one or more visits, as diagnosis can be complex.

### Key Features Your Doctor Will Ask About

**The four core features of ME/CFS:**

1. **Fatigue** — overwhelming physical and mental fatigue and loss of stamina that substantially reduces activity in all aspects of life, lasting more than 6 months, not caused by ongoing excessive exertion, and not substantially relieved by rest.

2. **Post-exertional malaise (PEM)** — worsening of symptoms after physical, mental, or emotional exertion that would not have caused a problem before the illness. Symptoms typically worsen 12 to 48 hours after the activity and may last days, weeks, or longer. The severity is out of proportion to the effort involved. For some people, sensory overload (light, sound) can also trigger PEM.

3. **Unrefreshing sleep** — sleep that does not restore energy, or disturbances in sleep quality or rhythm.

4. **Cognitive impairment or orthostatic intolerance (one or both required):**
   - Cognitive impairment: problems with thinking, memory, executive function, information processing, or attention ("brain fog")
   - Orthostatic intolerance: symptoms such as light-headedness, fainting, increased fatigue, cognitive worsening, headaches, or nausea when standing — caused by an abnormal nervous system response to being upright

### Other Symptoms Your Doctor May Ask About

- Onset: age, sudden or gradual, any triggering event (infection, trauma, immunisation, chemical exposure, anaesthetic, etc.)
- Course: stable, improving, or worsening
- Daily pattern: delayed fatigue (worse 12–48 hours after activity) and prolonged recovery (days to weeks) are typical
- Triggers: physical, cognitive, or emotional
- Sleep quality (insomnia, hypersomnia)
- Muscle pain and fatigability, headaches, sensitivity to light and sound, loss of temperature regulation, marked weight changes
- Impact on work, socialising, and family activities
- Co-existing fibromyalgia (widespread chronic pain is common)

### Diagnostic Criteria

Your doctor will use the **Institute of Medicine (IOM) 2015 criteria**, which require all three of the following:
- Substantial reduction in activity, persisting more than 6 months, accompanied by fatigue
- Post-exertional malaise (PEM)
- Unrefreshing sleep

Plus at least one of:
- Cognitive impairment, or
- Orthostatic intolerance

Symptoms must be present at least half the time with at least moderate severity.

### Severity Assessment

Your doctor will also assess severity:

- **Severe ME/CFS:** Unable to carry out most activities; severe cognitive difficulties; may need a wheelchair; often unable to leave the house; spends much of the day in bed; extremely sensitive to light and sound.
- **Very severe ME/CFS:** Bed-bound and dependent on care; needs help with personal hygiene and eating; very sensitive to sensory stimuli.

### Screening for Co-existing Conditions

Your doctor will screen for anxiety and depression, which are common co-existing conditions. Living with ME/CFS can itself cause depression and anxiety — this does not mean symptoms are psychological.

### Examination

Examination is generally normal in ME/CFS unless another condition is present. Your doctor will check:
- General appearance and weight
- Lying and standing heart rate and blood pressure (to look for orthostatic intolerance)
- Lymph nodes, thyroid, heart, and lungs
- Joints and connective tissues
- Neurological examination (to exclude conditions such as peripheral neuropathy, multiple sclerosis, myasthenia gravis)

### Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:
- Blood tests: full blood count, CRP, ferritin, electrolytes, kidney and liver function, calcium, magnesium, blood glucose/HbA1c, thyroid function, coeliac antibodies
- Vitamin D (if indicated)
- B12 (if specific indications present)
- Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, or HIV testing if symptoms followed a viral illness
- ECG and chest X-ray if palpitations, chest pain, or shortness of breath are present
- Additional tests (autoimmune markers, creatine kinase, cortisol) based on clinical findings

Investigations are not repeated unless there is a clear clinical reason.

**If symptoms may have been triggered by COVID-19 infection**, your doctor will also consider long COVID, which has significant overlap with ME/CFS.

## Management

Your doctor will make a diagnosis of ME/CFS once the criteria are met and alternative diagnoses excluded. Management is a developing field; your doctor will discuss options suited to your situation.

### Pacing (Managing Post-Exertional Malaise)

Pacing is an individualised approach to energy management aimed at minimising PEM. It involves identifying your personal activity threshold — the point at which exertion triggers PEM — and keeping the total of all daily activities below that limit (your "energy envelope").

Your doctor may advise you to:
- Identify which specific activities (physical and cognitive) trigger symptoms, rather than following a fixed schedule
- Balance energy across social, physical, cognitive, and work activities, with adequate rest
- Stay as active as possible without exceeding your limit, and increase activity only cautiously if energy levels improve
- Use practical strategies: activity/symptom diaries, step counters, heart rate monitors, shower chairs, dividing tasks into smaller steps with rest breaks, alternating cognitive and physical tasks, watching for body signals that more rest is needed

### Physical Activity and Exercise

There is no evidence that exercise cures ME/CFS. However, symptom-contingent physical activity may help maintain fitness and muscle tone if it does not trigger PEM.

Your doctor may advise:
- Exercise must match your current energy levels — never push through PEM
- If bed-bound, your doctor may consider a physiotherapy referral for passive exercises to protect range of motion
- Graded exercise therapy (GET) is **not recommended** under current ME/CFS guidelines due to potential harm

### Sleep

Unrefreshing sleep is a core symptom of ME/CFS. Your doctor may suggest:
- Sleep hygiene: quiet, dark, comfortable sleeping environment; consistent routines; limiting caffeine and alcohol; natural light exposure during the day
- Emphasis is on improving sleep quality, not reducing sleep length
- **Your doctor may consider:** low-dose tricyclic antidepressants taken at night for insomnia and pain — for example, amitriptyline or nortriptyline, starting at 5 mg two hours before bed, titrating up slowly
- **Your doctor may consider:** melatonin (1–2 mg, one hour before bedtime), noting there is currently no evidence that melatonin improves sleep specifically in ME/CFS
- ME/CFS patients are often more sensitive to medication side-effects; medications will be introduced at very low doses

### Orthostatic Intolerance and Autonomic Dysfunction

Orthostatic intolerance (OI) — when symptoms worsen on standing — is common in ME/CFS. Your doctor may suggest:

**Lifestyle measures:**
- Avoid prolonged lying; sit up or get out of bed as tolerated
- Raise the head of the bed by 20 cm
- Have regular, small meals with smaller portions of carbohydrates; avoid skipping meals
- Increase fluid intake (starting at 2 L/day, up to 4 L/day)
- Drink a glass of water before orthostatic challenges (getting up, showering, going shopping)
- Increase salt intake if blood pressure is normal or low (up to 5 g/day)
- Avoid overly long or hot showers; avoid prolonged standing or sitting
- Wear pressure garments (ideally toe-to-waist)
- Recumbent exercise within limits (e.g. swimming, recumbent cycling)
- Learn counter-pressure manoeuvres

**Your doctor may consider medication if lifestyle measures are insufficient:**
- Fludrocortisone (a mineralocorticoid that increases blood pressure via fluid volume) — starting at 50 micrograms daily, not exceeding 100 micrograms daily; blood pressure and electrolytes checked after 2 weeks; must be tapered slowly to stop

**If POTS (postural orthostatic tachycardia syndrome) is diagnosed**, your doctor may consider:
- Propranolol (beta blocker of choice for POTS): starting 10 mg twice daily — not used in asthma
- Bisoprolol 1.25 mg daily if propranolol not tolerated
- Citalopram (SSRI) 20 mg daily (serotonin helps venous return)
- Fludrocortisone as above, combined with high salt intake
- Midodrine (vasoconstrictor): starting 2.5 mg three times daily, monitoring blood pressure
- Pyridostigmine bromide: starting 30 mg three times daily for heart rate control

### Pain Management

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Low-dose tricyclic antidepressants (amitriptyline or nortriptyline, 5 mg at night) for pain and insomnia
- Low-dose naltrexone (off-label, not funded, must be obtained from a compounding pharmacy): starting at 0.5–1 mg daily, increasing slowly to a typical dose of 3–5 mg daily (not exceeding 5 mg). This is being evaluated for ME/CFS pain and fibromyalgia; evidence from large clinical trials is not yet available. Patients must not use opioids while taking naltrexone.

### Mental Health and Psychological Support

- Psychological support does not imply symptoms are psychological — this type of support is used across many chronic conditions.
- Counselling, mindfulness, and general psychological support may help with coping and prevent "push-through" behaviour.
- The Lightning Process and therapies based on it are **not recommended** (NICE guidelines).
- If mental health support is needed, your doctor can advise on referral options.

### Nutrition

Your doctor may advise:
- Eat from the main food groups daily and drink plenty of fluids
- Choose lower glycaemic index (GI) carbohydrates (oats, wholegrain bread, legumes, basmati rice)
- Plan and batch-cook meals for lower-energy days
- Limit caffeine (tea, coffee, cola, energy drinks), especially from mid-afternoon
- Restrictive diets (e.g. ketogenic) are not supported by evidence for ME/CFS and can cause nutritional deficiencies
- Dietitian referral may be considered if nutritional intake is a concern or if there is unintentional weight change

### Supplements and Complementary Therapies

- People with ME/CFS — especially those who are housebound or bed-bound — may be at risk of **vitamin D deficiency**; supplementation may be considered.
- Other commonly used supplements (without strong evidence): magnesium at night for cramps, coenzyme Q10 for muscle fatigue, B12 injections (some patients may wish to trial this after shared decision-making).
- Massage has limited evidence of benefit. There is no credible evidence for hypnosis, chiropractic, homeopathy, anti-Candida drugs, or thyroid supplements (in euthyroid patients).

### Antidepressants

- Antidepressants are **not recommended as a treatment for ME/CFS** (no significant benefit shown).
- If depression develops alongside ME/CFS, an SSRI is preferred as first-line, as it is less likely to worsen orthostatic symptoms or fatigue.
- In patients with overlapping ME/CFS, depression, and fibromyalgia symptoms, a trial may be considered.
- If trialled: start at very low doses, expect 2–3 weeks before any response, and discontinue if no improvement within 6–8 weeks (antidepressants can worsen fatigue).

### Immunisations

- Most people with ME/CFS tolerate standard immunisations. A small number experience temporary symptom worsening.
- Your doctor will time immunisations when you are relatively well and advise rest for 2–3 days afterward.
- There is no evidence that any vaccine causes ME/CFS.
- COVID-19 vaccination is worth discussing with your doctor — infection can trigger ME/CFS flares, and vaccination reduces this risk.

### Severe and Very Severe ME/CFS

If you have severe or very severe ME/CFS, your doctor will:
- Offer telehealth or home visits where appropriate
- Consider vitamin D supplementation if housebound
- Address pressure sore prevention if bed-bound
- Monitor weight regularly if eating is difficult
- Arrange support with daily living (personal care, eating, mobility)

### Support with Daily Living and Work

Your doctor can assist with:
- Advice on flexible adjustments to work or study
- Letters of support for employers or education providers (with your consent)
- Referral to occupational therapy if personal care is affected
- Information about disability allowance and supported living payment eligibility

### Follow-up

Your doctor will arrange regular reviews, including telehealth or home visits as needed. They will monitor for any new symptoms that might suggest an alternative or additional diagnosis.
