# Benign and Premalignant Skin Lesions

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of benign and premalignant skin lesions in primary care. See also the Melanoma and Non-melanoma Skin Cancer pathways.

---

## Suspicious Symptoms — See Your Doctor

A skin lesion should be assessed by a doctor promptly if it is:

- Changing — growing in size, changing shape or colour
- Not healing, or bleeding
- Thickened or indurated (firm, hard)
- Painful or tender to touch

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history to identify risk factors including:

- Personal or family history of skin cancer
- Significant sun exposure history (sunburns, outdoor occupation or hobbies, degree of sun protection)
- Immunosuppression
- Light or fair skin, blue or green eyes, blonde or red hair
- Older age or male sex

A **full body skin check** is performed, even if only one lesion prompted the visit — lesions on all body parts are examined including nails, soles of feet, and non-sun-exposed areas. Your doctor will document size (in mm), colour, margins, surface texture, and signs of ulceration or bleeding.

---

## Types of Skin Lesions

### Clearly Benign Lesions

These are symmetrical in shape, colour, and structure; stable or slowly evolving; and do not bleed spontaneously:

- **Ephelides (freckles)**
- **Epidermoid (sebaceous) cysts**

### Benign Lesions That Can Look Like Skin Cancer

Your doctor will assess these carefully:

- Atypical naevus (mole)
- Dermatofibroma (cutaneous fibrous histiocytoma)
- Lentigo simplex
- Pyogenic granuloma
- Sebaceous hyperplasia
- Seborrhoeic keratosis
- Skin tag
- Solar lentigo

### Benign Lesions Associated with Higher Cancer Risk

- **Congenital melanocytic naevus** — risk is higher in adults with large/giant lesions (>20 cm), lesions crossing the midline, multiple congenital satellite naevi, or neurocutaneous melanosis
- **Cutaneous horn** — can arise from benign, premalignant, or malignant skin lesions; no features can reliably confirm or exclude malignancy

### Premalignant Lesions

- **Actinic keratosis** — caused by cumulative sun damage; can progress to squamous cell carcinoma

---

## Management

Management depends on what the lesion is:

- **Pigmented lesions** — managed as per the Melanoma pathway
- **Suspected non-pigmented skin cancer** (BCC, SCC, amelanotic melanoma) — managed as per the Non-melanoma Skin Cancer pathway
- **Uncertain diagnosis** — your doctor may arrange a skin biopsy or excision biopsy, then manage based on the result

### For Clearly Benign, Asymptomatic Lesions

If the lesion is benign and causing no symptoms or aesthetic concerns, reassurance is appropriate — treatment is not necessary. However:

- **Epidermal (sebaceous) cysts** may be excised if affecting quality of life or if there is infection risk
- **Seborrhoeic keratoses** can be treated with cryotherapy, curettage and cautery, or shave excision if desired

### For Actinic Keratoses (Premalignant)

Your doctor may use one of the following treatments:

**Cryotherapy** (liquid nitrogen)
- Cheap and effective; causes crusting, blistering, and shedding of sun-damaged skin
- Avoid on the face or below the knees (risk of hypopigmentation and delayed healing)
- Lesions may recur and can be retreated

**Cautery**
- Burns the keratosis off; a scab forms and heals within a few weeks, leaving a small scar

**Fluorouracil cream (Efudix)**
- Fully funded; no specialist approval needed
- Useful for widespread actinic changes
- Complete clearance in ~50% of patients; reduction in lesion count in ~80%
- Applied thinly twice daily to one area at a time for 10–30 days (maximum area ~500 cm²)
- The skin will become red, raw, and uncomfortable — this is expected
- Stop if skin becomes very red with burning, swelling, and weeping
- Avoid sunlight; do not use sunscreen on treated areas during treatment
- Skin may take up to 6 weeks to return to normal after finishing treatment
- Review at 2 weeks (to confirm adequate reaction) and 2–3 months after completing treatment
- If lesions persist at 3 months, a biopsy or surgical excision is arranged — repeat treatment is not appropriate
- **Not for use in:** DPD enzyme deficiency, pregnancy, or breastfeeding

**Imiquimod cream (Aldara)**
- Fully funded; no specialist approval needed
- Complete clearance in 48–57% of patients
- Applied at bedtime, 5 nights per week for 6 weeks; wash treated area each morning
- Normal response includes inflammation (often worse in week 2), followed by skin erosion, then healing
- Pause treatment if inflammation becomes excessive; add paused time to complete the 6-week course
- Stop immediately if systemic symptoms occur (fever, flu-like illness, fatigue, headache, nausea, diarrhoea, muscle pain) and see your doctor
- Assess response at 3 months after treatment; if lesions persist, biopsy or surgical excision is needed — repeat treatment is not appropriate
- **Not for use in:** hyperkeratotic or hypertrophic lesions, lesions within 1 cm of hairline/eyes/ears/nose/lips, immunosuppressed patients, previous imiquimod treatment to same area, pregnancy or breastfeeding

### For Atypical or Congenital Melanocytic Naevi

- Annual skin check; consider serial photography
- Excision biopsy if any suspicious symptoms develop (see above)
- Specialist assessment is needed if the congenital naevus is >20 cm, crosses the spine, or there are multiple congenital naevi or neurocutaneous melanosis

### For Cutaneous Horn

- Complete excision is recommended because malignancy cannot be excluded on clinical grounds
- Higher suspicion of SCC if: older or male patient, large or painful lesion with indurated base, location on nose, ears, backs of hands, scalp, forearms, face, or penis, or a wide/red base

---

## Long-Term Follow-Up

For all premalignant lesions (actinic keratoses, atypical/congenital naevi, cutaneous horn):

- Follow-up after treatment to confirm resolution
- Annual skin checks ongoing
- Your doctor will assess the original lesion, perform a full body check, and review any other areas of concern

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor may refer you to a skin cancer specialist if:

- Congenital melanocytic naevus is >20 cm, crosses the spine, or there are multiple congenital naevi or neurocutaneous melanosis
- You are an organ transplant recipient with premalignant skin lesions (for surveillance)
- Excision of a benign or premalignant lesion is requested but cannot be performed in general practice

---

## Sun Protection

For all patients with skin lesions, particularly premalignant ones:

- Avoid direct sunlight during peak UV radiation hours
- Slip, Slap, Slop and Wrap
- Apply sunscreen at least 20 minutes before going outside, reapply 10–20 minutes after going outside, and every 2 hours thereafter
- Avoid sunbeds and artificial UV radiation

**Some medications increase sun sensitivity** — ask your doctor if any of yours do. These include tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, sulfonamides, NSAIDs, diuretics (frusemide, hydrochlorothiazide), retinoids (isotretinoin), sulfonylureas, phenothiazines, hydroxychloroquine, amiodarone, and quinine-containing medications.

Report any new or changing skin lesions to your doctor promptly.
