# Head Injury and Concussion in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers children aged under 15 years who have sustained a head injury or concussion. It guides the initial assessment of injury severity and determines the appropriate level of care.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance (111) immediately if the child has any of the following:

- Symptoms or signs of significant head injury, including any abnormal neurological findings
- Suspected non-accidental injury (NAI)
- Unexplained delay in seeking help after the injury
- Any abnormal AVPU score (not Alert), or child Glasgow Coma Scale (GCS) of 13 or less at any time
- GCS of 14 or less at 2 hours from the time of injury

---

## Background

Head injury in children differs from adults for several important reasons:

- Children's developmental stage, skull characteristics, and brain response to trauma are all different from adults
- In young or pre-verbal children, a history of loss of consciousness may be unobtainable
- The Glasgow Coma Scale can be unreliable in young children — the AVPU scale is used instead for children under 2 years
- Depressed skull fractures are more common after a direct blow in children
- Brain injury can occur without a visible skull fracture
- Blunt head trauma in children can be rapidly followed by acute brain swelling, even with minimal visible injury
- Significant blood loss can occur from a scalp haematoma (e.g. sub-galeal haematomas can hold large volumes)
- The true facts about how the injury occurred may not always be disclosed
- Shaken babies can sustain significant brain trauma without an obvious head injury history

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess the child by:

### History of the event

This is particularly important in children under 2 years, but applies to all ages. Your doctor will ask about:

- Who witnessed the injury and what exactly happened, including date and time
- The child's developmental level (e.g. rolling, standing, walking, climbing)
- Any features that seem inconsistent or concerning in the story
- Any unexplained delay in seeking care
- Past history of repeated injuries

### Non-accidental injury — when to suspect it

Your doctor will consider whether the injury may be non-accidental if:

- Injury details are absent, vague, or change over time or between caregivers
- The injury is inconsistent with the child's developmental stage (e.g. a newborn "rolling off a bed")
- There has been an unexplained delay in seeking help
- There is a history or findings of repeated injuries
- There is unexplained loss of consciousness, persistent vomiting, or bleeding from the nose or mouth in a young infant

### Symptom history

Your doctor will ask about:

- What the child recalls of the event
- Loss of consciousness (confirmed by a witness)
- Seizures
- Behaviour and activity since the injury
- Headache or neck pain
- Nausea or vomiting
- Drowsiness or irritability
- Dizziness
- Visual changes

Note: Seizures that occur immediately at the time of impact (impact seizures) are not predictive of serious brain injury on their own.

### Co-morbidities that increase risk

Your doctor will ask about:

- Previous head injuries
- Intra-cerebral shunt or VP shunt
- Arteriovenous (AV) malformation
- Bleeding disorders or history of coagulopathy

### Level of consciousness

Your doctor will assess:

- **AVPU scale** for children under 2 years or pre-verbal children:
  - **A**lert
  - Responds to **V**oice
  - Responds to **P**ain (purposeful or non-purposeful)
  - **U**nresponsive
- **Paediatric Glasgow Coma Scale** for children aged 2 years and older

### Physical observations

Your doctor will measure:

- Height and weight
- Head circumference (in all children under 1 year — important baseline)
- Temperature, pulse, blood pressure, respiratory rate
- Oxygen saturation
- Pupil size and reactivity, and whether a squint is present
- Blood glucose

### Full physical examination

Your doctor will examine the child from head to toe, including:

- **Head:** Scalp haematomas (can be subtle, especially in dark-skinned children or those with thick hair); depressed skull fractures; signs of base of skull fracture ("raccoon eyes," Battle sign — bruising behind the ear, blood or CSF from the ear canal, blood behind the eardrum); pinna bruising
- **Face:** Facial fractures, mouth injuries, frenulum tears (associated with non-accidental injury)
- **Neck:** Immobilised if cervical injury cannot be excluded
- **Trunk and limbs:** Bruises, swelling, deformity, burns
- **Neurological:** Cranial nerve examination and limb neurology

## Features of Significant Head Injury

Your doctor will check for the following — any one of these indicates significant head injury requiring emergency hospital assessment:

- Clinical evidence of depressed, open, penetrating, or base of skull fracture
- Tense fontanelle (in infants)
- GCS 14 or less at 2 hours after injury, or 13 or less at any time
- Child under 2 years with any abnormal AVPU score or abnormal paediatric GCS
- Child under 1 year with a scalp haematoma larger than 5 cm
- Any focal neurological deficit
- Bradycardia (slow heart rate) associated with hypertension — a sign of raised intracranial pressure
- High impact injury:
  - High-speed road traffic accident (pedestrian, cyclist, or vehicle occupant)
  - Fall from more than 1 metre
  - High-speed injury from a projectile or object
- Suspected non-accidental injury
- Intoxication
- Severe headache
- Abnormal drowsiness or irritability
- Out-of-character agitation or unusual behaviour
- Post-traumatic seizure (occurring more than 1 hour after injury)
- Witnessed loss of consciousness
- Post-traumatic amnesia lasting longer than 5 minutes
- Two or more separate episodes of vomiting after the injury
- History of coagulopathy (bleeding disorder)

## Management

### Immediate management

For an acutely unwell or unstable child:

- Your doctor will manage airway, breathing, and circulation
- Cervical spine protection if the mechanism is uncertain
- Airway protection if GCS is 8 or less
- Blood glucose checked, especially if there is prolonged sleepiness or reduced consciousness
- Transfer by ambulance to an emergency department if any features of significant head injury are present

Note: children under 3 years can be particularly difficult to assess, and if there is any uncertainty, hospital assessment is recommended.

### Monitoring in general practice

Your doctor may consider monitoring the child in the clinic for a minimum of 4 hours from the time of injury if:

- GCS = 14 at less than 2 hours after the event, without features of significant head injury, OR
- There are clinical concerns including:
  - Amnesia of short duration (less than 5 minutes)
  - A single episode of vomiting
  - Significant headache
  - Any symptoms or signs of concussion within the 4-hour window of injury
  - Unsuitable home circumstances for observation

During monitoring, the clinic will carry out:

- Neurological observations (AVPU or GCS, pupil reactivity, limb movements, oxygen saturation, heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate) every 30 minutes until GCS reaches 15, then every 30 minutes for a further 2 hours, then hourly until discharge
- Doctor reassessment at 2 hours and again at 4 hours
- Simple pain relief — paracetamol is used if needed. Aspirin and anti-platelet medications are avoided. If stronger analgesia seems necessary, your doctor will first consider whether a more serious or evolving brain injury may be the reason

### Discharge home

Your doctor will allow the child to go home when all of the following apply:

- No referral or further monitoring is indicated, or after the 4-hour observation period is complete
- GCS is 15 (fully alert and oriented)
- Symptoms have improved
- Vital signs are stable
- A responsible adult can closely monitor the child at home for the next 48 hours

At discharge your doctor will provide written information about head injury warning signs and what to watch for overnight. Your doctor may also call the following day to check on the child's progress.

---

## Return to Activities and School

Your doctor will advise:

- Physical and mental rest for 24 to 48 hours after the injury
- A graduated return to activities — only progress each step if the child has no symptoms. If symptoms return, step back to the previous level for 24 hours until symptoms resolve
- Informing the child's teacher, coach, or sports supervisor about the injury
- Full return to contact sport should only occur after a minimum of 21 days post-injury, once the child has progressed through stage 5 of their recovery pathway and remains symptom-free during training. Medical clearance is strongly recommended before returning to contact sport

Your doctor may advise older children and their families to follow the ACC Sport Concussion in New Zealand National Guidelines for a graduated return to education, work, and sport.

---

## Follow-Up (7 to 10 Days After Injury)

Your doctor will arrange a follow-up appointment to check for signs of concussion syndrome (mild traumatic brain injury):

### Children under 5 years

Your doctor will look for new and persistent signs such as:

- Excessive crying
- Persistent headache
- Poor attention
- Change in sleep patterns
- Regression or lack of progression of developmental milestones
- Balance or coordination problems

If any of these are present, your doctor will refer to an ACC concussion service.

### Children aged 5 to 15 years

Your doctor will use the SCAT5 Symptom Evaluation (scored out of 22):

- **Score of 1:** Review with repeat symptom evaluation in 1 to 2 weeks
- **Score of 2 to 5:** Your doctor will complete a full SCAT5 (or BIST tool if aged 8 years or older) and manage according to results:
  - If both symptoms and physical/cognitive signs of concussion are found: referred to a concussion clinic
  - If symptoms only (no physical or cognitive signs): repeat symptom evaluation in 2 weeks
- **Score of 5 or higher:** Indicates significant traumatic brain injury symptoms — your doctor will refer to an ACC concussion service

### Referral to ACC concussion service

Your doctor will refer to an ACC concussion service if:

- Child under 5 years with any new and persistent concussion signs or symptoms
- Child aged 5 to 14 years where SCAT5 or Child-SCAT5 assessment shows symptoms and physical or cognitive signs of concussion
- Child aged 8 years or older with a score of 66 or higher on the BIST symptom checklist
