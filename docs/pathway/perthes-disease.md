# Perthes Disease

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers Perthes disease — avascular necrosis of the femoral head in children — including how it is assessed and managed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

Your doctor will urgently rule out:

- **Septic arthritis** — joint infection requiring emergency assessment
- **Osteomyelitis** — bone infection requiring emergency assessment

If a child has limb pain severe enough that they refuse to bear weight or move the limb, this warrants acute orthopaedic assessment the same day.

---

## Background

Perthes disease occurs when blood flow to the femoral head (the ball at the top of the thigh bone) is interrupted, causing the bone to collapse and then gradually heal and reconstitute. Key facts:

- The healing process takes 2 to 4 years
- The cause of the initial loss of blood flow is unknown
- It affects children aged 2 to 12 years, most commonly aged 4 to 8
- It is at least 4 times more common in boys than girls
- Both hips are affected in 10 to 20% of cases
- It runs in families in approximately 10% of cases

Most children with Perthes disease are otherwise fit and healthy.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. Take a full developmental and medical history
2. Ask about:
   - An occasional limp (often the earliest sign)
   - Pain in the knee, thigh, groin, or hip when bearing weight on the affected side
   - Pain that is worse with activity and better with rest
   - Worsening limp and pain over time
3. Examine for:
   - Abnormalities of other joints and skin (to help distinguish from a more widespread condition)
   - Slight muscle wasting around the hip
   - Limb shortening (if the condition has been present for some time)
   - Reduced range of hip movement (stiffness)
4. Consider other diagnoses, including:
   - Bone or joint infection
   - Slipped upper femoral epiphysis (SUFE)
   - Transient synovitis
   - Skeletal injury
   - Malignancy
   - Systemic arthritis
5. Arrange an X-ray of the pelvis and lateral hip (including a frog-leg view)

**About the X-ray:** Early Perthes disease may show only subtle changes such as joint space widening or a crack beneath the bone surface. If symptoms continue for more than 6 weeks and the X-ray is initially normal, your doctor may repeat it. Later in the disease the femoral head appears dense and collapsed. MRI can detect early disease but is usually ordered by the specialist.

---

## Management

Management varies depending on the stage of disease and the child's age, ranging from activity restriction through to physiotherapy, bracing, or occasionally surgery. Your doctor will guide you through this process.

**The goal** is to keep the femoral head as spherical as possible and maintain hip range of movement so the child has good hip function in the long term. The disease does heal — management is about protecting the joint during that process.

### Activity Restriction

Your doctor will advise restricting the child's physical activities until they have been assessed by a specialist. This includes:

- Taking the child out of competitive and high-impact sports
- Walking and non-competitive swimming are generally allowed

Be aware that removing a child from sport can have negative psychological effects. It helps to explain to parents, teachers, friends, and coaches that the restriction is to allow healing and minimise future hip arthritis. Be honest about the time frame — it can be several years.

### Pain Relief

Your doctor may consider:

- **Paracetamol** for mild pain
- **NSAIDs** (e.g., ibuprofen) for more significant pain

### Mobility Aids

If the child has significant pain, your doctor may recommend crutches or a wheelchair to reduce weight-bearing and allow the hip to rest. This is usually arranged through the hospital.

### Specialist Referral

- If septic arthritis or osteomyelitis is suspected, or the child refuses to bear weight, your doctor will arrange **acute paediatric orthopaedic assessment**
- If the X-ray confirms Perthes disease, your doctor will arrange **non-acute paediatric orthopaedic assessment**

Age at diagnosis is the key factor in prognosis — younger children generally do better. The specialist will guide ongoing management based on imaging findings and age.
