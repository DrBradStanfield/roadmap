# Hidradenitis Suppurativa

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Hidradenitis suppurativa (HS) is a chronic inflammatory skin disease causing recurrent nodules, abscesses, and sinus tracts, mainly in skin folds. This pathway covers how it is assessed and managed.

---

## Background

Hidradenitis suppurativa is caused by follicular hyperkeratosis, dilation, rupture, and inflammation, resulting in discharging sinuses and scarring. It occurs mostly in the axillae, groins, under the breasts, buttocks, and the anogenital region.

- Onset is typically soon after puberty. Females are affected approximately 3 times more frequently than males.
- Prevalence is estimated at up to 4% globally. About a third of affected people have a family history of HS.
- Severity is worsened by smoking, other nicotine use, and obesity.
- Diagnosis is often delayed.
- Associated with increased risk of metabolic syndrome, cardiovascular disease, depression, anxiety, sexual dysfunction, inflammatory bowel disease, ankylosing spondylitis, and diabetes.
- Treatment typically involves multiple medications (often off-label, often long term) with modest and variable results. The most effective measures are smoking cessation, weight reduction, biologics such as adalimumab, and surgery.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Ask about onset, duration, treatments to date, current lesions, and any psychosocial or psychosexual distress.
- Ask about risk factors: nicotine use, obesity, hormonal factors (hirsutism, PCOS, metabolic syndrome), inflammatory bowel disease, other follicular occlusion disorders (acne, pilonidal disease, dissecting cellulitis of the scalp), and family history.
- Examine the scalp, trunk, skin folds, axillae, groins, buttocks, and anogenital region, looking for:
  - Painful papules and pustules (smaller than 5 mm), firm nodules (5 mm or larger), and fluctuant abscesses
  - Sinus tracts and fistulas
  - Hypertrophic scarring

### Hurley Staging

Your doctor will stage the disease using the Hurley classification:

| Stage | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **Hurley 1** | One or more abscesses without sinus tracts or hypertrophic scarring |
| **Hurley 2** | Recurrent single or multiple abscesses with sinus tract formation and hypertrophic scars; lesions widely separated |
| **Hurley 3** | Diffuse involvement or multiple interconnected tracts and abscesses across the entire area |

### Investigations

- Blood tests (FBC, electrolytes, HbA1c) when managing co-morbidities or complications.
- If cutaneous Crohn's disease is suspected, punch biopsy (or deep incisional biopsy if punch biopsy is negative but suspicion remains high).
- Swabs are **not** routinely collected — there is usually no bacterial pathogen.

### Differential Diagnosis

- Staphylococcal skin infections, including cellulitis
- Epidermoid cysts
- Cutaneous Crohn's disease (skin spots, plaques, nodules; non-caseating granulomas on histology)

### Co-morbidities to Consider

Hypertension, cardiovascular disease, stroke, obesity, diabetes, hyperlipidaemia, metabolic syndrome, ankylosing spondylitis, depression, anxiety.

## Management

### Step 1 — General Advice (All Stages)

These measures can control mild disease and prevent further progression:

- **Be nicotine-free.** All nicotine-containing products (cigarettes, nicotine replacement therapy, vaping) impair wound healing.
- **Maintain a healthy weight.**
- **Use simple analgesia** for pain.
- **Wound care:**
  - Apply an antiseptic skin cleanser to wounds once a day for 5 days to prevent relapses. Options include:
    - Vinegar soak (2 tablespoons of vinegar in 2 cups of warm water; can apply twice daily)
    - Benzoyl peroxide 5% wash (available at supermarkets)
    - Chlorhexidine gluconate 4% solution (adults; can be drying — moisturiser may be needed) or 1% lotion for sensitive skin
    - Octenidine wash lotion for those who cannot tolerate chlorhexidine
  - Soak a flannel or paper towel in the solution, apply to wet skin for at least 5 minutes, then rinse with warm water without rubbing.
  - Pat dry and apply emollient.
  - Use simple absorbent dressings where possible; avoid adhesive dressings and tapes.
- **Prioritise mental well-being.**
- **Hair removal** does not treat HS. If removing hair for cosmetic reasons, use care (all methods can cause microabrasions), apply shaving cream or gel when wet-shaving, or consider laser hair removal.

### Step 2 — First-Line Antibiotic

**Doxycycline 100 mg once daily for 3 months.** If there is a good response, repeat courses of doxycycline as needed to maintain disease control.

### Step 3 — Second-Line Antibiotics (Hurley Stage 2 or 3, poor response to doxycycline)

Your doctor will seek dermatology advice before prescribing. Treatment is **rifampicin + clindamycin for 10 weeks**:

- **Rifampicin 600 mg once daily**
  - Blood tests (creatinine, liver function) before starting.
  - Common side effects: nausea, gastrointestinal upset, orange-stained urine, staining of contact lenses.
  - Significant drug interactions — including reduced reliability of oral contraceptive pills and implants during treatment and for 1 month after. Alternative contraception (e.g. injection, IUD) is recommended during this period.
  - Liver function monitored in patients with alcohol dependence or liver disease.
- **Clindamycin 300 mg twice daily**

### Step 4 — Adjuvant Treatments

These may be used alongside antibiotics:

- **Metformin** (titrated to 1.5 g/day) — has anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, and metabolic benefits, and reduces follicular occlusion. Review at 12 weeks; continue long-term if beneficial.
- **In women:**
  - **Combined oral contraceptive (COC):** Reduces free androgens. COCs with anti-androgenic progestogens (cyproterone, drospirenone) are second-line due to higher VTE risk.
  - **Spironolactone** (25–200 mg daily, commonly 100 mg): Anti-androgen and anti-inflammatory. Usually prescribed alongside a COC. Not safe in pregnancy — reliable contraception required. Check blood pressure and renal function before starting; repeat potassium at 1 month.

### Step 5 — Other Off-Label Medical Treatments

- **Second-line:** Intralesional steroids (persistent localised disease), acitretin, or a short course of systemic steroids.
- **Third-line:** Isotretinoin, dapsone — dermatology advice and endorsement required before prescribing.

### Step 6 — Biologic Therapy (Hurley Stage 2 or 3, failure or intolerance of antibiotics)

Your doctor will refer for non-acute dermatology assessment to consider biologic therapy such as **adalimumab**. Clinical studies show adalimumab reduced HS severity in 42–59% of patients versus 27% on placebo.

Your doctor will include in the referral: clinical photographs, a quality-of-life score (e.g. DLQI), and a count of active lesions (nodules ≥1 cm, abscesses, draining fistulas).

### Step 7 — Surgery

Surgery can reduce morbidity in Hurley stage 2 or 3 disease not responding to medical treatment, though recurrence or new-site disease is common.

- **Incision and drainage:** Provides acute symptom relief for painful abscesses but does not alter the underlying disease.
- **De-roofing surgery:** A minimally invasive electrosurgical technique removing the roof of an abscess, cyst, or sinus tract; wound heals by secondary intention. Good patient acceptance.
- **Wide local excision:** Best outcomes but rarely performed due to disease extent and lengthy recovery. Performed under dermatology supervision; referral to plastic or general surgery made by the dermatologist if appropriate.

---

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

There are no specific emergency red flags for HS itself, but seek prompt assessment for:

- An acutely painful, fluctuant, rapidly enlarging abscess (may need incision and drainage for pain relief)
- Signs of secondary bacterial infection (spreading redness, systemic fever, malaise)
- Significant psychosocial or psychosexual distress

Always discuss new or worsening symptoms with your doctor.
