# Wrist Pain in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of wrist pain in adults, including acute and chronic presentations.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

Talk to your doctor promptly if wrist pain is associated with signs of **septic arthritis** (hot, swollen joint with fever or systemic illness). This requires urgent assessment.

Urgent imaging (within 2 weeks) is recommended for:

- Persistent deep pain unrelated to activity, or unexplained night pain
- New unexplained bony swelling or joint deformity

---

## Background

Wrist conditions can be either acute or chronic:

- **Acute pain** generally occurs as a result of trauma or inflammatory conditions (e.g. overuse, infection, gout)
- **Chronic wrist pain** is defined as pain lasting longer than 3 months

The wrist includes the radiocarpal joint, midcarpal joint, and distal radioulnar joint.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- **Trauma history** — type and force involved; whether the injury relates to an accident
- **Pain details** — location, type, severity, duration, timing (night pain, activity-related, rest pain, episodic), and triggers
- **Treatments already tried** — analgesics and other non-medication treatments
- **Other symptoms** — paraesthesia, numbness, swelling, radiation of pain, systemic features, other joints affected (including the neck)
- **Activities and function** — hand dominance, occupation, sport, hobbies, and impact of pain on daily life
- **Medical history** — diabetes, gout, peripheral neuropathy, arthritis (OA or inflammatory), skin conditions, smoking, previous surgery on hands or wrist

Physical examination typically includes:

- **Looking at the joint** — comparing both wrists for swelling (localised or generalised), wasting (e.g. thenar eminence in carpal tunnel syndrome), deformity, skin changes, and scars
- **Palpating the joints methodically** — working from the radial to the ulnar aspect, feeling for warmth, boggy swelling (synovitis), and tendon thickening; specific areas include the anatomical snuffbox (scaphoid), first CMCJ, radial styloid (de Quervain), and ulnar fovea (TFCC)
- **Assessing movement** — range of motion (flexion, extension, radial and ulnar deviation, supination, pronation) and motor nerve distribution
- **Examining adjacent joints** — especially the cervical spine for referred or radicular pain

**Special tests** your doctor may perform:

- **Tinel and Phalen tests** — for carpal tunnel syndrome. Neither is particularly sensitive or specific; negative tests do not exclude CTS, nor do positive tests confirm it
- **Grind test** — for first carpometacarpal joint osteoarthritis (grasp thumb metacarpal, circular movement, axial compression)
- **Finkelstein test** — for de Quervain tenosynovitis (flexion of thumb across palm followed by ulnar deviation causes sharp pain)

**Blood tests** may be arranged if inflammatory or systemic causes are suspected — for example FBC, CRP, urate, creatinine, LFTs, rheumatoid factor, anti-CCP, ANA.

---

## Imaging

Your doctor will determine appropriate imaging based on the suspected cause:

- **Urgent X-ray (AP and lateral, within 2 weeks)** — for persistent deep pain unrelated to activity or unexplained night pain, or new unexplained bony swelling or joint deformity
- **Routine X-ray** — for undiagnosed joint pain present more than 4 weeks (if not typical of OA), OA with significant functional impairment, multiple joint swelling for more than 6 weeks with suspected inflammatory arthritis, or pain in a previous arthroplasty
- **Ultrasound** — useful for suspected soft tissue or tendon injuries
- **MRI** — may be needed for specific diagnoses (e.g. TFCC tears, scapholunate instability); availability varies

## Possible Causes

### Radial Wrist Pain

**First Carpometacarpal Joint Osteoarthritis (CMCJ OA)**
- Tenderness and crepitus at the base of the thumb; squaring of joint on inspection
- Grind test positive
- X-ray shows OA changes

**Flexor Carpi Radialis Tendinitis**
- Middle-aged patients; related to trauma or arthritis
- Palmar-sided radial pain, tenderness over distal FCR tendon
- Pain with resisted wrist flexion and resisted radial deviation
- X-ray usually normal; MRI shows tendinitis if needed

**Scaphoid Fracture (Acute)**
- After a fall onto an outstretched hand
- Tenderness in the anatomical snuffbox
- Requires urgent assessment and imaging

**Non-union of a Scaphoid Fracture**
- Wrist pain associated with a poorly healed scaphoid fracture (may be recent or old)
- Pain on activity, relieved with rest; mild swelling over dorsoradial wrist; reduced range of motion and grip strength
- Long-standing non-union results in wrist arthritis
- X-rays will show chronic non-union and arthritis if old

**Scapholunate Instability**
- Disruption of the ligament holding the scaphoid and lunate together; related to trauma, inflammatory arthritis, or degenerative processes
- Clunking, feeling of wrist instability, weaker grip strength, radial-sided wrist pain
- X-ray may show widening of the scapholunate space; MRI may be needed

**De Quervain Tenosynovitis**
- Pain over the radial styloid; positive Finkelstein test

**C6 Radiculopathy**
- Referred pain from the cervical spine; assess neck movement and neurological signs

---

### Ulnar Wrist Pain

**Extensor Carpi Ulnaris Tendinopathy**
- Pain with rotational wrist activity after minor trauma or overuse
- Tenderness over ECU with mild swelling; pain on resisted ulnar deviation

**Triangular Fibrocartilage Complex (TFCC) Tears**
- TFCC connects the radius and ulna and stabilises the distal radioulnar joint
- History of fall onto outstretched hand with rotation or twist; degenerative tears may occur with minimal injury
- Pain over the fovea; possible prominence of ulnar head; reduced or painful forearm rotation; pain with gripping
- X-ray usually normal; MRI required for diagnosis

**Radial Malunions**
- After a radius fracture heals shortened, leaving a relatively longer ulna (ulnar-carpal impaction)
- Pain on ulnar side with gripping, lifting, and rotation; possible dinner fork deformity
- May result in carpal tunnel syndrome
- X-ray shows shortened radius and ulnar-carpal impaction

**Distal Radio-Ulnar Joint (DRUJ) Osteoarthritis**
- Middle-aged to elderly patients; ulnar-sided pain with activity (especially rotation)
- Possible history of fracture; prominent lower end of ulna; reduced rotation
- X-ray shows OA in DRUJ

**Ulnar Nerve Compression (UNC)**
- Most commonly occurs at the elbow; occasionally at the wrist (due to ganglion or trauma)
- Numbness and tingling over the little finger and ulnar side of ring finger
- Symptoms provoked by sustained elbow flexion, leaning on the elbow, or prolonged grip
- Weakness of intrinsic hand muscles (finger abduction/adduction, crossing); thenar muscles spared; may progress to claw hand

---

### Central Wrist Pain

**Carpal Tunnel Syndrome**
- Median nerve compression at the wrist
- Numbness and tingling in thumb, index, middle finger and radial side of ring finger
- Positive Phalen and/or Tinel tests (note: limited sensitivity/specificity)

**Kienböck's Disease (Avascular Necrosis of the Lunate)**
- Most common in males aged 20–40
- Marked wrist weakness, reduced grip strength
- Tenderness centrally over the lunate; possible reduced range of motion
- X-ray findings depend on disease stage (may show sclerosis or collapse of lunate)

**Ganglia**
- Fluid-filled cysts arising from joint or tendon sheath
- Visible or palpable lump, often dorsal or volar wrist

---

### Multiple Pain Locations

**Gout or Pseudogout**
- Often very severe pain with associated swelling and erythema
- Blood test: elevated urate (gout); crystals on joint aspiration

**Inflammatory Arthritis**
- Relentless pain, generalised swelling, often multiple joints
- Consider rheumatoid arthritis; blood tests (RhF, anti-CCP, ANA, CRP) support diagnosis

**Complex Regional Pain Syndrome (CRPS)**
- Continuing regional pain disproportionate to any inciting event
- Budapest diagnostic criteria require:
  1. Continuing pain disproportionate to the inciting event
  2. At least 1 symptom in 3 of 4 categories: sensory (hyperalgesia, allodynia), vasomotor (temperature or colour asymmetry), sudomotor/oedema, motor/trophic (reduced ROM, weakness, tremor, trophic changes)
  3. At least 1 sign observed at evaluation in 2 or more of the same categories
  4. No other diagnosis better explains the signs and symptoms
- Acute onset in children and adolescents should be treated and referred urgently

---

## Management

Management depends on the identified cause. Your healthcare provider may recommend:

### Radial Wrist Pain

**CMCJ Osteoarthritis**
- Analgesia, splinting, hand therapy
- Corticosteroid injection into the CMCJ
- Surgical options if conservative treatment fails

**Scaphoid Fracture (Acute)**
- Immobilisation and urgent orthopaedic assessment

**Scaphoid Non-union (without arthritis, younger patients)**
- Analgesia (paracetamol, NSAIDs), splint for symptomatic relief
- Surgical management (bone graft and open reduction internal fixation) may be an option

**Scaphoid Non-union (with arthritis, older patients)**
- Analgesia, hand therapy, splints
- Corticosteroid injection into the radiocarpal wrist joint (not into the non-union site)
- Surgical salvage options (partial or total wrist fusion) if secondary arthritic changes are present

**Scapholunate Instability**
- Analgesia and rest, hand therapy, splints
- Surgery if acute or if functional limitations are significant

**De Quervain Tenosynovitis**
- NSAIDs, rest, thumb spica splint
- Corticosteroid injection; surgery if persistent

**Flexor Carpi Radialis Tendinitis**
- NSAIDs, rest, splint
- Corticosteroid injection into the trapezial tunnel (ideally imaging-guided due to risk of tendon rupture)
- Surgical release if conservative treatment fails

---

### Ulnar Wrist Pain

**TFCC Tears**
- Analgesia, splint and rest for 6 weeks, followed by mobilisation and strengthening with a hand therapist
- Surgical repair may be needed, especially if complete avulsion

**Radial Malunions**
- Hand therapy; surgery after 6–9 months if symptoms are ongoing

**Ulnar Nerve Compression**
- Conservative measures for at least 6 weeks: avoid triggering activities (leaning on elbow, sleeping with elbow bent, repetitive hand tool use), activity modification, splints at elbow or wrist
- Surgery considered for persistent cases (3–6 months duration) or severe cases with significant functional impairment, persistent numbness, muscle wasting, or claw hand

**DRUJ Osteoarthritis**
- Analgesia, splintage
- Corticosteroid injection into the distal radioulnar joint may be beneficial
- Surgery is an option in appropriate patients

**Extensor Carpi Ulnaris Tendinitis**
- NSAIDs, rest with splintage
- Corticosteroid injection into the 6th dorsal compartment
- Surgery if symptoms are ongoing

---

### Central Wrist Pain

**Carpal Tunnel Syndrome**
- Wrist splinting (especially at night), activity modification
- Corticosteroid injection for temporary relief
- Surgical decompression (carpal tunnel release) if conservative treatment fails

**Kienböck's Disease**
- Immobilisation in a neutral below-elbow cast (thumb not included) for 6 weeks if acute
- Wrist splint for later presentations
- Surgical assessment if pain and disability persist; options depend on disease stage

**Ganglia**
- Many resolve spontaneously; observation is appropriate if asymptomatic
- Aspiration or surgical excision if symptomatic

---

### Multiple Locations

**Gout or Pseudogout**
- NSAIDs, colchicine, or corticosteroids for acute attacks
- Long-term urate-lowering therapy for recurrent gout — discuss with your doctor

**Inflammatory Arthritis**
- Discuss with your doctor; rheumatology referral may be recommended for disease-modifying therapy

**Complex Regional Pain Syndrome**
- Multi-disciplinary management; early referral recommended
- Acute onset in children and adolescents requires urgent assessment

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your healthcare provider may recommend specialist assessment for:

- Any red flags (signs of septic arthritis or suspected fracture) — urgent orthopaedic assessment
- Failure to resolve with conservative measures
- Significant functional impairment
- Diagnostically difficult cases where a second opinion is needed
- Hand therapy referral for help with splints, exercises, and rehabilitation
