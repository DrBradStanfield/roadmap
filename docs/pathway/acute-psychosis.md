# Acute Psychosis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers new or acute presentations suggestive of psychosis — including first episodes and early warning signs.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Help

Call 111 immediately if:

- Imminent risk of harm to self or others
- Unexplained generalised functional and social decline in a young person
- Suspected organic cause (e.g. brain tumour, delirium)
- Suicidal or violent intent
- Obvious psychotic symptoms, particularly if the person is agitated or distressed
- Command hallucinations with threatening content
- Severe self-neglect

---

## Background

Psychosis refers to symptoms of severe mental disorder characterised by:

- **Delusions** — false, fixed, irrational beliefs not shared by others of similar cultural background
- **Hallucinations** — sensory perceptions without external stimulus, most commonly auditory
- **Thought disorder** — disorganised thinking with disordered speech patterns

Other features may include:

- **Catatonia** — abnormality of movement and behaviour (e.g. repetitive overactivity, or catalepsy/resistance to passive movement)
- **Negative symptoms** — alogia (poverty of speech), flat affect, loss of motivation, anhedonia, social withdrawal
- **Cognitive deficits** — attention, memory, executive function difficulties
- **Secondary features** — sleep disturbance, depression, anxiety

Around 3 in 100 people experience a psychotic episode. Two thirds occur before age 25, often with a gradual onset over more than 6 months. Early detection and treatment significantly improves long-term outcomes.

### Causes of Psychosis

- Schizophrenia and related psychotic disorders
- Major depressive disorder with psychotic features
- Bipolar disorder (in both elevated/manic and depressed phases)
- Personality disorders
- Organic causes:
  - Underlying medical conditions (see Organic Causes section below)
  - Substance- or medication-induced psychosis

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you through direct observation and conversation. If you are clearly distressed, agitated, or acutely psychotic, they will not attempt a full assessment in the clinic — they will arrange urgent help instead.

### Early Warning Signs

Your doctor may consider a diagnosis of psychosis if early warning signs are present, including:

- Changes in mood (anxiety, depression, irritability, apathy, mood swings)
- Changes in thinking (poor concentration, memory problems, unusual preoccupations)
- Changes in behaviour (odd, aggressive, or disruptive behaviour; social withdrawal; diminished self-care)
- Attenuated perceptual abnormalities (e.g. hearing one's name called, mild suspiciousness or paranoid ideas)
- Sleep disturbance or loss of energy
- Declining performance at school or work
- Becoming distant or mistrustful of friends and family

### Symptoms of Psychosis

**Positive symptoms** (presence of abnormal mental processes):
- Delusions — false, fixed beliefs
- Hallucinations — most commonly auditory
- Disorganised thoughts and behaviours

**Negative symptoms** (absence of normal function):
- Avolition — unable to persist with goal-directed activities
- Alogia — diminished speech
- Anhedonia — reduced enjoyment
- Asociality — withdrawal from social activities
- Flattened affect — poor emotional expression

### Mental Health Screening

Your doctor will assess:

- Severity of symptoms and degree of functional impairment
- Insight into current illness
- Presence of other mental health conditions (e.g. mania, depression)
- Risk to self (financial, sexual, driving, self-harm, suicide, exploitation) and risk to others (threats, aggression, violence)
- Medication compliance and use of alcohol, supplements, or substances
- Stressors and quality of social support from family/whānau and community

### Medical, Social, and Personal History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Co-existing conditions such as alcohol and drug use, anxiety, eating disorders, personality disorders, ADHD, or metabolic syndrome
- Family history of mental illness and developmental history
- Education, work history, and current social situation (housing, finances, dependants)
- Recent major life events
- Premorbid personality (e.g. relationship patterns, impulsivity)

### Organic Causes to Exclude

Psychosis can result from underlying medical conditions. Your doctor will consider:

- Brain tumour or cyst
- Stroke or cerebrovascular accident (especially frontal, temporal, and limbic areas)
- Dementia (including Alzheimer's disease)
- Degenerative brain diseases (e.g. Parkinson's disease, Huntington's disease)
- HIV and other brain infections (e.g. herpes encephalitis, neurosyphilis)
- Epilepsy
- Delirium (from infection, metabolic derangement, or alcohol withdrawal)
- Substance intoxication — alcohol, amphetamine, hallucinogens, cannabis, cocaine, ecstasy, solvents
- Substance withdrawal — alcohol and sedatives
- Thyroid disorders (hyper- or hypothyroidism)
- Hypoglycaemia
- Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE)
- Wernicke's encephalopathy or Korsakoff's psychosis
- Wilson's disease
- Carbon monoxide poisoning

**Substance-induced psychosis** is common. Methamphetamine is the most frequent cause in New Zealand; synthetic cannabis is another. Symptoms may last hours to days, and concurrent alcohol and drug use can make the cause difficult to determine.

## Investigations

Your doctor may arrange tests to exclude underlying causes and to establish baseline measurements if antipsychotic medication is being considered.

**To exclude underlying causes:**
- Thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH)
- Phosphate and calcium (to screen for hyperparathyroidism)
- Urine drug screen (amphetamines, cannabinoids, opiates, benzodiazepines)
- STI screening including syphilis and HIV
- Pregnancy test
- Vitamin B12 and folate
- Brain imaging if an organic cause is suspected

**Baseline tests before antipsychotic medication:**
- Weight and BMI
- Waist circumference
- Fasting glucose and HbA1c
- Fasting lipid profile
- Full blood count
- Electrocardiogram (ECG)

## Possible Diagnoses

Your doctor will consider several possible diagnoses:

### Schizophrenia

Combination of delusions, hallucinations, thought disorder, disorganised behaviour, and negative symptoms — typically starting in young adulthood, with symptoms present for at least one month, and not better explained by substance use or a medical condition.

### Affective Psychosis

Psychotic symptoms occurring in the context of a clear mood episode (mania or depression). Psychotic symptoms are usually mood-congruent (e.g. grandiose beliefs if elated, nihilistic beliefs if depressed). Note: Borderline personality disorder can sometimes resemble affective psychosis, presenting with psychotic features and dissociative states.

### Substance-Induced Psychotic Disorder

Closely linked in time to substance use, intoxication, or withdrawal. Often characterised by prominent hallucinations (sometimes visual) and fewer negative symptoms. There are no definitive signs that clearly separate this from other causes of psychosis.

### Delusional Disorder

Usually presents in mid-to-late adulthood. Features non-bizarre delusions (possible, but false), with no other psychotic symptoms, and the person's behaviour is otherwise not unusual.

### Psychosis Due to an Organic Medical Condition

Suspected particularly in anyone over 25 presenting with psychosis for the first time — the likelihood of an underlying medical cause increases with age.

## Management

Management depends on urgency and the likely cause. Your doctor will determine the appropriate approach.

**If there is immediate danger:**
- Call 111 and request police assistance
- Do not leave the person alone while waiting for emergency services

**If you are acutely unwell with significant risk of harm:**
- Your doctor will arrange urgent mental health assessment
- Compulsory assessment may be needed in some circumstances

**For a first episode of psychosis (especially ages 15–30):**
- Your doctor will refer to an early psychosis intervention service
- Early referral significantly improves long-term outcomes

**If substance misuse is suspected:**
- Treat the acute psychosis
- Your doctor will recommend alcohol and drug reduction or withdrawal support

**For ongoing assessment or diagnostic uncertainty:**
- Your doctor will seek mental health specialist advice before starting medication
- Medication will not usually be started without specialist input

**If an organic medical condition is suspected:**
- Your doctor will investigate and manage the underlying cause directly

**Regarding dependants:**
- If you have children, your doctor may assess their welfare and connect you with appropriate support services
