# Croup

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of croup (laryngotracheobronchitis) in children aged 6 months to 6 years.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately if your child has:

- Hypoxia (low oxygen levels)
- Lethargy (unusual drowsiness or unresponsiveness)
- Toxic appearance (looks very unwell)

---

## Background

Croup (laryngotracheobronchitis) is inflammation of the upper airways, typically caused by a viral infection — most commonly parainfluenza or respiratory syncytial virus.

Key facts:
- Affects children aged 6 months to 6 years
- Symptoms are worse at night and usually peak on the second or third day
- Older children with an atopic (allergic) condition may develop spasmodic croup

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your child carefully while minimising distress. For moderate or severe croup, examination is kept brief and gentle — your child may be observed sitting on your knee rather than being formally examined.

### Symptoms your doctor will look for

- Coryzal prodrome (runny nose, mild fever before the barking cough starts)
- Barking cough (the hallmark symptom)
- Stridor (a harsh, high-pitched noise when breathing in)
- Accessory muscle use (visible effort to breathe)
- Respiratory distress developing over 12–24 hours

### Factors your doctor will consider

- Age — croup is uncommon under 6 months; most common in autumn and spring
- Upper respiratory tract symptoms usually precede airway obstruction by 1–3 days
- Your child may seem well during assessment, but this may not reflect how severe symptoms are at night or at home
- Anxiety makes symptoms worse — keeping your child calm is important

### Possible alternative diagnoses

Your doctor will consider other causes of stridor or respiratory distress, including:
- Allergic reaction (anaphylaxis)
- Inhaled foreign body
- Bacterial tracheitis
- Epiglottitis
- Peritonsillar, parapharyngeal, or retropharyngeal abscess
- Upper airway injury
- Congenital airway anomaly

### Severity assessment

Your doctor will grade the severity as mild, moderate, or severe:

| Feature | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| General appearance | Looks well | Looks unwell | Looks sick |
| Behaviour | Normal | Some or intermittent irritability | Altered mental state — lethargy, agitation, or restlessness |
| Stridor | Only when active or upset | Stridor at rest | Stridor at rest (may be soft despite marked effort) |
| Respiratory rate and effort | Normal, minimal effort | Increased rate and moderate effort | Markedly increased; may fatigue |
| Heart rate | Normal | Some tachycardia at rest | Marked tachycardia |
| Oxygen saturation | Normal | Normal | Less than 93% |

### Signs that suggest an underlying condition (recurrent croup)

If your child has had croup multiple times, your doctor will consider whether there is an underlying cause such as:
- Subglottic stenosis
- Laryngeal web
- Reactive airways (atopic disease or gastro-oesophageal reflux)
- Respiratory tract papillomatosis

Particular concern if there is no prodromal illness before episodes, hoarse or weak voice when well, abnormal neck anatomy, or known perinatal exposure to HPV.

## Management

### Severe croup

Your doctor may consider the following. Severe croup always requires hospital transfer.

- **Nebulised adrenaline** — provides rapid but temporary symptom relief; does not change the course of the disease. If given, hospital transfer by ambulance is required regardless of improvement.
  - Dose: 0.5 mL/kg of 1:1000 solution (maximum 5 mL), made up to at least 4 mL with normal saline, run with oxygen at 6 L/min
  - Adrenaline can be repeated if needed — there is no maximum total dose, but ongoing need reflects severity of obstruction
- **Steroids** — take several hours to have effect but then last 24–36 hours; usually only one dose is needed:
  - Dexamethasone 0.6 mg/kg orally (maximum 12 mg), or
  - Prednisolone 1–2 mg/kg orally (maximum 40 mg)
- **Oxygen** at 6 L/min if oxygen saturation is below 93%
- **Hospital transfer by ambulance** — all children with severe croup require acute paediatric medical assessment at hospital, even if they improve with treatment

If your child does not respond to treatment or respiratory failure is suspected, an ambulance is called immediately.

### Moderate croup

Your doctor may consider the following:

- Keep your child calm — anxiety makes symptoms worse
- Oxygen and intravenous lines are not usually needed
- **Steroids:**
  - Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg orally (maximum 12 mg), or
  - Prednisolone 1 mg/kg orally (maximum 40 mg) once a day for 2 days
  - If your child is distressed, wait until they are willing to take it — moderate croup usually improves 2–6 hours after oral steroids
- Nebulised adrenaline is not usually required but may be given if symptoms are severe — hospital transfer is required if so
- Your doctor will monitor your child until there is no stridor at rest, then reassess whether it is safe to go home
- If not improving, hospital transfer will be arranged

Hospital transfer will be considered if:
- Nebulised adrenaline was needed
- Not improving with treatment
- Risk factors for severe croup are present (history of previous severe croup, prolonged neonatal ventilation, congenital stridor, Down syndrome, or narrow upper airways)
- There are concerns about caregiver's ability to monitor the child at home or access emergency care quickly

### Mild croup

Your doctor may consider the following:

- Expectant management (watchful waiting without medication) or steroids — evidence does not clearly favour one approach
- Steroids may be especially useful on the first day of illness to help prevent worsening on days two and three
- **If steroids are given:**
  - Dexamethasone 0.15 mg/kg orally (maximum 12 mg) as a single dose, or
  - Prednisolone 1 mg/kg orally (maximum 40 mg per dose) once a day for 2 days
- There is no evidence that cough medicines or humidified air help

Most children with mild croup can go home. Your doctor will consider whether hospital review is needed based on the same risk factors above.

### Recurrent croup

If your child has had croup multiple times or there is concern about an underlying abnormality, your doctor may refer to a paediatric ENT specialist for further assessment.

---

## What to watch for at home

Your doctor will give you written information and instructions on what to do if symptoms worsen after hours — in particular if your child develops stridor at rest, increased work of breathing, or looks significantly unwell.

If your child is hospitalised for croup, influenza vaccination will be offered.
