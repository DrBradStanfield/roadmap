# Stroke

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial assessment and management of stroke and TIA (transient ischaemic attack) — from acute recognition through to long-term secondary prevention.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance (111) immediately if:

- Symptoms started within the last 24 hours
- The person is on anticoagulation (e.g. warfarin, rivaroxaban, dabigatran)

---

## Background

There are 2 main types of stroke, which are clinically indistinguishable but require different management. Urgent brain imaging is needed to tell them apart.

- **Ischaemic stroke (85%)** — caused by a blood clot blocking an artery in the brain. Time-critical: carefully selected patients may benefit from IV thrombolysis (clot-dissolving medication) or endovascular clot retrieval.
- **Intracerebral haemorrhage (15%)** — bleeding into the brain. Requires urgent blood pressure control, reversal of any blood thinning, and sometimes neurosurgical intervention.

All patients, regardless of age, severity, or gender, benefit from specialist stroke unit care.

---

## Assessment

### FAST Screening Tool

The **FAST** test (Face, Arm, Speech) is used to screen for stroke or TIA. Note: TIA symptoms may have already resolved by the time of assessment, so FAST may need to be applied based on what the person describes happening earlier.

- **Face** — Ask the person to smile. Is one side drooping?
- **Arm** — Ask the person to raise both arms to 90 degrees. Does one drift down or fall?
- **Speech** — Is there any new slurring, difficulty finding words, or trouble naming objects?
- **Take action** — If any of these are positive, emergency assessment is needed immediately.

### Other Neurological Features Suggestive of Stroke

- Unilateral (one-sided) weakness
- Unilateral altered sensation
- Dysphasia (difficulty with language)
- Visual changes, including monocular blindness or loss of half the visual field (hemianopia)
- Ataxia (loss of coordination)

### What Your Doctor Will Do

If stroke is confirmed and symptoms started **≥ 24 hours ago**, your doctor will:

- Take a history: previous TIAs, sudden vs progressive onset, headache, cardiovascular risk factors
- Perform a neurological examination: facial and limb weakness, sensory loss, speech problems, visual changes, coordination
- Check pulse (to look for atrial fibrillation), blood pressure, temperature, and oxygen levels
- Check blood sugar (to exclude hypoglycaemia, which can mimic stroke)
- Review current medications — particularly any anticoagulants or antiplatelets

If focal neurological symptoms and signs have fully resolved, your doctor will manage this urgently as a TIA.

## Management

### Acute Management (Symptoms Within 24 Hours)

**If symptoms started less than 24 hours ago**, emergency hospital admission is required so that thrombolysis and endovascular clot retrieval can be considered.

- **IV thrombolysis** must be given within 4.5 hours of symptom onset
- **Endovascular clot retrieval** can be considered within 24 hours
- In selected patients, advanced imaging (CT perfusion) may extend the thrombolysis window to 9 hours, and clot retrieval to 24 hours
- Time is measured from when the person was **last known to be well** — this includes when they went to sleep if they woke up with symptoms
- CT brain scan is required in all stroke patients; those being considered for thrombolysis or clot retrieval will also have CT angiography and CT perfusion

### Symptoms 24 Hours to 1 Week Ago

Your doctor may:

- Arrange acute stroke service assessment at the local hospital
- Keep the patient nil by mouth initially — 30–50% of stroke patients have swallowing difficulties
- Withhold aspirin and anticoagulants until brain imaging has excluded a bleed

### Symptoms More Than 1 Week Ago

- Emergency admission is generally not required
- Brain imaging (CT head) is not routinely recommended at this stage; your doctor will seek stroke service advice if considering it
- Aspirin or anticoagulants should not be started until a bleed has been excluded on imaging
- Swallowing must be assessed before giving any food, drink, or medication
- Your doctor may consider palliative care, secondary prevention, home support, or rehabilitation depending on the situation

## Long-Term Management and Secondary Prevention

After a stroke, the risk of another stroke remains elevated. Your doctor will focus on reducing this risk.

**Mortality context:** Approximately 20% at 1 month, then 5–10% per year thereafter — related to the stroke itself, immobility, and coexisting cardiovascular disease.

### Antiplatelet Therapy (if no atrial fibrillation)

Your doctor may consider:

- **Clopidogrel** — generally the preferred first-line antiplatelet for stroke secondary prevention
  - Loading dose: 300 mg, then 75 mg daily
  - For TIA or minor stroke: dual antiplatelet therapy (clopidogrel + aspirin) for 3 weeks, then clopidogrel alone
  - Dual antiplatelet therapy is **not** used after moderate or severe stroke
  - If a proton-pump inhibitor is needed alongside clopidogrel, pantoprazole is preferred over omeprazole
- **Aspirin** (100 mg daily) — an alternative, particularly if clopidogrel is not tolerated
- **Note (June 2025):** Dipyridamole 150 mg long-acting (Pytazen SR) has been discontinued. Anyone previously on this should be switched to clopidogrel or aspirin as a single agent. Aspirin + clopidogrel combined long-term is not recommended outside specialist direction.

### Anticoagulation (if atrial fibrillation)

If atrial fibrillation is present, your doctor will consider starting anticoagulation therapy for secondary prevention — antiplatelet agents alone are not adequate in this situation.

### Blood Pressure Lowering

Your doctor may consider:

- Starting or intensifying blood pressure medication
- Target: ≤ 130/80 mmHg — even if blood pressure is currently normal, reducing systolic by 10 mmHg is beneficial
- Typically starting with a low-dose ACE inhibitor or adjusting existing medications
- Gradual titration to minimise risk of falls or symptomatic low blood pressure

### Statin Therapy

Your doctor may consider:

- **Atorvastatin 80 mg daily** — shown to reduce recurrent stroke and vascular events
- Target fasting LDL: < 1.8 mmol/L
- Lower doses or simvastatin may be more appropriate in elderly or frail patients

### Lifestyle and Risk Factor Management

Your doctor will also address:

- Diabetes, hypertension, cholesterol, and atrial fibrillation control
- Smoking cessation, weight, alcohol intake, exercise, and rehabilitation
- Depression, anxiety, bladder and bowel problems, pressure areas, and falls risk

### Communication Difficulties

If the patient has aphasia or other communication difficulties, your doctor may consider a referral to speech language therapy for both assessment and rehabilitation.

### Driving

- Patients should not drive for at least one month after a stroke, and only if recovery is complete
- A doctor's clearance is required before resuming driving
- A formal occupational therapy driving assessment may be arranged if there is any concern about safety or a need for retraining

### Legal and End-of-Life Planning

Your doctor may raise:

- Setting up Enduring Power of Attorney (EPA) and creating a will
- Advance Care Planning: aged residential care preferences, resuscitation wishes, attitudes to limiting future interventions
- Palliative care if the condition is terminal

### Follow-Up

- Review every 3 months initially, including medication adherence
- If poor adherence is suspected, community pharmacy medicines support may be arranged
