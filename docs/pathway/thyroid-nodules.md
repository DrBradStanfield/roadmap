# Thyroid Nodules

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways (last updated July 2025)*

Thyroid nodules are a common finding both on examination and incidentally through radiological imaging. The main concern is whether a nodule is benign or malignant. There are two pathways: one for palpable nodules (felt on examination) and one for impalpable nodules (found incidentally on imaging).

---

## Background

- Thyroid nodules are present in up to 50% of adults. They can be detected by palpation in 10% of women and 2% of men — only 5% of palpable nodules are malignant.
- Approximately 10% of people will develop small papillary thyroid cancer in later life that never causes any ill health (found only at post-mortem). There is no health benefit in finding these cancers.
- Approximately 5% of thyroid nodules harbour an important thyroid cancer (one likely to cause ill health in the future).
- With sensitive imaging (ultrasound, CT, or MRI), the prevalence of thyroid nodules is as high as 50%. Only a small minority are malignant. Ultrasound can be used to stratify the risk of malignancy, but cannot reliably identify all benign nodules.
- Optimal management of thyroid nodules is uncertain. Practices vary considerably between centres and countries. Management cannot accurately exclude or diagnose malignancy in all cases.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

The following features indicate **high suspicion of thyroid cancer** and require prompt specialist attention:

- Unexplained voice change or stridor
- Thyroid nodule in a child or young person
- Cervical lymphadenopathy (swollen neck lymph nodes)
- Painless thyroid mass that has been **rapidly enlarging** over 2–3 months
- Family history of multiple endocrine neoplasm

An FNA (biopsy) result of **Bethesda 4** (risk of malignancy up to 30%) requires endocrinologist or surgeon review, even though it is not classified as "high suspicion of cancer".

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:
- Symptoms of thyroid hormone imbalance (weight loss, sweating, tremor, palpitations)
- Symptoms of thyroid eye disease (gritty or irritable eyes, eyelid swelling, diplopia, reduced vision, colour perception changes)
- Thyroid cancer risk factors:
  - History of neck irradiation in childhood
  - Personal or family history of thyroid adenoma
  - Hashimoto's thyroiditis (risk of lymphoma)
  - Cowden's syndrome
  - Familial adenomatous polyposis
  - Familial thyroid cancer
  - Family history of multiple endocrine neoplasm
- Duration and growth of the nodule, including any sudden increase in size or tenderness (may indicate haemorrhage into a nodule)
- Compressive symptoms:
  - Hoarseness (compression or malignant infiltration of the recurrent laryngeal nerve)
  - Difficulty swallowing (dysphagia)
  - Facial swelling or distended neck veins (superior vena cava obstruction)
  - Breathlessness, especially positional or exertional (airway obstruction)
  - Stridor or wheezing at rest (severe tracheal compression — diameter less than 5 mm)

Your doctor will examine the neck to assess the nodule, check whether it moves with swallowing, and examine for lymph nodes.

A **TSH blood test** is arranged for all patients with a thyroid nodule.

---

## Palpable Thyroid Nodule

A palpable nodule is one your doctor can feel on examination.

### Immediate Priorities

If there are **severe compressive symptoms** (significant airway obstruction, stridor at rest, superior vena cava obstruction), your doctor will refer urgently to an ENT specialist.

If there is **high suspicion of thyroid cancer** in an adult, your doctor will:
- Arrange a thyroid ultrasound
- Refer urgently to a thyroid specialist
- Not delay referral while waiting for the ultrasound result

If the patient is **under 15 years old**, your doctor will arrange TSH and refer urgently to paediatric surgery. Thyroid nodules are uncommon in children but often malignant.

### For All Other Patients

Your doctor will manage the nodule based on the TSH result and clinical findings. They may:
- Arrange a thyroid ultrasound
- Refer to a thyroid specialist (endocrinologist, general surgeon, or ENT/head and neck surgeon)

**Thyroid specialist assessment is typically needed for:**
- Any new palpable thyroid nodule, irrespective of TSH level
- Suspicious features on ultrasound
- Compressive symptoms (even mild to moderate)
- Cosmetic concerns from a visible neck swelling
- Any uncertainty about management

**If a CT or MRI scan incidentally finds a thyroid nodule**, your doctor will arrange a follow-up thyroid ultrasound if:
- The nodule is larger than 15 mm and the patient is over 35 years
- The nodule is larger than 10 mm and the patient is under 35 years

If the patient is clinically hyperthyroid or hypothyroid, your doctor will manage according to the relevant pathway.

---

## Impalpable (Incidental) Thyroid Nodule

An impalpable nodule is found on imaging (CT, MRI, ultrasound) done for another reason and cannot be felt on examination. If the nodule is palpable, the palpable nodule pathway applies.

### Initial Testing

Your doctor will arrange a **TSH test only** (Labtests/Awanui do reflex testing if TSH is abnormal).

### If TSH is Elevated or Normal

Your doctor will consider whether a thyroid ultrasound is needed. Criteria for ultrasound:
- Further assessment recommended by the reporting radiologist
- CT/MRI appearances are suspicious (suspicious lymph nodes, local invasion)
- Patient is under 35 years and nodule is larger than 10 mm
- Patient is over 35 years and nodule is larger than 15 mm

If these criteria are not met but uncertainty remains, your doctor may seek specialist advice before deciding.

### If TSH is Reduced (Suppressed)

Your doctor will arrange a radionuclide (nuclear medicine) thyroid scan. If you are pregnant, a nuclear scan is contraindicated and your doctor will seek specialist advice.

**Based on the scan result:**
- **Hot nodule** (high isotope uptake): indicates a benign but functioning lesion with low likelihood of cancer. Your doctor will manage this as subclinical hyperthyroidism.
- **Cold nodule** (low isotope uptake): may indicate cancer. A thyroid ultrasound will be arranged.

---

## TI-RADS: Understanding Your Ultrasound Result

After a thyroid ultrasound, nodules are classified using the **TI-RADS** (Thyroid Imaging Reporting and Data System) scale — a risk stratification tool used in New Zealand (ACR TI-RADS).

| TI-RADS Score | Risk of Significant Cancer |
|---------------|---------------------------|
| TI-RADS 1–2   | Very low (benign/normal)  |
| TI-RADS 3     | Low — approximately 1–3%  |
| TI-RADS 4     | Intermediate — approximately 5% |
| TI-RADS 5     | High — 10–20% for nodules ≥10 mm |

If your ultrasound report does not include a TI-RADS classification, your doctor will request an updated report from the radiologist.

---

## Fine Needle Aspiration (FNA) Biopsy

FNA is the main diagnostic tool for detecting malignancy in thyroid nodules. It is an ultrasound-guided needle biopsy performed by a specialist.

**FNA is typically recommended when:**
- TI-RADS 5 and the nodule is 10 mm or larger
- TI-RADS 4 and the nodule is 15 mm or larger
- TI-RADS 3 and the nodule is 25 mm or larger
- The nodule is FDG-avid on a PET/CT scan
- Enlarged or atypical cervical lymph nodes are present

**FNA may not be appropriate for:**
- Patients with significant co-morbidities that may limit life expectancy
- Patients who prefer active surveillance
- Patients with multinodular goitre — only TI-RADS 5 nodules, or nodules that look different from the others, would typically be considered

**FNA results are reported using the Bethesda system:**
- Bethesda 1–3: typically benign or indeterminate
- Bethesda 4: intermediate risk (up to 30% risk of malignancy) — specialist review required
- Bethesda 5–6: high risk of cancer — urgent specialist referral

There is no evidence to support continued ultrasound surveillance of FNA-proven benign nodules.

---

## Active Surveillance

For thyroid nodules where FNA is not indicated, or where the patient prefers surveillance over biopsy, active surveillance with repeat ultrasound is appropriate. Thyroid cancer is typically slow-growing.

**Surveillance schedule:**
- TI-RADS 3: repeat ultrasound at 1, 3, and 5 years
- TI-RADS 4: repeat ultrasound at 1, 2, 3, and 5 years
- TI-RADS 5: repeat ultrasound at 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 years

If a nodule remains stable with no changes at 5 years, surveillance can be stopped with no further imaging required.

If the nodule changes in size or appearance at any point, your doctor will seek specialist advice.

**Some TI-RADS nodules also require follow-up ultrasound even without FNA:**
- TI-RADS 5 nodules between 5 and 9 mm
- TI-RADS 4 nodules between 10 and 14 mm
- TI-RADS 3 nodules between 15 and 24 mm

Your doctor will advise you to return promptly if any new or progressively enlarging neck lumps develop.

---

## When Specialist Assessment is Needed

Your doctor will decide on referral based on the clinical picture. Referral to an endocrinologist or thyroid surgeon is typically indicated for:

- Any palpable thyroid nodule (new or known with concerns)
- TI-RADS 3 nodule ≥25 mm, TI-RADS 4 nodule ≥15 mm, or TI-RADS 5 nodule ≥10 mm (for FNA)
- FNA result of Bethesda 4 or higher
- FDG-avid nodule on PET/CT
- Abnormal or atypical cervical lymph nodes
- Suspected thyroid cancer (urgent referral)
- Subclinical hyperthyroidism with other thyroid concerns or persisting more than 12 months
- Any uncertainty about management

---

## Important Notes for Patients

- A thyroid nodule finding does not mean you have cancer — the vast majority are benign.
- Management decisions involve balancing the risk of missing a significant cancer against the risk of over-investigating very common, clinically unimportant nodules.
- Your doctor will guide you through the investigation process and make referral decisions based on your individual circumstances.
- If you develop new neck lumps, voice changes, difficulty swallowing, or breathing difficulties, see your doctor promptly.
