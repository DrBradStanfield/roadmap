# Bow Legs (Genu Varum)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of bow legs (genu varum) in children — distinguishing normal developmental bowing from conditions that require further investigation or specialist review.

---

## Background

In the majority of children, bow legs is a benign, developmental pattern that requires no investigation, treatment, or specialist referral.

- Children's lower limbs often appear bowed in the first couple of years of life. This physiological bowing is a normal part of skeletal growth and will typically improve as the child approaches 2.5 to 3 years old without any intervention.
- Most physiological bowing is diffuse and centred at the knee. Pathological bowing is often unilateral and focal in a limb segment (e.g., tibial bowing).
- In rare cases, bow legs can be due to disorders of calcium and vitamin D metabolism (rickets), Blount disease, renal disease, or skeletal dysplasia.
- Concerned parents can take photos of their child standing as the child grows to demonstrate improvement over time.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Associated complaints such as limp or pain
- Walking age — early walking (before 1 year) is associated with Blount disease
- Family history of bow legs, metabolic bone disease, very short stature, or skeletal dysplasia
- Any history of trauma or infection in the lower limbs
- Whether the child is otherwise healthy with adequate nutrition

### Risk Factors for Vitamin D Deficiency

Your doctor will check for risk factors including:

- Darker skin pigmentation, particularly in children with South Asian or African ethnicity
- Prematurity (less than 37 weeks) or low birth weight (less than 2,500 g)
- Disability — often leads to reduced sun exposure, mobility problems, dietary deficiencies, and use of medications that reduce vitamin D
- Reduced sun exposure due to health, social, religious, or environmental factors

Diet factors:
- Exclusive breastfeeding, or mixed breast milk and formula feeding
- Delayed introduction of solids in infants who are exclusively breastfed
- Low vitamin D diet (e.g., fussy eater, vegetarian, vegan), especially in older children

Family factors:
- Being born to a vitamin D-deficient mother — more common in dark-skinned, veiled, or recent migrant mothers
- Siblings of a vitamin D-deficient child

Chronic disease:
- Malabsorption syndromes (e.g., coeliac disease, pancreatic insufficiency)
- Chronic liver or renal disease

Medications that can affect vitamin D metabolism or bone health:
- Oral steroids
- Some anticonvulsants — sodium valproate, carbamazepine, phenytoin
- Antiretrovirals, rifampicin, and some chemotherapy agents

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will typically:

- Plot age, weight, and height against recommended growth standards — stature below the third percentile raises concern for metabolic bone disease or other bone conditions
- Assess symmetry of bowing
- Compare symmetrical bowing with normal tibio-femoral (TF) angle for the child's age
- Examine the lower limb joints for abnormality
- Carry out a brief neurological examination, looking for spasticity or weakness in the lower limbs
- Photograph the severity of bow legs if possible, for later comparison

### Blood Tests

Your doctor may consider blood tests if there are risk factors for vitamin D deficiency or suspicion of chronic disease:

- Serum calcium
- Phosphate
- Vitamin D levels

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following based on assessment findings:

- **If blood tests are normal and the child is under 2.5 years and growing normally:** Reassure parents that this is physiological bowing that will improve or resolve in most children. The child will be monitored until around 2.5 years of age.
- **If bowing persists after 2.5 to 3 years old and blood tests are normal:** Your doctor will refer for a non-acute paediatric orthopaedic assessment.
- **If there is unilateral bowing, joint movement limitation, or unilateral tibial deformity, or if symmetrical bowing significantly deviates from normal values for age:** Your doctor will refer for paediatric orthopaedic assessment.
- **If vitamin D, calcium, or phosphate levels are abnormal:** Your doctor will manage this according to the Vitamin D Deficiency in Children pathway.
- **If there is concern about the child's growth and development:** Your doctor will refer for a non-acute paediatric assessment.

### About Blount Disease

Blount disease is a developmental disorder characterised by disordered growth of the medial aspect of the proximal tibial growth plate, causing progressive bowing. It is more common in overweight children who walk early (before 1 year). Severe or persistent bowing after age 2 years may represent infantile Blount disease. An X-ray of the tibia can help differentiate physiological bowing from Blount disease and other causes. Best outcomes occur when diagnosed and treated early (e.g., with bracing or osteotomy).
