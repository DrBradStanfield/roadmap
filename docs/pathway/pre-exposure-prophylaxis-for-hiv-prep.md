# Pre-exposure Prophylaxis for HIV (PrEP)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and prescribing of PrEP (pre-exposure prophylaxis) for HIV prevention in adults at elevated risk. PrEP is a funded medication in New Zealand for eligible patients.

---

## What is PrEP?

PrEP is the use of antiretroviral medications (ARVs) by HIV-negative people to reduce their risk of acquiring HIV. It consists of a fixed-dose combination of two ARVs — tenofovir disoproxil + emtricitabine — taken either daily or on demand (2-1-1 method).

When taken as directed, PrEP reduces the risk of HIV acquisition by approximately 99%.

---

## Who is at Elevated Risk? (Eligibility)

Your doctor will assess whether you are at elevated risk of HIV exposure. Risk categories include:

### Men who have sex with men (MSM)

High risk if, in the previous 3 months (or foreseeably in the next 3 months):
- Condomless intercourse with any casual male partner(s)
- Diagnosis of rectal gonorrhoea, chlamydia, or infectious syphilis
- Methamphetamine use or sexualised drug use
- Condomless intercourse with a regular HIV-positive partner not on treatment or with detectable viral load (> 200)
- Travelling to a high HIV-prevalence country with anticipated risk

### Trans and gender-diverse people

High risk if, in the previous 3 months (or foreseeably in the next 3 months):
- Condomless anal intercourse with any casual MSM partner (risk is lower for condomless vaginal intercourse)
- Diagnosis of rectal or vaginal gonorrhoea, chlamydia, or infectious syphilis
- Methamphetamine use or sexualised drug use
- Condomless intercourse with a regular HIV-positive partner not on treatment or with detectable viral load (> 200)
- Travelling to a high HIV-prevalence country with anticipated risk

### Heterosexual people

High risk if, in the previous 3 months (or foreseeably in the next 3 months):
- Condomless intercourse with any casual MSM partner
- Condomless intercourse with a regular HIV-positive partner not on treatment or with detectable viral load (> 200)
- Travelling to a high HIV-prevalence country with anticipated risk

Most heterosexual cisgender people are not considered at elevated risk in New Zealand.

### People who inject drugs

High risk if they have shared injecting equipment with an HIV-positive individual, or with MSM of unknown HIV status, in the previous 3 months (or foreseeably in the next 3 months).

---

These lists are not exhaustive — clinical discretion applies. People may be reluctant to disclose HIV risk; your doctor will apply a low threshold for prescribing when someone from a higher-risk group asks for PrEP.

---

## Funded PrEP Criteria

Both of the following must be met:

1. The patient has tested negative for HIV, has no signs or symptoms of acute HIV infection, and has been assessed for HIV seroconversion.
2. The prescriber considers the patient is at elevated risk of HIV exposure and that PrEP is clinically appropriate.

---

## Baseline Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Check for signs or symptoms of **acute HIV infection** (see below) — PrEP cannot be started if these are present
- Measure blood pressure, weight, and BMI
- Perform a sexual health examination (or arrange self-collected swabs for asymptomatic patients)

**Signs of acute HIV infection** (occur 1–6 weeks after exposure, resembles glandular fever):
fever, night sweats, fatigue, myalgia, arthralgia, rash, headache, pharyngitis, generalised lymphadenopathy, diarrhoea

### Baseline investigations

- HIV serology
- Syphilis serology
- Hepatitis serology: A, B (HBsAg, anti-HBs, anti-HBc), and C
- FBC, creatinine and eGFR, LFT
- Urine protein:creatinine ratio
- STI screen (gonorrhoea, chlamydia)
- Pregnancy test if at risk of pregnancy

---

## Contraindications and Cautions

Your doctor will check for these before prescribing.

### Contraindications (PrEP cannot be started)
- Acute or chronic HIV infection
- Significant renal impairment (eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²)

### Cautions (require closer monitoring or specialist input)
- Mild renal impairment (eGFR 60–90 mL/min/1.73 m²)
- Concurrent nephrotoxic medications: liquorice, lithium, methotrexate, NSAIDs, zoledronic acid — most can be continued with close renal monitoring
- Chronic or acute hepatitis B — PrEP may need to be lifelong; specialist advice required before starting or stopping
- Pregnancy, planning pregnancy, or breastfeeding — current evidence suggests PrEP is safe; seek sexual health advice
- Protein powders/supplements — can affect renal function tests; stop 1–2 weeks before testing

### Drug interactions to consider
- Exogenous estrogen — may reduce efficacy of on-demand (2-1-1) PrEP
- Valaciclovir, tacrolimus — increase tenofovir levels
- SGLT2 inhibitors (dapagliflozin, empagliflozin) — increase fracture/osteoporosis risk

## Management

### If HIV exposure occurred within the last 72 hours

Your doctor will follow the Post-exposure Prophylaxis for HIV (PEP) pathway — a different treatment course.

### Before starting PrEP, your doctor will also

- Treat any identified STIs
- Offer vaccinations: hepatitis B (if not immune), HPV (if eligible), hepatitis A (if MSM — unfunded)
- Seek specialist advice if: HBsAg positive, pregnant or under 18, eGFR < 60, or significant drug interactions

### Prescribing: tenofovir disoproxil + emtricitabine

**Daily PrEP** (suitable for all at elevated risk):
- One tablet daily at approximately the same time each day
- Effective after 7 days of consistent daily dosing
- MSM may achieve immediate protection by taking 2 tablets 2–24 hours before first sexual contact, then one tablet daily thereafter

**On-demand PrEP (2-1-1)** (suitable for cisgender men, transgender women, and non-binary people assigned male at birth with less frequent intercourse):
- Contraindicated if chronic hepatitis B
- Not suitable if using exogenous estrogen
- Take 2 tablets 2–24 hours before sexual contact, then 1 tablet daily until 48 hours (minimum 2 doses) after last sexual contact

---

## Adherence and Side Effects

Your doctor will counsel on:

**Adherence:**
- Missed doses increase HIV acquisition risk
- Non-adherence can lead to HIV acquisition and drug resistance
- PEP may be needed if exposure occurs during missed doses
- If a dose is missed: take it as soon as remembered; if < 12 hours until next dose (on daily regimen), skip and continue normally

**Side effects:**

Initiation side effects (usually resolve within 2–3 weeks): nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, flatulence, weakness, headache, dizziness

Longer-term potential effects (uncommon): reduced bone mineral density (generally minor), rarely kidney/liver/pancreatic complications, rarely rash

---

## Harm Reduction Advice

Your doctor will advise:
- Use condoms (PrEP does not protect against other STIs)
- Use clean injecting equipment; never share
- Test regularly for HIV and know the HIV status of partners
- Test regularly for STIs and seek treatment promptly

---

## Follow-up and Monitoring Schedule

| Timepoint | What is reviewed |
|---|---|
| 1 month after starting | Side effects, HIV test, STI screen, adherence, syphilis |
| Every 3 months ongoing | HIV test, full STI screen (syphilis, gonorrhoea, chlamydia, hep B if not immune), pregnancy test if relevant, adherence, side effects, behavioural counselling |
| 3 months after starting, then every 6 months | Renal function, urine protein:creatinine ratio |
| Annually | Hepatitis C serology (more frequently if ongoing risk) |

**Check renal function every 3 months** (rather than 6-monthly) if: baseline eGFR 60–90, age > 40 years, or renal risk factors present.

A mild eGFR dip is common after starting PrEP. Your doctor will seek nephrology advice if there is a > 25% reduction or sustained decrease of 15% or more.

---

## Stopping PrEP

If stopping daily PrEP:
- **Cisgender males:** continue for 48 hours (2 doses) after last potential exposure
- **Transgender people and cisgender women:** continue for 7 days after last potential exposure
- **Chronic hepatitis B:** do not stop without specialist advice — risk of HBV reactivation

---

## When Your Doctor Will Seek Specialist Advice

Your doctor may seek sexual health specialist advice if:
- Uncertain about drug interactions
- Uncertain whether PrEP is indicated (particularly for cisgender heterosexual people)
- Patient is pregnant, planning pregnancy, breastfeeding, or under 18

Your doctor may seek nephrology advice if:
- eGFR < 60 mL/min/1.73 m²
- eGFR drops > 25% or sustained 15% after starting PrEP

Specialist advice is needed before starting or stopping PrEP in patients with chronic hepatitis B.
