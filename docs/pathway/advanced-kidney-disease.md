# Advanced Kidney Disease

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers symptom management for adults with advanced (stage 5) chronic kidney disease — typically when the estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) is below 15 mL/min/1.73 m².

---

## Background

Advanced (stage 5) kidney disease is defined as an eGFR below 15 mL/min/1.73 m². It is usually associated with symptoms, and for some patients symptoms begin when the eGFR falls below 30.

Treatment options include kidney replacement therapy (dialysis or kidney transplantation) or supportive care without dialysis. Some patients choose not to start dialysis based on co-morbidities and quality of life considerations — this is a decision made together with the patient, specialists, and family. Supportive care remains important as symptoms may persist even with dialysis.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Identify whether you have advanced kidney disease (eGFR below 15 mL/min/1.73 m², with or without dialysis treatment)
- Assess and prioritise your symptoms

**Common symptoms of advanced kidney disease include:**
tiredness, pain, loss of appetite, constipation, anxiety, depression, sleep disturbance, itch, cramps, and restless legs.

- Symptoms may also be due to conditions other than kidney disease

---

## Management

Your doctor will plan and document care with you and your family, and discuss end-of-life issues if appropriate. Palliative care input may be requested. Kidney disease constrains the use of many medicines — your doctor will take this into account when prescribing.

**End-of-life issues your doctor may consider discussing:**
- Your concerns, and those of your family or carers
- Withdrawal from dialysis (if applicable)
- Preferred place of care
- Cardiopulmonary resuscitation preferences
- Community support options
- Advance care plan (ACP) — creating or reviewing one

For dialysis-related issues (blood pressure, fluid overload, technical problems), your doctor will seek nephrology advice. Patients on dialysis can also contact their dialysis team directly.

---

### Fatigue

Fatigue affects 70–100% of patients with advanced kidney disease and is one of the most difficult symptoms to manage.

Your doctor may look for and correct reversible causes:
- Electrolyte disturbances (low potassium, calcium, sodium, or magnesium)
- Poor nutrition
- Depression
- Sleep disturbance
- Pain
- Anaemia

**Anaemia:** Your doctor may treat anaemia if it is likely to improve your quality of life or reduce fatigue.
- Anaemia is defined as Hb below 130 g/L in men, or below 115 g/L in women
- If iron deficiency anaemia is found and non-renal causes excluded:
  - eGFR above 30: diet and oral iron
  - eGFR below 30: intravenous (IV) iron infusion may be considered (oral iron is usually ineffective at this level)
- Correcting iron deficiency may also help restless legs and fatigue
- If Hb is below 100 g/L with normal iron stores, your doctor will seek nephrology advice about erythropoietin therapy

**Sleep disturbance:** Your doctor may assess for specific causes (sleep apnoea, restless legs, cramps, itch) and recommend sleep hygiene measures (reducing caffeine, avoiding alcohol, limiting daytime naps). If specific causes are excluded and general measures don't help, a short-term hypnotic such as temazepam 5–10 mg may be considered.

Your doctor may also consider referral to physiotherapy and review of social supports.

---

### Pain

Your doctor may use a stepwise approach:

- **Paracetamol** is the preferred analgesic for mild-to-moderate pain. The dose does not need adjustment (1 gram taken 3 or 4 times a day).
- **Tramadol** (adjusted dose) may be used with caution:
  - On dialysis or eGFR 15–30: starting dose 50 mg twice a day, maximum 100 mg twice a day
  - eGFR below 15 and not on dialysis: maximum 50 mg twice a day
- **Opioids** — your doctor will seek nephrology or palliative care advice. Options include methadone or fentanyl patch. Breakthrough analgesia is prescribed alongside.
- **Morphine, oxycodone, and codeine are not recommended** in advanced kidney disease due to accumulation of toxic metabolites.
- Laxatives are prescribed alongside opioids to prevent constipation.

---

### Nausea

Your doctor will treat nausea based on the most likely cause:

- **Gastroparesis** (nausea worse after meals or relieved by vomiting): metoclopramide 5–10 mg three times a day
- **Uraemia** (persistent nausea, not necessarily with vomiting): metoclopramide 5–10 mg three times a day, haloperidol 0.5 mg at night, or domperidone 10 mg three times a day
- **Drug-induced**: haloperidol 0.5 mg twice a day or cyclizine 25–50 mg three times a day (cyclizine causes sedation and is used cautiously if cardiac co-morbidities)

---

### Restless Legs

Restless legs is an urge to move the legs, occurring at rest and worse at night. It can disturb sleep and worsen fatigue and depression.

Your doctor may:
- Check iron studies and give iron replacement if depleted
- Prescribe clonazepam 0.5–1 mg at night
- Prescribe gabapentin 100 mg every second night (or after haemodialysis)

---

### Itch

Your doctor may recommend:
- Trimming nails, keeping skin moist with emollients, and using non-soap cleansers
- Emollient cream (e.g. cetomacrogol cream) applied liberally to dry skin
- If emollients are not effective: gabapentin 100 mg every second night or after haemodialysis (used with caution if not on dialysis)
- Evening primrose oil 1000 mg twice a day (note: reduces platelet function — care with aspirin)
- Antihistamines and ondansetron are not recommended
- If other treatments fail: UVB therapy may be considered — your doctor will refer for nephrology assessment

---

### Depression

Depression affects around one in four patients with advanced kidney disease and can be difficult to diagnose given overlapping symptoms.

Your doctor may consider a selective serotonin re-uptake inhibitor (SSRI) or tricyclic antidepressant. Citalopram appears to be the safest option.

---

### Dry Mouth

Your doctor may recommend:
- Artificial saliva preparation every 1–2 hours
- Ice chips if fluid restriction is required
- Reducing medicines that cause dry mouth (e.g. cyclizine, tricyclic antidepressants) where possible

---

### Constipation

Constipation is common. Causes include fluid restriction, reduced mobility, medications (phosphate binders, calcium, iron, opioids), poor dietary intake, and depression.

Your doctor may:
- Prescribe laxatives alongside opioids to prevent constipation
- Consider a combination softener and stimulant, e.g. docusate sodium + sennoside B (Coloxyl with senna) 1–2 tablets twice a day

**Avoid:** magnesium-based, citrate-based, or phosphate-based laxatives, and macrogols (e.g. Molaxole) which require fluid intake.

---

### Anorexia

Food intake often decreases as kidney disease advances.

Your doctor may:
- Check mouth hygiene
- Suggest small, frequent meals
- Consider referral to a dietitian
- Consider nutritional supplementation
- Review adequacy of dialysis if applicable (seeking nephrology advice)

---

### Ongoing Review

Your doctor will review your symptoms and psychological wellbeing every 3–6 months. Planning for end-of-life care is considered in advance, especially if you are thinking about stopping dialysis or if wellbeing is declining despite management of all reversible causes.

---

## Care of the Dying Patient

If you are approaching the end of life, your doctor will:

- Work collaboratively with palliative care and nephrology teams
- Discuss with you and your family:
  - That the goal of care is maintaining comfort
  - Your preferred place of death
  - Resuscitation status

- Stop unnecessary tests and medications not directed at symptom control
- If oral medications cannot be taken, consider subcutaneous infusions via a syringe driver

**Pain (dying patient):** Paracetamol or an NSAID may be used. The preferred opioids are methadone, transdermal fentanyl, or parenteral fentanyl. Morphine, codeine, and oxycodone are not recommended as they are excreted by the kidneys and can cause toxicity.

**Myoclonus or muscle stiffness:** Your doctor may consider midazolam subcutaneous infusion (5–10 mg over 24 hours) and/or clonazepam 0.5 mg at night.

**Anxiety and distress:** Your doctor may prescribe midazolam subcutaneously or lorazepam sublingual. If agitation worsens, levomepromazine may be considered.
