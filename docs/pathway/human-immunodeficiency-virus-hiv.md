# Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: June 2020.*

HIV is usually passed on through exposure to the genital secretions or blood of a person whose HIV infection is not being treated. Modern treatment usually removes any risk of passing it on, lets the immune system recover, and prevents AIDS illnesses, allowing an essentially normal life expectancy.

> **Always discuss your symptoms, testing and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Seek urgent medical advice if:**
- You may have been exposed to HIV in the last 72 hours. Your doctor may consider urgent post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP). This applies to sexual exposures and to needlestick or other blood or body fluid exposures.
---

## Background

- About 7% of men who have sex with men (MSM) in New Zealand have HIV infection.
- About 1 in 5 men with HIV do not know they have it.
- Infection is much less common in heterosexual people.
- Approximately 3,500 people in New Zealand have HIV infection, including about 700 who have not been tested and do not know they have it.
- Approximately 70% of people with HIV infection are MSM. Most heterosexual men and women with HIV acquired it before moving to New Zealand.
- Transmission by injecting drug use, paid sex, or from mother to baby is very rare in New Zealand.
- New infections peaked in 2016 and have declined since, probably due largely to pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP) being funded for MSM at high risk.

**Course of infection:**
- The usual period of infection without symptoms is about 8 years.
- Without treatment, AIDS usually develops about 8 years after infection.

**Outlook:** HIV is now viewed as a chronic infection with a near-normal life expectancy. However, people with HIV, even when it is well controlled, have a higher rate of ischaemic heart disease, hepatitis-related diseases, and some cancers.

### Doxy-PEP (preventing syphilis and chlamydia)

Post-exposure doxycycline prophylaxis (doxy-PEP) is used to prevent syphilis and chlamydia in eligible people at risk. A single 200 mg dose of doxycycline taken within 72 hours of condomless sex is shown to significantly reduce the risk of chlamydia and syphilis infection. It can be started by your GP during a routine sexual health consultation. If you are a sexual contact of someone with a known or suspected sexually transmitted infection (STI), you will still be managed for that infection, even if you take doxy-PEP.

### Syphilis testing

Syphilis cases have recently risen in Auckland, including among heterosexual men, young women, and pregnant women. GPs are encouraged to routinely test for syphilis (and HIV), especially in women of reproductive age: as part of a sexual health check, as part of antenatal care, or if signs or symptoms of syphilis are present.

## Assessment

### Who should be offered an HIV test

Your doctor may offer HIV testing if:
- You have an illness that suggests a low CD4 count, such as severe persistent thrush (candidiasis) or unexplained persistent pneumonia.
- You have a glandular fever-like illness.
- You ask for a test.
- Your history suggests a higher risk of HIV, for example:
  - Men who have sex with men.
  - Men and women who have been sexually active in countries where heterosexually transmitted HIV is common, especially Sub-Saharan Africa and Southeast Asia.
  - People who inject drugs.
  - People who use methamphetamine, which is increasingly used as a sexual stimulant by MSM (chemsex).
  - Sex workers.
  - Partners of people known to have HIV.
  - People who have had unprotected sex with many partners of unknown HIV status.
- You have another infection such as hepatitis B, hepatitis C, tuberculosis, or an STI.
- A sexual health risk assessment suggests it.
- You are pregnant (routine antenatal screening).
- You are a healthcare worker who does exposure-prone procedures.
- You have been exposed to blood or body fluids (for example, a needlestick injury).

**Pregnancy:**
- HIV screening is offered to all pregnant women with their first antenatal blood tests.
- Without treatment, the risk of passing HIV from mother to baby is 30 to 40%. It can happen during pregnancy, delivery, or breastfeeding.
- Antiretroviral therapy during pregnancy reduces this risk to less than 1%.

### Consent

Testing needs your informed consent. This means you agree to the test, understand how it works and why it is being done, and can weigh what the result would mean for you. It is also a chance to talk about what you know about HIV and how to prevent it.

### When infection may have happened

- **Last 72 hours:** see "When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention" above.
- **Recent weeks:** About 50% of people have a primary HIV ("seroconversion") illness 3 to 6 weeks after becoming infected.
  - It resembles glandular fever but can also be non-specific.
  - Symptoms may include fever, swollen glands, sore throat, rash, muscle aches, joint pain, and headache.
  - Symptoms typically last about one week.
  - Mouth or genital ulcers are very suggestive.
- **Years:** HIV is often without symptoms for several years while the CD4 count slowly falls. This raises the risk of HIV-related conditions and then AIDS illnesses.

**HIV-related conditions your doctor may look for:**
- Oral thrush
- Diarrhoea
- Weight loss
- Recurrent respiratory infections
- Skin conditions such as shingles, seborrhoeic dermatitis, recurrent oral or genital herpes, and folliculitis
- Unusual neurological disorders, such as toxoplasma brain abscesses

### CD4 count

- CD4 (helper) lymphocytes help regulate the immune system.
- A normal CD4 count is about 1000 (range 500 to 1500).
- Without treatment, the CD4 count usually drops by about 60 to 80 each year.
- Most people with HIV will not develop an AIDS illness until their CD4 count falls below 200. The lower it falls, the higher the risk.
- Treatment usually leads to a gradual recovery of about 70 each year, and prevents AIDS illnesses.

### The HIV test

- The screening blood test detects antibodies to HIV and an HIV antigen.
- It is not usually positive until about 3 to 4 weeks after infection, but then stays positive for life.
- A positive screening test is confirmed with a second test.
- Point-of-care rapid tests and finger prick tests are available through the New Zealand AIDS Foundation.

### Getting your results

- It will probably be a few days before definitive results are ready. Negative results are likely within 24 hours, but positive results may take several days.
- Your doctor may ask you to book a face-to-face appointment in 5 to 7 days for your results. You may wish to bring a support person.
- It is widely recommended that positive results are not given by phone.

## Management

> **Practice point:** People with HIV, even with excellent long-term control, remain at higher risk of some cancers and cardiovascular disease. Your doctor may screen for specific cancers and manage heart risk factors aggressively.

### While waiting for results

- Use condoms or other barrier methods during sex, or avoid sex.
- Return if you develop glandular fever-like symptoms (tiredness, fever, sore throat, headache, rash, swollen glands).

### If the result is positive

- Your doctor will give you the result in person.
- Treatment will be offered at your first clinic appointment. It completely controls the infection, prevents transmission to others, and allows the immune system to recover, with the expectation of a normal life span.
- Your diagnosis stays completely confidential. Think carefully about the benefits and risks before telling others.
- Your doctor may offer a follow-up appointment in the coming days or weeks, and check what support you have. Free counselling and support is available (see Patient Resources).
- If there are concerns about your mental health, your doctor may refer you for a mental health assessment.
- To prevent passing on HIV, avoid sex or use condoms.
- Contact tracing (partner notification) will be organised by the hospital team.

### If the result is negative

- Your doctor will choose how to tell you the result (in person, by phone, or another way). Your result is confidential and will not be shared with partners, family, friends, or clinicians not involved in your care without your permission.
- If your most recent risk was in the last 3 to 4 weeks, the result may be a false negative. Your doctor may recommend a follow-up test in 2 to 4 weeks.
- If you are at ongoing risk of HIV, your doctor may discuss pre-exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). PrEP is the use of antiretroviral medicines by people without HIV to reduce their risk of getting it. It is funded in New Zealand for some people at high risk.
- Your doctor may also talk with you about what led to the risk and ways to reduce it.

### If the result is indeterminate

- A small number of people get a result that neither confirms nor rules out infection. This may be an infection that cannot yet be diagnosed (for example, during seroconversion) or a non-specific reaction.
- This can be a distressing and uncertain time.
- Your doctor will seek specialist advice. Immediate extra testing (HIV viral load) may be appropriate to find out quickly whether you have HIV.

## Ongoing Care

### Screening

- Women with HIV: cervical screening every year.
- MSM with HIV aged more than 50 years: a careful digital rectal examination every year to check for anal squamous cell carcinoma.

### Other health issues your doctor may manage

- Insulin resistance and metabolic syndrome, managed aggressively if present.
- Cardiovascular risk, assessed every year, especially if you smoke. HIV and HIV medicines raise heart risk above baseline. Your doctor may consider starting a statin at a lower threshold and check for drug interactions.
- Mental health. Depression and other psychiatric illnesses are more common in people with HIV.
- Drug and alcohol issues.
- Social issues such as housing.

### Viral load and CD4 monitoring

The hospital team will regularly check your HIV viral load, but may not need to check your CD4 count.

- **CD4 count:** the best guide to the risk of an AIDS illness in the coming months. It is usually measured at diagnosis but rarely after that, because it is expected to rise once treatment starts. Treatment is now recommended for everyone, whatever the CD4 count.
- **Viral load:** key to judging the response to treatment. It is measured at diagnosis, then at 6 weeks, 3 months and 6 months after starting treatment, then every 6 months.
- Treatment aims to lower the viral load below the limit of detection, which is less than 20 copies/mL. In most people with successfully treated HIV, results are either below this limit or "undetected".
- Brief, modest rises ("blips") can happen, for example during infections, after vaccinations, or for no clear reason. They almost never harm long-term control.

### Antiretroviral therapy (ART)

- Treatment combines three antiviral medicines.
- Once started, treatment is life-long. If you take it very consistently, drug resistance is extremely unlikely.
- Most widely used regimens have minimal, if any, short- or long-term side effects. Medicines are now often combined, so most people take only 1 or 2 pills once daily.
- An undetectable viral load maintained by taking HIV medicines consistently is virtually 100% effective at preventing sexual transmission.
- HIV medicines may be dispensed by hospital outpatient pharmacies and community pharmacies.
- Your doctor will check for interactions with HIV medicines before starting any new medicine, including over-the-counter ones.
- Your doctor may also check for kidney problems and dental problems.

### Vaccinations

Your doctor will make sure your vaccinations are up to date, including:
- Hepatitis B
- HPV (encouraged for all MSM)
- Influenza
- Pneumococcal
- Meningococcal

A CD4 count below 200 means live vaccines should not be given. Examples include MMR (measles, mumps, rubella), oral rotavirus, oral polio, oral typhoid, BCG (tuberculosis), varicella zoster (chickenpox and Zostavax), and yellow fever.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor will refer you if:
- **You are newly diagnosed with HIV:** urgent referral to an infectious diseases or sexual health clinic. This outpatient appointment is usually within a few weeks.
- **Your test result is indeterminate:** your doctor will seek infectious diseases or sexual health advice.
- **There are concerns about your mental health:** referral for a mental health assessment.
