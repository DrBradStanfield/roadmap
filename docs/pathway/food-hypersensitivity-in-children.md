# Food Hypersensitivity in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to adverse reactions to food in children — helping distinguish food allergy (immune-mediated) from food intolerance (non-immune), and guiding assessment and management.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance immediately if:

- Allergic reaction with **respiratory or cardiac signs or symptoms** (possible anaphylaxis)
- **Shock** due to food protein-induced enterocolitis syndrome (FPIES)

---

## Background

Food hypersensitivity affects 4–8% of children under 5 years and appears to be becoming more common. It includes two main categories:

### Food Allergy (immune-mediated)

Food allergy may be IgE-mediated, non-IgE-mediated, or a combination of both. Eight foods cause up to 90% of allergic reactions:

- Cow's milk
- Eggs
- Peanuts
- Tree nuts
- Fish
- Shellfish
- Soy
- Wheat

### Food Intolerance (non-immune)

Caused by:

- **Enzyme deficiency** — e.g., lactose intolerance (lactase deficiency). Symptoms include diarrhoea, abdominal pain, bloating, and flatulence. Not common in babies and pre-school children. The condition is uncomfortable but not dangerous — it does not cause rash or anaphylaxis. Small amounts of milk and hard cheeses or yoghurt are usually tolerated.
- **Toxicity** — e.g., food poisoning
- **Pharmacological reactions** — e.g., caffeine, MSG, salicylates, amines
- **Psychological food avoidance**

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess the situation as follows:

**1. If acutely unwell**, they will first do a rapid assessment of airway, breathing, circulation, and consciousness — to consider anaphylaxis, FPIES, and non-allergy causes.

**2. History** — your doctor will ask about:

- Which food is suspected (milk, egg, peanut, nut, sesame, fish, shellfish, kiwifruit, wheat, and soy account for most reactions)
- Route of exposure (ingestion, touch, inhalation), quantity, raw or cooked
- Timing of symptoms after eating
- Symptoms suggesting IgE-mediated allergy (typically within minutes to 2 hours):
  - Skin: hives (urticaria), swelling (angio-oedema), flushing, itching
  - Gut: vomiting, abdominal pain, diarrhoea
  - Systemic: pallor, dizziness, collapse
  - Respiratory: runny nose, sneezing, tongue swelling, hoarseness, cough, wheeze, stridor
- In children under 2 years: severe eczema unresponsive to treatment, poor growth, ongoing diarrhoea with blood loss, or refractory vomiting may suggest food allergy
- Duration of symptoms
- Other triggers (exercise, heat, cold, emotional stress) that may cause similar symptoms
- Personal or family history of atopic disease or food allergy

Risk of a life-threatening reaction is higher with: increasing age (especially adolescents), asthma, or peanut/nut allergy.

**3. Determining the type of allergic reaction** (if food allergy is suspected):

*IgE-mediated reactions* — rapid onset within minutes to 2 hours. Presentations include:
- Anaphylaxis: severe reaction involving 2 or more body systems (systemic collapse, respiratory symptoms)
- Oral allergy syndrome (OAS): itching, burning, and swelling of lips/mouth/tongue — usually only with raw fruit and vegetables. A cross-reaction caused by pollen sensitisation (grass, birch).

*Non-IgE-mediated reactions* — generally delayed (more than 2 hours, sometimes days). Symptoms are predominantly gastrointestinal. Presentations include:
- **Coeliac disease**
- **Food protein-induced proctocolitis (FPIP)**: visible blood with or without mucus on the stool surface. Infant is otherwise well with normal growth. Most common cause is cow's milk. Occurs in breast- and formula-fed infants.
- **Eosinophilic oesophagitis**: feeding difficulties, vomiting, or reflux not responding to standard treatment; in older children, dysphagia or food impaction. Diagnosed on endoscopy.
- **Infantile colic**: mechanisms poorly understood; usually improves by 4 months without treatment.
- **Cow's milk protein-induced GORD**
- **Food protein-induced enterocolitis syndrome (FPIES)**: profuse vomiting (sometimes diarrhoea) 2–4 hours after eating. Child may become pale, floppy, hypothermic, or have low blood pressure. Most common causes are cow's milk, soy, and rice. Do not retry the food.

**4. Allergy testing** (for IgE-mediated allergy only):

Your doctor may consider allergy testing for specific suspected allergens — not broad panels. Two types of test are used:

- **Skin prick test (SPT)**: suitable from 6 months of age. Antihistamines must be stopped 3 days before. A positive result indicates sensitisation — diagnosis is based on history plus the test result together.
- **Serum specific IgE (ssIgE) test**: useful if SPT cannot be done (e.g., extensive eczema, inability to stop antihistamines), or if SPT is negative despite strong clinical concern. More expensive than SPT.

Important notes on testing:
- Do not test foods that are already tolerated in the diet — a positive result is not a reason to remove a tolerated food
- Do not routinely do both SPT and ssIgE — if one is negative despite a suggestive history, consider the other
- There are no validated tests for non-IgE-mediated reactions
- Total serum IgE measurement has no role in food allergy diagnosis
- If IgE-mediated cow's milk allergy is suspected, test for soy as a potential alternative

## Management

> **Practice point:** Do not remove tolerated foods. If a child is eating and tolerating a food, do not remove it based solely on a positive test result. Maintaining exposure to tolerated foods supports ongoing tolerance.

### Acute Management

If acutely unwell, your doctor will manage according to the likely diagnosis:

- **Suspected anaphylaxis**: call an ambulance immediately. While waiting, intramuscular adrenaline (e.g., EpiPen) should be given according to the anaphylaxis protocol.
- **Suspected acute FPIES**: call an ambulance. Your doctor may consider intravenous rehydration. Ondansetron may be used. Note: adrenaline and antihistamines are not useful for FPIES.
- **Rectal bleeding suggesting FPIP**: seek urgent paediatric advice.

### IgE-Mediated Food Allergy

Your doctor may consider the following:

1. **For urticaria or angio-oedema alone** (non-anaphylactic IgE-mediated reaction), your doctor may prescribe an antihistamine for symptom relief. Note that antihistamines do not prevent progression to anaphylaxis. Corticosteroids (prednisolone, hydrocortisone) have no proven benefit in acute IgE-mediated reactions.

   Antihistamine options (avoid sedating antihistamines such as promethazine):
   - **Loratadine**:
     - 1–2 years: 2.5 mg once daily
     - 2–12 years (under 30 kg): 5 mg once daily
     - 2–12 years (over 30 kg): 10 mg once daily
     - Over 12 years: 10 mg once daily
   - **Cetirizine**:
     - 1–2 years: 250 microgram/kg twice daily (max 5 mg/day)
     - 2–6 years: 2.5 mg twice daily
     - 6–12 years: 5 mg twice daily
     - Over 12 years: 10 mg once daily

2. **Eliminate the suspected food allergen from the diet** — strict elimination means reading labels carefully for hidden sources. For cow's milk protein allergy (CMPA), unsuitable alternatives include goat's milk, A2 milk, lactose-free milk, rice/almond/oat milks (in infancy), and partially hydrolysed formulas (e.g., NAN-HA).

   Suitable formula options for CMPA:
   - If breastfed: continue breastfeeding. Maternal allergen avoidance is rarely required.
   - If formula-fed under 6 months: extensively hydrolysed formula (e.g., Aptamil Gold, Pepti-Junior — Special Authority required).
   - If formula-fed over 6 months: try soy formula (e.g., S26 Soy) first. If no improvement after 2–4 weeks or immediate IgE reaction occurs, use extensively hydrolysed formula.

3. **Provide an action plan** — copies should be kept at school, nursery, and kindergarten. Severity of a reaction cannot be predicted by past reactions or test results, though it does relate to dose of allergen ingested.

4. **Prognosis**: Most children eventually tolerate milk, egg, soy, and wheat (approximately 80% of cow's milk allergy resolves by age 5). Many children allergic to undercooked milk or eggs will tolerate them in baked form. Peanut, tree nut, fish, and shellfish allergies resolve in only 10–20% of cases.

5. **Adrenaline autoinjector (EpiPen)** — your doctor will prescribe this if:
   - There is a history of food anaphylaxis
   - Or there is an IgE-mediated generalised reaction plus asthma (especially poorly controlled) or the child lives/travels far from medical help

   Prescribing:
   - 7.5–20 kg: junior autoinjector
   - Over 20 kg: adult autoinjector
   - Autoinjectors have a limited shelf life and require regular replacement
   - Training in use is essential when prescribing

6. **Optimise eczema management** — preserving the skin barrier is important. Eczema does not generally respond to dietary changes.

7. **Optimise asthma management** — poorly controlled asthma is a risk factor for severe food-allergic reactions, especially in adolescents.

8. **Immunisations** — MMR and influenza vaccines are safe for children with egg allergy, even those with a history of severe reactions.

9. **MedicAlert** — your doctor may consider this for older school-aged children eating outside the home.

### Non-IgE-Mediated Food Allergy

Your doctor may consider the following:

1. **Eliminate cow's milk protein (CMP)** if gastrointestinal symptoms suggest non-IgE-mediated CMP allergy:
   - If breastfed: try maternal CMP elimination
   - Under 6 months: extensively hydrolysed formula (e.g., Aptamil Gold, Pepti-Junior)
   - Over 6 months: try soy formula first (e.g., S26 Soy); if no improvement after 2 weeks, switch to extensively hydrolysed formula
   - Most diet-responsive infants with colic will improve within one week of dietary modification
   - If no improvement after 2–4 weeks, your doctor may try amino acid formula (e.g., Neocate, EleCare — Special Authority required) or reintroduce CMP

2. **Follow-up by condition**:
   - **FPIP**: symptoms should resolve once causative food is removed. Most infants develop tolerance to CMP by 12 months. Resolution occurs in 50% by age 6 months and 95% by age 9 months. Reintroduction is generally recommended after 6 months of elimination or at 12 months of age.
   - **Infantile colic**: encourage reintroduction of dairy after 4 months (when colic typically resolves) to avoid developing intolerance.
   - **CMP-induced GORD**: reintroduction of CMP usually successful by age 12–18 months.
   - **FPIES**: follow-up determined by the treating paediatrician.

### Food Intolerance — Lactose Intolerance

Your doctor may advise:
- Reducing or avoiding dairy products containing lactose
- Lactose-free cow's milk-based formula is available from supermarkets and pharmacies
- Nut, cereal, or plain goat's milk is not recommended as a main milk source for infants under 12 months

### Preventing Food Allergy

Your doctor may advise:
- Breastfeed for at least 6 months. Excluding allergenic foods from the maternal diet during pregnancy or breastfeeding has not been shown to reduce the risk of food allergy in the child.
- If breastfeeding is not an option, standard formula is recommended. Hydrolysed formulas have not been shown to reduce allergy risk.
- Soy or other mammalian milks (e.g., goat) are not recommended for allergy prevention. Standard cow's milk can be introduced after 12 months.
- Solids can be introduced from 4–6 months. Introduce one food at a time every 2–3 days. Delaying allergenic foods (eggs, nuts, wheat, fish) does not prevent allergy.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor will refer to paediatrics if:

- Suspected or confirmed anaphylaxis
- Suspected cow's milk protein allergy
- Sensitisation or allergy to peanut
- Multiple food allergy
- Serious non-IgE-mediated allergy (e.g., FPIES)
- Possible eosinophilic oesophagitis requiring investigation
- Food allergy coexists with asthma
- Food allergy persists past age 5
- Uncertain about diagnosis or management
- Concerns about growth
- Severe intolerance or malabsorption of extensively hydrolysed formula
- Possible food challenge needed (diagnostic uncertainty or suspected outgrown allergy)
