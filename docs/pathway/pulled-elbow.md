# Pulled Elbow

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of pulled elbow (radial head subluxation) in young children.

---

## Background

Pulled elbow is most common in children under 5 years old.

It typically occurs when a child has been lifted by the hand — for example, when an adult tries to prevent a child from falling. It may not take much force to cause it. A young child who suddenly refuses to use their arm may have a pulled elbow.

When the forearm is pulled, the radial head moves downward and the annular ligament becomes torn and trapped in the joint. Reduction is generally a quick and straightforward procedure that does not usually require pain relief.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess for the typical presentation of a pulled elbow, which includes a child who is:

- Not using their arm
- Supporting the affected arm or wrist
- Holding the affected arm semi-flexed, drawn in toward the body, and with the palm facing down
- Not in pain unless the elbow is moved

**Your doctor may consider an elbow X-ray if:**

- The presentation is atypical
- There is a history of significant trauma
- A fracture is suspected (significant tenderness with swelling, bruising, or deformity)
- Reduction is unsuccessful after 2 to 3 attempts

Note: The X-ray will be normal if the diagnosis is a pulled elbow. The radial head will often click back into position just from the positioning required for the X-ray.

---

## Management

Your doctor will attempt to reduce the elbow using one or both of the following techniques:

### Supination-Flexion Technique

1. Place the arm in pronation with the elbow at 90° flexion and apply gentle pressure over the radial head.
2. With the wrist firmly supinated (turned palm up), maintain pressure on the radial head and fully flex the elbow.

### Hyperpronation Technique

1. Support the child's elbow flexed at 90° with one hand, applying gentle thumb pressure over the radial head while stabilising the upper arm.
2. Hold the child's hand with the other hand as if shaking hands, then turn the palm down (pronation) and straighten the elbow while maximally pronating the forearm.

An audible click is often felt or heard over the radial head with either technique — this indicates a high probability of successful reduction.

**After reduction**, your doctor will observe the child for 10 minutes. If the child is using their arm normally, no further treatment is required.

**If reduction is unsuccessful after 2 to 3 attempts**, your doctor may:

- Arrange an elbow X-ray
- Place the arm in an above-elbow back slab and reassess the following day
- Request an acute orthopaedic assessment
