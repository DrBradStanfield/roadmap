# Pelvic Mesh Complications in Women

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway is for women who develop problems after surgical mesh was inserted for pelvic organ prolapse or urinary stress incontinence. Complications can arise soon after surgery or develop gradually over many years.

---

## Background

Surgical mesh is a porous polypropylene material used by urologists and gynaecologists to repair prolapse and treat urinary incontinence. It is also used by general surgeons for hernia repair and rectopexy, where complication rates are much lower.

Complications are more common after transvaginal prolapse surgery than other procedures, and further surgery is required in approximately 16% of patients. Complications include:

- Infection of the bladder or vagina
- Mesh moving into (exposing into) the bladder or urethra
- Mesh working its way into (extruding into) the vagina

Surgery to remove mesh may not resolve all symptoms, and can be complex — particularly for prolapse mesh, where larger sheets are used that can involve pelvic nerves.

Note: Some women may not be aware that mesh was used during their surgery.

---

## Symptoms That May Suggest Mesh Complications

Symptoms can arise at the original surgical site or in seemingly unrelated areas (such as the leg). Your doctor will consider mesh complications if you have had any pelvic surgery and experience:

- Signs of a fistula — non-healing wound, leakage of faeces from the vagina or bladder, or urine leaking from the vagina or rectum
- Urinary incontinence
- Bowel incontinence
- Overactive bladder
- Difficulty sitting or walking
- Urinary tract or vaginal infections (especially if recurring)
- Musculoskeletal problems
- Pelvic, groin, or leg pain (location, duration, and nature)
- Feeling or seeing exposed mesh
- Vaginal adhesions or scarring
- Unexplained vaginal bleeding or discharge
- Pain or discomfort during sexual activity

Symptoms of possible autoimmune involvement may include joint pain and swelling, skin problems, digestive issues, recurring fever, or swollen glands. There is some anecdotal evidence these may be linked to mesh, though this is not yet proven.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Ask about symptom frequency, duration, and what makes them better or worse
- Explore how symptoms affect your mood, mobility, relationships, sleep, and ability to work or carry out daily activities
- Ask about fatigue, recurrent infections, and any new musculoskeletal or autoimmune symptoms
- Examine the abdomen, vagina, and pelvis for tenderness, visible or palpable mesh, scarring, and signs of infection
- Perform a neurological examination if pain extends outside the pelvis (e.g. down the leg)
- Perform a pelvic examination if clinically appropriate — this is not always required for referral, and may not be possible if pain is severe

Your doctor may also:

- Review operation records from 1995 onwards to confirm whether mesh was used (it may be recorded as tape, sling, mini-sling, TVT, TVT-O, Monarc, or similar terms)
- Arrange blood tests (e.g. full blood count, CRP, kidney and liver function) based on your presentation
- Arrange a urine test (microscopy, culture, and sensitivity)
- Take vaginal swabs if infection or discharge is present
- Arrange imaging if needed to distinguish mesh complications from another diagnosis

---

## Management

Your doctor will:

- Discuss what mesh is and what may be happening, and validate your concerns — many women with mesh complications are already well-informed about this issue
- Treat urinary tract infections and other acute conditions as usual, while noting that infections near exposed mesh may not fully resolve until the mesh is addressed
- Monitor your symptoms over time, as mesh-related issues can worsen gradually — you should return if symptoms recur or change
- Consider referral to a gynaecologist, urologist, or specialist mesh service if it is unclear whether mesh is involved

If mesh complications are thought to be the cause of your symptoms, your doctor may consider:

- Starting an ACC treatment injury claim on your behalf
- Discussing the New Zealand Female Pelvic Mesh Service (FPMS) — a multidisciplinary team of specialists (urology, urogynaecology, complex pain, allied health) who assess women with mesh-related problems. The FPMS offers pelvic floor imaging, surgery, continence care, chronic pain management, and psychological support. It accepts ACC-funded and publicly funded patients and does not require an accepted ACC claim before assessment
- Referring you to local services such as physiotherapy (bladder retraining, pelvic floor exercises), continence services, chronic pain management, gynaecology, or urology — depending on your symptoms and preferences
- If mesh removal surgery is needed, advising that this must be performed by a credentialed surgeon with specialist expertise in mesh complications

For chronic pain extending outside the pelvis, your doctor will follow a chronic pain management approach.
