# Radiation in Medical Imaging

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This page helps you understand the radiation involved in common diagnostic imaging procedures, how doses compare to everyday background radiation, and why some patients need extra caution.

---

## Background: Natural Radiation

Everyone is exposed to low-level radiation from the environment every day — from soil, building materials, cosmic rays, and altitude. This is called natural background radiation.

- The average person receives approximately **3 millisieverts (mSv) per year** from natural background radiation.
- A millisievert (mSv) is the unit used to measure radiation dose, adjusted for biological effect.
- Radiation from diagnostic imaging is measured in the same units, which makes comparison straightforward.

---

## Which Scans Use Radiation?

Not all imaging uses ionising radiation:

- **X-ray, mammography, CT, fluoroscopy** — use ionising radiation
- **Ultrasound and MRI** — do **not** use ionising radiation

Your doctor weighs the benefit of the information gained against the small radiation risk when choosing an imaging test.

---

## Radiation Dose Comparison

The table below shows approximate radiation doses for common procedures and how long it would take to receive the same dose from natural background radiation alone:

| Procedure | Approximate dose | Equivalent background radiation |
|---|---|---|
| Hand, wrist, ankle, or foot X-ray | Less than 0.001 mSv | Less than 3 hours |
| DEXA (bone densitometry) | 0.001 mSv | 3 hours |
| Chest X-ray | 0.100 mSv | 10 days |
| Mammogram | 0.400 mSv | 7 weeks |
| Lumbar spine X-ray | 1.400 mSv | 6 months |
| CT head | 1.600 mSv | 7 months |
| CT chest | 6.100 mSv | 2 years |
| CT colonography (CTC) | 6.000 mSv | 2 years |
| CT abdomen/pelvis | 7.700 mSv | 2.6 years |
| PET-CT scan (whole body) | 22.000 mSv | 7 years |

*Source: RadiologyInfo — Radiology Dose in X-Ray and CT Exams*

## Organ Sensitivity to Radiation

Different body tissues vary in how sensitive they are to radiation, based on how rapidly their cells divide:

- **Higher sensitivity:** Gonads, bone marrow, lymphatic tissue, colon, stomach, bladder, and eyes
- **Medium sensitivity:** Breast, brain, liver, thyroid, and lung
- **Lower sensitivity:** Blood vessels, muscle, bone surface, and nerves

---

## Special Considerations

### Children

Children are at greater risk from radiation than adults because:

- Their cells divide more rapidly (supporting growth), making them more susceptible to radiation-related disruption
- They have a longer life expectancy ahead, giving more time for any radiation effects to become apparent

Your doctor will carefully consider the appropriateness of any radiation-based imaging in children, and will prefer ultrasound or MRI where these can answer the clinical question.

### Pregnancy

- The developing fetus is at increased risk due to rapidly dividing cells
- Risk is highest in early pregnancy, particularly between **weeks 4 and 8**
- Always tell your doctor or imaging staff if you are pregnant or think you might be pregnant before any imaging procedure

Your doctor and the radiology team will discuss pregnancy status before ordering any radiation-based imaging, and will consider whether alternative imaging (ultrasound or MRI) is appropriate.

---

## What Your Doctor Considers

When ordering any imaging that uses radiation, your doctor will consider:

- Whether the scan is appropriate for your clinical situation
- How urgently the imaging is needed
- Relevant history from previous scans (to minimise cumulative exposure)
- Whether an alternative without radiation (such as ultrasound or MRI) could provide the same information
- Your specific circumstances, including age and pregnancy status

All decisions about imaging should be discussed with your doctor.
