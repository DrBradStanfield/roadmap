# Open-angle Glaucoma

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and ongoing management of open-angle glaucoma (OAG) and related risk factors.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

**Acute angle-closure glaucoma (AACG)** is a medical emergency — symptoms include:

- Sudden eye pain
- Recent loss of vision
- Red eye
- Nausea

If these symptoms are present, seek emergency ophthalmology assessment immediately.

---

## Background

Open-angle glaucoma has **no symptoms until it is very advanced**, at which point it causes irreversible loss of vision.

Key definitions:

- **Open-angle glaucoma** — optic disc and visual field changes that provide good evidence of glaucoma
- **High risk of developing glaucoma** — intraocular pressure (IOP) of 28 mmHg or higher, or closure of the angle of the anterior chamber

OAG is underdiagnosed in Māori and Pacific communities — access to eye care may contribute to this disparity.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Family history of glaucoma
- History of direct eye injury
- Long-term steroid use (any route — systemic, topical, inhaled, intranasal, or dermal)
- Previous use of glaucoma eye drops
- Regularity of eye checks — recommended every 2 to 5 years from age 40, especially for Māori and Pacific people

**Steroid use and eye pressure:** Long-term steroid use carries approximately a 20% risk of raised intraocular pressure (IOP). Patients on long-term steroids should have routine annual IOP checks with an optometrist. Patients on intermittent low-dose steroids (e.g. inhaled or intranasal) used infrequently do not routinely require testing.

---

## Management

Management depends on individual circumstances. Your doctor may consider:

### If you are already on glaucoma drops

- Repeat prescriptions will be provided — ongoing use is important, even if drops cause mild irritation
- Your doctor will seek specialist ophthalmology advice if you have:
  - Constant eye pain
  - Blurred vision
  - A dermatitis-like skin reaction around the eyes
- Your doctor will check that your eye pressure and optic disc are being monitored regularly by an ophthalmologist or an optometrist with prescribing scope for glaucoma

### If you are starting steroid medication and have known glaucoma

- Your doctor will seek ophthalmology advice before starting systemic or high-dose topical steroid treatment

### If you have glaucoma but have not had recent specialist follow-up

- Your doctor will arrange a non-acute ophthalmology assessment to re-establish monitoring

### If you have risk factors but no confirmed diagnosis

- Your doctor will arrange assessment with a community optometrist or ophthalmologist if you have risk factors such as family history of glaucoma, history of eye injury, or long-term steroid use
