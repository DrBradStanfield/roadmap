# Shin Pain

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of shin pain in adults, most commonly caused by exercise-related overuse injuries.

---

## Background

Shin pain is usually caused by exercise, especially with changes in intensity or type of activity. It is an overuse or repetitive-load injury of the shin leading to persistent dull anterior leg pain.

Conditions include:

- **Shin splints** (medial tibial stress syndrome, MTSS)
- **Stress fractures**
- **Tendinitis** (usually tibialis posterior)
- **Chronic exertional compartment syndrome** (lateral shin splints) — due to swelling of the tibialis anterior

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Exercise history and any recent changes in intensity or type
- Whether pain occurs after exercise, during exercise, or at rest
- Site of the pain (medial or lateral tibial)
- Contributing factors: poorly fitting shoes, exercising on hard surfaces, previous foot problems, weight gain

Physical examination typically includes:

- Gait assessment
- Examination of the shin, ankle, and foot for local tenderness and swelling

**Shin splints (MTSS):**
- Tenderness just medial to the tibia over a diffuse area
- Common in athletes; causes are often multifactorial and related to biomechanical abnormalities
- Early stages: pain with exertion only. Over time: pain with less activity and at rest

**Stress fracture:**
- Localised bone pain and swelling, typically tender over a specific point on the tibia
- May be visible on X-ray or bone scan

**Chronic exertional compartment syndrome:**
- Pain typically over the lateral shin
- Due to swelling of the tibialis anterior muscle during exercise (causing relative muscle ischaemia)
- May be associated with a numb big toe

**Tibialis posterior dysfunction (flat foot syndrome):**
- Dysfunction of the tibialis posterior tendon contributing to progressive flat foot deformity

If a stress fracture is suspected, your doctor may arrange:

- A leg X-ray (may show a stress fracture 2 to 12 weeks after onset of symptoms)
- If X-rays are normal but stress fracture is still suspected, a radionuclide bone scan (shows increased bone activity at the fracture site)

---

## Management

Your doctor will tailor management to the specific diagnosis:

### General (all shin pain)

Your doctor may advise:

- Rest, elevation, ice, and pain relief at symptom onset — do not wait for imaging
- Avoid the aggravating activity for 8 to 10 weeks, then gradually return at reduced distance, frequency, and intensity
- Low-impact and cross-training exercises during rehabilitation (e.g. water-based activities, fitness machines, cycling)
- Regular stretching
- Shoe modification — orthotics may be helpful; your doctor may refer to podiatry if required
- Symptoms may take up to a year to settle completely

### Shin Splints (Medial Tibial Stress Syndrome)

Your doctor may consider:

- Initial rest
- Physiotherapy to address potential biomechanical abnormality
- Podiatry or sports medicine assessment for biomechanical evaluation
- Encouraging avoidance of aggravating activities

### Stress Fractures

Your doctor may consider:

- Referral for orthopaedic assessment
- Follow-up X-rays to monitor healing

### Tibialis Posterior Dysfunction (Flat Foot Deformity)

Your doctor may consider:

- Physiotherapy — eccentric exercises
- Podiatry — orthotics (medial arch support and medial heel wedge)
- Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) if tolerated

If pain progresses and you develop all of the following, your doctor may consider referral for orthopaedic foot and ankle assessment:

- Hindfoot deformity on weight-bearing but with a flexible foot
- Increasing pain about the medial hindfoot
- Tenderness and pain in the subfibular (lateral hindfoot) region

Note: as the deformity becomes more fixed and arthritic, treatment becomes more difficult — early assessment is important if symptoms are progressing.

### Chronic Exertional Compartment Syndrome

Your doctor may advise:

- Modifying contributing activities
- Rest and avoiding overuse of affected muscles
- Changing exercise routine — low-impact exercises or training on a softer surface (e.g. a soft track instead of hard concrete)
- Orthotics or cushioning in soles
- Anti-inflammatory medications
- In persistent cases, referral to sports medicine or an orthopaedic surgeon
