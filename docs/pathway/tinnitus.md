# Tinnitus

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of tinnitus — the perception of sound (buzzing, ringing, roaring, or hissing) in the absence of external stimuli.

---

## Background

Tinnitus is the perception of sound in the absence of external stimuli. It is commonly described as buzzing, ringing, roaring, or a hissing sound. It can be unilateral or bilateral, intermittent or constant, and pulsatile or non-pulsatile.

Tinnitus is common, especially in men and smokers, and its incidence increases with age.

Common causes include:

- Hearing loss (e.g. age-related hearing loss, presbycusis)
- Noise exposure
- Neck or head injuries (e.g. whiplash)
- Ear-related problems (e.g. infection, barotrauma from scuba diving, ear wax blocking the canal)
- Stress or anxiety
- Raised blood pressure
- Ototoxic medications (medications toxic to the ear)

There is a wide spectrum of experience and impact on quality of life from tinnitus. Alongside assessment and treatment of medical causes, desensitisation and reducing the impact of tinnitus on daily life are the main aims of management.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Care

Tinnitus is usually not an emergency, but your doctor will want to investigate promptly if you have:

- Unilateral (one-sided) tinnitus — this requires an audiogram to rule out vestibular schwannoma (acoustic neuroma) or arteriovenous malformation
- Pulsatile tinnitus (in time with your heartbeat) — your doctor will assess for a vascular cause
- Asymmetric hearing loss on audiogram — may indicate a vestibular schwannoma and warrants specialist referral
- Neurological symptoms alongside tinnitus — such as facial weakness, loss of facial sensation, vertigo or imbalance, or cerebellar signs

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will typically assess tinnitus by asking about:

**Nature and history of the tinnitus:**

- **Constant tinnitus** — possible causes include recent trauma (loud noise, head injury, or barotrauma), noise exposure, or ototoxic medications. Tinnitus due to trauma should resolve over hours.
- **Fluctuating unilateral tinnitus with episodic vertigo and hearing loss** — your doctor may consider Ménière's disease (episodic vertigo, tinnitus, and sensorineural hearing loss, usually low frequency; attacks last 1–24 hours; onset typically aged 20–50 years).
- **Pulsatile tinnitus** — your doctor will consider vascular causes including atherosclerosis, aneurysms, arteriovenous malformations, vascular tumours, or benign intracranial hypertension.

**Medications that may cause tinnitus (ototoxic medications):**

- Salicylates
- Nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs)
- Antibiotics including gentamicin, vancomycin, macrolide antibiotics, minocycline, and chloramphenicol
- Diuretics
- Quinine and antimalarials
- PDE5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil)
- Platinum-based chemotherapy agents
- Certain ear drops (e.g. Sofradex, Soframycin, Kenacomb) — small risk of inner ear damage

**Psychological factors:** Stress, anxiety, and depression are commonly associated with chronic tinnitus.

**Physical examination** typically includes:

- Inspecting the ear canal and removing any wax
- Checking the eardrum for signs of effusion, infection, or other abnormalities
- Tuning fork tests (Weber and Rinne tests) to assess the type of hearing loss
- Examination of the head and neck — including listening for bruits (blood flow sounds) if pulsatile tinnitus is present
- Brief cranial nerve examination if tinnitus is unilateral — looking for facial weakness, loss of corneal reflex, nystagmus, or cerebellar signs

**Investigations:** Your doctor will arrange an audiogram (not just a hearing screen) to check for asymmetric sensorineural hearing loss.

## Management

Your doctor will manage tinnitus based on the identified cause. They may consider:

**Referral for specialist assessment:**

- Your doctor may request a non-acute ear, nose, and throat (ORL/ENT) assessment if you have unilateral pulsatile tinnitus.
- Your doctor may consider referral to a private otolaryngologist (ENT specialist) or audiologist with a special interest in tinnitus for:
  - Noise-induced hearing loss
  - Bilateral tinnitus associated with age-related hearing loss (presbycusis)
  - Education and counselling about tinnitus
  - Tinnitus treatments, including sound therapy devices
  - Hearing aids if tinnitus is associated with hearing loss — hearing aids often help significantly

**Reviewing medications:**

- If you are taking any ototoxic medications, your doctor will consider whether these should be stopped to prevent progression to hearing loss or balance problems, even if the tinnitus does not resolve. Tinnitus is often the first sign of ototoxicity.

**Managing the impact of tinnitus on quality of life:**

Once a serious underlying cause has been ruled out, treatment focuses on reducing tinnitus's impact on daily life. Your doctor may consider:

- Teaching relaxation techniques — tinnitus is closely associated with, and aggravated by, stress
- Advising lifestyle changes:
  - Avoid exposure to loud sound; use hearing protection in noisy environments
  - Exercise regularly, use relaxation strategies, and avoid becoming overtired
  - Manage blood pressure
  - Avoid silence — use sound therapy at bedtime (e.g. soothing music, white noise, a fan, or a sound therapy device)
  - Reducing or eliminating nicotine, alcohol, caffeine, cheese, salty food, and quinine (tonic water) may be helpful
- Providing education and patient information

**Medications:** Generally, medication is of no benefit for tinnitus symptoms. Tricyclic antidepressants and benzodiazepines may have limited effectiveness, but benzodiazepines should be avoided due to the risk of dependence.
