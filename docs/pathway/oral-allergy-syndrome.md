# Oral Allergy Syndrome

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of oral allergy syndrome — a common cause of mouth tingling or swelling after eating raw fruits and vegetables in people with pollen allergy.

---

## Background

Oral allergy syndrome (OAS) describes local allergic reactions in the mouth to raw fruits and vegetables. Key facts:

- Affects about 1 in 10 people with pollen sensitisation
- Caused by cross-reactive proteins in raw plant foods that resemble pollen allergens — cooking destroys these proteins, which is why most people tolerate cooked versions
- Symptoms are typically mild and localised to the mouth; they respond to antihistamines
- Anaphylaxis is very rare in OAS but may occur if there is a concurrent true food allergy
- Birch pollen allergy is the most common association — 40–60% of people with birch pollen allergy develop OAS
- Latex-fruit syndrome is similar to OAS and occurs in people sensitised to latex, typically with reactions to tropical fruits (e.g. papaya, avocado, kiwifruit)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- **Symptoms with raw fruits and vegetables** — tingling, itching, redness, and/or swelling of the mouth, lips, or sometimes the throat, typically within 5–10 minutes of eating raw fruits or vegetables, while tolerating cooked versions
- **Background pollen allergy** — OAS usually occurs in people with seasonal allergic rhinitis (hay fever)
- **Pollen-food cross-reactions** — common patterns include:
  - Birch pollen: almond, apple, apricot, carrot, cherry, hazelnut, nectarine, peach, peanut, plum, potato, soy
  - Grass pollen: kiwifruit, melon, orange, tomato
  - Ragweed pollen: banana, chamomile, melon
  - Not all people react to all foods on these lists; reactions to tropical fruits (papaya, avocado, kiwifruit, chestnut) may indicate latex-fruit syndrome
- **History of anaphylaxis** — any episode involving compromised airway or low blood pressure

Your doctor will also consider alternative diagnoses, such as irritation from spicy or acidic foods, or perioral dermatitis.

Investigations (e.g. allergy skin prick tests or IgE blood tests) are not routinely needed — the diagnosis is usually clear from the history alone. Food-specific IgE testing should not be performed without a clinical history suggestive of IgE-mediated food allergy.

---

## Management

There is no cure for OAS. Treatment focuses on symptom management and avoiding trigger foods.

- **Antihistamines:** Non-sedating oral antihistamines (e.g. cetirizine, loratadine) are the main treatment. Your doctor may increase the dose up to 4 times the standard dosage if needed. Cetirizine is fully funded in New Zealand and is generally thought to be more effective than loratadine, though it is mildly sedating in about 10% of people.
- **Food preparation:** Most people tolerate cooked fruits and vegetables. Peeling, freezing, or microwaving may also be enough to prevent symptoms.
- **Avoidance:** If you react to cooked fruit and vegetables, or have had systemic reactions, your doctor will advise avoiding these foods. Citrus fruits and pineapple are usually well tolerated.
- **If you have a history of anaphylaxis:** Your doctor will consider seeking specialist immunology advice or referring for a non-acute immunology assessment.

---

## When to Seek Specialist Input

Discuss with your doctor and consider immunology referral if:

- You have a history of anaphylaxis (compromised airway or low blood pressure) with food
- Symptoms extend beyond the mouth (e.g. throat tightening, widespread hives, vomiting, dizziness)
- The diagnosis is uncertain
