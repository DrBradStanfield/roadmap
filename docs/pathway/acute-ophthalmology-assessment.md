# Acute Eye Problems

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to acute eye problems — identifying conditions that need urgent ophthalmology assessment.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Attention

The following conditions require same-day or urgent specialist assessment. See your doctor immediately or call for medical advice if you experience any of these:

### Adults

- **Giant cell arteritis (temporal arteritis)** — severe headache, scalp tenderness, jaw pain, and any visual symptoms in someone over 50
- **Acute iritis (anterior uveitis)** — eye pain, redness, light sensitivity, blurred vision
- **Acute angle-closure glaucoma** — sudden severe eye pain, headache, nausea, blurred vision with haloes around lights, red eye
- **Corneal ulcer or hazy cornea** — eye pain, redness, discharge, reduced vision
- **Scleritis** — deep, severe, boring eye pain (often waking you at night), eye redness; necrotising forms are particularly urgent
- **Acute eye injury** — chemical burn, penetrating injury, or blunt trauma with reduced vision
- **Acute infective conditions** — e.g., acute dacryocystitis (swelling and pain at the inner corner of the eye near the nose)
- **Sudden vision loss** — any sudden decrease or loss of vision in one or both eyes

### Children (additional concerns)

- Absent, very dull, white, or markedly asymmetric red reflex in a very young child
- Photophobia (extreme light sensitivity) in a very young child
- Eyelids swollen shut
- New onset squint (strabismus) or involuntary eye movement (nystagmus)
- Any of the adult conditions above

> **If in doubt, contact your doctor promptly.** Many acute eye conditions can lead to permanent vision loss if treatment is delayed.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your eye problem. This typically includes:

### History

- Nature and onset of symptoms (pain, vision change, discharge, redness)
- Any recent injury, chemical exposure, or foreign body
- Previous eye problems or surgery
- Relevant medical history (e.g., diabetes, high blood pressure, autoimmune conditions)
- Impact on daily life and vision

### Visual Acuity

Your doctor will test how well you can see:

- Each eye is tested separately, with your glasses if you wear them
- A pinhole test may be used if vision is reduced
- If you cannot read any letters on the chart, testing continues with finger counting, hand movements, and light perception
- Near vision is also checked

> Central corneal ulcers or abrasions usually reduce vision, while those on the peripheral cornea may leave vision intact.

### Eye Examination

Your doctor will examine:

- **Red reflex** — checking for abnormalities inside the eye using a light
- **Fundoscopy** — examining the optic disc and retinal blood vessels
- **Visual fields** — checking peripheral vision if relevant
- **Pupillary light reaction** — how the pupils respond to light
- **Colour vision** — any change from normal
- **Fluorescein staining** — a dye test to detect corneal damage, used if there is pain, injury, inflammation, or a visible foreign body

## Management

Management depends on the identified or suspected condition:

- **Giant cell arteritis:** Your doctor will start high-dose corticosteroids urgently and arrange specialist review — vision loss can be permanent if untreated
- **Acute iritis:** Your doctor may prescribe corticosteroid eye drops and pupil-dilating drops, with specialist follow-up
- **Acute angle-closure glaucoma:** Emergency treatment to lower eye pressure; your doctor will arrange urgent specialist assessment
- **Corneal ulcer:** Your doctor may prescribe antibiotic eye drops and arrange urgent ophthalmology review
- **Scleritis:** Your doctor may consider oral NSAIDs and will seek specialist advice; necrotising forms require urgent treatment
- **Chemical burn or penetrating injury:** Immediate irrigation (for chemical burns) and emergency specialist assessment
- **Sudden vision loss:** Urgent investigation and specialist review — some causes (e.g., retinal artery occlusion) are time-critical
- **Acute dacryocystitis:** Your doctor may prescribe oral antibiotics and arrange specialist review if needed

For conditions not meeting criteria for emergency assessment, your doctor may arrange an urgent outpatient appointment with an ophthalmologist.

---

## When to Go to the Emergency Department

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if:

- You have a penetrating eye injury
- You have a severe chemical burn to the eye — rinse with water immediately and continuously while seeking help
- You have sudden, complete vision loss
- You have severe eye pain with nausea, vomiting, and headache (possible acute glaucoma)
