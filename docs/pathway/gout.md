# Gout

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: August 2025.*

Gout is caused by deposition of monosodium urate (MSU) crystals in and around joints, causing episodes of acute inflammation with the potential for joint and soft tissue damage.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Get same-day or urgent medical review if:**
- A joint is exquisitely painful with reduced movement, especially with fever, rapid heart rate, or low blood pressure — this may indicate **septic arthritis**, which requires urgent assessment.
- You have a swollen, hot, severely painful joint for the first time and are unsure of the diagnosis.

**Septic arthritis vs gout:** Both can present as a single swollen painful joint developing over 6–12 hours. They can occur at the same time (especially in patients with tophi). Up to 40–60% of patients with septic arthritis may be afebrile — do not rely on the absence of fever to rule it out.

---

## Background

- Affects about 5.7% of New Zealand adults. Māori men have a prevalence of about 14% and Pacific men approximately 23%.
- The gold standard for diagnosis is demonstration of MSU crystals in synovial fluid or tophi, but in most cases the diagnosis is made clinically.
- Long-term management targets serum urate below 0.36 mmol/L with urate-lowering therapy (ULT). There is no evidence to support ULT in asymptomatic hyperuricaemia.
- Poorly controlled gout is associated with: cardiovascular disease, renal failure, erosive joint damage, and significant social impact.

---

## Assessment

### History

Ask about:
- Which joints are involved:
  - First metatarsophalangeal (MTP) joint involved in 50% of all flares, 70% of first flares.
  - Other common joints: knee, foot, wrist, ankle, hand, elbow.
- Timing and severity of pain:
  - Typical features: rapid onset of excruciating joint pain with swelling, erythema, and tenderness; peak intensity within 24 hours; resolves over 1–3 weeks without treatment.
- Previous episodes and frequency.
- Systemic symptoms: fever and malaise.
- Current medications (including OTC) and adherence.
- Signs of **chronic tophaceous gout** (if gout has been recurring or untreated):
  - Development of tophi (firm subcutaneous nodules; common at olecranon, distal interphalangeal joints, ear pinnae).
  - More frequent flares, more joints affected, incomplete resolution between flares.
  - Chronic inflammatory arthritis, erosive joint damage, urate nephropathy, renal calculi.
- Family history of gout.

### Examination

- Vital signs (temperature, pulse, blood pressure) to assess for systemic illness.
- Joint assessment: soft tissue swelling, erythema, tophi, evidence of joint damage.
- Screen for associated conditions: hypertension, cardiovascular disease, renal impairment, diabetes, obesity, hyperlipidaemia.

### Differential diagnoses to consider

- **Septic arthritis** — must be excluded, especially with systemic signs.
- **Calcium pyrophosphate disease (CPPD)** — previously called pseudogout; CPP crystal inflammation; may present similarly to acute gout; affects the knee in >50% of acute attacks; more common in older adults and those with osteoarthritis.
- **Inflammatory arthritis**
- **Acute rheumatic fever** (in high-risk populations)

### Investigations

- **Serum urate** — levels are normal in up to 50% of patients *during* a gout flare; a normal result does not rule out the diagnosis.
- **Serum creatinine, FBC, CRP, LFTs.**
- **Screening for associated conditions** if clinically appropriate: UACR (kidney screening, especially in Māori and Pacific peoples), HbA1c, lipids.
- **Plain X-ray** — consider only if inflammation persists >12 weeks.
- **Joint aspiration** — consider if there is diagnostic doubt.
  - Do not delay urgent referral to aspirate if septic arthritis is most likely.
  - Fluid sent for cell count, crystals, and Gram stain and culture.
- Consider using the **acute gout diagnosis rule** in patients with monoarthritis (developed and validated in primary care).

## Management

> **Practice point:** For all patients with gout, encourage early initiation and long-term urate-lowering therapy. The goal is sustained urate control, not just treating flares.

### Acute gout flare treatment

NSAIDs, colchicine, and corticosteroids are all effective. Your doctor will choose based on age, kidney function, and co-morbidities (e.g. diabetes, heart failure).

#### NSAIDs (e.g. naproxen 500 mg twice daily)

- Use in lowest effective dose for shortest time.
- Consider adding a proton-pump inhibitor (PPI).
- **Contraindications:** peptic ulcer or active GI inflammatory disease; previous NSAID intolerance; chronic kidney disease; heart failure. Use with caution in patients >75 years or frail.
- Avoid the "triple whammy" combination of ACE inhibitor + diuretic + NSAID (risk of blood pressure rise and kidney injury).

#### Colchicine (low-dose regimen)

Colchicine has a **narrow therapeutic margin**. Overdoses from 7 mg can be fatal and there is no reversal agent. Your doctor will consider whether it is safe in your situation.

- **Low-dose regimen:**
  - Take 1.0 mg (two tablets) at first sign of a flare, then 0.5 mg one hour later. Do not repeat within 3 days.
  - If symptoms persist after 12 hours, take 0.5 mg once to twice daily until the flare settles.
- **Reduced dose** (age >75 years, renal or hepatic impairment, or weight <50 kg):
  - Initial dose: 0.5 mg (one tablet)
  - No more than two tablets in first 24 hours
  - Total maximum 1.5 mg over three days; do not repeat within 3 days
- Stop colchicine if toxicity signs appear: diarrhoea, nausea, vomiting, abdominal pain, or palpitations.
- Safe storage is important — keep out of reach of children and vulnerable persons. Ask for child-proof packaging.

#### Prednisone

Used if the patient is elderly, or if NSAIDs and colchicine are contraindicated.
- Give 20–40 mg daily until the episode resolves, then taper over 1–2 weeks.
- Caution in athletes (prohibited by WADA).

#### Intra-articular corticosteroids

Consider (e.g. triamcinolone acetonide) if a single accessible joint is affected.

### After an acute flare

- If already taking allopurinol or other ULT, continue it — do not stop.
- If response is poor, your doctor will check medication adherence and may arrange joint aspiration to confirm diagnosis.
- Blood tests (serum urate, FBC, CRP, LFTs, creatinine) and clinical review 2–3 weeks after the flare resolves.

## Urate-Lowering Therapy (ULT)

Your doctor will discuss starting ULT at the **first confirmed gout diagnosis** and will strongly recommend it after the **second presentation**, especially if any of the following apply:

**Definite indications for ULT:**
- Recurrent gout (2 or more flares per year)
- Gouty tophi
- Chronic gouty arthritis
- Evidence of joint damage on X-ray

**High risk of severe gout:**
- Early-onset gout
- Strong family history
- Serum urate >0.6 mmol/L

There is no evidence to support ULT for asymptomatic hyperuricaemia.

### Treat-to-target-urate approach

- **Target:** serum urate below 0.36 mmol/L (lower target if tophi are present).
- Dose is adjusted monthly until target is reached.
- Once at target, serum urate is checked every 6–12 months.
- It can take **more than 12 months** after reaching target for flares to stop, and years for tophi to dissolve.
- Continue long-term medication even if a flare occurs.
- Think of your serum urate like HbA1c in diabetes — it needs to be maintained, not just treated during flares.

### Allopurinol (first-line ULT)

Allopurinol can be started during a gout flare (at low dose alongside flare treatment and prophylaxis), or 2 weeks after flare resolution.

**Before starting:**
- Your doctor may test for **HLA-B*5801** (a genetic marker) in certain Asian patients before starting allopurinol, due to the risk of severe hypersensitivity reactions:
  - All patients of Chinese or Thai descent
  - Korean patients with CKD stage 3 or worse
- **Avoid** combining allopurinol with azathioprine or mercaptopurine (serious interactions).

**Starting dose (based on kidney function/eGFR):**
- eGFR ≤30: 50 mg alternate days
- eGFR 31–60: 50 mg daily
- eGFR >60: 100 mg daily

**Dose escalation** (every 4 weeks, aiming for target serum urate):
- eGFR ≤60: increase by 50 mg each step
- eGFR >60: increase by 100 mg each step
- Maximum dose: 900 mg per day
- Kidney function alone does not limit the maximum dose — your doctor will advise.

**Monitoring:**
- Serum urate monthly until target achieved
- FBC, LFTs, and renal function every 3 months during dose escalation
- Serum urate every 6–12 months once at target

**Important:** Stop allopurinol and see your doctor promptly if you develop a rash. Severe hypersensitivity usually occurs in the first 6 weeks.

Use **gout flare prophylaxis** (see below) while starting allopurinol.

### Alternative ULT (if allopurinol is contraindicated, not tolerated, or ineffective)

**Probenecid:**
- Used when allopurinol is not suitable or not working well.
- Avoid if kidney impairment or known kidney stones.
- Maintain high fluid intake (>2 L/day).
- Caution in athletes (prohibited by WADA).

**Febuxostat** (requires Special Authority):
- Consider if urate remains above target despite maximum doses of allopurinol and probenecid, or if allopurinol is not tolerated, or if probenecid is contraindicated due to kidney impairment.
- Starting dose: 80 mg daily; increase to 120 mg daily after 2–4 weeks if urate >0.36 mmol/L.
- Do not exceed 80 mg/day with mild hepatic impairment.
- Provide flare prophylaxis for ≥6 months when starting febuxostat.
- Liver function checked before starting, at 1 and 3 months, then as needed.
- Patients with previous allopurinol rash or kidney disease may also develop hypersensitivity to febuxostat (mostly in first month).
- Avoid combining with azathioprine.

## Gout Flare Prophylaxis (When Starting ULT)

To reduce the risk of flares triggered by starting urate-lowering therapy, your doctor will prescribe prophylaxis for **3–6 months after target serum urate is achieved**.

Options:
- **Colchicine 0.5 mg daily or twice daily** (depending on kidney function):
  - eGFR 30–60: 0.5 mg daily
  - eGFR 10–30: 0.5 mg alternate days
  - eGFR <10: consider alternative
  - Use with care in patients >75 years; check for drug interactions.
- **NSAIDs** if appropriate (e.g. naproxen 250–500 mg twice daily, or ibuprofen 400 mg three times daily).
- **Prednisone** 5–10 mg daily.

A gout flare during allopurinol initiation is **not** a reason to stop allopurinol.

## Medication Review

Some medications raise serum urate — your doctor will review these:
- **May raise urate:** thiazide diuretics, loop diuretics.
- **Neutral:** lisinopril.
- **May lower urate (useful adjuncts — not substitutes for ULT):** losartan, SGLT2 inhibitors, calcium channel blockers.

---

## Lifestyle Advice

Lifestyle changes help but most people **cannot reach target serum urate with lifestyle alone** — ULT is almost always needed.

- Maintain a healthy weight.
- Eat regular meals (both fasting and overeating can trigger flares).
- Drink 2 L of water per day.
- Limit alcohol if intake is above recommended levels.
- Reduce sugar and sugar-sweetened drinks.
- Avoid foods that have previously triggered your flares. Once ULT is established, food triggers may be tolerated.
- **Do not restrict purines strictly** — dietary intake is only a small component of serum urate. A balanced diet is more important.
- Manage associated conditions: hypertension, cardiovascular disease, kidney impairment, diabetes, obesity, hyperlipidaemia.

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor will refer you if:

- **Urgent orthopaedic assessment:** septic arthritis is the most likely diagnosis.
- **Orthopaedic advice:** concern about septic arthritis but less likely; or uncertainty about joint aspiration in a poorly-responding patient.
- **Rheumatology advice or non-acute rheumatology referral:**
  - ULT failure despite good adherence (i.e. recurrent flares with achieved target urate, or progressive joint damage — not simply flares during the first 12 months at target or failure to escalate dose).
  - Intolerance to urate-lowering therapies.
  - Diagnosis still unclear after joint aspiration.
  - Acute flare symptoms persist >2 weeks despite treatment.
  - Renal impairment with concerns about allopurinol dose.

---

## Key Concepts for Patients

- Gout is a chronic disease — it needs long-term treatment, not just treatment of flares.
- The target serum urate is below 0.36 mmol/L. Ask your doctor for your result.
- Even after reaching your target urate, flares can continue for over a year as crystals slowly dissolve.
- Do not stop allopurinol if you have a flare — continue it and treat the flare separately.
- Always discuss any new symptoms or medication questions with your doctor before making changes.
