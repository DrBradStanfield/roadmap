# Dengue Fever

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of dengue fever, primarily in people who have recently returned from overseas travel to dengue-endemic regions.

> **Always discuss with your doctor.** This information is a general guide only. Your doctor will assess your specific situation and determine the right course of action.

---

## Background

Dengue fever is a viral illness endemic in over 100 tropical and sub‑tropical countries. It is the most common cause of febrile illness in travellers returning from Asia, and should be considered in anyone returning from:

- Pacific Island countries
- Asia
- Central and South America
- Africa
- Northern Queensland

There is no specific treatment or widely available vaccine, but early detection and proper medical care lowers fatality rates to less than 1%.

Dengue is transmitted by Aedes mosquitoes (which are not found in New Zealand). It cannot spread via saliva, respiratory droplets, or sexual contact (unless blood is involved).

There are 4 serotypes of dengue virus. Having one serotype does not protect against others — a second dengue infection (secondary dengue) significantly increases the risk of severe disease.

### Disease Phases

The illness has 3 phases:

1. **Febrile phase** — typically lasts up to 7 days. Risk of dehydration, febrile seizures, neurological manifestations.
2. **Critical phase** — usually follows the febrile phase (may begin as early as day 3), lasts 1 to 2 days. This is when severe dengue and shock can develop.
3. **Recovery phase** — lasts 3 to 5 days. Fever absent for more than 48 hours with clinical stability and diuresis.

Approximately 10% of cases progress to severe disease.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Call an ambulance (111) or go to the emergency department if any of the following develop:

**Signs of severe dengue fever:**
- Significant bleeding or bruising (nose, gums, haematemesis, rectal or vaginal bleeding, petechiae, ecchymoses, purpura)
- Dizziness, hypotension, or signs of shock:
  - Cool extremities
  - Weak, thready peripheral pulse
  - Delayed capillary refill
  - Fast heart rate in the absence of fever
  - Narrowing pulse pressure
- Severe abdominal pain or swelling (ascites)
- Breathlessness (pleural effusion)
- Persistent vomiting
- Confusion or impaired consciousness
- Other signs of organ failure

---

## Warning Features — Seek Urgent Medical Review

These features suggest impending severe dengue. Contact your doctor promptly if any of these develop:

- Persistent abdominal pain or tenderness
- Persistent vomiting
- Signs of fluid accumulation (swelling, breathlessness)
- Fall in blood pressure or postural dizziness (especially if aged 65 or older)
- Mucosal bleeding
- Lethargy or unusual restlessness
- Laboratory finding of rising haematocrit and rapidly falling platelet count

Note: Severe dengue fever can also occur with no warning features.

---

## Symptoms of Dengue Fever

Infection may be asymptomatic. Symptoms can range from mild febrile illness to life-threatening shock. Common symptoms include:

- Sudden onset of high fever (not all patients have fever)
- Severe headache
- Pain behind the eyes (retro-orbital pain)
- Muscle or joint pain (myalgia or arthralgia)
- Rash (may be itchy or cause skin hypersensitivity)
- Loss of appetite
- Foul or metallic taste
- Abnormal bleeding or bruising
- Nausea, inability to tolerate food or fluids
- Diarrhoea

---

## Risk Factors for Severe Dengue

Your doctor will consider whether you are at higher risk of severe disease:

- Previous dengue fever (secondary infection significantly increases risk)
- Age less than 1 year old
- Pregnancy
- Underlying conditions — diabetes, high blood pressure, cardiovascular or renal disease, other chronic conditions
- Aged 65 years or older
- Immunocompromise

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Recent travel — exact destinations, dates of travel and return, accommodation type, activities
- Dates of fever onset and duration (typical fever duration: 2 to 7 days)
- Current phase of illness (febrile, critical, or recovery)
- Warning symptoms and severe symptoms
- Risk factors for severe dengue
- Social support and ability to attend for monitoring

Your doctor will examine for signs of dengue, warning signs, and signs of severe disease.

### Investigations

Your doctor will arrange blood tests. It is important to include travel dates, destination, date of onset of symptoms, and clinical features on the laboratory request form — the laboratory needs this information to run the correct tests.

Key tests include:

- **Dengue NS1 antigen** — for days 1 to 9 of illness
- **Full blood count (FBC)** — thrombocytopenia (low platelets) and leucopenia are almost universal in dengue; a rising haematocrit with rapidly falling platelets suggests impending severe dengue
- **Liver function tests (LFTs)** — rising ALT and falling albumin are concerning
- **Mosquito-borne virus PCR panel** — if it is uncertain whether the diagnosis is dengue, Zika virus, or chikungunya (best on days 0 to 5 of illness)

Other tests may be arranged depending on the differential diagnosis.

### Alternative Diagnoses to Consider

Dengue can look like many other illnesses, including: other childhood viral infections, HIV seroconversion, chikungunya, Zika virus, COVID-19, influenza, hepatitis A or B, malaria, typhoid fever, leptospirosis, urinary tract infection, and non-infectious conditions such as ITP or drug reactions.

---

## Management

There is no specific antiviral treatment for dengue fever. Management is supportive.

### Supportive Care

- **Pain relief:** Paracetamol. Do **not** take aspirin or NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen) — these can worsen bleeding risk.
- **Fluids:** Careful fluid replacement with close monitoring of intake and losses (vomiting, urine, diarrhoea).
- **Rest:** Bed rest.

### Monitoring

Your doctor will monitor you regularly from the third day of illness until 1 to 2 days after fever subsides. Monitoring includes:

- Heart rate, blood pressure (narrowing pulse pressure is significant), mental status, fluid balance
- Assessment for warning features and severe features
- Blood tests (platelets and haematocrit) — frequency based on risk factors; some patients require daily testing

### Notification

Dengue fever is a notifiable condition. Your doctor is required to notify public health authorities.

### Dengue Prevention (for travellers to endemic regions)

**Personal protection:**
- Wear insect repellent (DEET, picaridin, oil of lemon eucalyptus, IR3535, or 2-undecanone) indoors and outdoors — reapply every few hours
- Dengue mosquitoes bite mainly during the day, especially at dawn and dusk
- Wear long, loose clothing covering arms and legs
- Wear shoes rather than sandals; wear a hat
- Apply permethrin to clothing (not skin)
- Stay in screened accommodation; use air conditioning where available

**Around accommodation:**
- Remove standing water (buckets, tyres, planters, pet bowls) — mosquitoes breed in small amounts of water
- Ensure window and door screens are in good repair

---

## When to Escalate

Your doctor will arrange urgent hospital assessment if:

- Severe dengue fever is present or suspected
- Warning features of impending severe dengue develop
- Significant blood test abnormalities:
  - Haematocrit rising 20% or more above baseline
  - Platelet count below 50,000 in adults or below 100,000 in children

For children with dengue fever, your doctor will seek paediatric advice.

If management is uncertain, your doctor will seek infectious diseases specialist advice.
