# Urinary Tract Infection (UTI) in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of urinary tract infections (UTIs) in children aged under 15 years.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Your child needs urgent hospital assessment if:

- Aged under 3 months (regardless of severity)
- Appears septic or seriously unwell
- Known structural abnormalities of the urinary tract

---

## Background

UTIs are one of the most common infections in children, affecting approximately 5% of children across all populations. They may be difficult to recognise because symptoms are often nonspecific, particularly in younger children.

- Girls are more commonly affected than boys, except in the first few months of life
- Most UTIs are caused by *Escherichia coli*; a culture of unusual organisms can indicate underlying pathology
- A UTI may indicate underlying renal or urological structural abnormalities, but very rarely causes those abnormalities
- Asymptomatic bacteriuria (bacteriuria without pyuria) in children with normal urinary tracts does not require treatment

**About bag urine samples:** Bag urine samples have a high false positive rate (around 50%) and should be used only to exclude a UTI via dipstick. They should not be sent for culture. If the dipstick is positive, a more definitive sample (clean catch or catheter) is required.

---

## Assessment

Consider UTI in any child aged under 2 years with unexplained fever ≥ 38°C for over 24 hours, or any child with risk factors.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- **Older children:** fever, dysuria, urinary frequency, urinary incontinence, lower abdominal or loin pain
- **Infants and young children:** vomiting, lethargy, irritability, poor feeding, failure to thrive, jaundice, haematuria, or offensive urine

### Risk Factors

Factors that increase the likelihood of UTI:

- Aged under 1 year
- Female anatomy
- History of recurrent fever without focus
- Constipation
- Congenital abnormality of the renal tract, or family history of renal tract abnormality or renal disease
- Previous history of UTI
- Bladder dysfunction (e.g., daytime wetting)

### Examination

Your doctor will typically assess:

- Vital signs: weight, temperature, heart rate, respiratory rate, and hydration
- Signs of sepsis (see table below)
- Blood pressure and growth
- Abdominal palpation for tenderness, enlarged bladder, or loin tenderness
- Genitalia if indicated (e.g., balanitis, tight phimosis, vulvovaginitis)
- Other foci of infection (ears, nose, throat, chest)

**Normal Vital Signs in Children**

| Age (years) | Heart rate (BPM) | Respiratory rate (per min) | Systolic BP (mmHg) |
|---|---|---|---|
| < 1 | 110–160 | 30–40 | 70–90 |
| 1–2 | 100–150 | 25–35 | 80–95 |
| 2–5 | 95–140 | 25–30 | 80–100 |
| 5–12 | 80–120 | 20–25 | 90–110 |
| > 12 | 60–100 | 15–20 | 100–120 |

*Adapted from the Advanced Paediatric Life Support guidelines.*

Signs suggestive of sepsis include: fever or hypothermia, tachycardia, hypotension, warm or cold shock, tachypnoea, confusion, or altered conscious state. Persistent tachycardia in an afebrile and non-distressed child can point towards sepsis.

### Urine Collection and Dipstick

#### Aged under 3 months
Do not attempt urine collection. Urgent hospital paediatric assessment is required regardless of severity.

#### Aged 3 months to 3 years
Collect urine using:
- **Clean catch urine** (preferred): Clean the urogenital orifice with sterile water. Catch urine in a sterile container. Urination can be stimulated using the Quick-Wee method — soak gauze in fridge-cold water and apply to the suprapubic skin in a circular motion.
- Midstream urine (MSU)
- Bag urine (dipstick only — do not send for culture; use clean catch if dipstick is positive)
- Catheter specimen if experienced in obtaining in younger children

#### Aged over 3 years
Collect using clean catch urine or midstream urine (MSU).

#### Interpreting Dipstick Results

Urine dipstick is a useful screening test. The presence of both leucocytes and nitrites suggests UTI.

- Nitrites are not produced by all organisms — a negative nitrite does not exclude UTI
- Pyuria (leucocytes) can occur with other febrile illnesses — pyuria alone does not confirm UTI
- Pyuria may be absent in early infection or in very young children
- Dipstick results are less reliable in neonates and young infants

If both leucocyte esterase and nitrite are negative, do not send for microscopy and culture unless there are other indications (see below).

**Other indications to send urine for culture:**
- Diagnosis of acute pyelonephritis or upper UTI
- High to intermediate risk of serious illness
- A single positive result for leucocyte esterase or nitrite
- Recurrent UTI
- Infection not responding to treatment within 24–48 hours
- Immunosuppressed child or on biologics
- Clinical symptoms and dipstick tests do not correlate

#### Alternative Diagnoses to Consider

If dipstick is negative and UTI is unlikely, consider:
- Diabetes (check blood glucose if frequency or dysuria suggests this)
- Balanitis (redness of meatus)
- Vulvovaginitis (common in young girls after soapy baths)
- Chlamydia (urinary PCR in sexually active teens)
- Glomerulonephritis
- Sexual abuse (consider if recurrent dysuria without UTI)

## Management

### If Sepsis is Suspected

If the child appears septic or seriously unwell, urgent hospital assessment is needed. Do not delay seeking emergency care.

### Atypical Infection — Hospital Assessment Required

Your healthcare provider will arrange urgent paediatric assessment if any of the following are present:
- Seriously ill child
- Poor urine stream
- Abdominal or bladder mass
- Septicaemia
- Hypertension
- Poor response to treatment at 48 hours
- Immunosuppressed child

*Note: infection with non-E. coli organisms is mildly atypical but does not alone justify increased imaging.*

### Antibiotic Treatment

Your healthcare provider will select antibiotics based on your child's age, clinical picture, and dipstick/culture results.

**Oral antibiotic options:**

- **First-line:** Cefalexin 25 mg/kg/dose (maximum 500 mg) three times daily
- **Alternatives:**
  - Nitrofurantoin 1.25–1.75 mg/kg/dose four times daily (maximum 50 mg/dose). Do not use if pyelonephritis is suspected or if there is renal impairment.
  - Amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 30 mg/kg/dose three times daily (maximum 625 mg/dose)
  - Co-trimoxazole (sulfamethoxazole 200 mg + trimethoprim 40 mg per 5 mL) — 24 mg/kg/dose (0.5 mL/kg) twice daily (maximum 960 mg/dose)

**Duration:**
- Simple UTI in children aged ≥ 3 months: 5 days (or longer if slow to resolve)
- Suspected pyelonephritis: 7–10 days

Your healthcare provider will check culture results when available and adjust antibiotics to narrow-spectrum agents where appropriate.

#### Age-Based Approach

**Aged 3 months to 3 years:**
- If both leucocyte esterase and nitrite are negative: do not start antibiotics
- If either leucocyte esterase or nitrite is positive: start oral antibiotics and send urine for microscopy and culture
- If microscopy shows pyuria with or without bacteriuria: start or continue antibiotics; if culture is negative, stop antibiotics and consider other causes

*Children aged 3–6 months require careful clinical assessment. If there are any concerns about suitability for outpatient management, hospital assessment is needed.*

**Aged over 3 years:**
- If both positive: start oral antibiotics
- If nitrite positive, leucocyte esterase negative:
  - High clinical suspicion: start empiric antibiotics and send urine for culture
  - Low clinical suspicion: send urine for culture and await results before deciding on antibiotics
- If leucocyte esterase positive, nitrite negative, and symptomatic: start oral antibiotics without waiting for culture

### Recurrent UTI

**After a second UTI**, your healthcare provider will:
- Advise on preventing recurrence
- Arrange a renal ultrasound 6 weeks after the most recent episode

**After 3 or more UTIs**, a non-urgent paediatric review is recommended, and a renal ultrasound will be arranged if not already performed.

---

## Preventing Recurrence

Your healthcare provider will discuss the following at every visit:

- Ensure the child is drinking enough fluids
- Review toileting and wiping technique (front to back)
- Assess for and manage vulvovaginitis in girls and pinworms in all children
- Identify and treat constipation — this is present in almost all children with recurrent UTI and is the most important modifiable risk factor

**Constipation treatment with laxatives:** Laxatives may need to be given daily for at least 3 months (often 6 months or longer). The goal is one soft, formed stool per day. Your healthcare provider will titrate the dose and advise on when and how to wean. Prolonged use of softening laxatives is safe in children and does not cause dependence.

- **First-line:** Lactulose (osmotic laxative) — start at age-appropriate doses, can take 48 hours to work. Not for children with lactose intolerance or galactosaemia.
- **Alternatives:** Macrogol, poloxamer (aged ≤ 3 years), docusate (aged > 2 years), picosulfate sodium elixir, magnesium hydroxide. Your healthcare provider will select and titrate based on the child's age and response.

- Encourage regular voiding every 2–3 hours
- Encourage good fluid intake
- Discuss whether treatment for pinworms may be required

**Prophylactic antibiotics** are rarely used. They reduce recurrence risk by only a modest amount and are generally reserved for children at high risk (e.g., infants with serious initial illness or known structural abnormalities). Your healthcare provider will seek specialist advice before considering this.

---

## Renal Ultrasound

A renal ultrasound is recommended (performed at least 6 weeks after the episode) if:

- First UTI and the child is aged under 6 months
- Atypical UTI at any age
- Recurrent UTI (two or more culture-positive episodes, or high-suspicion missed episodes)

If the ultrasound shows abnormalities, your healthcare provider will arrange the appropriate specialist referral:
- Significant renal pelvic dilatation (> 15 mm, or > 10 mm bilateral) or possible obstruction: urgent surgical assessment
- Renal impairment, dysplasia, parenchymal defect, or other persisting abnormality: non-urgent paediatric medicine review

---

## When to Seek Hospital Assessment

Discuss with your healthcare provider or seek hospital assessment if:

- Child is aged under 3 months
- Child aged 3–6 months with any concern about managing at home
- Atypical infection features (seriously ill, poor urine stream, bladder/abdominal mass, septicaemia, hypertension, poor response at 48 hours, immunosuppressed)
- Known significant renal disease — specialist advice on management is required before starting treatment
- Renal ultrasound shows significant abnormalities (as above)
- Three or more UTIs — non-urgent specialist review warranted
