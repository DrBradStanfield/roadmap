# ADHD in Children and Youth

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: January 2026.*

This pathway covers the assessment of possible attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD) in children and young people aged 18 years or younger. Care after a diagnosis, including medication side-effects and dose changes, is covered in a separate pathway: ADHD in Children and Youth, Ongoing Care.

> **Always discuss your child's symptoms and any treatment decisions with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Help

- If a child or anyone else is in immediate danger, call 111. Concerns about a child's safety can also be raised with child protection services (Oranga Tamariki).
- If ADHD-like symptoms are happening in the context of emotional, sexual, or physical abuse or neglect, your doctor will follow child abuse and neglect guidance.

---

## Background

- ADHD is a complex neurodevelopmental disorder. It is strongly influenced by genetic factors and associated with psychosocial risk factors. It can have a significant impact on social, academic, and self-care functioning.
- ADHD occurs in an estimated 7% of children. It is more commonly diagnosed in boys, runs in families, and can persist into adulthood.
- Māori children are more likely to meet the criteria for ADHD than non-Māori children, but are less likely to be dispensed medication.
- The main characteristics are inattention, hyperactivity, and/or impulsivity that are excessive, long-term, and occur in more than one setting, such as home and school.
- In girls, inattention can be the more common presentation, often with fewer obvious or disruptive behaviours. Because of this, girls may be diagnosed later than boys, or not at all.
- Up to 70% of children with ADHD have sleep problems, almost twice the rate in children without ADHD. Poor sleep can also affect behaviour and mimic ADHD symptoms.
- ADHD frequently coexists with other conditions, such as anxiety, autism spectrum disorder, oppositional behaviours, learning difficulties, or intellectual disability.
- Treatment focuses on behavioural and educational strategies. Medication is generally recommended for moderate to severe ADHD, or when non-drug strategies alone do not manage symptoms and impairment well enough.

## Assessment

Assessing possible ADHD needs a comprehensive assessment, including information from home and school. It is likely to take more than one appointment.

If the child is aged 5 years or younger, your doctor will assess them for developmental concerns instead. ADHD is rarely diagnosed in preschool children.

### History

Your doctor may ask about:
- **The problem:** details of the concerning behaviour and when it began; its impact on school and home life; any other mental health concerns; any harm to self, others, or animals.
- **Development and medical history:** pregnancy or birth complications, prematurity, exposure to drugs or alcohol in pregnancy; developmental concerns, including language and motor skills; learning difficulties; vision or hearing problems; head injury; other physical conditions, such as absence seizures.
- **Sleep:** behaviour at bedtime, family dynamics, sleeping arrangements, other medical or psychiatric conditions, and medication side-effects can all affect sleep. Children with neurodevelopmental issues, including ADHD, often take a long time to fall asleep. Your doctor may use the BEARS questions:
  - **B**edtime issues: trouble going to bed or falling asleep.
  - **E**xcessive daytime sleepiness, or being overactive, inattentive, or easily frustrated.
  - **A**wakenings at night.
  - **R**egularity and duration of sleep: bedtimes and wake times on school days and weekends.
  - **S**noring or noisy breathing on most nights.
  - Your doctor may also ask about bedtime routines, signs of obstructive sleep apnoea, and any behavioural measures or over-the-counter remedies you have tried.
- **Home situation:** parental separation and who the child lives with; siblings and other important relationships; any involvement with social services.
- **Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs):** highly stressful or traumatic events, such as abuse, neglect, exposure to domestic violence, loss of a parent, or living with someone who abuses drugs or alcohol, has a serious mental illness, or has been in prison. ACEs may produce symptoms that look like ADHD. For this reason, assessment is best avoided during periods of significant stress or change, such as current trauma or active changes in the family.

For older children, your doctor may use the HEEADSSS framework. It covers home, education and employment, eating and exercise, activities and friends, drugs, cigarettes, and alcohol, sexuality and gender identity, suicide, self-harm, depression, and mood, and safety.

### Forms and questionnaires

- Parents or caregivers are usually given forms to complete and bring to a second appointment.
- A school report or questionnaire is usually requested from the child's school.
- Screening forms may include the Vanderbilt Assessment Scale (a parent version for each household and a teacher version) or the SNAP-IV assessment.
- Private ADHD providers may have their own preferred forms.

### Diagnosis

Your doctor may consider a diagnosis of ADHD if, for 6 months or longer, the child has at least 6 inattention symptoms and/or at least 6 hyperactive or impulsive symptoms, and the symptoms:
- were present before the age of 12 years, and are excessive for the child's developmental level
- are present in more than one setting, such as school and home
- impair social, academic, or self-care activities
- are not better explained by another condition.

**Inattention symptoms include:** often having a hard time paying attention or daydreaming; often not seeming to listen; being easily distracted; careless mistakes; not following through on instructions or finishing tasks; being disorganised; often losing or forgetting things; avoiding tasks that need ongoing mental effort.

**Hyperactive symptoms include:** constant motion, as if "driven by a motor"; difficulty staying seated; squirming and fidgeting; talking excessively; running, jumping, and climbing when not allowed; not being able to play quietly.

**Impulsive symptoms include:** trouble taking turns; calling out answers before the question is finished; interrupting others.

### Conditions that can look like ADHD or occur with it

- **Normal childhood behaviour:** at different ages, children can show symptoms similar to ADHD without meeting the criteria.
- **Psychological trauma:** inattention, poor concentration, and hyperactivity can reflect adverse childhood experiences.
- **Physical conditions:** hearing loss, visual impairment, thyroid disorders, or absence seizures.
- **Sleep difficulties:** short or poor-quality sleep can affect attention and behaviour. Obstructive sleep apnoea and restless leg syndrome are more common in ADHD.
- **Autism spectrum disorder (ASD):** having both is common. About half of those with ASD have ADHD-type symptoms. Signs of ASD include little interest in others or joint play, loss of language skills, unusual use of language, restricted interests, repetitive play, difficulty coping with change, and over- or under-sensitivity to sounds, movement, touch, or sights.
- **Learning disorders:** specific learning disorders such as dyslexia and dyspraxia are often present alongside ADHD. With intellectual disability, a child may be several years behind in their behaviour.
- **Mental health conditions:** mood or anxiety disorders and eating disorders can share symptoms with ADHD, such as poor concentration. The key difference is that ADHD symptoms date back to childhood.
- **Oppositional defiant disorder (ODD) and conduct disorder (CD):** a child with ADHD can also have ODD or CD, and has a higher risk of developing both. ODD is a pattern of negative, hostile, and defiant behaviour lasting 6 months. CD is a persistent pattern of aggression, deceitfulness, destruction of property, or breaking rules.

## Management

### Information about ADHD

Your doctor may explain that:
- Children with ADHD have brain-based challenges in regulating their emotions, behaviour, and attention, at home, at school, and in the community.
- ADHD is a well-researched and common condition which often has a genetic basis.
- Treatment focuses on behavioural and educational strategies, and medication for moderate to severe ADHD.

### Support at school

- Families are encouraged to discuss any concerns with the school.
- For older children, the school may apply for special exam conditions, such as a quieter room or allowed breaks. Your doctor may be asked to provide information to support the application.

### Parenting support

- Your doctor may suggest a parenting programme or counselling, such as Incredible Years or Triple P. These give caregivers strategies to manage and prevent problem behaviours, with a focus on a calm, structured environment with fewer distractions.
- Being offered a parenting programme does not reflect negatively on your parenting. It acknowledges that children with ADHD may benefit from specialised approaches.
- Look after your own well-being, and seek support for yourself if needed.
- If more support is needed, your doctor may seek child and youth mental health advice.

### Behavioural strategies

People with ADHD may have difficulties with executive functioning, working memory, and processing speed. Strategies can be tailored to these, such as:
- keeping consistent routines
- scheduling regular mealtimes
- agreeing contracts for challenging situations, such as device use
- giving instructions one at a time
- giving positive reinforcement for desired behaviour
- practising active listening and paraphrasing.

A neuro-affirming approach encourages parents to:
- view ADHD in a positive light, such as a unique way of looking at the world
- choose activities that match the child's interests
- support the development of emotional regulation and self-management skills
- take part in enjoyable activities together.

Screen time is worth managing, balancing its benefits and potential harms.

### Sleep, exercise, and diet

- Regular physical activity and good sleep habits are recommended: limit night-time screen time, keep a short but consistent bedtime routine, and keep consistent sleep and wake times.
- Your doctor may consider melatonin if the child takes a long time to fall asleep despite good sleep habits.
- A healthy diet is important. There is no conclusive evidence for dietary supplements or elimination diets.

### Management by age

- **Aged 5 years or younger:** diagnosing ADHD and treating it with medication is uncommon at this age. If ADHD is suspected, your doctor may encourage parenting programmes and non-drug approaches, and watch and wait until the early school years.
- **Aged 6 to 18 years:** your doctor may refer for specialist assessment, or to a private ADHD provider (see below).

### Family support

Families managing ADHD may be eligible for government support, depending on their situation. This may include a child disability allowance, childcare assistance, carer support, and help at school.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer:
- **Aged 5 years or younger:** for a non-urgent paediatric assessment if development is delayed or disordered, including significant learning problems or suspected autism spectrum disorder.
- **Aged 6 to 18 years with suspected ADHD:** for specialist assessment. Both the paediatric service and the child and youth mental health team manage ADHD. Which team sees the child depends on age, and on whether there are mental health concerns, significant trauma, or developmental concerns. Your doctor will make sure you know that both teams manage ADHD, as this can be confronting for some people.
- **To a private ADHD provider:** for children who do not meet the criteria for public specialist services, but where diagnosis and treatment may be needed. The completed caregiver forms, screening forms, and school questionnaires go with the referral.

---

## Key Concepts for Patients

- ADHD is common, often runs in families, and can continue into adulthood.
- Assessment usually takes more than one appointment and needs information from both home and school.
- Poor sleep, stress, trauma, hearing or vision problems, and other conditions can look like ADHD, so your doctor will check for these.
- Behavioural and educational strategies are the foundation of treatment. Medication is generally for moderate to severe ADHD, or when other strategies are not enough.
