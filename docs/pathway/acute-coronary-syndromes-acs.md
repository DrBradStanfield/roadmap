# Acute Coronary Syndromes (ACS)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers suspected acute coronary syndrome — including heart attack and unstable angina — occurring within the last 72 hours, and follow-up care after a confirmed ACS diagnosis.

---

## Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Call an ambulance (111) or go to the emergency department immediately if you have:

- Ongoing chest pain or signs of cardiovascular collapse (haemodynamic instability)
- Crescendo angina (rapidly worsening angina episodes)
- New changes on an ECG suggesting ischaemia
- An elevated troponin blood test result

---

## About Acute Coronary Syndromes

Acute coronary syndromes (ACS) is an umbrella term that includes:

- **ST elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI)** — a major heart attack with a specific pattern on ECG
- **Non-ST elevation ACS (NSTEACS)** — which includes:
  - Non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI)
  - Unstable angina, such as:
    - Angina that has appeared recently (within the past month), is severe or frequent
    - More intense or frequent angina on top of previously stable angina
    - Angina occurring at rest or with minimal effort

Unstable angina typically causes a heavy, tight, or uncomfortable feeling rather than classic "pain." Symptoms are often more intense and last longer than stable angina, and may be accompanied by sweating or nausea. It can be difficult to distinguish from NSTEMI on initial assessment.

---

## Assessment

### ECG

Your doctor will perform an ECG promptly. An important point: **a normal ECG does not rule out ACS.** Your doctor may repeat the ECG after a few minutes.

Your doctor will look for:

- ST elevation in multiple leads (suggesting STEMI)
- New left bundle branch block (LBBB)
- Widespread ST depression
- T-wave inversion, especially in leads V2, V3, and V4

### Symptoms Your Doctor Will Ask About

Your doctor will ask in detail about your pain or discomfort. Some people describe it as heaviness, tightness, or pressure rather than pain. Atypical presentations are more common in women and people with diabetes.

ACS is more likely if:

- Discomfort is predominantly in the chest
- Pain radiates to the arm, shoulder, or jaw
- Discomfort has lasted more than 15 minutes
- Associated sweating
- Symptoms worsen with exertion
- This feels similar to a previous angina or heart attack
- There has been a sudden worsening of previously stable angina

ACS is less likely if:

- Pain occurs or worsens when breathing in (pleuritic pain)
- Pain can be reproduced by pressing on the chest

Your doctor will also consider your cardiovascular risk factors. If you are of Maori, Pacific, or South Asian ethnicity, cardiovascular disease tends to be more common and can present at a younger age.

### Vital Signs and Monitoring

Your doctor will monitor your blood pressure, heart rate, and oxygen saturation.

### EDACS Score (for lower-risk presentations)

If your doctor has a lower suspicion of ACS and you have no red flags, they may calculate your Emergency Department Assessment of Chest Pain Score (EDACS). This tool helps determine whether it is safe to continue monitoring in the community or whether hospital assessment is needed. The EDACS score is only used when:

- Risk of ACS appears low
- No significant ECG findings
- You are 18 years or older
- Symptoms are consistent with ACS
- Vital signs are normal
- Pain is not ongoing and there is no crescendo pattern

## Management

### If Red Flags Are Present or ACS Is Strongly Suspected

An ambulance will be called immediately. While waiting for the ambulance, your doctor may:

- **Give aspirin 300 mg** to chew and swallow — even if you already take low-dose aspirin regularly
- **Give glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) spray** for pain relief, repeated every 5 minutes up to 3 doses (provided blood pressure stays above 90 mmHg). Your doctor will ask whether you have taken any erectile dysfunction medications (such as sildenafil) in the past 48 hours, as combining these with GTN can cause a severe drop in blood pressure
- **Consider intravenous morphine** in small increments (typically 2–3 mg every 5 minutes) for pain not relieved by GTN
- **Give supplemental oxygen** only if you are in shock or your oxygen saturation falls below 93% (target 94–98%). Routine oxygen is not given, as it has not been shown to be beneficial and may be harmful in some cases
- Monitor you closely and document all medications given

All documentation (medications, doses, times, and ECG copy) will be sent with you to the hospital.

### EDACS Score 16 or More (or Ongoing Clinical Concern)

Your doctor will treat this as high risk and arrange immediate hospital assessment with ambulance transport, giving all appropriate medications before transfer.

### EDACS Score 15 or Less

Your doctor will consider one of these approaches depending on your situation:

- **If there is still clinical concern:** Hospital assessment will be arranged
- **If there is some suspicion of ACS and you are within 72 hours of the episode with a normal ECG:** Your doctor may arrange a troponin blood test in the community

### Troponin Testing

Troponin is a protein released by heart muscle when it is damaged. Laboratories now use highly sensitive cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) assays.

Key points:

- A diagnosis of heart attack requires a change in troponin level, with at least one elevated value in the context of suspected cardiac ischaemia
- A normal troponin rules out an acute heart attack, but does not fully rule out ACS — follow-up testing (such as a treadmill stress test) may still be arranged to exclude unstable angina
- Elevated troponin can also result from many non-cardiac causes, including arrhythmias, pulmonary embolism, heart failure, severe illness, renal failure, myocarditis, and strenuous exercise

Depending on timing:

- If more than 6 hours have passed since symptom onset, one troponin test may be sufficient
- If 6 hours or less since onset, your doctor will test troponin at the time and again at least 3 hours later

**Troponin result interpretation:**

- **Elevated result** (Troponin T 15 nanogram/L or above, or Troponin I 40 nanogram/L or above): hospital assessment will be arranged
- **Normal result with ongoing concern:** your doctor may seek specialist cardiology advice

### While Waiting for Troponin Results

Your doctor may advise that you can wait away from the clinic under certain conditions (going to a known address, not driving, having a responsible adult with you, and being reachable by phone). You should:

- Avoid all physical activity (no gardening, exercise, or housework)
- Not drive
- Have someone with you
- Stay by a phone
- Call 111 immediately if you develop any chest pain, tightness, shortness of breath, or palpitations

Your doctor will explain that while the risk of a heart attack is considered low, there remains a small possibility, and a cardiac arrest can occur in people who have had a heart attack. This information will be documented in your notes.

## Long-Term Management After ACS

After an ACS, you will need lifelong management of your cardiovascular risk — approximately half of all ACS events occur in people who have already had one.

Your doctor will:

1. **Review your discharge summary and medications** — including intended duration of any new medicines (for example, ticagrelor is commonly prescribed for 12 months)

2. **At 3 months after your ACS, your doctor will:**
   - Check whether you have completed cardiac rehabilitation
   - Ask about your return to activities (work, driving, sexual activity) and offer support
   - Screen for depression and anxiety
   - Check your medication compliance and understanding
   - Check that blood pressure and lipid levels are within target ranges
   - Review lifestyle risk factors (smoking, exercise, diet) and offer support
   - Provide an angina action plan

3. **Annually, your doctor will:**
   - Review lifestyle risks and offer support
   - Check medication compliance and understanding
   - Check blood pressure and lipid levels
   - Update your angina action plan
