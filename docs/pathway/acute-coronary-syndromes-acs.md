# Acute Coronary Syndromes (ACS)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: July 2026.*

This pathway covers suspected acute coronary syndrome (ACS), including heart attack and unstable angina, and follow-up care after a confirmed ACS diagnosis.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Call 111 or go to the emergency department immediately if you have:

- Ongoing chest pain or signs of cardiovascular collapse (haemodynamic instability)
- A history that suggests unstable angina: chest pain (angina) that is new and severe or frequent, more severe, prolonged or frequent than your usual angina, or that comes on at rest or with minimal exertion
- New changes on an ECG suggesting ischaemia
- An elevated troponin blood test result

---

## About Acute Coronary Syndromes

ACS is a sudden drop in blood flow to the heart muscle, most often from a ruptured fatty plaque and a blood clot in a coronary artery.

Acute coronary syndromes (ACS) is an umbrella term that includes:

- **ST elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI)**, a heart attack with ST elevation on the ECG
- **Non-ST elevation ACS (NSTEACS)**, which is either:
  - Non-ST elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI), or
  - Unstable angina, including:
    - Angina that started recently (less than 1 month ago) and is severe or frequent
    - Severe, prolonged, or more frequent angina on top of previously stable angina
    - Angina occurring at rest or with minimal effort

The pain of unstable angina is similar to stable angina, though often more intense and longer lasting. It may also come with other signs, such as sweating and nausea. It can be difficult to tell apart from NSTEMI on initial assessment.

## Assessment

### ECG

If ACS is suspected, your doctor will perform an ECG immediately. **A normal ECG does not rule out ACS.**

Your doctor will look for:

- ST elevation of 1 mm or more in 2 or more limb leads (suggesting STEMI)
- ST elevation of 2 mm or more in 2 or more chest leads (suggesting STEMI)
- New left bundle branch block (LBBB), although it can be hard to tell whether LBBB is new
- ST depression
- T-wave inversion or biphasic T waves, especially in leads V2, V3, and V4. This may indicate Wellen's syndrome, which is suspicious for a critical narrowing of the left anterior descending artery

### Symptoms Your Doctor Will Ask About

Your doctor will ask in detail about your pain.

ACS is more likely if:

- Pain is mainly in the chest
- Pain spreads to the neck, arm, or shoulder (either arm or shoulder)
- Pain has lasted longer than 15 minutes
- Associated sweating
- Previously stable angina has suddenly worsened, with frequent episodes on little or no exertion. This likely represents unstable angina
- This feels similar to a previous angina or heart attack

However, ACS can be present even without these symptoms. There is no typical pattern to ACS. Older adults, women, and people with diabetes or heart failure are more likely to have vague symptoms.

ACS is less likely if:

- Pain occurs or worsens when breathing in (pleuritic pain)
- Pain can be reproduced by pressing on the chest
- Symptoms last less than 5 minutes

Angina is often (but not always) a heavy, crushing, or dull pain behind the breastbone that may spread to the neck, jaw, or arms. It follows a predictable pattern on exertion and eases with rest.

### Risk Factors Your Doctor Will Consider

- Current or past smoking
- A parent or sibling who was hospitalised with, or died from, a heart attack or stroke before the age of 50 years
- Ethnicity: in Māori, Pacific, and South Asian people, cardiovascular disease is significantly more common and presents at a younger age. Māori and Pacific people have almost double the death rate from coronary heart disease compared with New Zealand European people
- Other conditions: diabetes, high cholesterol (hyperlipidaemia), high blood pressure, chronic kidney disease, obesity or overweight, significant mental illness, and systemic inflammatory conditions, including inflammatory arthritis and cancer

### Vital Signs and Monitoring

Your doctor will examine you, including your general appearance (such as sweating or paleness), vital signs with blood pressure in both arms, pulses, heart, and lungs. They will check whether pressing on your chest, abdomen, or upper back, or moving your upper back, reproduces the pain, and look for signs of a deep vein thrombosis.

### EDACS Score (for lower-risk presentations)

If none of the emergency features above are present, your doctor may calculate your Emergency Department Assessment of Chest Pain Score (EDACS). It is only used when:

- There are no significant acute ischaemic ECG findings
- You are 18 years or older
- Your chest pain or symptoms mean ACS cannot be excluded without further testing
- Vital signs are normal
- There is no ongoing pain or unstable presentation

It predicts heart attack and other adverse heart events within 30 days. It does not predict whether you have cardiac ischaemia or underlying coronary disease.

## Management

### If Red Flags Are Present or ACS Is Strongly Suspected

Your doctor will call an ambulance straight away if STEMI is found, if you have any of the emergency features above, or if there is a high suspicion of ACS.

While waiting for the ambulance, your doctor may:

- **Give aspirin 300 mg** to chew and swallow, even if you already take low-dose aspirin regularly
- **Give glyceryl trinitrate (GTN) spray** for pain relief, 1 to 2 puffs under the tongue
  - This may be repeated every 5 minutes, up to a maximum of 3 doses, as long as blood pressure stays above 90 mmHg
  - Your doctor will ask whether you have taken an erectile dysfunction medicine (such as sildenafil) in the past 24 to 48 hours, as using it with GTN may cause a severe drop in blood pressure
- **Consider intravenous morphine**, usually in 2 to 3 mg increments every 5 minutes
- **Give supplemental oxygen** only if you are in cardiogenic shock or your oxygen saturation is less than 93%, aiming for 94 to 98%
  - Routine oxygen has not been shown to be beneficial and may be harmful
  - With COPD and a risk of carbon dioxide build-up, the aim is 88 to 92%
- Monitor you closely

All medications given, with doses and times, and a copy of your ECG will be sent with you to the hospital.

### EDACS Score 16 or More (or Ongoing Clinical Concern)

With a score in this range, your doctor will arrange urgent ambulance transport to the emergency department. They will also do this if:

- Your EDACS score is 15 or less, but there is ongoing concern for ACS and either your doctor is not comfortable managing you in the community, or tests cannot be done in a realistic time frame (for example, the clinic closes at the end of the day, or you may be hard to reach with results)
- After discussion with you and your whānau, hospital admission and possible invasive treatment are felt to be appropriate

### EDACS Score 15 or Less

Community management is only considered if your EDACS is 15 or less. If there is ongoing concern about possible ACS, hospital assessment will be arranged.

Your doctor may consider managing you in the community if:

- There is low suspicion of ACS
- Your doctor is comfortable managing you in the community
- Staffing at the practice allows it
- Blood test results and follow-up can be managed safely
- After discussion with you and your whānau, hospital admission and invasive treatment are felt not to be suitable for you

If you are managed in the community, your doctor will arrange a troponin blood test and discuss what to do if you have any further episodes of chest pain.

### Troponin Testing

Troponin is the blood test used to diagnose an acute heart muscle injury. It may be done in a laboratory or as a point-of-care test.

Key points:

- Troponin takes 2 to 6 hours to rise after a heart attack, and can stay raised for up to 14 days after ACS
- A diagnosis of heart attack requires a change in troponin level, with at least one elevated value in the context of suspected cardiac ischaemia
- The first troponin can be taken at any time. It does not show when a heart event may have happened. The level, or how fast it changes, is what points to an event
- Absence of a troponin rise rules out an acute heart attack, but not other ACS, such as unstable angina, or underlying coronary artery disease
- Even "negative" results close to the laboratory's upper limit are linked with significant 1-year and 5-year heart event rates
- Raised troponin can also have other causes besides ACS, including arrhythmias, severe pulmonary embolism, heart failure, myocarditis, sepsis, renal failure, stroke, and strenuous exercise

**Troponin result interpretation:**

- Your doctor will read the result with the flow chart for the specific troponin test used, and a second test may be required
- If the flow chart recommends specialist review, hospital assessment will be arranged
- If concerns about blocked coronary arteries continue, your doctor may consider a non-urgent cardiology referral for follow-up testing, such as an exercise ECG. Follow-up testing (such as a treadmill test or CT coronary angiogram) can help rule out unstable angina

### While Waiting for Troponin Results

Staying at the practice is the recommended option if you have had pain that day. You may be able to wait elsewhere if you:

- Are going to a known address
- Are not going to drive
- Have a responsible adult with you
- Can be reliably reached by phone to receive results
- Understand you might have to go to hospital without delay, depending on the results
- Have a copy of the [information for people awaiting blood test results for acute chest pain](https://hostedfiles-newzealand.communityhealthpathways.org/CHPNZLive/./patientinfo/HealthInfo_InformationForPeopleAwaitingBloodTestResultsForAcuteChestPain.pdf)

If chest pain, tightness, breathlessness or palpitations return while you wait, call 111 straight away.

## Long-Term Management After ACS

After an ACS, you will need lifelong management of your cardiovascular risk.

Your doctor will:

1. **Review your discharge summary and medications**, including how long to take any medicines that are not long-term. Dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT) and anticoagulation are often now tailored to the individual. If prescribed, your notes and prescriptions should clearly record when each is to stop

2. **At 3 months after your ACS, your doctor will:**
   - Check whether you have completed cardiac rehabilitation. If not, they may consider a non-urgent cardiology assessment or seek cardiology advice
   - Ask about your return to activities (work, driving, sexual activity) and offer support
   - Screen for depression and anxiety
   - Check your medication compliance and understanding
   - Check that blood pressure, lipids, and diabetes (if present) are within target ranges
   - Review lifestyle risk factors (smoking, exercise, diet) and offer support
   - Provide an angina action plan

3. **Annually, your doctor will:**
   - Review lifestyle risks and offer support
   - Check medication compliance and understanding
   - Check blood pressure and lipid levels
   - Update your angina action plan
