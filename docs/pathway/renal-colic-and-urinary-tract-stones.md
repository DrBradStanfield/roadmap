# Renal Colic and Urinary Tract Stones

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of renal colic and urinary tract stones in adults, including what to expect from your doctor at each stage.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if you have kidney stone symptoms alongside any of the following:

- Fever above 38°C
- Signs of sepsis or peritonitis
- Severely reduced kidney function (creatinine > 160 µmol/L or eGFR < 45 mL/min)
- Known stone in a solitary (single) kidney
- Known bilateral ureteric stones (stones in both ureters)

---

## Background

- Renal colic is a common condition seen in general practice.
- Kidney stones may be found when investigating loin pain, or incidentally on imaging done for another reason.
- Most stones are made of calcium; less commonly uric acid, struvite, or cysteine.
- Many ureteric stones pass on their own — smaller stones are much more likely to pass spontaneously:
  - Less than 4 mm — about 90% pass without intervention
  - 4 to 6 mm — about 50% pass
  - Greater than 6 mm — about 10% pass
- Up to 50% of people will have a recurrence within 5 years.
- Blood in the urine (haematuria) is present in about 85% of people with renal colic.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history and ask about:

**Symptoms**, which may include:
- Sudden, severe pain from the loin (flank) radiating to the groin — constant, with no relieving position
- Nausea and vomiting
- Blood in the urine (visible or detected on dipstick)
- Urinary urgency, frequency, or a feeling of incomplete emptying
- Pain in the penis, testicles, or labia

**Risk factors**, including:
- Personal or family history of kidney stones
- Medical conditions such as diabetes, gout (hyperuricaemia), or obesity
- Chronic urinary tract infections
- Recent major surgery or chemotherapy
- Spinal injury or being wheelchair-bound

Your doctor will also examine you, checking:
- General appearance and whether you are in pain
- Vital signs (temperature, blood pressure, heart rate) — fever raises concern for infection; low blood pressure raises concern for sepsis or vascular causes
- Abdomen — feeling for masses, tenderness, or signs of peritoneal irritation (which may suggest a different diagnosis)
- Loin percussion — tenderness here may indicate infection

**Important:** Your doctor will always consider other diagnoses. In older patients especially, abdominal aortic aneurysm must be considered. Other possibilities include appendicitis, pyelonephritis, ovarian or testicular conditions, biliary colic, and herpes zoster.

**Investigations your doctor may arrange:**
- Urine dipstick (haematuria supports the diagnosis, but is absent in about 15% of cases)
- CT scan of the kidneys, ureters, and bladder (CT KUB) — the main diagnostic imaging for kidney stones; ultrasound is used if you are pregnant
- Pregnancy test for women of childbearing age
- Blood tests (kidney function, electrolytes, calcium, urate) once the acute episode has settled

## Management

### Acute Episode (Suspected Renal Colic)

Your doctor will assess urgency first. Emergency department referral is needed if you have any red flags (see above) or if pain cannot be controlled.

**Pain relief:** Your doctor may consider:
- Diclofenac 75 mg by injection (intramuscular) — the preferred first-line option, provided there is no dehydration, kidney impairment, or contraindications to anti-inflammatory medicines (NSAIDs)
- A repeat dose after 30 minutes if needed (maximum 150 mg total in 24 hours by any route — oral, rectal, or injection)
- If NSAIDs are contraindicated: morphine sulphate 5–10 mg combined with ondansetron 4 mg (wafer or oro-dispersible tablet) as an antiemetic
- Note: oral and rectal routes of diclofenac have less consistent absorption than injection

**Fluids:** IV fluids are only given if you are dehydrated or vomiting — there is no evidence they speed up stone passage. Adequate oral fluid intake of 3 litres per day is advised.

If pain settles, your doctor will arrange the investigations above, prescribe take-home analgesia, and advise you to return if pain recurs or new symptoms develop.

If pain does not settle within 30 minutes, your doctor will give additional analgesia and observe further. If still inadequate, emergency department referral follows.

---

### Ureteric Stone Confirmed on Imaging

Once a stone is confirmed, management depends on size and location.

Your doctor will advise you to:
- Sieve your urine (e.g. using a strainer) to catch any stone that passes — send it to the lab for analysis
- Drink 3 litres of water per day, spaced throughout the day, to keep urine clear
- Seek urgent medical help if you develop fever, rigors, or worsening flank pain (signs of infection)

**By stone size:**

**Stone less than 4 mm**
- Very likely to pass on its own — no specialist referral needed
- Managed in the community with analgesia
- Alpha blockers are not used for stones this small
- Your doctor will review you at 4 weeks and may arrange follow-up imaging to confirm the stone has passed

**Stone 4 mm to 6 mm**
- Likely to pass, but not certain — your doctor may consider a referral to urology for non-urgent assessment
- Managed in the community with analgesia
- For distal ureteric stones of 5 mm or larger, your doctor may discuss the option of an alpha blocker to help the stone pass:
  - Doxazosin (starting at a low dose, increasing to 4 mg at night if tolerated) for 4 weeks or until the stone passes — other alpha blockers such as tamsulosin may also be used
  - Alpha blockers slightly increase stone clearance and may help the stone pass around 3 days sooner, but also slightly increase the risk of dizziness, low blood pressure, headache, and (in men) erectile dysfunction
  - First-dose low blood pressure is common — taking the dose at bedtime reduces this risk
  - Alpha blockers are generally avoided in pregnancy and breastfeeding

**Stone 7 mm or larger**
- Unlikely to pass on its own — your doctor will seek urology advice, as surgery may be needed
- Surgical options your doctor may discuss include extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL), ureteroscopy and laser, or (for large kidney stones) percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL)

**Note on sepsis with a ureteric stone:** This is a medical emergency. A drainage procedure (nephrostomy or ureteric stent) may be urgently required.

### Intra-Renal Stones (Stones Within the Kidney)

**Stone smaller than 10 mm**
- If causing no symptoms, specialist referral is usually not needed
- Your doctor will arrange blood tests and advise a high fluid intake
- Follow-up imaging is arranged after 1 year if the stone has not been seen to pass
- Your doctor may refer to urology if the stone grows, new stones appear, or you develop frequent urinary tract infections (clearing kidney stones reduces recurrent UTIs in about 50% of cases)
- Certain professions (e.g. pilots, flight crew) may require urology clearance even for small asymptomatic stones

**Stone 10 mm or larger**
- Your doctor will refer to urology for assessment

---

### Patients With a Ureteric Stent

A ureteric stent is a small flexible tube placed temporarily after urological surgery to allow urine drainage. It is usually removed within 3–4 months.

**Common symptoms with a stent** (affecting 30–50% of patients):
- Flank pain, especially during urination or exertion
- Intermittent blood in the urine (without clots)
- Urinary frequency and urgency
- Red and white blood cells on urine dipstick — this is expected and does not mean infection on its own

Your doctor may consider:
- Reassurance and explanation
- Avoiding heavy exertion if it worsens symptoms
- Regular analgesia (NSAIDs preferred)
- Anticholinergic medication for urinary frequency (e.g. oxybutynin 2.5–5 mg three times a day as needed)
- Urine culture to check for superimposed infection — if confirmed, antibiotics guided by culture results:
  - Cefalexin 1 g three to four times a day for 7 days (preferred oral option)
  - Alternatives if severe penicillin or cephalosporin allergy: trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole (co-trimoxazole) 960 mg twice daily for 7 days, or ciprofloxacin 500 mg twice daily for 7 days
  - Note: nitrofurantoin is not used for kidney infections as it does not reach adequate concentrations in the kidney
- KUB X-ray to check stent position if displacement is suspected (rare)
- Urology advice if fever with confirmed UTI, or if clot retention occurs

---

### Stone Prevention

Regardless of stone type or size, your doctor will provide prevention advice:

- **Fluids:** Drink 3 litres of water per day, spread throughout the day. Water is best — avoid high-calorie or carbonated beverages.
- **Diet:** Maintain adequate (not excessive) dietary calcium, eat plenty of fruit and vegetables, and reduce sodium intake.
- **Avoid:**
  - Calcium-containing supplements
  - Vitamin C supplements (converted to oxalate in the body, which can promote stone formation)
  - Supplements with calcium fillers
  - Restrictive diets (e.g. ketogenic diet) that may increase stone risk
- Specific dietary interventions (e.g. low-oxalate diet) require specialist input — stone analysis and 24-hour urine testing are needed first.

**Recurrence:** The risk of a further stone is about 5% per year for the first 5 years, with a lifetime recurrence rate of about 50%. If you have had multiple stones, your doctor may arrange further blood tests and consider referral to nephrology for metabolic assessment and 24-hour urine testing.
