# Bariatric Surgery Complications

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the recognition and initial management of complications following bariatric (weight loss) surgery, including gastric banding, sleeve gastrectomy, and Roux-en-Y gastric bypass.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if you have had bariatric surgery and develop:

- Persistent and progressive fast heart rate (greater than 120 beats per minute lasting more than 4 hours)

---

## Background

Bariatric surgery carries the expected short-term risks of any major surgery, such as wound infection, bleeding, and blood clots (venous thromboembolism).

There are also three serious complications specific to bariatric surgery that can be easy to miss:

- **Staple line leak** — a leak at the surgical join
- **Bowel obstruction** — which can include internal hernia, intussusception, or adhesions (especially after Roux-en-Y bypass)
- **Gastric prolapse or slipped band** — the band shifts out of position

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will look for symptoms lasting longer than 4 hours, including:

- Persistent and progressive fast heart rate (>120 bpm)
- Nausea and retching
- Abdominal pain or colic
- Retrosternal (behind the breastbone) or chest pain

Because bariatric surgery rearranges the anatomy, symptoms may be atypical — for example, pain after eating in the left upper abdomen. Signs may be minimal. Septic shock can occur before obvious abdominal tenderness appears.

Your doctor will also consider:

- **Abdominal X-ray** for anyone with an adjustable gastric band, to check band position. Note that plain X-rays and CT scans can appear normal even with serious complications.
- Whether diagnostic laparoscopy (surgical camera procedure) is needed.
- **Avoid endoscopy** to investigate reflux if you have had gastric banding — this is not appropriate.

### Complications by Surgery Type

**Adjustable gastric band:**
The band can cause strangulation of part of the stomach, presenting as severe nausea with or without pain — this can occur years after surgery.

**Sleeve gastrectomy or Roux-en-Y gastric bypass:**
A staple line leak can develop, usually within 14 days but sometimes up to 60 days after surgery. It may present as upper abdominal discomfort and feeling generally unwell. A low-grade fever (38°C) and fast heart rate (>120 bpm) are often the only signs. Radiological signs can be subtle and easily missed. Symptoms may be mistaken for a chest infection (atelectasis or pneumonia).

**Roux-en-Y gastric bypass:**
Internal hernias can develop at any time — even years after surgery — with vague abdominal pain, bloating, and nausea. The degree of pain may seem out of proportion to other findings.

### Vitamin Deficiencies

Your doctor will consider vitamin deficiencies if you have neurological symptoms. **Wernicke's encephalopathy** (thiamine/vitamin B1 deficiency) can develop early after bariatric surgery — even after just 3 weeks of vomiting.

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider:

- **If you have a gastric band and develop nausea, vomiting, or upper abdominal pain:** Emergency deflation of the band by aspirating fluid from the subcutaneous port (typically 4–14 mL). If symptoms don't resolve within 30 minutes, or if deflation isn't possible, urgent surgical assessment is needed.
- **Observation** for worsening symptoms, especially a rising heart rate, with referral for acute surgical assessment.
- **Contacting the original bariatric surgeon** if complications arise in the immediate postoperative period.

If a bariatric surgery complication cannot be ruled out, urgent surgical assessment is required.
