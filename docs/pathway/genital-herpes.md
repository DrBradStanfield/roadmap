# Genital Herpes

Genital herpes is caused by herpes simplex virus 1 and 2 (HSV‑1 and HSV‑2). More than 50% of primary genital infections in young people are caused by HSV-1. Recurrences are more common in the first year with HSV-2.

Transmission occurs during skin-to-skin contact when the virus is being shed — this can happen when a person has symptoms or when they have no symptoms at all (asymptomatic shedding).

It is not possible to clinically distinguish:
- HSV-1 from HSV-2 infection
- Whether the infection is recently acquired
- Past asymptomatic infection from new clinical manifestations

Genital herpes lesions increase the risk of HIV transmission in men who have sex with men (MSM).

---

## Symptoms

**Primary episode (initial infection):**
- Multiple, widespread, painful blisters or ulcers on or near the anogenital region — usually bilateral
- Fever
- Tender groin lymph nodes
- Dysuria (pain passing urine)
- Urethral or vaginal discharge
- Flu-like symptoms: fevers, headaches, muscle aches, malaise, lower back pain
- Cervicitis, urethritis, or proctitis
- Less typical: single lesions, genital swelling, skin irritation, fissures

**Recurrent episodes (typically milder):**
- Localised, usually one-sided painful lesions
- More subtle presentations: skin splits, irritation, fissures, single lesions, redness with itching or tingling
- Prodrome: posterior leg pain, burning, tingling, or itching before lesions appear

---

## Diagnosis

Diagnosis is clinical, confirmed by laboratory testing. **Your doctor will:**
- Swab any active lesion for HSV PCR (from the base of the ulcer or de-roofed blister)
- If a painless ulcer is present, arrange a Treponema pallidum PCR swab to rule out syphilis
- Offer HIV and syphilis serology, and consider full STI screening

**Important:** HSV blood tests (serology) are **not** routinely used for diagnosis — they lack sensitivity and specificity. Your doctor will advise if serology is appropriate in your specific situation.

---

## Complications to be aware of

Most complications occur during the primary episode. Seek urgent medical attention if you experience:

- Signs of **encephalitis**: confusion, altered consciousness, personality change, seizures, fever, stiff neck
- Signs of **meningitis**: fever, severe headache, photophobia, stiff neck, nausea and vomiting
- **Urinary retention** (inability to pass urine)
- Widespread rash or blistering skin (Stevens-Johnson syndrome)

Other complications include:
- Acute nerve pain causing urinary retention, constipation, or erectile dysfunction (herpetic lumbosacral radiculoneuropathy)
- Postherpetic neuralgia
- Bell's palsy
- Erythema multiforme

> **Seek emergency care immediately** if you have signs of encephalitis, meningitis, or Stevens-Johnson syndrome.

## Treatment

> Treatment decisions — including whether antivirals are appropriate, which drug, and what dose — should be made by your doctor, who will assess your kidney function and overall health before prescribing.

### First episode

Antiviral treatment is started as soon as possible, without waiting for test results.

**Standard treatment (7 days), if kidney function is normal:**
- Valaciclovir 500 mg twice daily (preferred), or
- Aciclovir 400 mg three times daily

Treatment may be extended to 10 days if new lesions appear during treatment.

**Symptom relief:**
- Adequate pain relief (pain can be severe)
- Topical 2% lidocaine gel (brief use only — risk of irritation)
- Urinating in a warm shower or bath to ease dysuria
- Saline bathing to prevent adhesions

### Recurrent episodes

Your doctor may offer one of two approaches:

**Episodic treatment** (taken at the start of each outbreak):
- Best for people with infrequent outbreaks and a recognisable prodrome
- Should be started at the very first sign of symptoms
- Valaciclovir 500 mg twice daily for 3 days, or aciclovir 800 mg three times daily for 2 days

**Suppressive (preventive) therapy** (taken daily to reduce recurrences):
- Valaciclovir 500 mg once daily (can increase to twice daily if needed), or aciclovir 400 mg twice daily
- Usually trialled for 12 months, then reviewed
- Significantly reduces — but does not eliminate — asymptomatic viral shedding and transmission risk
- Consider for: frequent or severe outbreaks, immunosuppression, psychosexual distress, or wishing to reduce transmission risk to a partner
- Full suppressive effect takes about 5 days from starting treatment

## Pregnancy

Neonatal HSV infection is rare but serious. **If you are pregnant and have genital herpes, tell your doctor and lead maternity carer (LMC) promptly.**

Key points:
- **All pregnant people with known genital herpes** are routinely started on suppressive antiviral therapy from **36 weeks' gestation**
- The risk of transmission to the baby is highest with a primary (first-ever) infection close to delivery
- The risk is much lower with recurrent herpes or asymptomatic infection at delivery
- Antiviral medications have been widely used in pregnancy with no apparent adverse effects

**If you have a first episode during pregnancy**, your doctor will prescribe antivirals, liaise with your LMC, and arrange obstetric assessment if needed.

## Reducing transmission

- Avoid sexual contact when sores, blisters, or other symptoms are present
- Condoms reduce transmission by at least 75%
- The virus can be shed and transmitted even without visible symptoms
- Suppressive therapy can further reduce (but not eliminate) the risk of transmission to partners

---

## Psychosocial support

A diagnosis of genital herpes can be distressing, often because of misinformation. Important points:
- Genital herpes is very common — HSV-1 affects 70–80% of the population, HSV-2 affects 12–15%, and most people have no or mild symptoms
- Getting genital herpes in a long-term relationship does not necessarily mean your partner has had other sexual contacts
- You can continue intimate relationships — your doctor can help with strategies to reduce transmission risk
- Women with recurrent herpes can still safely plan a pregnancy; the risk of transmission during delivery is low
- Contact tracing and notification are not legally required for herpes

## When to see a doctor

**Urgently (same day or emergency department):**
- Confusion, seizures, severe headache, stiff neck, fever (possible encephalitis/meningitis)
- Unable to pass urine (urinary retention)
- Severe blistering or rash involving skin and mucous membranes

**Soon (within days):**
- First suspected outbreak of genital herpes — confirm diagnosis and start treatment early
- Painful outbreak preventing normal function
- Pregnancy + any genital herpes symptoms or concerns
- Recurrent outbreaks affecting quality of life

**For ongoing review:**
- Frequent or troublesome recurrences — your doctor can discuss suppressive therapy
- Questions about transmission and protecting your partner
- Immunocompromised — specialist sexual health advice is recommended
