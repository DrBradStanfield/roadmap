# Acute Paediatric ORL / Head and Neck Surgery Assessment

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway applies to children younger than 15 years. It covers acute ear, nose, throat, and head and neck conditions that may require urgent specialist assessment.

---

## Conditions Requiring Emergency or Urgent ORL Specialist Assessment

The following conditions generally require urgent assessment by an ear, nose, throat, and head and neck (ORL HNS) specialist. Your doctor will arrange this.

- **Upper airway obstruction** — any sign that the airway is compromised
- **Head, neck, or upper aerodigestive tract bleeding** — e.g., bleeding after tonsillectomy (post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage), bleeding from oropharyngeal conditions
- **Upper aerodigestive tract foreign body** — e.g., bone or coin stuck in the throat or oesophagus
- **Severe head and neck infection** with pain, fever, or swelling — e.g., peritonsillar abscess (quinsy), parapharyngeal abscess
- **Complications of sinus or ear disease**
- **Otitis externa (outer ear infection) with signs of complicated infection** — see signs listed below
- **Nasal fractures** — assessment ideally within 1 week of injury to avoid displaced fixation
- **Epistaxis (nosebleed) not responding to standard treatment**, or posterior epistaxis
- **Complications of recent ORL or head and neck surgery** — including haematoma, seroma, bleeding, or epistaxis

## Signs of Complicated Ear Infection

These are warning signs that an ear infection has spread or become severe. Your doctor will arrange urgent assessment if any of these are present.

### Ramsay Hunt Syndrome

- Herpes zoster (shingles) infection affecting the facial nerve (VII cranial nerve)
- Blisters and scabs may appear on the face, outer ear, ear canal, throat, or upper neck
- Facial palsy (weakness or paralysis of one side of the face) is common
  - Without treatment and with complete palsy: approximately 10% chance of full recovery
  - Without treatment and with partial palsy: approximately 68% chance of full recovery
- Facial weakness typically peaks within 1 week, but nerve damage can continue up to 14 days after onset
- Other cranial nerves may also be affected (e.g., VIII, IX, X), causing hearing loss or swallowing difficulty
- Complications can include permanent facial paralysis, post-herpetic neuralgia, and hearing loss

### Lateral Skull Base Osteomyelitis (Malignant Otitis Externa)

- Ear pain out of proportion to what is seen on examination
- Fever, feeling systemically unwell, headache, swollen lymph nodes, or jaw stiffness may be present or absent
- Discharge from the ear may be present
- May show severe swelling of the ear canal, or more subtle signs such as a small granulation on the canal floor
- Can involve multiple cranial nerves (VII to XII)
- More common in people with diabetes or who are immunocompromised
- Raised CRP (C-reactive protein) on a blood test can help distinguish invasive from non-invasive otitis externa

### Mastoiditis

- Fever, tenderness or swelling over the mastoid region (behind the ear), and feeling unwell

### Complicated Cholesteatoma

- Signs suggesting intracranial spread — e.g., facial palsy, meningitis, or abscess

### Intracranial Infection (from ear or sinus source)

- Fever combined with headache or neurological symptoms

### Cellulitis Extending Beyond the Ear Canal

- Spreading skin infection beyond the ear canal with the child feeling systemically unwell

## Conditions Managed by Other Specialists

Some conditions that may seem like ORL problems are best assessed by a different specialist. Your doctor will direct the referral appropriately:

- **Non-bony food bolus stuck in the oesophagus** — assessed by gastroenterology, not ORL
- **Bell's palsy (idiopathic facial palsy)** — managed differently from Ramsay Hunt syndrome; see the facial palsy pathway
- **Suspected meningitis** — even if it appears to be a complication of a sinus or ear problem, this requires acute general medicine assessment
- **Traumatic ear amputation (pinna)** — requires plastic surgery assessment
- **Dental/tooth infections (odontogenic infections)** — managed through the toothache/dental pathway
- **Facial trauma other than an isolated nasal fracture** — requires oral and maxillofacial surgery (OMFS) assessment
- **Mandibular (jaw) fractures** — requires OMFS assessment
- **Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) disorders** — managed through the TMD pathway

---

## What to Expect

If your doctor determines that urgent ORL specialist assessment is needed, they will contact the on-call specialist and arrange hospital assessment or admission. This is a referral-based pathway — your doctor manages the process of getting specialist review.

For airway compromise or severe bleeding, this is a medical emergency and 111 should be called immediately.

---

*Always discuss your child's symptoms with your doctor. This information is a guide to how doctors approach these conditions — it is not a substitute for clinical assessment.*
