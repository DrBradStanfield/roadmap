# Shoulder Pain

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of shoulder pain in adults, helping identify the cause and the appropriate treatment approach.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

The following require urgent medical assessment:

- Suspected **septic arthritis** (red, hot, swollen joint with fever or systemic illness)
- **Dislocation** — especially after trauma, epileptic fit, or electric shock
- Suspected **tumour** — severe and persistent pain without clear cause

---

## Background

Shoulder pain is common. In patients presenting with shoulder pain:

- **65%** have subacromial pain (e.g. impingement, rotator cuff problems, or calcific tendinitis)
- **20%** have glenohumeral or capsular pain (e.g. osteoarthritis or frozen shoulder)
- **10%** have acromioclavicular (AC) joint pain
- **5%** have referred pain from the cervical spine

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Whether there was any trauma (including epileptic fit or electric shock), which may suggest dislocation
- Whether the pain is in one or both shoulders — bilateral pain raises the possibility of polymyalgia rheumatica (PMR)
- Pain onset, site, type, radiation, severity, and what makes it better or worse
- Whether pain may be referred from the heart, lungs, diaphragm, or neck
- Occupational factors (e.g. overhead or repetitive work)

Physical examination typically includes:

- Looking for wasting, nerve damage, asymmetry, or signs of septic arthritis (red skin, fever, systemic illness)
- A 4-step shoulder examination:
  1. Check neck movement for stiffness or pain radiating down the arm
  2. Assess the acromioclavicular (AC) joint for tenderness
  3. Check glenohumeral joint external rotation — asymmetry suggests a glenohumeral problem
  4. Assess the subacromial space — pain on elevation or internal rotation at 90°, or a painful arc, suggests a subacromial problem

### Causes by Location

#### Neck (Cervical Spine)
- Normal neck range of movement without pain makes a cervical cause unlikely
- Reduced neck extension, rotation, or lateral flexion to the same side as the painful shoulder may reproduce symptoms

#### Acromioclavicular (AC) Joint
- **Acute:** usually from trauma
- **Chronic:** usually osteoarthritis — pain localised to the AC joint or upper trapezius, aggravated by overhead activities, may be worse at night

#### Glenohumeral Joint
- **Frozen shoulder (adhesive capsulitis):** typically affects people aged 40–60, associated with diabetes. Insidious onset, global restriction of active and passive movement, pain often worse at night and can radiate down the arm. Progresses through three phases:
  - *Painful/freezing phase* (2–3 months): severe pain, increasing stiffness; steroid injection often helpful
  - *Stiff/frozen phase* (3–12 months): less pain but significant loss of movement; physiotherapy beneficial
  - *Recovery/thawing phase* (6–12 months): gradual return of function; may be mild residual restriction
- **Glenohumeral osteoarthritis:** typically older age, gradual onset, global reduction in range especially external rotation, may have crepitus

#### Subacromial Space
- **Rotator cuff tears:** limited active movement, variable weakness; may follow trauma (even minor in older adults). Full thickness or subscapularis tears require prompt specialist assessment
- **Impingement and calcific tendinitis:** limited active movement, moderate to severe pain, history of repetitive overhead activity; painful arc from 70–120° is the classical sign of impingement

#### Sternoclavicular (SC) Joint
- **Traumatic:** usually high-impact mechanism; posterior dislocations are less common but may injure vital structures in the neck and require immediate assessment
- **Non-traumatic:** causes include osteoarthritis, rheumatoid arthritis, or infection

### Investigations

Your doctor may consider:

- **X-ray** of the shoulder if there is acute trauma and pain prevents examination, to exclude fracture
- **Ultrasound scan** if severe pain, significant trauma, or suspected full-thickness or subscapularis tear, or if not responding to conservative treatment
- For most chronic shoulder conditions, imaging is not routinely needed before a trial of conservative treatment
- For SC joint non-traumatic pain: inflammatory markers (FBC, CRP, rheumatoid factors, anti-CCP, ANA) and imaging as appropriate

## Management

Management depends on the cause. Your doctor may consider:

### Most Shoulder Conditions (including osteoarthritis)

Conservative management first:

- **Analgesia:** paracetamol for pain relief. NSAIDs (e.g. ibuprofen, naproxen, celecoxib) may be used with caution — they carry side-effects including gastrointestinal risk and cardiovascular risk, and may not have an analgesic advantage over paracetamol. Avoid long-term NSAID use. Topical NSAIDs may be used for superficial joints
- **Physiotherapy** to improve range of movement and build strength
- **Corticosteroid injection** if conservative measures are not sufficient — your doctor may administer this or refer to a colleague

### Frozen Shoulder

- Reassurance that most cases improve over time — recovery may take up to 18 months
- Analgesia (paracetamol; NSAIDs with caution)
- **Glenohumeral steroid injection** is most useful in the acute painful phase for short-term relief
- Once the acute painful phase has settled, physiotherapy is beneficial
- If pain is intolerable despite steroid injections, or in patients with diabetes, specialist assessment may be considered for surgical intervention (arthroscopic capsular release)

### Subacromial Conditions (Rotator Cuff Tear, Impingement)

- Reassurance regarding natural history — impingement often improves over time
- Analgesia (paracetamol; NSAIDs with caution)
- Physiotherapy: restoring range of movement in the acute phase, then a strengthening programme
- **Subacromial steroid injection** may be considered
- Full-thickness or subscapularis tears in a surgically fit patient require prompt specialist referral (seen within 4 weeks; outcomes are better if repaired within 6 months)
- Partial thickness tears that fail conservative treatment may also need specialist assessment

### AC Joint Conditions

- Analgesia (paracetamol; NSAIDs with caution)
- Activity modification
- Physiotherapy
- **AC joint steroid injection** may provide short-term pain relief (usually done under imaging guidance)
- If severe symptoms fail all of the above, specialist assessment may be considered

### SC Joint Conditions

- Traumatic dislocation: requires acute specialist assessment
- Non-traumatic (osteoarthritis or rheumatoid arthritis): managed conservatively with topical or oral NSAIDs
- If sepsis is suspected: acute specialist assessment required

### Urgent Assessment

Your doctor will arrange urgent assessment for:

- Septic arthritis, tumour, or fracture
- Traumatic SC joint dislocation

### When Conservative Treatment Fails

If all treatment options have been explored without adequate relief, your doctor may refer to a musculoskeletal specialist or follow a chronic pain pathway.
