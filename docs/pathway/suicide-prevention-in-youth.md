# Suicide Prevention in Youth

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers young people aged 18 years and under (or up to 19 years if still at school) who present with suicidal ideation or risk. Young people differ meaningfully from adults in how they experience and express suicidal distress, and this pathway identifies those key differences.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Immediate Help

If any of the following apply, this requires urgent assessment — contact your doctor immediately or call emergency services:

- Talking about suicide, making a plan or preparations, or a recent suicide attempt
- The "co-morbidity triangle": depression, substance abuse, and being a victim of abuse — occurring together
- Unable to identify any reasons to live or protective factors
- Profound feelings of hopelessness, helplessness, and disconnection from others
- Recent loss of a friend or family member to suicide

---

## Background

Young people lack the emotional and behavioural regulation capacity, and life experience, that adults draw on when managing stress and solving problems. Events that may seem minor to adults can precipitate a suicide attempt in a young person.

- Impulsive suicide attempts are more common in youth than planned attempts. They are often associated with intoxication, anger, recent relationship breakdown, or mood dysregulation.
- Depression is a major precipitant of suicide in young people.
- Suicide in children under 15 is uncommon. The highest-risk age group is 15–24 years — particularly Māori male youth. New Zealand's youth suicide rate (males and females combined) is the second highest in the OECD.
- The Māori youth suicide rate is approximately 2.5 times higher than that of non-Māori youth.
- More than half of all adolescents who attempt suicide have visited a GP within the previous month.

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess multiple dimensions of risk. Suicides occur across all risk levels, so vulnerability factors are always addressed regardless of apparent severity.

### Suicide Risk Factors

Your doctor may ask whether any of the following apply:

- Previous suicide attempts or self-harm
- Mental illness — particularly depression, substance abuse, schizophrenia, eating disorders, or personality disorder (e.g., borderline or antisocial type)
- Cultural disconnection
- Recent loss or isolation — relationship breakdown, involvement with courts, custody, or probation
- Family history of suicide, or a close friend or family member who has recently died by or attempted suicide
- Chronic pain or chronic illness
- Expressed feelings of hopelessness

### Vulnerability Factors

- Low self-esteem or poor social skills
- Family conflict, lack of warmth or affection
- Bullying or peer rejection
- Frequently changing home or school
- Homelessness
- Parents with mental illness, or problematic alcohol or substance use
- Coming from a low socio-economic community
- Suspension or disengagement from school
- History of abuse or neglect

### Protective Factors

Your doctor will also explore what might reduce risk:

- A stable, positive relationship with at least one trusted adult
- Sense of belonging to school, friends, and family
- Belonging to a group or community
- Developing a skill, participating in sports, volunteering, or contributing to a cause
- Strong cultural identity
- Spiritual beliefs that discourage suicide
- Access to a caring and accepting adult to talk to
- Harmonious whānau (family) environment

### How Young People May Present

Young people with suicide risk do not always present directly. Your doctor is trained to recognise that a young person may:

- Present with physical complaints rather than emotional ones
- Bring what appear to be trivial concerns — small issues can feel overwhelming when someone lacks problem-solving experience
- Communicate indirectly — through others, or through social media

### Questions Your Doctor May Ask

If there are concerns about suicidality, your doctor may ask direct questions. This is recognised good practice — asking about suicide does not increase risk, and can be a relief for a young person to be asked openly. Questions may include:

- Have you had thoughts that life is not worth living?
- Have you thought about doing anything about it?
- In the past few weeks, have you wished you were dead?
- In the past few weeks, have you felt that you or your family would be better off if you were dead?
- In the past week, have you been having thoughts about killing yourself?
- Have you ever tried to kill yourself?

### Assessing Level of Concern

Your doctor will consider risk across two categories — constant factors (fixed background risk) and variable factors (changeable, acute risk).

**Constant risk factors include:**
- History of mental illness (severe depression, emerging psychotic disorder, eating disorder, conduct disorder, borderline or antisocial personality problems)
- Previous suicide attempts
- Previous family dysfunction or parents with unstable mental health
- Ethnicity — Māori at highest risk; also refugee or immigrant status
- Male gender
- Substance abuse
- Bullying or social isolation
- Sexual or other abuse history
- Knowing someone who has died by suicide
- Identity issues — cultural or sexual orientation
- School or employment failure
- Poor coping skills

**Variable (changeable) risk factors include:**
- Presence of a plan, access to means, lethality of plan, degree of determination
- Hopelessness and helplessness
- Interpersonal or family crisis, bereavement (especially by suicide)
- Alcohol or drug use, impulsivity when intoxicated
- Peer group influences, including through social media
- Acute or chronic housing instability
- Significant recent stressor

**Features of high concern:**
- Has a plan with access to means
- Several significant risk factors present
- Previous serious attempts
- Few supports and few protective factors
- Extreme mood changes — overwhelming hopelessness, anger, or worthlessness
- Withdrawn, poorly engaged, resists help
- One or more acute stressors without an acceptable solution

**Features suggesting lower (but not absent) risk:**
- Good supports and protective factors
- No plan or only a vague plan
- No significant risk factors
- No previous attempts
- Surmountable stressors
- Actively seeking help
- Can engage with their GP and attend follow-up
- Can focus on the future

## Management

### Confidentiality

Your doctor will explain what confidentiality means in this context. A young person's health information is kept private within the treating team — but if safety concerns arise, others may need to be involved. The Health Information Privacy Code permits disclosure when necessary to prevent serious and imminent risk to life.

Parents or caregivers do not automatically have access to a young person's health information. However, if there is significant risk, your doctor may need to involve family or other adults — and will discuss this with the young person first wherever possible.

### Safety — Immediate Steps

Your doctor's first responsibility is to keep the patient safe. This may involve:

- Working with the young person and their whānau (family), friends, or support people to make the environment safe
- Minimising access to alcohol and other substances
- Removing access to lethal means — this includes ropes, dog leads, and firearms
- Establishing contact with parents, caregivers, or trusted adults who can provide close observation
- Contacting the young person's school, school counsellor, or community agencies if appropriate
- If aged under 17 years and family is unable or unwilling to assist with safety, your doctor may contact Oranga Tamariki (Ministry for Vulnerable Children)

### Safety Planning

Your doctor may help create a written safety plan. The immediate focus of the plan is:

- Making the environment safe
- Identifying immediate personal supports — whānau, friends, trusted adults
- Having 24-hour emergency contacts available

A tool your doctor may use is the **Ko Wai Ahau? (Who Am I?)** personal safety plan — developed specifically for rangatahi (Māori youth) in New Zealand. It includes early warning signs of deteriorating mental health, reasons to live, actions to improve safety, and emergency contact details.

The safety plan is reviewed and updated regularly as the young person's situation changes.

### Medication

Medication is **not** recommended as a first-line treatment for youth with suicidality — and some medications can initially increase suicidal thoughts. Your doctor will seek specialist child and youth mental health advice before considering medication.

### Ongoing Management

Depending on the level of concern, your doctor may:

- Arrange urgent acute mental health assessment if there is high concern or immediate risk
- Refer for non-acute child and youth mental health assessment if ongoing concern exists
- Refer to e-therapy or a school or university guidance counsellor for mild to moderate mental health issues
- Refer to youth alcohol and drug support services if substance use is a factor
- Arrange regular follow-up — by phone, text, email, or appointment — with frequency matching the level of risk
- Contact the young person's school, counsellor, or community agencies to coordinate support
- Treat any underlying mental disorder with evidence-based therapy

If a young person has been admitted to hospital, the week following discharge is a very high-risk period. Your doctor will:

- Review the discharge summary to confirm a clear follow-up plan
- Arrange contact within one week of discharge
- Ensure family and supports can provide close observation and have removed access to means
