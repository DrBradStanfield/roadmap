# Chronic Abdominal Pain in Children and Youth

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers chronic or recurrent abdominal pain in children and young people — assessing possible causes and guiding management.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Medical Attention Promptly

Contact your doctor promptly or go to the emergency department if your child has:

- Blood in stools
- Unexplained fever
- Failure to thrive or significant weight loss
- Diarrhoea lasting more than 4 weeks
- Concerns about abuse or neglect

---

## Background

Episodic recurrent abdominal pain is common, particularly in school-aged children.

A widely accepted definition of recurrent or chronic abdominal pain is **4 or more bouts of pain severe enough to affect activities each month, for at least 2 months**.

### Functional vs Organic Causes

In most cases, chronic abdominal pain is a manifestation of a **functional abdominal pain disorder** — defined as pain without evidence of a pathological (disease-based) process. This is not the same as the pain being "made up" — the pain is real, but it arises from how the gut and brain communicate rather than from tissue damage or disease.

The Rome IV Criteria defines 4 types of functional abdominal pain in children:

- **Functional abdominal pain – not otherwise specified** (most common; occurs in up to 15% of children aged 5–12 years)
- **Abdominal migraine**
- **Functional dyspepsia**
- **Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)**

A minority of children have an **organic cause** (e.g., coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, infection). These children are more likely to have other symptoms and abnormal findings on examination.

The natural history of functional abdominal pain is improvement over time for most children.

## Assessment

Your doctor will perform a thorough assessment to look for organic causes and distinguish between functional and organic pain.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Onset, frequency, duration, severity, and location of pain
- What makes the pain better or worse (e.g., eating, passing stool)
- Stool history — frequency, consistency, blood or mucus
- Associated symptoms including bloating, fever, night sweats, weight loss, vomiting, mouth ulcers, skin rashes, joint pain, urinary symptoms, or headaches
- Dietary history — specific food triggers such as gluten or dairy
- Recent gastrointestinal infection or use of medications (e.g., antibiotics, NSAIDs)
- Family history of coeliac disease, IBS, inflammatory bowel disease, or GORD
- School attendance, stressors, bullying, academic difficulties, and family relationships
- Mental health and wellbeing

**Symptoms suggesting a possible organic cause include:**

- Weight loss, slow weight gain, or falling off growth percentiles
- Blood in stools
- Recurrent vomiting, bilious vomiting, or diarrhoea
- Waking with pain at night
- Unexplained fever, joint pain, or recurrent mouth ulcers
- Polydipsia, polyuria with acute weight loss
- Urinary symptoms (pain passing urine, frequent urination, blood in urine)
- Gynaecological symptoms in teenage girls
- Delayed puberty

For adolescent girls, your doctor will also ask about menstrual history and consider gynaecological causes.

### Examination

Your doctor will examine your child, including:

- General appearance, hydration, and vital signs (heart rate, blood pressure, respiratory rate)
- Height, weight, and growth tracking
- Lymph nodes, mouth, skin, and joints
- Abdominal examination — tenderness, masses, bowel sounds
- Perianal area if relevant

### Investigations

Your doctor will only arrange investigations if clinically indicated. If a functional cause is suspected, investigations are often not required.

If investigations are needed, they may include:

- **Blood tests:** Full blood count (FBC), ferritin, CRP, liver function, kidney function
- **Coeliac serology:** Tissue transglutaminase antibodies (tTG IgA) and total IgA — the child should have been eating gluten-containing food for at least 6 weeks before this test
- **Stool tests:** Microscopy, culture, and sensitivities (MCS)
- **Faecal calprotectin** (in children over 4 years): Can help distinguish IBD from IBS. Note this is an expensive test and not useful if there is active rectal bleeding or recent diarrhoea of less than 6 weeks
- **Midstream urine:** To exclude urinary tract infection (UTI)
- **Pregnancy test** if indicated
- **Abdominal ultrasound** if clinically indicated

> Note: Routine abdominal X-rays are not recommended for non-specific abdominal pain in children. CT scans, allergy skin prick testing, and IgE food antibody tests are generally not helpful in this context.

Your doctor may also ask you or your child to complete a **food and symptom diary** — recording time of day, pain features, possible triggers, treatments tried, and impact on activities.

## Management

Management depends on whether an organic or functional cause is identified.

### If an Organic Cause Is Suspected

Your doctor will manage the most likely diagnosis. Common organic causes include:

- Coeliac disease
- Recurrent urinary tract infection (UTI)
- Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)
- Infectious gastroenteritis, including giardia
- Renal colic or urinary tract stones
- Chronic pelvic pain (if a gynaecological cause is suspected)
- Food hypersensitivity

If there is uncertainty about an organic cause, your doctor may refer to a paediatrician for further assessment.

Your doctor may urgently refer if they suspect:

- An abdominal mass
- A hepatobiliary disorder (including jaundice)
- A pancreatic disorder

### If a Functional Cause Is Identified

Functional abdominal pain is a real condition involving gut-brain interaction — it is not caused by disease, but the pain is genuine and can significantly affect daily life and school attendance.

**Your doctor may:**

- Explain the diagnosis clearly and provide reassurance
- Advise that pain usually improves over time and that returning to normal activities is both safe and helpful
- Provide patient information resources (see below)
- Discuss a plan to return to normal function — including school attendance
- Encourage healthy behaviours and advise parents to avoid reinforcing pain behaviours
- Address school absenteeism early, as this becomes more difficult to resolve once entrenched
- Address environmental and psychosocial stressors (family relationships, bullying, school pressures)
- Discuss chronic pain coping strategies including distraction and relaxation techniques (e.g., deep breathing, muscle relaxation, mindfulness apps such as Smiling Mind or Headspace)
- Arrange regular follow-up (e.g., fortnightly or monthly) to monitor progress

**Management by functional subtype:**

- **Constipation:** Treat constipation specifically
- **IBS:** Dietary adjustments, fibre, and symptom management
- **Reflux/GORD:** Treat the underlying reflux
- **Abdominal migraine:** Identify and avoid triggers (e.g., lack of sleep, dietary triggers). Manage similarly to headache migraine. If episodes are significant or disabling, referral to a paediatrician may be needed.

**Medications your doctor may consider:**

Medications are not generally indicated for functional abdominal pain. However, your doctor may consider a short-term trial (e.g., 4 weeks) of:

- Peppermint oil
- Probiotics
- Water-soluble fibre (if associated with abnormal bowel movements)

Simple analgesics (e.g., paracetamol, NSAIDs) may be discussed for pain relief — used sparingly and within recommended dosing.

> Antidepressants are **not** prescribed for functional abdominal pain unless under specialist advice.

### Mental Health

If anxiety, depression, or other mental health concerns are identified alongside abdominal pain, your doctor will address these appropriately. Counselling or cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) may be recommended, particularly if pain is severe and not responding to other measures.

---

## When to Re-Present

Advise your doctor promptly if new or worsening symptoms develop, including:

- Blood in stools
- Vomiting
- Fever
- Weight loss
- Any other concerning changes

Your doctor will be ready to re-evaluate symptoms at any point and revise the diagnosis if needed.
