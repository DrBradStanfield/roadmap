# Family Violence

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers how clinicians approach identifying, assessing, and responding to family violence — including partner violence, child abuse, and elder abuse.

---

## Background

The Domestic Violence Act 1995 defines family violence as "violence against that person by any other person with whom that person is, or has been, in a domestic relationship."

Family violence covers a broad range of controlling behaviours that are physical, sexual, and/or psychological in nature:

- It includes partner violence, child abuse and neglect, elder abuse, and abuse of current or past intimate partners, including same-sex couples.
- It can happen to any gender and in any socioeconomic, religious, or cultural group.
- Few people volunteer information about their abuse, and most people do not have obvious signs or symptoms.

General practice can offer:
- Asking questions and identifying abuse early
- Emotional support and early intervention
- Risk assessment
- Discussion of options
- Referral to appropriate agencies

When intervening, clinicians recognise:
- It is not the role of general practice to "fix" the problem or investigate the abuse.
- A patient's self-esteem and energy levels may be depleted — choosing to make changes may take time.
- Safety of the patient and staff is paramount.
- Strict confidentiality must be maintained.

## When to Ask About Family Violence

Your doctor may ask about family violence as part of a routine consultation. This is standard practice — similar to asking about alcohol intake or smoking.

**Situations where asking is routine:**
- As part of a routine health history
- During a health check (e.g., smear)
- During antenatal and postnatal care
- At sexual health consultations
- At mental health consultations
- When presenting with chronic undiagnosed pain
- All patients aged 12 to 15 years with signs and symptoms that suggest abuse
- Women aged 16 years or older during an appropriate consultation

**Situations where asking is deferred:**
- Urgent medical needs are present
- Patient is under the influence of drugs or alcohol
- Patient has a cognitive disability
- Patient is emotionally traumatised
- Language barriers prevent private communication
- Partner is present, or there is no opportunity to speak privately
- A child older than 2 years is present

## Indicators of Abuse

Your doctor may look for the following indicators of current or historical abuse:

**History:**
- Documentation or suspicion of previous abuse
- Substantial delay between time of injury and presentation for treatment
- Multiple presentations for unrelated injuries

**Patient's manner:**
- Hesitant or evasive when describing injuries
- Distress disproportionate to injuries
- Explanation that does not account for the injury (e.g., "I walked into a door")

**Physical symptoms or injuries without a verifiable cause:**
- Headaches, migraines
- Musculoskeletal complaints
- Gynaecological problems, gastrointestinal disorders
- Chest pain, palpitations, hyperventilation
- Chronic pain, malaise, fatigue
- Drug or alcohol misuse
- Depression, eating disorders, anxiety, insomnia
- Suicidal ideation or attempts

## Assessment — Disclosure

If a patient discloses family violence, their doctor will:

1. **Provide emotional support** — acknowledge and validate the experience ("this is not your fault", "no one deserves to be treated like this").
2. **Ask permission before further questioning** — e.g., "May I ask you a few more questions in order to identify the best support for you?"
3. **Assess current risk** using targeted questions.

If a patient discloses acute sexual assault, the doctor will not investigate further during that consultation and will refer to appropriate specialist services.

### Risk Assessment Questions

For current abuse, your doctor may ask:

- Where is the abuser now?
- Are you afraid of the abuser?
- Are you afraid to go or stay home?
- Has the physical violence escalated in frequency or severity?
- (For female patients) Are you pregnant?
- Has anyone physically abused the children?
- Have children witnessed violence in the home?
- Has the abuser ever tried to choke or strangle you or anyone else?
- Does the abuser make threats of homicide or suicide?
- Is there alcohol or substance abuse involved?
- Is there a gun in the house?

## Management by Risk Category

### No Disclosure

- If no concerning indicators: the patient is reassured about why the question was asked.
- If concerning indicators are present: the doctor will offer support and reiterate that the practice is a safe place to discuss the issue in future.

### Immediate Danger

(e.g., risk of homicide, further serious assault, access to weapons, strangulation, suicide or self-harm, harm to children, pregnancy)

Your doctor will:
- Inform you of their concern for your safety
- Notify police if further serious assault is likely
- Seek emergency refuge support (e.g., Women's Refuge)
- Arrange urgent mental health referral if there is risk of suicide or self-harm
- Contact police and Oranga Tamariki if there is risk to children

### Safety Is a Concern, but No Immediate Danger

Your doctor will discuss:
- Support from family, friends, and family violence community providers
- Somewhere safe to stay (e.g., with family, friends, or a women's refuge)
- Legal options (e.g., protection orders, family violence services)

Injuries will be documented accurately in case the information is needed for medico-legal purposes. Strangulation (sometimes incorrectly called "choking") is a common form of intimidation in family violence — your doctor will assess this carefully.

If you are pregnant, your doctor may ask permission to liaise with your lead maternity carer (LMC).

### No Safety Concerns

- Your doctor will discuss how family violence can affect health and encourage you to seek help if needed.
- Information about family violence community support will be provided.
- Injuries will be documented accurately if present.

## National Helplines and Support

If you or someone you know is affected by family violence, these services are available across New Zealand:

- **Women's Refuge** — 0800 733 843 (24/7 crisis line). [womensrefuge.org.nz](https://www.womensrefuge.org.nz)
- **Are You OK?** — 0800 456 450 (24/7 family violence helpline). [areyouok.org.nz](https://www.areyouok.org.nz)
- **Shine** — 0508 744 633 (domestic abuse support). [2shine.org.nz](https://www.2shine.org.nz)
- **Oranga Tamariki (Child, Youth and Family)** — 0508 FAMILY (0508 326 459) — for concerns about child safety
- **NZ Police** — 111 for emergencies; 105 for non-emergencies
- **1737** — Need to talk? Free call or text 1737 for mental health support
