# Multiple Sclerosis (MS)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the diagnosis and management of multiple sclerosis (MS) — including assessment, distinguishing a true relapse from a pseudo-relapse, disease-modifying drug (DMD) monitoring, and management of MS-related symptoms. **Always discuss any new neurological symptoms with your doctor.**

---

## What Is Multiple Sclerosis?

MS is an inflammatory disease of the central nervous system (CNS) resulting in neurological deficits. It is diagnosed when there have been 2 episodes of demyelination.

Key facts:
- Patients with a single demyelinating episode are considered to have **clinically isolated syndrome (CIS)** and do not meet the criteria for MS.
- Peak onset is between age 20 and 40 years.
- Three times more common in women and more common in people of northern European ancestry.
- Considered to be an autoimmune disease, possibly precipitated by an infectious agent priming the immune system against myelin antigens.
- More common with increasing distance from the equator.
- Divided into the **relapsing remitting** (more common, better prognosis) or **progressive** forms of the disease.
- Pregnancy appears to be protective against relapses, but there can be an increased risk of relapses postpartum.

---

## Diagnosis

**Practice point: A single demyelinating episode is not MS.**

MS is a clinical diagnosis when there have been both:

- Two episodes of focal neurological symptoms (at least 1 month apart) consistent with demyelination
- MRI confirming characteristic demyelinating lesions. A single MRI scan does not diagnose MS.

### Symptoms Your Doctor Will Ask About

Focal neurological symptoms consistent with demyelination typically increase in severity over days, then stabilise over days to weeks, followed by gradual resolution by 1 month, and are generally associated with fatigue. Your doctor will ask about:

- **Visual symptoms** — features of optic neuritis include:
  - Monocular decrease in visual acuity
  - Usually with pain on eye movement
  - Diplopia
  - Nystagmus
- Progressive motor weakness of limbs
- Sensory impairment over the face or limbs (typically ascending over days; sensory symptoms lasting less than 24 hours rarely represent demyelinating disease)
- Vertigo
- Progressive balance disturbance
- Bladder, bowel, and sexual dysfunction
- Cognitive symptoms, especially decreased memory and processing speed
- Symptomatic episodes separated by time and space, occurring months or years apart, affecting different anatomic locations

### Clinically Isolated Syndrome (CIS)

CIS is considered when a single demyelinating episode occurs with:
- A first episode of neurological symptoms that lasts at least 24 hours
- Caused by inflammation or demyelination of the brain, spinal cord or optic nerves
- Approximately 35–50% of those diagnosed with CIS will have a further demyelinating event within 2 years and fulfil the criteria for MS

### Neurological Examination

Your doctor will examine:
- Features of optic neuritis: visual acuity, eye movements, colour vision, fundoscopy
- Muscle tone, reflexes, power, and coordination
- Cerebellar signs: nystagmus, ataxia, dysdiadochokinesia, hypotonia
- Sensory impairment of face or limbs
- Cognitive function (Mini-ACE: scored out of 30; a score of 25 or less indicates likely cognitive impairment; 21 or less indicates likely significant impairment)

### Investigations

- **MRI** is usually arranged by a neurologist. CT scan is **not** helpful.
- Blood tests to exclude alternative diagnoses: urine dipstick, FBC, CRP, TSH, creatinine, B12, LFT

## New Neurological Episodes in Patients with Known MS or CIS

Your doctor will determine whether a new episode is a relapse, pseudo-relapse, or treatment complication.

### Relapse

Suspect a relapse if new neurological signs or symptoms, or worsening of previous symptoms that:
- Last longer than 24 hours
- Occur in the absence of fever or other infective symptoms, with urinary tract infection excluded

Relapses are an important determinant of disease activity. Your doctor will arrange neurology assessment.

### Pseudo-relapse

A pseudo-relapse is **not** caused by new demyelinating lesions. It is a common cause of neurological worsening in MS, often related to damage from previous demyelinating lesions.

Triggered by:
- Increased body temperature (fever, over-exercising, hot bath or spa, sun exposure)
- Infection even in the absence of fever
- Trauma
- Surgery
- New medications
- Other medical conditions (e.g. hyperglycaemia)
- Psychological stress
- Fatigue

Suspect a pseudo-relapse when:
- Symptoms fluctuate, especially if they completely resolve and then return
- Old symptoms return
- The pattern of symptoms is inconsistent with a lesion in the brain or spinal cord

## Disease-Modifying Drugs (DMDs)

Following confirmation of a second demyelinating episode, the diagnosis of MS is made and the patient may benefit from disease-modifying drugs (DMDs), initiated by a neurologist.

### Types of DMDs

**Immunomodulators:**
- Fingolimod (Gilenya) – oral once daily
- Dimethyl fumarate (Tecfidera) – oral once to twice a day
- Teriflunomide (Aubagio) – oral once daily
- Glatiramer acetate (Copaxone) – subcutaneous injection daily

**Interferons** (now rarely used):
- Interferon beta-1a (Avonex) – intramuscular injection once a week
- Interferon beta-1b (Betaferon) – subcutaneous injection every alternate day

**Monoclonal antibodies:**
- Natalizumab (Tysabri) – intravenous infusion every 4 to 6 weeks in hospital

### Monitoring by Drug

**Glatiramer acetate:**
- No routine monitoring required
- Adverse effects: Immediate post-injection reaction (facial reddening, chest pain, racing heart, anxiety, difficulty breathing — usually short-lived); local injection-site erythema (manage by rotating injection sites)
- Considered safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding

**Interferons:**
- FBC and LFT: at 1 month, then every 3 months for the first year, then annually
- Adverse effects: Flu-like symptoms (administer at night, add paracetamol or ibuprofen); local injection-site erythema
- Avoid in pregnancy — effective contraception required

**Natalizumab:**
- JC polyomavirus (JCV), LFT, FBC every 6 months (at time of infusion)
- Adverse effects: New neurological symptoms may indicate progressive multifocal leukoencephalopathy (PML) — seek urgent medical advice
- Considered safe in pregnancy; may be continued until 34 weeks gestation

## Progressive Multifocal Leukoencephalopathy (PML)

PML is a rare but serious complication associated with natalizumab and other immunosuppressive therapies:
- Caused by JC polyomavirus (JCV)
- Characterised by progressive demyelination damage or inflammation of the brain
- Often results in severe disability or death
- New neurological symptoms in a patient on natalizumab require **urgent medical assessment**

## Treatment of Acute Relapses

Your doctor will decide whether treatment is needed. Key principles:
- **Do not use prednisone** — it is no better than placebo for MS relapse
- Do not treat mild attacks (sensory symptoms only, or fatigue only) or relapses where symptoms have stabilised or are improving
- Only treat if the relapse impacts significantly on quality of life — treatment hastens recovery but does not affect the likelihood or degree of final recovery, or future relapse rate

**Treatment:**
- Consider intravenous or oral methylprednisolone 1 g per day for 3 to 5 days (seek neurology advice)
- Be aware of adverse drug reactions: mood changes, psychosis, avascular necrosis, hyperglycaemia
- Consider co-prescribing omeprazole and zopiclone to help reduce side-effects

## General Symptom Management

Your doctor will manage MS-related symptoms. Neurology MS nurse advice is available for symptom questions.

### Overheating

Encourage patients to limit strenuous exercise, exposure to hot weather, and hot spas, baths, or showers. Overheating can exacerbate the symptoms and signs of MS.

### Balance and Coordination

Assess falls risk. Neurology physiotherapy referral may be arranged.

### Behavioural Changes

Mood changes, personality, and impulsiveness can occur in relation to disease location, living with MS, or medication side-effects. Management:
- Consider changes in environment, increasing recreational activity, and specific behavioural management
- Provide support to carers with problem-solving
- Consider counselling referral

### Bladder Problems

Bladder dysfunction affects up to 75% of patients with MS. Can involve:
- Failure to store urine — urgency, frequency, urge incontinence
- Failure to empty — interrupted stream, incomplete voiding, residual urine, frequency
- Abnormal sensation — urine retention, interrupted stream

Management:
- Check for UTI
- Provide continence aids if needed
- Try solifenacin succinate
- If other treatment for bladder spasm is not effective, consider referral to urology for botulinum toxin type A (Botox)

### Bowel Dysfunction

Can include constipation, difficulty initiating a bowel motion, and faecal incontinence. Management:
- Constipation: see constipation guidelines
- Difficulty initiating: try bisacodyl, glycerol suppositories, or digital stimulation
- Faecal incontinence: consider loperamide hydrochloride; if constipation with overflow — treat impaction with rectal sodium citrate enemas, then oral laxatives

### Cognitive Impairment

- Mild cognitive impairment is common, affecting memory, planning, focus, and concentration
- Consider depression as a contributing cause
- Occupational therapist assessment can provide strategies for improved function

### Coughing and Choking

- Poor cough can lead to inability to clear secretions
- Consider speech language therapy assessment

### Depression and Anxiety

- Depression may be a reaction to the diagnosis, physical disability, chronic illness, or related to MS lesion location
- SSRIs and SNRIs can be effective, but CNS adverse effects are more common — start with lower doses and titrate slowly
- See Depression and Anxiety pathways

### Dysphagia

See Dysphagia and Managing Swallowing Difficulties pathways.

### Fatigue

Fatigue often worsens as the day progresses, aggravated by heat and seen with relapses. Consider contributing factors: sleep disturbances, depression, medications, pain, thyroid dysfunction, anaemia, sleep apnoea. Management:
- Non-pharmacologic: exercise programs, active cooling strategies, energy conservation techniques
- Pharmacologic: amantadine hydrochloride

### Grief Reaction

Grief is the normal response to loss in the setting of MS diagnosis and disease progression. Consider counselling referral and peer support.

### Pain and Sensory Symptoms

- Sensory symptoms are very common: numbness, paraesthesia, tightness or cold sensation, itch, and pain
- Pain may be:
  - Musculoskeletal (from immobility or spasticity)
  - Neuropathic (see Peripheral Neuropathy pathway for medication options)
- Management:
  - Avoid triggers: heat, wind, tight clothing, fatigue
  - Pressure garments (hosiery or gloves) to convert painful sensations to pressure sensations
  - Exercise for core strength and flexibility (e.g. yoga or Pilates)
  - Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) or hypnosis
  - Occupational therapy or physiotherapy
  - Cannabis-based product: for neuropathic pain in MS, requires specialist neurologist signature and ministerial approval; applications can be made by general practitioners

### Sexual Dysfunction

Sexual dysfunction is common in patients with MS. Management:
- Address pain, spasticity, bladder and bowel dysfunction
- Treat depression adequately
- Ensure adequate lubrication
- Consider adverse effects of medications (if SSRIs contributing, consider daytime bupropion hydrochloride)
- Consider phosphodiesterase type-5 inhibitors (e.g. sildenafil) for erectile dysfunction in men with MS

### Sleep

Common sleep disorders in MS include insomnia, restless legs syndrome, and sleep-related breathing disorders.

### Spasticity and Spasms

**General management:**
- Exercises and muscle strengthening (e.g. yoga or Pilates)
- Occupational therapy assessment
- Manage aggravating factors (pain, infection, constipation)

**Medications:**
- **Baclofen:** nocturnal symptoms only — 10 to 25 mg at night; continuous symptoms — 5 to 25 mg three times a day. Withdraw slowly. Adverse effects: sedation, confusion, dizziness.
- **Orphenadrine**
- **Dantrolene sodium:** 25 mg once or twice daily, mainly for bed-bound patients. Frequent adverse effects.
- **Diazepam:** 2 to 10 mg three times a day, alone or as add-on to baclofen. Beware of dependence, ataxia, and sedation.
- **Cannabis-based product:** approved for moderate to severe spasticity in MS patients who have not responded adequately to other anti-spasticity medication. Unfunded; requires neurologist recommendation and appropriately endorsed prescription.

## Vaccination

- Give seasonal influenza vaccination
- Keep up to date with COVID-19 vaccination
- Vaccine-associated fever may exacerbate MS symptoms and cause a pseudo-relapse; relapses after vaccination have rarely been reported
- **Live attenuated vaccines are contraindicated** with fingolimod, teriflunomide, dimethyl fumarate, and natalizumab. Examples: MMR, oral polio, BCG, oral typhoid, varicella (chickenpox and shingles), yellow fever
- Seek advice for travel vaccinations from travel vaccination clinics or neurology

---

## Pregnancy and MS

Your doctor will advise on DMD management during pregnancy. Key points:
- Pregnancy is safe in patients with MS and may provide some disease protection
- Some DMDs are **teratogenic and absolutely contraindicated in pregnancy** (e.g. fingolimod, teriflunomide)
- **Do not stop DMDs without neurologist advice**
- Decisions about pregnancy require consideration of disease severity, degree of disability, fatigue, social supports, psychological and economic factors
- IVF is associated with an increased risk of relapse following an unsuccessful cycle
- Pre-conception planning is important — specialist pre-conception advice should be sought, particularly if on DMDs
- If pregnancy is diagnosed on teriflunomide, urgent neurology advice is needed for consideration of an accelerated elimination procedure

---

## Employment

Work can benefit both physical and mental well-being. MS symptoms (fatigue, limited mobility, cognitive changes), physical work environment, or work role may cause problems. Your doctor can help direct early advice and support regarding employment.

---

## Driving

MS may affect the ability to drive safely because of relapses or disease progression. Discuss driving fitness with your doctor. Mobility parking cards may be available for eligible patients.

---

## When to Seek Medical Advice

**Urgent — contact your doctor or seek emergency care:**
- New neurological symptoms in a patient on natalizumab (may indicate PML)
- Acute, disabling MS relapse (requires same-day assessment)
- New diagnosis of pregnancy while on DMDs

**Soon — contact your doctor:**
- New neurological symptoms in MS — uncertain whether relapse or pseudo-relapse
- Relapse in a patient with MS or CIS
- MS symptom management questions or abnormal blood test monitoring results
- Pregnancy planning while on DMDs

**Routine:**
- Initial investigation and confirmation of MS diagnosis — referral to neurology
- Development of a management plan, including consideration of DMDs
- Annual neurology clinic review (for patients on DMDs)
