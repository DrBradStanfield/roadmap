# Haemorrhoids

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of haemorrhoids (piles) in adults.

---

## Background

Haemorrhoids are enlarged, congested patches of the mucosa and submucosa at the level of the anorectal junction. They often develop between the ages of 20 and 50 years. Chronic constipation or frequent defecation is the most common cause.

---

## Symptoms

The most common symptoms are:

- Perianal discomfort, itching (pruritus), and mucus discharge
- Bleeding — bright red
- Palpable lump after defecation

**Pain is not a typical symptom** of haemorrhoids, unless there is an acute thrombosed external haemorrhoid or perianal haematoma.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Care

Go to the emergency department or seek urgent surgical advice if:

- Rectal bleeding is severe
- There are severe strangulated or thrombosed haemorrhoids (haemorrhoidectomy within 2–3 days may reduce pain)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. Take a history of symptoms and assess the nature and extent of any bleeding. If bleeding is not clearly from haemorrhoids, further assessment of colorectal symptoms will be needed.
2. Examine for signs of anaemia, abdominal tenderness or masses, and enlarged lymph nodes.
3. Inspect the perianal area (with the patient lying on their side or in lithotomy position), looking for:
   - Prolapsed internal haemorrhoids (may be strangulated or thrombosed)
   - Perianal haematomas or abscesses
   - Skin tags, fissures, rashes
   - Other lesions (polyps, warts, condyloma, anal cancer)
4. Perform a rectal examination.
5. Perform proctoscopy if equipment is available — this provides much more information than rectal examination alone.

### Differentiating from Perianal Haematoma

A perianal haematoma (also called an external anal haematoma) is not a true haemorrhoid. It presents as a painful, pea-sized, purple or black lump, occurring in young people — often after a hard or loose bowel motion. It typically resolves over several weeks.

---

## Management

### Lifestyle Measures

Your doctor will advise simple measures to relieve symptoms and prevent recurrence:

- Maintain adequate dietary fibre **and** fluid intake (both are required)
- Exercise regularly
- Limit alcohol and fatty foods, which can worsen constipation
- Respond promptly to the urge to defecate
- Use a regular toileting routine — morning or shortly after meals when the bowel is most active
- Use correct toileting position: knees higher than hips, feet flat on the floor, lean forwards with elbows on knees, bulge out abdomen, straighten spine. A small footstool can help.
- Avoid straining or sitting on the toilet for extended periods (e.g., reading)

### Laxatives (if constipated)

Your doctor may consider:

- **Stool-softening agents** (e.g., docusate sodium / Coloxyl) — avoid in intestinal obstruction
- **Osmotic laxatives** — draw water into the bowel; onset 24–48 hours; avoid in intestinal obstruction
  - Lactulose
  - Macrogols (e.g., Molaxole, Lax-Sachets, Movicol) — can be used long-term if needed
  - Magnesium sulfate (Epsom salts) — avoid long-term use, especially in renal impairment
- **Bulk-forming laxatives** (e.g., psyllium / Metamucil, Mucilax, Konsyl-D) — increase faecal mass and stimulate peristalsis; adequate fluid essential; avoid in patients on opioids or clozapine, or with intestinal obstruction

### Local Preparations

Your doctor may prescribe:

- **Soothing preparations** — provide symptomatic relief
  - Zinc oxide + peru balsam + benzyl benzoate (Anusol) — not funded, available over the counter
- **Compound preparations** — corticosteroid combined with local anaesthetic and soothing agents; use for at least one week but avoid prolonged use beyond 3–4 weeks (risk of skin atrophy)
  - Hydrocortisone + cinchocaine (Proctosedyl) — fully funded
  - Cinchocaine + fluocortolone (Ultraproct) — fully funded (note: supply issues as of September 2025; Proctosedyl is the recommended alternative in the interim)

### Procedural and Surgical Options

Management depends on the type of haemorrhoid:

**Internal haemorrhoids:**
- Not prolapsing — may be treated by sclerotherapy (though often no more effective than lifestyle measures alone)
- Prolapsing but reducible (spontaneously or digitally) — sclerotherapy or banding; banding is often more effective
- Prolapsing and irreducible — best treated by haemorrhoidectomy

**External haemorrhoids:**
Usually asymptomatic (covered with squamous epithelium) unless acute thrombosis occurs.

**Strangulated or thrombosed haemorrhoids:**
- If severe, urgent surgical assessment should be considered — haemorrhoidectomy within 2–3 days may reduce pain
- For most patients, conservative treatment (analgesics, laxatives, local soothing preparations) is preferred

**Perianal haematoma:**
- If severely tender and thrombosed, your doctor may consider incision under local anaesthetic
- Otherwise, reassurance that it will resolve over several weeks is appropriate

---

## When Your Doctor May Refer

Referral to general surgery should be considered when:

- Rectal bleeding is severe — urgent surgical advice
- Severe strangulated or thrombosed haemorrhoids — acute surgical assessment (haemorrhoidectomy within 2–3 days may be beneficial)
- Symptomatic internal haemorrhoids not responding to conservative management — non-acute surgical assessment
- Haemorrhoids remain symptomatic after 6 weeks of treatment — surgical advice

Referrals include a symptom score and function score:

**Symptom score:**
- Severe — dominates life, occurs most days, requires significant pain relief
- Moderate — persistent and frequent moderate symptoms
- Minor — infrequent, mild severity
- Minimal — few or no symptoms

**Function score:**
- Severe disturbance — unable to work or participate in social activities
- Moderate disturbance — decreased capacity to work, severely impaired participation in life
- Minor disturbance — decreased participation in recreational and social activities
- Minimal — little or no disturbance to function
