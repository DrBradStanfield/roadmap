# Psoriasis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of psoriasis in people aged 15 years and older.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Care

Your doctor will arrange **emergency assessment** for:

- Erythrodermic psoriasis (widespread red skin covering most of the body, often with fever, malaise, or feeling very unwell) — hospital admission is required
- Extensive psoriasis with unexplained fever
- Generalised pustular psoriasis (widespread sterile pustules with systemic symptoms)

These are uncommon but serious presentations that require urgent treatment.

---

## Background

Psoriasis is a chronic skin condition where abnormal keratin production causes the skin to thicken and become scaly. It affects about 1–2% of adults.

Key points:
- Psoriasis can also affect nails, joints, and tendons
- About one‑third of people with psoriasis have an affected family member
- Psoriasis is associated with increased risk of cardiovascular disease, metabolic syndrome, depression, inflammatory bowel disease, coeliac disease, and diabetes
- Obesity, excess alcohol, and smoking are associated with more severe psoriasis that responds poorly to treatment
- There is no known cure, but symptoms can be well managed
- Psoriasis does not respond to specific exclusion diets

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Onset and duration of the rash
- Known triggers (infections, skin injury, stress, medications including lithium, antimalarials, or steroid withdrawal; lifestyle factors including smoking and alcohol)
- Body areas affected, including genital involvement
- Impact on daily life and psychological wellbeing
- Nail changes, joint pain, or tendon pain
- Previous treatments used
- Family history of psoriasis

Physical examination typically includes:

- Looking for raised red patches with adherent thick silvery scale — usually symmetrical, especially over elbows and knees
- Checking flexural areas (skin folds: groin, armpits, behind ears, navel, natal cleft) for non-scaly rash
- Examining nails, joints, and scalp

Your doctor may assess severity using the [Psoriasis Area Severity Index (PASI) Score Calculator](https://www.pasitraining.com/calculator/step_1.php) or ask you to complete a quality-of-life questionnaire.

### Patterns of Psoriasis

**Plaque psoriasis** (most common) — thick, sharply defined plaques with silvery scale, usually symmetrical on elbows, knees, scalp, and trunk. Often itchy. May involve nails and joints.

**Guttate psoriasis** — widespread small thin plaques (less than 1 cm), mostly on the trunk. Typically occurs 2–3 weeks after a streptococcal throat infection in young adults. Usually improves over 3 months.

**Facial psoriasis** — three subtypes: hairline extension of scalp psoriasis; sebopsoriasis affecting eyebrows and nasolabial folds; or plaque psoriasis on any part of the face.

**Flexural psoriasis** — non-scaly rash in skin folds, symmetrical with sharply defined borders. Can be mistaken for fungal infection.

**Scalp psoriasis** — silvery scale over erythematous patches; scale may stick to the hair shaft; often described as severe dandruff.

**Palmoplantar psoriasis** — resembles persistent dermatitis on palms and soles; symmetrical red plaques.

**Pustular psoriasis** — localised form (palmoplantar pustulosis) affects palms and soles with chronic pustules; strongly associated with smoking. Generalised form is rare and serious.

**Erythrodermic psoriasis** — generalised redness covering most of the body; associated with fever and systemic illness; requires hospital admission.

**Nail psoriasis** — pitting, separation of the nail from the nail bed (onycholysis), and thickening. Strongly associated with psoriatic arthritis (80–90% of cases).

### Co-morbidities to Consider

Your doctor may check for conditions commonly associated with psoriasis:
- Hypertension, cardiovascular disease, stroke
- Obesity, diabetes, high cholesterol
- Depression and psychological disorders
- Inflammatory bowel disease (especially Crohn's disease)
- Coeliac disease
- Uveitis (eye inflammation)
- Psoriatic arthritis

## Management

### General Measures

Your doctor may recommend:

- Weight loss if overweight — improves treatment response
- Reducing alcohol intake
- Quitting smoking
- Gentle sun exposure (2–3 times per week in spring, summer, and autumn) — can improve psoriasis, particularly guttate type. Avoid sunburn (can trigger a flare). Avoid sunbeds (wrong UV spectrum, increases skin cancer risk).

### Emollients (Moisturisers)

Your doctor will prescribe an emollient for itch, dryness, and scale. It can also be used as a soap substitute. Regular use is an important part of management for all types of psoriasis.

### Treatment by Affected Area

#### Trunk and Limb Plaques

Your doctor may consider:

- **Calcipotriol + betamethasone dipropionate** (brand names: Daivobet ointment or gel, Enstilar foam spray) — a combination of a vitamin D derivative and a corticosteroid. Used once daily for 2–4 weeks. Gel is preferred for widespread areas; ointment or foam for thick scale. Do not use on face, skin folds, or genitals.
  - An alternative steroid-sparing approach uses plain calcipotriol (Daivonex) on weekdays and the combination product on weekends.
  - Calcipotriol has a maximum safe weekly dose (5 mg) — your doctor will monitor calcium levels if high quantities are used.
- **Potent topical corticosteroids** (e.g. betamethasone valerate, mometasone furoate, methylprednisolone aceponate) — applied once daily for 2–4 weeks. Not for use on face, skin folds, or genitals.
- Scale-removing agents to improve penetration:
  - Topical salicylic acid (applied first, only to plaques)
  - Urea cream
  - Coal tar preparations (soften scale, relieve itch, but can stain clothing)

**Important:** Topical corticosteroids should not be used continuously for more than about 4 weeks on stable plaques — prolonged use causes skin thinning, rebound flare on stopping, and reduced effectiveness (tachyphylaxis).

#### Face

Your doctor may consider:

- Emollient plus weak hydrocortisone 1% cream initially
- If insufficient: moderate-potency triamcinolone acetonide 0.02% cream for intermittent (not continuous) use
- Maintenance with once or twice weekly moderate-potency corticosteroid once stable
- Second-line: topical calcineurin inhibitors (pimecrolimus cream or tacrolimus ointment) for up to 4–6 weeks — these are self-funded (pimecrolimus ~$47/tube, tacrolimus ~$45–$90/tube)

#### Scalp

For **mild scaling**, your doctor may consider:

- Coco-Scalp ointment (coal tar 12% + salicylic acid 2% + sulfur 4%) — applied for one hour then washed off, once or twice a week
- Calcipotriol + betamethasone gel (Daivobet) or foam (Enstilar) — daily for up to 4 weeks, then no more than twice a week
- Topical corticosteroid scalp applications or lotions (potency chosen based on severity; daily for 4 weeks during flares, twice weekly for maintenance)

For **thick scaling**, your doctor may recommend:

- Coco-Scalp applied daily for 3–7 days, left overnight under a shower cap
- Adding Daivobet gel or Enstilar foam after washing out in the morning

#### Nails

Nail psoriasis is difficult to treat and response is slow (months).

Your doctor may consider:

- Checking for coexisting fungal infection (nail clipping)
- Steroid lotion applied to the cuticle and under the nail at night, plus moisturiser several times daily
- Assessment for psoriatic arthritis (nail psoriasis is associated with joint disease in 80–90% of cases)
- Referral to a dermatologist if nails are disabling — systemic treatments have a benefit for nail psoriasis

#### Flexural Psoriasis (Skin Folds)

Your doctor may consider:

- Creams rather than ointments (better tolerated in skin folds)
- Triamcinolone acetonide cream (Aristocort) or miconazole + hydrocortisone 1% cream (Micreme-H) — applied daily for 5–7 days, then reduced to 2–3 days per week
- For macerated skin or excessive sweating: cornflour after washing, or antiperspirant powders
- Second-line: topical calcineurin inhibitors (pimecrolimus or tacrolimus) for up to 4–6 weeks

#### Guttate Psoriasis

Your doctor may consider:

- Throat swab if streptococcal trigger suspected; treating any underlying streptococcal infection
- Topical corticosteroids, calcipotriol + betamethasone combination, and/or coal tar preparations
- Sun exposure 2–3 times per week (spring, summer, autumn)
- Referral for narrowband UVB phototherapy (typically 20–30 sessions over 7–10 weeks) for suitable patients

#### Palmoplantar Psoriasis

Your doctor may consider:

- Keratolytic agents (salicylic acid, urea) to thin scaling and improve penetration of other treatments
- Regular emollient application
- Very potent topical corticosteroid: clobetasol propionate 0.05% cream or ointment (Dermol) — applied daily for 2–4 weeks, then tapered to 2 days per week for maintenance. Cotton gloves overnight improve absorption.
- Referral for phototherapy or systemic agents if severe or persistent

### Specialist Referral — Non-Acute

Your doctor may refer you to a dermatologist (non-urgently) if:

- Psoriasis has not responded to adequate treatment in primary care, or causes significant discomfort, affects lifestyle, or covers more than 10% of body surface area
- Psoriasis is inflamed or unstable
- Disabling nail psoriasis
- Guttate psoriasis (for phototherapy consideration)
- Palmoplantar pustulosis (localised pustular psoriasis)
- Severe or persistent palmoplantar psoriasis

Specialist options include narrowband UVB phototherapy and systemic immunomodulating therapies (methotrexate, acitretin, adalimumab, ciclosporin). These require shared care monitoring between the specialist and your GP.

If you have psoriasis and signs of inflammatory arthritis (swollen or stiff joints), your doctor may also refer you to a rheumatologist.
