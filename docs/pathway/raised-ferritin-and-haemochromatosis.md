# Raised Ferritin and Haemochromatosis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the investigation and management of raised ferritin, including distinguishing haemochromatosis (iron overload) from more common non-iron-overload causes.

---

## Background

### Raised ferritin

Incidental raised ferritin is a common finding on blood tests. Haemochromatosis is a rare cause — other causes are far more common and need to be considered first.

Ferritin is an acute phase protein. A raised level can indicate:
- Infection
- Inflammation (e.g. autoimmune disorder, inflammatory arthritis)
- Malignancy
- Renal disease
- Liver disease
- Metabolic syndrome
- Alcohol use
- Thyrotoxicosis

The majority of patients with elevated ferritin and negative gene studies will have fatty liver. These patients do **not** benefit from venesection.

A key test in the further investigation of unexpected raised ferritin is **serum transferrin saturation**.

### Haemochromatosis

Haemochromatosis is a condition of iron overload due to excess intestinal absorption. Historically it presented with fatigue, joint pains, bronzed skin, diabetes, and cirrhosis. Today it most commonly presents with incidental findings of raised ferritin or abnormal liver function tests.

- Life expectancy is not shortened in the absence of end-organ damage (e.g. cirrhosis), provided iron overload is avoided or treated.
- Treatment is venesection (therapeutic blood removal).
- Not all patients with haemochromatosis or iron overload are suitable for venesection.

## Assessment

> **Important note:** Iron deficiency in patients with anaemia of chronic disease can be challenging to identify — ferritin may be normal or raised due to the underlying condition and does not represent iron available for erythropoiesis.

Your doctor will:

- Check transferrin saturation at the same time as ferritin if a raised result is anticipated.
- Consider an incidental raised ferritin in context of all possible causes before investigating for haemochromatosis.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Other medical conditions — especially inflammatory, metabolic, or neoplastic
- Alcohol consumption
- Medications — including iron supplements or multivitamins
- Transfusion history
- Family history of iron overload disorders

### Examination

Your doctor will weigh you and calculate BMI, and look for signs of common causes of raised ferritin not related to iron overload.

### Blood tests

Your doctor will arrange repeat blood tests after allowing any acute illness to resolve:

- FBC
- Ferritin
- CRP
- Urea and electrolytes (U&Es)
- LFT
- Coagulation screen (if ferritin > 1000 microgram/L)
- HbA1c
- Transferrin saturation
- Lipid test

**Further investigation based on results:**
- If transferrin saturation > 50% and FBC normal → HFE gene testing
- If abnormal liver function tests → investigation for liver disease

### Family screening

If you are over 18 years old and have a parent or sibling with C282Y homozygous haemochromatosis, your doctor will offer you screening blood tests (FBC, LFT, ferritin, iron and transferrin) and HFE gene testing. Screening is **not** offered to extended family members if the family member is identified as a C282Y/H63D heterozygote.

## Management

Your doctor will manage you based on your ferritin level and transferrin saturation result.

### Very high ferritin (>10,000 microgram/L)

Rare serious causes such as Still's disease or haemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis must be considered. Your doctor will refer you to a specialist.

### Raised ferritin with transferrin saturation less than 50%

**If you have anaemia:**
- Low MCH and/or MCV → your doctor will follow the Iron Deficiency pathway
- Normal MCH and MCV → blood film arranged
- High MCV → B12, folate, TSH, alcohol intake, and medication review; if macrocytosis unexplained, haematology assessment to exclude myelodysplastic syndrome

**If ferritin persistently raised >1000 microgram/L on 2 readings taken 3–6 months apart:**
- Hepatitis B, C and HIV blood tests arranged
- Haematology advice sought

**If ferritin moderately raised (<1000 microgram/L) with no cause found:**
- Your doctor will treat any identifiable common causes (see list above)
- Lifestyle measures: weight loss, reduced alcohol, dietary improvement, and management of diabetes and cardiovascular risk factors
- Most patients in this category have **metabolic hyperferritinaemia** — not iron overload
- Repeat ferritin and transferrin saturation after 3–6 months; if ferritin remains raised with normal transferrin, liver fibrosis risk is assessed using the FIB-4 score — see the MASLD (Fatty Liver) pathway

### Raised ferritin with transferrin saturation more than 50%

Your management depends on genetic testing results:

- **Homozygous C282Y or compound heterozygous C282Y/H63D** → therapeutic venesection arranged
- **Genetic testing excludes hereditary haemochromatosis** → specialist haematology assessment to exclude rarer causes of iron overload
- **Associated anaemia** → iron-loading anaemia considered (e.g. thalassaemia intermedia, haemoglobin H disease); haematology advice sought

### Genetic counselling for confirmed hereditary haemochromatosis (HH)

Haemochromatosis is an autosomal recessive disorder:
- Siblings of an index case have a 1 in 4 chance of having the same genotype
- If a partner of an index case carries the HFE mutation, offspring have a 1 in 2 chance of having haemochromatosis; otherwise they will be obligate carriers but will not develop iron overload

Your doctor will screen all adult first-degree relatives with ferritin and transferrin saturation blood tests. Children and adolescents are **not** screened (iron overload damage does not occur at this age, except in very rare juvenile haemochromatosis due to hepcidin or haemojuvelin gene mutations).

HFE gene testing in relatives is arranged only if transferrin saturation > 55%, or ferritin ≥ 200 microgram/L in women or ≥ 500 microgram/L in men. This targeted approach is used because 1 in 9 people carry haemochromatosis with no health implications, and only 2% of homozygous patients develop clinical effects.

> **Note:** Two first-degree relatives both with HH may have significantly different iron overload levels for unclear reasons. Even if the index case has mild iron overload, all first-degree relatives are still offered biochemical screening.

If there is significant diagnostic uncertainty in interpreting genetic, ferritin, or transferrin results, your doctor will seek haematology advice.

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will refer you or seek specialist advice in the following situations:

- **Ferritin > 10,000 microgram/L with suspected Still's disease** → non-acute rheumatology assessment
- **Ferritin > 10,000 microgram/L without suspected Still's disease** → acute haematology advice
- **Raised ferritin and transferrin > 50% but genetic testing excludes hereditary haemochromatosis** → non-acute haematology assessment (to exclude rarer iron overload causes)
- **Homozygous C282Y or compound heterozygous C282Y/H63D** → therapeutic venesection
- **Unexplained macrocytosis with anaemia** → haematology assessment (to exclude myelodysplastic syndrome)
- **Suspected iron-loading anaemia** → haematology advice
- **Persistent unexplained ferritin > 1000 microgram/L on 2 readings 3–6 months apart** → haematology advice
- **Significant diagnostic uncertainty in genetic, ferritin, or transferrin interpretation** → haematology advice
