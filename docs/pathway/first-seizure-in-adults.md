# First Seizure in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to an adult who has had a possible first seizure. If you have established epilepsy, this page may still be useful background, but your doctor will manage you according to your specific situation.

> **Important:** The information here is a general guide. Your doctor needs to assess you in person — seizure diagnosis and management requires clinical judgement and, in most cases, specialist input.

---

## Key Facts

- Approximately 60% of single seizures recur within two years.
- The probability of recurrence is highest within the first few months after a first seizure.
- Anti-epilepsy medication is usually **not** started after a single seizure — most doctors wait until a second seizure occurs, and always with specialist input.

---

## When to Go to Hospital Immediately

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if:

- You are not recovering or returning to normal consciousness
- You have a new neurological deficit (e.g. weakness on one side, difficulty speaking)
- The seizure lasted longer than 5 minutes or seizures are occurring back-to-back

---

## How Your Doctor Assesses a First Seizure

### History

Your doctor will want a detailed account of what happened — ideally from both you and an eyewitness.

**From an eyewitness, they will ask:**
- What were the circumstances and any trigger?
- How long did the event last?
- Was there any loss of response or awareness?
- Were there any movements (jerking, stiffening)?
- What was the person like after the event?

**From you, they will ask:**
- What were you doing before it happened?
- Did you have any warning or prodromal symptoms (aura)?
- Did you lose consciousness or awareness?
- Did you bite your tongue or lose bowel/bladder control?
- How did you feel afterwards — confused, tired, sore?
- Can you remember the event?

**Tip:** If further episodes occur, try to video them on a phone — this is very helpful for your specialist.

### Medication and Drug History

Your doctor will review your medications and ask about alcohol and recreational drug use, as these can cause seizures.

Common medication causes include:
- Tramadol, tricyclic antidepressants, clozapine, SSRIs, antihistamines (diphenhydramine)
- Abrupt withdrawal of benzodiazepines, anticonvulsants, or barbiturates

Common substance causes include:
- Alcohol intoxication or withdrawal
- Sympathomimetics (e.g. amphetamines, MDMA/ecstasy, synthetic cannabis)

### Examination

Your doctor will examine you for any focal neurological deficit (weakness, sensory changes, coordination problems).

## Differential Diagnosis — What Else Could It Be?

Not every episode that looks like a seizure is a seizure. Your doctor will consider:

### Vasovagal Syncope (Common Faint)

Vasovagal syncope is common and can sometimes involve brief jerking or stiffening as the brain briefly loses blood flow. Key distinguishing features:

**Features suggesting a faint rather than a seizure:**
- Clear trigger (pain, sight of blood, prolonged standing, heat, medical procedure)
- Prodrome: dizziness, nausea, feeling hot, darkening vision or hearing fade
- Very rapid recovery (usually < 60 seconds)
- Patient was upright before the event

**Features suggesting a seizure rather than a faint:**
- Tongue biting (on the side of the tongue)
- Cyanosis (blue colouring)
- Prolonged confusion afterwards (> 10 minutes)
- Postictal muscle aching

Common situations for vasovagal syncope: prolonged standing, heat exposure, bathrooms, medical settings, early morning, alcohol hangover, pregnancy.

### Cardiac Syncope

Around 5–10% of syncopal episodes are due to a cardiac cause (arrhythmia or structural heart disease). This is important to exclude because it carries a poor prognosis.

Features suggesting cardiac cause:
- Occurs during exertion
- Preceded by palpitations or chest pain (angina)
- Rapid onset and rapid offset
- Known cardiac condition

If cardiac syncope is suspected, hospital admission and monitoring (including telemetry) is usually required.

### Psychogenic Non-Epileptic Seizure

No single feature is pathognomonic. This diagnosis is made by a neurologist — not in primary care. Advise family to video further episodes to assist the specialist.

### Other Causes

- Intracranial pathology (tumour, bleed, infection)
- Drug-induced seizure
- If pregnant or recently postpartum: eclampsia, cerebral venous thrombosis, meningioma

---

## Investigations

- **Blood tests** are usually not helpful after a first seizure in a healthy adult.
- **Urine drug screen** — considered if substance misuse is suspected. Tests for amphetamines, cannabinoids, opiates, benzodiazepines. Separate requests needed for alcohol, methadone, synthetic cannabinoids, GHB, buprenorphine, methylphenidate.
- **ECG** — performed if there are any cardiac symptoms, at extremes of age, or any previous cardiac history.
- **CT head or MRI** — arranged if your doctor is certain the event was a seizure and you are over 20 years old. Low-risk patients (single unprovoked typical seizure, returned to normal health, no suggestion of brain pathology) can often be managed without urgent imaging.

---

## Management

### After a First Seizure

- Most people recover fully within minutes and do not require hospital admission.
- Your doctor will determine the most likely cause.
- Imaging (CT or MRI) is usually arranged if the event was clearly a seizure and you are over 20.
- **Anti-epilepsy medication is not usually started after a single seizure.** Specialist input is required before starting any anti-seizure medication.
- A non-urgent neurology referral is arranged if: the diagnosis is uncertain, or imaging is abnormal.

### Driving

You will need to stop driving after a first seizure. Your doctor will advise you about the specific requirements, which are set by NZTA (New Zealand Transport Agency) for neurological conditions. This is a legal obligation.

A disability allowance may also be available to help with transport costs — ask your doctor.

### Other Safety Precautions

Until the situation is clarified by a specialist, your doctor will advise you to avoid:
- Swimming alone
- Working at heights
- Other hazardous activities where a seizure could be dangerous

### Advice for You and Your Family

- Learn first aid for seizure management (see patient information resources below)
- Record any further episodes: video on a phone or diary of events, timing, and recovery

---

## If a Second Seizure Occurs

- A diagnosis of epilepsy becomes likely.
- Anti-seizure medication (ASM) may be started, but only after a non-urgent neurology assessment and with specialist advice.
- If you have not yet seen a neurologist, your GP will discuss with or refer to neurology before starting any medication.

---

## When Neurology Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will arrange a non-urgent neurology assessment if:
- The diagnosis is uncertain
- CT or MRI findings are abnormal
- You are under 20 years old
- A second seizure occurs

Emergency admission is needed if:
- You are not recovering
- There is a neurological deficit (new weakness, speech problems, etc.)

---

## Topiramate — Important Safety Note (October 2025)

If topiramate is ever considered as a treatment:

- Topiramate during pregnancy increases the risk of birth defects, neurodevelopmental problems, and the baby being small for gestational age.
- Pregnancy testing is required before starting topiramate in anyone who could become pregnant.
- Effective contraception (e.g. IUD, contraceptive injection, or the pill plus a barrier method) is strongly recommended.
- If you become pregnant while taking topiramate, seek specialist advice immediately.
