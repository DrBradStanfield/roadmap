# Otitis Media with Effusion (Glue Ear)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: August 2024.*

Otitis media with effusion (OME), or glue ear, is fluid in the middle ear without signs of acute inflammation. This pathway covers children who have features of middle ear fluid but no symptoms of an acute middle ear infection.

> **Always discuss your child's symptoms and any treatment with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Background

- Peak incidence is at age 5 years.
- 80% of children will have had one episode of OME by age 10 years.
- The mean duration is 6 to 10 weeks.
- Most cases resolve within 3 months without treatment.
- OME differs from acute otitis media (AOM) by the absence of significant pain or fever, and only minimal inflammation of the eardrum. However, the two can overlap on examination.
- Persistent OME with hearing loss may impair language development. Surgery for persistent OME is insertion of ventilation tubes (grommets).
- In New Zealand, Māori and Pacific children aged 0 to 4 years have lower rates of grommet insertion, despite a higher incidence of OME.

---

## Assessment

### History

Your doctor may ask about:
- Signs of hearing loss. These may be noticed by whānau, childcare, or school, or picked up by a failed hearing screen or hearing test.
- How long the hearing loss has lasted: from when symptoms started, if known, or from when OME was diagnosed.
- Possible signs of speech and language delay (see below).
- Learning or behaviour difficulties at childcare, school, or home.
- Current ear symptoms and past ear infections.
- Balance problems.
- Risk factors for developmental difficulties (see below).
- Exposure to second-hand smoke, or living in a damp home.

### Possible signs of speech and language delay

- Not babbling by the age of 15 months.
- Not talking by the age of 2 or 3 years.
- Unable to speak in short sentences by the age of 3 or 4 years.
- Difficulty following directions.
- Poor pronunciation.
- Difficulty putting words together in a sentence, or leaving words out.

### Risk factors for developmental difficulties

- Down syndrome or cleft palate (these also increase the risk of OME).
- Craniofacial disorders that include cognitive, speech, or language delays.
- Permanent hearing loss not caused by OME.
- Blindness, or vision problems that cannot be corrected.
- Intellectual disability, learning disability, or developmental delay.
- Neurodivergence, including autism spectrum disorder and ADHD.

### Examination

Your doctor may look at the eardrum for:
- Signs of fluid, such as a dull or retracted eardrum, or an air-fluid level.
- Complications of long-standing OME, such as a retracted eardrum, a retraction pocket, or a cholesteatoma.
- Chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM).

**Cholesteatoma** is a destructive, expanding growth of skin through a perforated eardrum into the middle ear, or a build-up of dead skin in a retraction pocket of the eardrum. It can erode bone and cause hearing loss, spread inwards and cause vertigo, or become infected and cause a chronically draining ear.

**CSOM** is middle ear infection with discharge from the ear for more than 6 weeks. The discharge is usually painless and often affects both ears.

### Tympanometry

Tympanometry is a simple test of how well the eardrum moves. Your doctor may arrange it to check for fluid:
- **Type A:** normal, no fluid.
- **Type B:** a flat line, suggesting fluid or a hole in the eardrum. It is 100% sensitive but only 75% specific, so about one in four children with a Type B result do not have fluid.
- **Type C:** suggests some fluid, or Eustachian tube dysfunction. It must be read alongside other findings.

Some doctors also use pneumatic otoscopy, which shows reduced or absent eardrum movement in OME. It needs experience to interpret.

## Management

### When your doctor may refer to an ENT specialist

When OME is first diagnosed, your doctor may refer your child to a paediatric ENT (ear, nose, and throat) service if there is:
- Apparent major hearing loss.
- Possible speech and language delay. Your doctor may also refer to speech language therapy.
- A risk factor for developmental difficulties.
- 3 or more months of symptoms, hearing loss, or other symptoms from OME (if the start date is known).
- A complication of long-standing OME. Treatment usually begins while waiting for review.

The ENT team will arrange a hearing test before the appointment if needed.

### Watchful waiting and non-surgical treatments

If glue ear affects both ears, there is no reported hearing loss or whānau concern, and your child does not need referral straight away, your doctor may consider:
- Avoiding second-hand smoke. Stopping smoking in the household is encouraged and supported.
- **Intranasal steroids**, for example fluticasone 50 micrograms into each nostril once daily for 6 weeks. These can be used safely at any age. Blow the child's nose first, and spray back, not upwards. A trial is more likely to be worthwhile if the child has large turbinates and a blocked nose, a history of allergic rhinitis, or obstructive sleep apnoea with smallish tonsils suggesting large adenoids.

Antibiotics, nasal decongestants, oral steroids, and antihistamines are not recommended, as they have no proven benefit.

Your doctor may discuss the possible need for grommets and review your child again at 3 months from when symptoms started (or from diagnosis, if the start is unknown). If the review at 3 months still shows OME, your doctor may refer to ENT for grommets. A community ear nurse may help with follow-up.

### Treating complications

**Chronic suppurative otitis media (CSOM).** Your doctor may consider:
- Cleaning the ear canal carefully. The canal is inflamed and easily damaged. Microsuction is the best method, and your doctor may refer to an ear clinic for it. The ear should not be syringed with water.
- Dry mopping every 6 hours with tissue spears (twisted tissue paper).
- After cleaning, antibiotic and steroid ear drops, 3 to 4 times daily for 2 to 3 days after the discharge has settled.
  - Evidence suggests that quinolone antibiotic drops, such as ciprofloxacin, are most effective.
  - Some ear drops (for example Sofradex, Otodex, Soframycin, Kenacomb) carry a very small risk (1:1000 to 1:10,000) of inner ear damage if there is a direct path to the middle ear, such as a perforation or an open grommet. In an acute perforation, when drops are used for less than 1 week, the risk is very low. Ear drops concentrate medicine in the ear and clear discharge better than oral antibiotics.
  - For long-term use in chronic perforations, local specialists recommend non-ototoxic drops such as Ciproxin HC (not funded). Leaving these ears untreated can also increase the risk of hearing loss.
  - Local specialists have concerns about flumetasone pivalate with clioquinol drops when there is a perforation. These drops can stain hair, clothes, and bed linen. They may be used if there is fungal overgrowth.
- Using an ear swab result, if available, to guide treatment.
- Seeking ENT advice if the ear does not respond to treatment, or if swelling of the canal prevents cleaning or drops.

**How to use ear drops:**
- Mop the ear with a tissue spear or cotton wool first.
- Lie with the affected ear pointing upwards.
- Put in the prescribed number of drops without the dropper touching the ear.
- Press gently on the tragus (the flap in front of the ear canal) several times, in a pumping action.
- Stay in position for 3 minutes.
- Do not plug the ear canal afterwards.
- Keep the ear dry. Use cotton wool smeared with petroleum jelly to fill the outer ear bowl when near water, wear ear plugs, and use a shower cap when showering.

**Cholesteatoma.** Your doctor may seek ENT advice if unsure of the diagnosis. Keep the child's ears dry. If there is discharge, it is managed as for CSOM.

**Retraction or perforation of the eardrum.** Keep the child's ears dry. If there is discharge, it is managed as for CSOM.

### Other steps

- If your child lives in a damp home, your doctor may check whether you are eligible for help to make the home healthier.
- Your doctor may discuss immunisations if they are not up to date.
- Your doctor may give you information about how glue ear usually settles on its own.
