# Differences of Sex Development (DSD)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to differences of sex development (DSD) in newborns and infants — identifying red flags, guiding assessment, and outlining ongoing management.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Care

Contact your doctor or seek emergency care if your baby has:

- Clinical signs of dehydration or poor weight gain
- Clinical signs of low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia)
- Prolonged jaundice

These may indicate congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), which can be life-threatening and requires urgent treatment.

---

## Background

For some individuals, sex characteristics are more diverse than either typical male or female bodies. For approximately 1.7% of all births, medically classifiable variations will occur. These variations are referred to as "differences of sex development (DSD)" — a large group of congenital conditions affecting the urogenital tract and reproductive system, influencing sex determination and/or differentiation.

The most acute condition associated with DSD is **congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH)**, which inhibits normal cortisol and aldosterone production. CAH can be life-threatening and requires acute medical treatment.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will use gender-neutral language during the assessment (e.g. your baby/they, folds, gonads, urethral opening, external sexual organs, genitalia).

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- **Pregnancy history** — whether the pregnancy was natural, IVF, or hormone-induced; scans done during pregnancy; any maternal drug ingestion, infection, or exposure to teratogens; whether an amniocentesis was done (XX or XY chromosomes); any androgenic changes in the mother (e.g. male-pattern baldness, hirsutism, severe acne, deepening of voice, menstruation changes)
- **Birth history** — type of delivery (vaginal, instrumental, breech, or caesarean), birth weight, Apgar scores, oxygen requirements, presence of newborn jaundice, any neonatal hypoglycaemia
- Whether parents are blood-related (which may suggest autosomal recessive inheritance)
- Whether there are a reduced number of male relatives on the mother's side (which may suggest X-linked inheritance)
- Whether any previous sibling died during the newborn period
- Whether any siblings went through early puberty (e.g. early pubic hair development)

### Examination

Your doctor will note that genital appearance is not a medical emergency in itself, but that congenital, metabolic, or endocrine issues arise in approximately 25% of DSD cases.

**General assessment** — your doctor will look for signs of congenital adrenal hyperplasia (CAH), including:

- Vomiting
- Dehydration
- Failure to thrive or gain weight
- Prolonged jaundice (a rare CAH presentation)

**Signs of low blood sugar (hypoglycaemia)** your doctor may look for:

- Cyanosis (bluish skin colour)
- Rapid breathing or pauses in breathing (apnea)
- Irritability or listlessness
- Tremors, shaking, or seizures
- Poor feeding
- Vomiting

**Hydration assessment** — your doctor may assess the degree of dehydration:

| Severity | Symptoms | Physical Signs |
|----------|----------|---------------|
| Mild | Thirsty, restless | None, or slightly dry mouth |
| Moderate | Lethargic, irritable | Dry mucous membranes, absent tears, sunken eyes and fontanelle, decreased urine output, decreased skin elasticity |
| Severe | Limp, drowsy | Altered neurological status, shock signs (fast heart rate, poor pulses, cool peripheries), deep acidotic breathing |

Your baby is at higher risk of dehydration if two or more of the following apply: poor oral intake, frequent vomiting, frequent stools, or aged under 6 months.

**Genital examination** — your doctor will:

- Check whether gonads are palpable in the labioscrotal or inguinal regions (palpable gonads are likely testicular in origin)
- Assess penile length and width, and position of the urethral opening (expected penile length 2.5–4.5 cm in a full-term infant, width ≥1 cm)
- Look for genital pigmentation or dysmorphic features
- Check for pseudo-ambiguity (e.g. growth-restricted or premature female neonates may have minimal genital/labial fat that appears more prominent; breech delivery can cause clitoral and vulvular oedema)

## Management

### Initial Management

Your doctor may consider the following steps:

- **If signs of dehydration or low blood sugar suggest CAH** — urgent referral to paediatric diabetes and endocrinology for acute admission
- **If your baby is systemically stable and well** — your doctor will seek paediatric diabetes and endocrinology advice for clinical management
- **If prolonged jaundice is present without other CAH signs** — your doctor will investigate alternative causes of jaundice

### Ongoing Management

DSD is a wide-ranging condition and surgical management may not be the primary focus early on. Your doctor may consider:

- **For CAH-related DSD** — your baby may require ongoing oral corticosteroids, salt solutions, and hormone replacement. Specialist endocrinology will guide this. Primary care may re-prescribe hormones and may support teaching caregivers to administer solu-cortef injections if indicated
- **Well Child checks** — your doctor will ensure these are completed on schedule
- **Childhood immunisations** — your doctor will check these are up-to-date
- **Mental health support** — if the mother or caregiver has any history of mental health difficulties, or new depressive symptoms arise in the postnatal period, your doctor will follow the perinatal mental health pathway
- **Family support** — feeding support (including breastfeeding support where appropriate), information on well-child providers, community and peer support resources, and parenting programmes
