# Leg Ulcers

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of leg ulcers in adults — determining the underlying cause and appropriate treatment.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Care

See your doctor urgently or go to the emergency department if you have:

- Signs of critical limb ischaemia (severe rest pain in the foot, gangrene, absent pulses)
- Diabetic foot ulcer
- Cellulitis (spreading redness, warmth, swelling around the ulcer)

---

## Background

- Leg ulcers are full-thickness wounds on the lower limb, most often developing after minor trauma.
- Chronic ulcers are defined as wounds present for 4 weeks or longer.
- The most common underlying cause is venous insufficiency (venous ulcers).
- Arterial ulcers (from arterial insufficiency) are less common and require specialist assessment for revascularisation.
- Around 10–20% of leg ulcers have a mixed arterial and venous cause.
- Compression therapy — the main treatment for venous ulcers — is contraindicated if significant arterial disease is present. Your doctor will check for this before starting compression.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess the type of ulcer by looking for:

### Signs of venous insufficiency

- Varicose veins
- Ankle flare — tiny varicose veins on the inner foot and ankle
- Haemosiderin staining — red-brown discolouration of skin between ankle and knee (the "gaiter region" — 95% of venous ulcers occur here)
- Atrophie blanche — lacy white areas of avascular tissue with tiny red spots
- Lipodermatosclerosis — hard, fibrosed skin at the ankle (the leg may look like an inverted champagne bottle)
- Venous eczema — wet or dry eczematous skin changes
- Pitting oedema of the lower limb
- Ulcers typically on the medial lower leg with sloped edges

### Signs of critical limb ischaemia (arterial disease)

- Rest pain — nocturnal foot pain or numbness on elevating the leg, relieved by hanging the foot down
- Absent or reduced foot pulses
- Gangrene of the toes or forefoot
- Dependent rubor — deep red-purple colour when the leg is dependent
- Ulcer positioned on the toe, foot, or ankle
- Pale or cyanosed feet
- Atrophied, shiny skin; hair loss; poor nail health

### Signs of diabetic foot ulcer

- Altered sensation or paraesthesia in a sock or stocking distribution
- May have absent foot pulses
- Callus formation on the sole of the foot
- Foot deformities (hammer toes, hallux valgus, Charcot deformity)
- Foot oedema or dry skin/fissures from autonomic dysfunction

### Other causes to consider

- Lymphoedema — non-pitting swelling, skin changes
- Trauma
- Malignancy — if the ulcer is non-healing or atypical, your doctor may consider biopsy

## Factors Affecting Healing

Your doctor will consider factors that may slow healing:

| Factor | What to know |
|--------|-------------|
| Arterial or venous disease | Both impair circulation needed for wound healing |
| Diabetes | Impairs immune response and circulation |
| Nutrition | Good nutrition supports all stages of healing |
| Mobility | Gentle walking improves circulation; prolonged standing slows healing |
| Medications | Steroids, NSAIDs, and anticoagulants can affect healing |
| Smoking | Significantly impairs healing, especially in arterial ulcers |
| Infection | Competing inflammation delays healing |
| Sleep and rest | Poor sleep can impair the healing process |

---

## Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- **Ankle brachial pressure index (ABPI):** Measures blood flow to the leg using a handheld Doppler device and blood pressure cuff. An ABPI below 0.9 is abnormal; below 0.5 indicates severe arterial disease.
- **Wound assessment using the T.I.M.E.S. model:**
  - **T**issue — checking for non-viable tissue (necrosis or slough) and blood supply
  - **I**nfection — signs of increased bacterial load, wound breakdown, malodour, increasing pain, purulent discharge, surrounding redness or warmth
  - **M**oisture — assessing exudate levels; too much causes maceration, too little delays healing
  - **E**dge advancement — whether the wound edges are healing normally
  - **S**urrounding skin — turgor, colour, signs of infection or maceration
- **Blood tests** — if an underlying condition such as anaemia or heart failure may be contributing

## Management

Your doctor will determine an overall goal of care (wound healing, wound maintenance, or palliation) and manage based on the type of ulcer.

> **Important:** Don't wait for critical limb ischaemia or ABPI to drop below 0.5 before seeking specialist input. Early referral leads to better outcomes.

### Venous leg ulcer

Your doctor may consider:

1. Cleansing the wound and surrounding skin
2. Moisturising the surrounding skin with a simple emollient (ointments for very dry skin)
3. Managing venous eczema with regular emollients and short courses of moderately potent topical steroids for flare-ups
4. Pain relief — paracetamol is generally preferred to NSAIDs
5. Debridement of firm eschar or slough to provide a clean wound bed
6. Treating infection — systemic antibiotics are used only for clinically infected wounds; dressings for non-infected wounds
7. Compression therapy — applied by specially trained nurses; provides sustained graduated compression to improve venous return. This is the cornerstone of venous ulcer treatment but requires arterial disease to be excluded first.
8. Encouraging regular walking and elevating legs when resting; avoiding prolonged standing
9. Preventing recurrence by treating the underlying varicose veins or venous insufficiency

> Note: Oxypentifylline (Trental) is not recommended — there is no consistent evidence supporting its use in leg ulcer treatment.

### Arterial leg ulcer

Your doctor may consider:

1. Specialist vascular surgery assessment for revascularisation (angioplasty, surgery, or conservative management)
2. Strong analgesia — arterial ulcer pain can be severe
3. Appropriate wound care:
   - Dry wounds: keep dry to prevent infection; apply betadine and a padded dry dressing
   - Wet wounds: manage exudate with foams, fibres, or hydrocolloids; consider topical antimicrobial dressings if needed
4. Pressure redistribution — protecting bony prominences from pressure damage
5. If the ulcer has not healed 3 months after revascularisation, further vascular surgery assessment is needed

### Lymphatic leg ulcer

Your doctor may consider:

1. A combination of massage, exercise, compression therapy, and pain relief
2. Referral to lymphoedema specialist services

### Suspected malignant ulcer

Your doctor may consider:

1. Biopsy with surgical excision
2. Further assessment to exclude venous hypertension, ischaemia, or lymphoedema once excised

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will arrange urgent specialist assessment if:

- Critical limb ischaemia is present (acute vascular surgery)
- ABPI is below 0.8 (urgent non-acute vascular surgery)
- Arterial ulcer is suspected (vascular surgery for revascularisation assessment)
- Diabetic foot ulcer with palpable pulses (podiatry via diabetes service)
- Cellulitis with active ulceration in a diabetic foot (acute medical or surgical assessment)
- Wounds fail to heal after 6 weeks (specialised wound management nursing)
- Venous ulcer requiring compression therapy (specialised wound management nursing)
