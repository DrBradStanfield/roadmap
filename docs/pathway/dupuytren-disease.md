# Dupuytren Disease

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of Dupuytren disease — a progressive fibrotic condition of the palmar fascia that causes finger flexion contractures.

---

## Background

Dupuytren disease involves progressive fibrosis of the palmar fascia. Your doctor should know:

- Most commonly affects the ring finger, followed by the little finger and middle finger, but can affect any digit.
- Commonly bilateral (45% of cases); unilateral cases more often affect the right hand.
- Disease progresses through stages: palmar skin pitting and thickening → firm painless nodule fixed to skin and fascia → cord formation → cord contraction pulling the metacarpophalangeal (MCP) and proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joints into progressive flexion deformity.
- Deformity can affect activities of daily living and occupation.
- Onset is usually in the mid-50s for men and mid-60s for women.
- Autosomal dominant inheritance with variable penetrance is suggested.
- A more aggressive form can occur with early age of onset, strong family history, and bilateral hand involvement.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will check for risk factors including:

- Smoking
- Alcohol excess
- Diabetes
- Hypercholesterolaemia
- Dupuytren-like nodules in the feet (plantar fibromas)
- Genital involvement (Peyronie's disease)
- Family history of Dupuytren disease

Your doctor will assess functional impairment:

- Impact on work and ability to place the hand flat on a table (Heuston tabletop test)
- Ability to remove the hand from a pocket without catching
- Pain on gripping
- Difficulty releasing objects from the hand

Physical examination typically includes:

- Thickening or nodule over the palmar aspect of the hand, with or without pain
- Restricted or reduced range of motion of affected fingers (flexion contracture or fixed flexion)
- Fixed firm nodules — mobile nodules may indicate tendon pathology
- Painless cords
- Skin changes
- Metacarpophalangeal (MCP) and proximal interphalangeal (PIP) joint contractures or fixed deformity
- Dorsal knuckle pads over PIP surfaces — may indicate more aggressive disease

Your doctor will also estimate joint contractures, ideally using a goniometer:

- MCP joint contracture is measured in the anatomical position while applying passive extension to the PIP joint.
- PIP joint contracture is measured while the MCP joint is held in flexion to relax the cord — this reflects the actual (not apparent) contracture.

---

## Management

Many people with Dupuytren disease do not develop significant disability and will not need treatment.

**What your doctor will not prescribe:**

- Physiotherapy, splints, exercises, or steroid injections — these do not appear to alter the course of the condition.

**Your doctor may consider surgery if:**

Surgery aims to restore hand function and prevent progression. All of the following criteria must be met:

- The condition has been present for at least 6 months
- There is functional disability
- A contracture is present
- You are ready and willing to have surgery

Surgery timing:
- Ideally performed when the MCP joint contracture is greater than 30°, or when the PIP joint contracture is greater than 30°.
- Should be performed before the contracture reaches a fixed flexion deformity of 90° at the MCP joint or 60° at the PIP joint.
- Sometimes a full-thickness skin graft is required, taken from the forearm on the same side as the affected joint (if non-hairy) or the groin area.

Surgery may also be considered in younger patients with a strong family history of Dupuytren and knuckle pads, even if the above criteria are not all met.

**Postoperative management:**

- Hand therapy is needed after surgery.
- Hand function may take several months to return to normal; the wounds can be tender during recovery.
- Up to 1 in 7 patients may develop a postoperative Dupuytren flare (swollen hand joints with pain and difficulty making a fist). This responds well to a course of oral prednisone — your doctor will seek orthopaedic advice if this occurs.
