# Topical Corticosteroids

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers how topical corticosteroids (steroid creams and ointments) are selected, prescribed, and used safely for inflammatory skin conditions.

---

## What Are Topical Corticosteroids For?

Topical corticosteroids reduce skin inflammation. They are useful for conditions such as eczema, psoriasis, and other inflammatory skin diseases. They are **not** effective for:
- Itching without a rash (e.g. non-inflammatory pruritus)
- Infestations (e.g. scabies)
- Non-inflammatory skin disorders (e.g. urticaria/hives)

---

## Potency Classification

The potency of a topical corticosteroid is determined by its **molecular structure, not the percentage**. For example, hydrocortisone 1% is less potent than hydrocortisone 17-butyrate 0.1%.

Your doctor may label prescriptions with the potency category (mild, moderate, potent, very potent) to reduce confusion.

### Mild
- Hydrocortisone 0.5% — available over the counter
- Hydrocortisone 1% — pharmacist-only or prescription (depending on quantity)

### Moderate (2–25 times as potent as hydrocortisone)
- Clobetasone butyrate 0.05% (e.g. Eumovate)
- Triamcinolone acetonide 0.02% (e.g. Aristocort)
- Hydrocortisone 17-butyrate 0.1% (e.g. Locoid) — reclassified to moderate potency in 2025

### Potent (100–150 times as potent as hydrocortisone)
- Betamethasone dipropionate 0.05% (e.g. Diprosone) — most potent in this group
- Betamethasone valerate 0.1% (e.g. Beta, Betnovate)
- Mometasone furoate 0.1% (e.g. Elocon)
- Methylprednisolone aceponate 0.1% (e.g. Advantan)

### Very Potent (up to 600 times as potent as hydrocortisone)
- Betamethasone dipropionate 0.05% in optimised vehicle (Diprosone OV ointment) — note: different from standard Diprosone which is only potent
- Clobetasol propionate 0.05% (e.g. Dermol)

## Choosing the Right Formulation

| Formulation | Oil content | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ointment | Highest | Dry skin; most effective for absorption |
| Cream | Intermediate | General use; preferred during the day |
| Lotion | Lowest | Hair-bearing areas |

- Ointments are greasier but generally more effective for dry skin.
- Creams are better than lotions overall.
- Avoid alcohol-containing lotions or scalp applications on broken or inflamed skin — they sting.
- When skin is broken or oozy, use ointments (no alcohol) or creams.

---

## Choosing the Right Potency

Use the lowest effective potency for the area and severity:

| Body area | Mild disease | Moderate disease |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Face | Mild | Moderate |
| Trunk or limbs | Moderate | Start with potent |
| Thickened skin (palms, soles, lichenified skin) | Potent or very potent usually required | — |
| Flexural areas (neck folds, armpits, groin) | Mild | — |

## How to Apply Topical Corticosteroids

**Apply liberally once daily** to inflamed skin to form a visible shiny layer. Applying thinly or sparingly is a common cause of treatment failure.

- **Once daily** (typically at night) is usually enough and minimises side effects.
- Apply to all affected areas during a flare, then taper as the skin improves.
- Stop when redness has resolved and skin has returned to normal thickness.
- Continue emollients (moisturisers) even when using steroids — they can be applied in any order.

**Fingertip unit rule:** Squeeze a thin line of cream along the last joint of an adult index finger. One fingertip unit covers an area approximately equal to 2 adult palms.

**Soak and smear** (for severe or resistant disease):
1. Soak in a lukewarm bath for 20 minutes before bed.
2. Pat dry until damp but not dripping.
3. Smear a generous layer of topical corticosteroid onto damp skin.
4. Wear loose clothing and leave overnight.
5. Moisturise in the morning.
6. Repeat nightly until skin clears.
7. See your doctor if no improvement after 2 weeks of daily use.

**Wash your hands** after applying (unless hands are the treatment site) to avoid accidentally applying steroid to your face.

---

## Treatment Duration

- **Standard course:** 2 weeks in most cases.
- **Lichenified (thickened) skin:** Up to 4 weeks.
- Beyond 4 weeks: your doctor should review the diagnosis and treatment.

**Maintenance for recurrent flares:** After the skin settles, 2 days per week of topical corticosteroid to previously affected areas (for 3–6 months) can reduce the frequency and severity of future flares.

**Return to your doctor if** you need to use topical corticosteroids on the same area for:
- More than 2–3 weeks daily, or
- More than 3–4 times per week most weeks

At that review, your doctor will reassess potency, application technique, soak and smear method, concurrent treatments (emollients, irritant avoidance), and reconsider the diagnosis.

## Side Effects

Side effects are **rare** when topical corticosteroids are used appropriately. They become more likely with long-term inappropriate use of potent or very potent steroids.

**Cutaneous (skin) side effects:**
- Skin thinning, stretch marks (striae), spider veins (telangiectasia), lightening of skin colour
- Increased skin fragility and bruising
- Localised hair growth (hypertrichosis) — resolves with time
- Triggering or worsening rosacea, acne, or periorificial dermatitis

Skin thinning is not seen in children with periodic appropriate use of potent steroids.

**Systemic side effects (very rare):**
- Suppression of the adrenal glands (HPA axis suppression), which can cause Cushing's syndrome or adrenal insufficiency
- Risk is higher in: children (large surface-area-to-volume ratio); skin under occlusion (e.g. under a nappy or bandage); thin skin areas (eyelids, genitals, flexures); concurrent oral, inhaled, or intranasal corticosteroids
- Effects are reversible on stopping

**Note:** Oral prednisone is not generally recommended for eczema or psoriasis due to significant rebound flaring on stopping.

---

## Steroid Hesitancy

Fear of side effects (steroid hesitancy) is common and is a frequent cause of treatment failure. Your doctor can reassure you that appropriate use — correct potency, correct duration — is rarely associated with harm. Topical corticosteroids should not be used as a chronic ongoing treatment, but used appropriately for flares they are safe and effective.

---

## Topical Steroid Withdrawal (TSW)

TSW (sometimes called steroid addiction) is a rare rebound reaction that can occur after stopping topical corticosteroids following prolonged overuse. It is more likely with moderate-to-very potent steroids used for a prolonged period.

**Symptoms** (typically develop within 48 hours to 3 months of stopping):
- Stage 1: Acute burning red, oozy skin, possibly spreading to untreated areas
- Stage 2: Dry, itchy, peeling skin
- Stage 3: Skin begins recovering but remains sensitive with intermittent flares
- Stage 4: Full recovery — may take weeks to years

**Features that may suggest TSW:**
- Burning pain, severe itch, skin sensitivity
- Diffuse redness
- "Elephant wrinkles" (thickened skin with reduced elasticity, extensor surfaces)
- Red sleeve sign (erythema of limbs, sparing palms and soles)
- Headlight sign (erythema of face, sparing the nose and perioral skin)
- Skin peeling, swelling, fluid weeping
- Papules, pustules, visible small blood vessels
- Fatigue, dizziness (low blood pressure)
- Low mood and significant psychological burden

**Conditions sometimes mistaken for TSW:**
- Flaring of the underlying eczema after stopping steroids
- Contact dermatitis from an ongoing irritant or allergen (e.g. preservatives in cream)
- Misdiagnosis (e.g. tinea corporis worsens with topical corticosteroids)
- Periorificial dermatitis or rosacea induced by topical corticosteroids

**If you're concerned about TSW, discuss with your doctor.** Your doctor can:
- Acknowledge your concerns and work with you on a practical plan
- Support a gradual taper if you wish to stop
- Consider referral to a dermatologist if systemic treatment is needed to prevent rebound flaring
