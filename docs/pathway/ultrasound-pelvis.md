# Pelvic Ultrasound

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers when a pelvic ultrasound is appropriate for gynaecological and pelvic symptoms in adults and children.

---

## When a Pelvic Ultrasound May Be Considered

Your doctor may consider arranging a pelvic ultrasound for the following conditions. This is a specialist imaging test — your doctor will assess whether imaging is appropriate based on your clinical situation.

---

### Abnormal Uterine Bleeding (AUB)

Abnormal uterine bleeding includes heavy periods (menorrhagia), bleeding between periods (intermenstrual bleeding), and irregular bleeding.

A pelvic ultrasound is useful to identify structural causes such as fibroids and polyps. It is **not** used alone to assess the risk of endometrial cancer — your doctor will consider the full clinical picture.

Your doctor may consider pelvic ultrasound if you have AUB and any of the following apply:

- A pelvic mass is suspected on examination and no prior imaging has been done — may be arranged urgently (within 2 weeks)
- You have **two or more** risk factors for endometrial cancer or hyperplasia (see below) — may be arranged urgently
- You are **under 45** and have one or more risk factors for endometrial cancer, or have not responded to at least 3 months of first-line treatment
- You are **45 or older** and lower genital tract (non-uterine) causes have been excluded
- You are perimenopausal, on combined hormone therapy for more than 6 months, with ongoing irregular bleeding despite a trial of increased progestogen

Your doctor will generally **not** arrange imaging before starting treatment if you have heavy periods with no risk factors, no related symptoms, and a normal examination.

**Risk factors for endometrial cancer or hyperplasia include:**

- BMI 30 or higher (risk increases with higher BMI)
- Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS)
- Chronic anovulation (fewer than 4 menstrual cycles in 12 months)
- Nulliparity (no pregnancies) in women over 35
- Early menarche (before age 10–12) or late menopause (after age 55)
- Oestrogen-secreting tumour
- Long-standing unopposed oestrogen menopause hormone therapy (MHT)
- Tamoxifen therapy
- Diabetes mellitus
- Iron deficiency anaemia (haemoglobin less than 100)
- Family history of endometrial, ovarian, breast, or bowel cancer

> **Equity note:** Māori and Pacific peoples have a significantly higher rate of endometrial hyperplasia and cancer (relative risk of approximately 2.5 and 5 times higher respectively compared with other ethnicities). Your doctor may consider investigations earlier at initial presentation.

---

### Postmenopausal Bleeding (PMB)

Postmenopausal bleeding is any vaginal bleeding that occurs 12 months or more after your last period (in women aged 40 or older, or where premature menopause has been diagnosed).

Your doctor may arrange an **urgent pelvic ultrasound (within 2 weeks)** if:

- You have vaginal bleeding after 12 or more months without a period
- You are on continuous combined hormone therapy and have persistent bleeding more than 6 months after starting

Your doctor will usually do a pelvic examination and cervical smear (if not done in the last 3 months) before arranging the scan.

---

### Ovarian Cancer Symptoms

Your doctor may consider urgent pelvic ultrasound (within 2 weeks) if ovarian cancer is suspected and you have:

- A pelvic or abdominal mass, or fluid in the abdomen (ascites)

Or based on CA125 blood test results:
- **Premenopausal:** CA125 greater than 200 units/mL; or CA125 persistently 35–200 units/mL or rising on repeat testing at 6 weeks; or CA125 less than 35 units/mL with ongoing symptoms at 3-month follow-up
- **Postmenopausal:** CA125 greater than 35 units/mL

---

### Ovarian Cyst (Clinically Suspected)

Your doctor may arrange a pelvic ultrasound if:

- A pelvic mass or one-sided tenderness is found on abdominal or pelvic examination
- There is no mass found clinically, but you have had persistent one-sided pelvic pain for more than 4 weeks

---

### Chronic Pelvic Pain

Your doctor may consider pelvic ultrasound for chronic pelvic pain if all of the following apply:

- Pain lasting at least 6 weeks
- Pain unrelated to your menstrual cycle
- Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) has been excluded

Also considered if:
- A pelvic mass is identified
- An ovarian cyst is suspected with persistent one-sided pain for more than 4 weeks, or a mass or one-sided tenderness on examination
- You have primary dysmenorrhoea that has not responded to appropriate medical treatment (oral contraceptive, NSAIDs)

> **Note on endometriosis:** Ultrasound has limited value for diagnosing endometriosis — the tissue has the same appearance as surrounding tissue on imaging. However, an ultrasound may detect endometriomas (ovarian cysts containing complex fluid) or the effects of adhesions. See the endometriosis section below.

---

### Dysmenorrhoea (Painful Periods)

Your doctor may consider pelvic ultrasound if:

- A pelvic mass is found on examination
- Symptoms are severe and have not responded to at least 3 months of appropriate medical treatment

---

### Endometriosis

Ultrasound cannot directly visualise endometriosis tissue, but it may show:
- Endometriomas (ovarian cysts with a typical appearance)
- Effects of adhesions and ligament thickening

Your doctor may arrange ultrasound if there are:

- Abnormal findings on pelvic examination
- Features indicating specialist referral is needed, including:
  - Deep pain during intercourse (deep dyspareunia)
  - Frequent acute presentations for pelvic pain
  - Signs of deep endometriosis (fixed retroverted uterus, nodularity on examination)
  - Desire for fertility and failure to conceive after 6 months of regular intercourse
  - Risk of developing chronic pain requiring multidisciplinary care
  - Need for neuromodulator medications for pain control
  - Failure to respond to 6 months of optimised medical management

---

### Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)

Imaging is **not required** for a PCOS diagnosis if the clinical criteria are already met.

A pelvic ultrasound (preferably transvaginal) may be considered when the diagnosis is uncertain — for example, if other PCOS features are present but only one of the standard diagnostic criteria is fulfilled.

Your doctor will generally **not** arrange ultrasound for PCOS if:

- You are an adolescent or less than 8 years after your first period (larger, multi-follicular ovaries are normal in adolescents)
- No other diagnostic criteria are met, or another diagnosis is more likely
- You have taken hormone medications (e.g. oral contraceptive pills) within the last 3 months — this can cause false negative imaging

---

### Amenorrhoea (Absent Periods)

Your doctor may consider pelvic ultrasound for **primary amenorrhoea** (periods have never started) with no breast development, where the presence of a vagina or uterus cannot be confirmed by physical examination.

For other types of amenorrhoea, your doctor will follow a dedicated clinical pathway.

---

### Cervical Polyps

Pelvic ultrasound may be arranged if:

- The polyp appears to be coming from within the uterus, or
- Simple twisting does not result in easy removal

---

### Endometrial Cells on Cervical Smear

If endometrial cells are found on a cervical smear, your doctor may arrange an urgent pelvic ultrasound (within 1–2 weeks) depending on the findings and your risk profile:

- **Atypical endometrial cells (AG2):** Urgent scan recommended
- **Benign endometrial cells:** Urgent scan if you are postmenopausal, symptomatic (intermenstrual, postcoital, heavy, or irregular bleeding), have an abnormal examination, or are considered high risk

High risk is defined as age over 35 **and** one or more of:
- BMI greater than 35
- Exposure to oestrogen without progestogens
- Nulliparity or PCOS
- Lynch syndrome (HNPCC) or family history meeting Amsterdam criteria

---

### Fibroids

Your doctor may arrange a follow-up pelvic ultrasound in 1 year if:

- You have symptoms (e.g. abnormal uterine bleeding), **and**
- A fibroid greater than 4 cm has been found during investigations — to monitor for interval change in size and risk of cancerous change

Pelvic ultrasound is **not** indicated for asymptomatic fibroids or routine follow-up of known fibroids.

---

### IUD / IUCD-Related

Your doctor may arrange a pelvic ultrasound if:

- The IUD is broken or missing parts after removal
- The IUD strings cannot be seen (lost IUCD strings)

> **Note on lost strings:** Your doctor will first perform a speculum examination before referring for ultrasound — inability to feel the strings is not sufficient on its own. If the strings are not visible and the IUD is due for removal, your doctor may attempt removal first before arranging imaging.

---

### Retained Products of Conception

Your doctor may arrange imaging if retained products of conception (RPOC) are suspected after a miscarriage, abortion, or delivery.

---

## Conditions Where Pelvic Ultrasound Is Usually Not Indicated

Your doctor will generally **not** arrange a pelvic ultrasound for:

- Acute pelvic pain or pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) — managed through different pathways
- Post-coital bleeding — gynaecology assessment is usually preferred; ultrasound is not indicated unless AUB or postmenopausal bleeding has been excluded
- Subfertility — managed through a dedicated fertility pathway
- Maternity scanning — a separate pregnancy ultrasound pathway applies
- Screening for a family history of ovarian cancer — a specialist pathway applies
- Follow-up of simple ovarian cysts 5 cm or smaller in premenopausal women
