# Head Lice

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of head lice, a common condition particularly in school-age children.

---

## Background

Head lice are 2 to 3 mm insects that live in hair and feed on blood from the scalp. Key points:

- They can cause itching but do not pose a risk to physical health and do not spread disease
- They are not a sign of poor hygiene — social stigma is probably the greatest harm they cause
- They spread through direct head-to-head contact (e.g., between family members and school classmates)
- They do not survive long off the scalp — maximum 24 to 48 hours without food

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will look for signs of head lice by combing the scalp (dry or wet) with a fine-toothed nit comb.

**Signs to look for:**

- Live lice are 2–3 mm, and are often found above and behind the ears and at the nape of the neck
- Unhatched eggs (nits) are darker and laid a few millimetres from the scalp, on the hair shaft
- Hatched eggs are whitish and found further from the scalp

**Confirming the diagnosis:**

- Presence of live lice confirms the diagnosis
- Treatment should also be considered if there are eggs within 1 cm of the scalp
- Empty egg cases may remain after successful treatment — this does not mean treatment has failed

**If no lice are found, other causes of nit-like symptoms include:**

- Crusted scabies
- Seborrhoeic dermatitis or eczema with dandruff
- Hair styling products
- Dirt or sand particles

## Management

Your doctor may discuss the following treatment options with you:

### First-Line Options

**Dimethicone 4% lotion** (fully subsidised on prescription)

Dimethicone is a non-toxic, effective, and well-tolerated treatment that works by suffocating lice.

- Use 50–100 mL per person per treatment
- Apply to dry hair, wet thoroughly with lotion, and comb through with a regular comb
- Leave on for at least 8 hours before washing off
- Repeat treatment at 7 to 10 day intervals — expect at least 70% successful eradication after two applications
- Non-viable eggs remain present after treatment; they can be removed with a nit comb if social stigma is a concern

**Physical removal (wet combing)**

This method mechanically removes lice and nits, and is effective if performed thoroughly.

- Apply hair conditioner or vegetable oil to thoroughly saturate hair
- Work systematically, combing from scalp to the ends of the hair with a plastic nit comb
- Remove nits and lice by wiping on a tissue after each comb-through; dispose of tissue in a sealed bag
- Repeat at 4-day intervals for at least 2 weeks, until no lice are found on 3 consecutive sessions

### Second-Line Options

Your doctor may consider these if first-line treatments have not worked:

- **Chemical insecticides** (e.g., malathion, permethrin) — use lotions or liquid formulations only (shampoos are not effective). Resistance to malathion and permethrin has been reported. Use with caution in children under 2 years due to potential low-level toxicity; do not use more frequently than the manufacturer recommends
- **Oral sulfamethoxazole/trimethoprim** (off-label use) — works by killing bacteria in the gut of the lice so they starve. Reserved for cases of treatment failure because of the potential for side effects

---

## General Advice

Your doctor may advise:

- Check all household members for head lice
- Treat each person with live lice, eggs close to the scalp, or who shares close contact (e.g., shares a bed) at the same time
- Repeat treatment after 7 to 10 days
- Notify the school or preschool if children have confirmed head lice — children do not need to be excluded from school
- Wash all hair combs, brushes, hair ties, and head covers with hot, soapy water at the time of treatment
- Hot washing of clothing and bedding is generally not necessary to prevent spread; consider it only if treatment fails

**Prevention:**

- Check for head lice and eggs weekly
- Avoid sharing hair brushes, combs, hats, and sports headgear
- Tie up long hair
