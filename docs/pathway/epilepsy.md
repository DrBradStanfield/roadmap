# Epilepsy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: August 2019.*

This pathway is for people with an established diagnosis of epilepsy.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Topiramate and Pregnancy

A safety update from October 2025:

- Topiramate taken during pregnancy increases the risk of birth defects, neurodevelopmental disorders (including learning and behavioural problems), and the baby being small for gestational age.
- Anyone who could become pregnant should have a pregnancy test before starting topiramate.
- Effective contraception is important, e.g. a contraceptive injection, an intrauterine device (IUD), or the combined oral contraceptive pill with a barrier method.
- If you become pregnant while taking topiramate, tell your doctor promptly. Your doctor will seek prompt specialist advice. Referral for antenatal monitoring and counselling is also recommended.

See Medsafe: [Topiramate: New Measures and Advice to Prevent Exposure During Pregnancy](https://www.medsafe.govt.nz/safety/Alerts/Topiramate-pregnancy.asp).

## Background

- Epilepsy is diagnosed once a person has had two identified epileptic seizures, more than 24 hours apart.
- **Types of epilepsy:**
  - In teens, most epilepsy is primary generalised. The commonest type is juvenile myoclonic epilepsy (morning jerks, tonic clonic seizures, and sensitivity to flashing light in 30%). This usually needs life-long treatment with anti-epileptic drugs (AEDs).
  - Juvenile myoclonic epilepsy responds best to sodium valproate, lamotrigine and levetiracetam. Carbamazepine may make the jerks worse.
  - 90% of adult epilepsies are focal. Focal epilepsy may respond to any anticonvulsant.
- **Common seizure types:**
  - **Tonic clonic:** the person goes stiff, often with a cry, followed by fast jerks that usually fade as the attack ends. There is noisy breathing, often with blue lips or skin, then deep drowsiness (stupor), confusion, and gradual recovery. Lasts 1 to 4 minutes, not counting the recovery period afterwards.
  - **Focal motor with dyscognitive features (altered awareness; previously called complex partial):** the person stops what they are doing and stares, sometimes with fiddling hand movements, lip smacking or swallowing. There may be some confusion afterwards. Lasts 1 to 2 minutes, not counting the recovery period.
  - **Focal motor without dyscognitive features (previously called simple partial):** jerking of the face, arm or leg (or a combination), with no loss of awareness. Lasts 1 to 2 minutes.
  - **Generalised absence:** a very brief pause in activity with a stare. Lasts 1 to 5 seconds. No confusion afterwards.
- The goal of treatment is complete control of seizures. For some people this may not be possible without side effects they cannot tolerate, and a balance may be acceptable.

## Assessment

At a review, your doctor may:

- Ask about seizure control: how often seizures happen, how severe they are, and what type.
- Check whether you are taking your medicines as prescribed and whether you have side effects.
- Suggest a seizure diary, which may be useful.
- Ask about your mood. Anticonvulsants have been associated with an increased risk of anxiety, depression, and suicidal thoughts.
- For women of childbearing age:
  - Ask about contraception and remind you about pregnancy planning.
  - Recommend folic acid 5 mg daily, especially if you take sodium valproate, even if you are not planning pregnancy. More than 50% of pregnancies are unplanned.
  - If you are not already taking folic acid, recommend starting 5 mg daily at least three months before trying to conceive and continuing 5 mg daily throughout pregnancy.

## Management

### Medicines

If seizures are poorly controlled and you are taking your medicines as prescribed, your doctor may consider:

- Slowly increasing the dose of your current medicine until the seizures stop or side effects appear.
- Or changing to another first-line anti-epileptic medicine. The new medicine is increased gradually before the original one is withdrawn.

**Do not stop an anti-epileptic medicine suddenly.** Abrupt withdrawal may cause a seizure or status epilepticus.

Other points your doctor may consider:

- **Sodium valproate** is avoided in women of childbearing potential because it can harm an unborn baby and is associated with developmental delay. It should only be prescribed to women of childbearing age by a specialist.
- If you have side effects from your medicine, a lower dose may be considered.
- **Blood levels of the medicine** can be useful with phenytoin, with lamotrigine in pregnancy, after starting the combined oral contraceptive pill, or if missed doses or toxicity are suspected. The sample is taken just before the next dose (a trough level).

### Driving

- You must stop driving for 12 months after any seizure, minor seizure, or aura.
- Commercial driving: people with epilepsy are permanently disqualified, but may be considered if seizure-free for 5 years without medicine and after a neurologist's assessment.

Your doctor can discuss the NZ Transport Agency rules with you.

### Alcohol

Alcohol lowers the seizure threshold. A small amount, 1 to 2 drinks per occasion but no more than 3 to 6 drinks per week, is thought to be safe.

### Bone Health

Anti-epileptic drugs are associated with lower bone density and a higher risk of fractures.

### Stopping Treatment

Stopping treatment may be appropriate if you have been seizure-free for the previous 2 to 5 years. Before this, your doctor will discuss:

- **The chance of seizures coming back:**
  - Primary generalised seizures: the relapse rate is more than 90%, so lifelong medicine is usually needed.
  - Adult onset and seizure-free for 2 years: the relapse rate is about 40%.
  - Adult onset and seizure-free for more than 5 years: the relapse rate is about 20 to 25%.
- **Driving:** you must stop driving during withdrawal and for 6 months after withdrawal is complete. If you have a seizure during or after withdrawal and medicine is restarted, the usual stand-down rules apply.
- Your doctor will seek neurology advice.

### Triggers and Support

- Look at your own seizure triggers, e.g. lack of sleep, alcohol, stress.
- Patient information: [Healthify: Epilepsy](https://healthify.nz/health-a-z/e/epilepsy).

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you to a neurologist if:

- Seizure control remains poor, or the medicines are poorly tolerated.
- You have been seizure-free for the last 2 to 5 years and would like to consider stopping or reducing treatment (neurology advice, after discussing driving and the chance of relapse).
- You become pregnant while taking topiramate (prompt specialist advice).
