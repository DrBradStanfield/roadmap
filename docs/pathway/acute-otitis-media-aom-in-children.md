# Acute Otitis Media (AOM) in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: November 2024.*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of acute middle ear infections in children, including when antibiotics are needed and when to seek urgent care.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Call 111 or go to the emergency department if your child has an ear infection and any of the following:

- Suspected mastoiditis (one ear sticking out, redness or tenderness behind the ear, or a high fever that does not settle quickly with fever medicine)
- Facial nerve palsy (drooping of one side of the face)
- Vertigo (a spinning feeling), imbalance, or new abnormal eye movements (nystagmus)
- Signs of meningitis (headache with nausea or vomiting, fever, neck stiffness, sensitivity to light, confusion, rapid deterioration)

---

## Background

Most children have had an episode of acute otitis media (AOM) by the age of 5 years. Some children have both glue ear (otitis media with effusion) and repeated AOM.

Key points:
- Ear pain usually lasts between 12 and 48 hours
- Fluid in the middle ear (effusion) may persist for several weeks after an episode of AOM
- For most children, antibiotics at the first visit are not necessary
- However, antibiotics should be prescribed for any child under 6 months, or any child under 2 years with a severe or bilateral infection

AOM is more common and more severe in Māori and Pacific children. Factors include socioeconomic deprivation, overcrowded and substandard housing, and exposure to secondhand smoke.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your child by:

**Taking a history.** AOM cannot be diagnosed from ear pain alone. Your doctor will ask about:
- Duration of ear pain and fever
- Symptoms of a recent cold or upper respiratory infection
- Ear discharge
- Tinnitus or vertigo
- Use of pain relief or fever medication
- Exposure to passive smoke or damp living conditions

**Risk factors your doctor will consider for earlier treatment and close monitoring:**
- Prolonged ear pain (it usually settles within 24 to 48 hours)
- Recurrent AOM: 3 episodes within the previous 6 months
- Cochlear implant or sensorineural hearing loss
- Underlying conditions such as craniofacial abnormalities or a weakened immune system

**Physical examination:**
- Vital signs (heart rate, temperature, respiratory rate)
- Examination of the eardrum (tympanic membrane) for bulging and discharge. In AOM the eardrum is red and bulging, but redness alone does not suggest AOM. Crying, fever, a cold, and injury can also make the eardrum red.

**Diagnosis of AOM requires all three of:**
1. Presence of an upper respiratory tract infection
2. Ear pain that began less than 48 hours ago
3. Redness and bulging of the eardrum with middle ear fluid, or a perforated eardrum with discharge

Bulging and redness without ear pain may mean AOM that has recently settled.

**If discharge is present, your doctor will distinguish between:**
- Otitis externa (outer ear canal infection). There may be a history of swimming, with pain when the ear is pulled and tenderness at the front of the ear canal.
- Otitis media with perforation (the eardrum has burst). There is no ear canal tenderness.

**Serious but uncommon complications to consider:**
- **Mastoiditis:** An uncommon but serious bacterial infection of the mastoid bone behind the ear. It is more common in children. Signs include one protruding ear with loss of the skin crease behind it, high fever that does not respond quickly to fever medicine, persistent throbbing ear pain, marked tenderness over the mastoid bone, and redness or soft swelling behind the ear. The ear may be pushed down and forward, especially in very young children. Mastoiditis may present as an abscess behind the ear.
- **Facial nerve palsy**
- **Meningitis:** Headache with nausea or vomiting, fever, confusion, neck stiffness, sensitivity to light, rapid deterioration

**Investigations:**
- Ear swabs are only considered if there is infection after ear surgery other than grommets. Routine swabbing at first presentation is not recommended.

## Management

### AOM Without Discharge

1. **Pain relief:** Your doctor will recommend regular pain relief for the first 48 hours, such as paracetamol and an anti-inflammatory.

2. **Antibiotics:** Antibiotics are given at the first visit if:
   - Your child is under 6 months old
   - Your child is under 2 years with a severe or bilateral (both ears) infection
   - An older child has a high fever, severe pain, or a discharging ear
   - There is a perforated eardrum or discharge
   - There are systemic symptoms (e.g., fever)
   - There is no improvement within 48 hours

   Your doctor may also consider antibiotics at the first visit if:
   - Your child has had 3 or more ear infections in 6 months, or 4 or more in 12 months
   - There are concerns about being able to return for review

   If antibiotics are not prescribed immediately, your doctor may give a "back-pocket prescription" to use if your child still has symptoms after 48 hours.

   **Antibiotic options your doctor may consider:**

   *First-line:*
   - **Amoxicillin** 15 mg/kg three times daily for 5 days
   - For severe or recurrent infection, or if a recent antibiotic course was given: **amoxicillin** 30 mg/kg (max 1 g) three times daily for 5 to 7 days

   *If a recent course of high-dose amoxicillin has failed:*
   - **Amoxicillin + clavulanic acid** 30 mg/kg (max 625 mg) three times daily for 5 to 7 days

   *If allergic to penicillin:*
   - **Trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole** (co-trimoxazole), for children over 6 weeks: 0.5 mL/kg/dose of oral liquid (40 + 200 mg/5 mL) twice daily for 5 to 7 days (max 20 mL/dose). A child who can swallow tablets may take 80 + 400 mg tablets instead (one tablet equals 10 mL of the liquid). Not for infants under 6 weeks, because of the risk of jaundice (hyperbilirubinaemia).
   - **Erythromycin:** Total daily dose split into 3–4 doses:

     | Weight | Daily dose |
     |--------|-----------|
     | < 5 kg | 30–50 mg/kg/day |
     | 5 to < 7 kg | 200 mg/day |
     | 7 to < 13 kg | 400 mg/day |
     | 13 to < 24 kg | 800 mg/day |
     | 24 to < 45 kg | 1200 mg/day |
     | ≥ 45 kg | 1600 mg/day |

3. **Avoid:** Nasal decongestants, systemic decongestants, and antihistamines. Evidence has not shown any benefit.

4. **Reassurance:** Fluid in the middle ear may take 8 to 12 weeks to resolve.

---

### AOM With Perforation and Discharge (Eardrum Has Burst)

1. **Oral antibiotics** are prescribed for all children (same antibiotic options as above).

2. **Ear drops** containing antibiotic and steroid may also be prescribed, used 2 to 3 times daily for 5 days. Your doctor will not prescribe ear drops if there is a perforation without discharge.

   - Note: Ear drops carry a very small risk (1 in 1,000 to 1 in 10,000) of inner ear damage when there is an open pathway to the middle ear. Local specialists consider this risk very low in an acute perforation when drops are used for less than 1 week. Drops concentrate medicine in the ear and clear the discharge better than oral antibiotics.
   - If ear drops are prescribed, the ear canal may need careful cleaning first. The canal is inflamed and easily damaged. Microsuction is the best method, and your doctor may refer your child to a community ear clinic for it. Do not syringe with water.
   - Dry mop the ear with tissue spears every 6 hours.

3. **Review at 2 weeks:** Your doctor will check whether the eardrum has healed. If discharge continues at 2 weeks, your doctor may seek ENT specialist advice about further assessment.

---

## Follow-Up

Your doctor may arrange a follow-up assessment after 8 weeks if your child has:
- Multiple previous ear infections
- A history of glue ear (otitis media with effusion)
- Existing hearing or language difficulties

**Reducing risk of further infections:**
- Avoid exposure to secondhand smoke
- Address damp living conditions
- Catch up on any overdue immunisations, and ask about pneumococcal vaccination

If your child has 6 or more episodes of AOM within 12 months, or 4 or more within 6 months, your doctor may refer for specialist ENT assessment.

---

## When Your Doctor Will Refer to a Specialist

Your doctor will arrange urgent hospital ENT assessment if:
- Suspected mastoiditis (including abscess behind the ear)
- Facial nerve palsy
- Failure to respond to second-line antibiotic treatment

Your doctor will seek specialist advice if:
- Discharge continues from a perforated eardrum at 2 weeks
- Severe ear canal swelling prevents cleaning or ear drop administration
