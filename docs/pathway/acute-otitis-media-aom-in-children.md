# Acute Otitis Media (AOM) in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of acute middle ear infections in children, including when antibiotics are needed and when to seek urgent care.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Take your child to the emergency department or call an ambulance if any of the following are present:

- Suspected mastoiditis (ear protruding forward, tenderness and redness behind the ear, high fever not responding to paracetamol)
- Facial nerve palsy (drooping of one side of the face)
- Vertigo, imbalance, or new abnormal eye movements (nystagmus)
- Signs of meningitis (headache, neck stiffness, sensitivity to light, confusion, rapid deterioration)

---

## Background

Most children experience at least one episode of acute otitis media (AOM) by age 5. AOM is a bacterial or viral infection of the middle ear, often following an upper respiratory tract infection (cold).

Key points:
- Ear pain typically lasts 12–48 hours
- Fluid in the middle ear (effusion) may persist for several weeks after the infection clears
- For most children, antibiotics at the first visit are not necessary
- However, antibiotics should be prescribed for any child under 6 months, or any child under 2 years with a severe or bilateral infection

AOM is more common and more severe in Maori and Pacific children. Factors include socioeconomic deprivation, overcrowded housing, and exposure to secondhand smoke.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your child by:

**Taking a history, asking about:**
- Duration of ear pain and fever
- Symptoms of a recent cold or upper respiratory infection
- Ear discharge
- Tinnitus or dizziness
- Use of pain relief or fever medication
- Exposure to passive smoke or damp living conditions

**Risk factors your doctor will consider for earlier treatment:**
- Pain lasting longer than 48 hours
- Recurrent AOM: 3 or more episodes in the past 6 months
- Cochlear implant or sensorineural hearing loss
- Craniofacial abnormalities or immune deficiency

**Physical examination:**
- Vital signs (heart rate, temperature, respiratory rate)
- Examination of the eardrum (tympanic membrane) for redness, bulging, and discharge

**Diagnosis of AOM requires all three of:**
1. Presence of an upper respiratory tract infection
2. Ear pain of less than 48 hours duration
3. Redness and bulging of the eardrum with middle ear fluid, or a perforated eardrum with discharge

**If discharge is present, your doctor will distinguish between:**
- Otitis externa (outer ear canal infection — often with pain when the ear is pulled, history of swimming)
- Otitis media with perforation (eardrum has burst — no canal tenderness)

**Serious complications to consider:**
- **Mastoiditis:** Unilateral protruding ear, loss of the skin crease behind the ear, high persistent fever, throbbing ear pain, tenderness or redness over the mastoid bone (behind the ear). The ear may be pushed forward, especially in young children.
- **Facial nerve palsy**
- **Meningitis:** Headache with nausea or vomiting, fever, confusion, neck stiffness, sensitivity to light, rapid deterioration

**Investigations:**
- Ear swabs are only considered if there is discharge following non-grommet ear surgery. Routine swabbing at initial presentation is not recommended.

---

## Management

### AOM Without Discharge

1. **Pain relief:** Your doctor will recommend regular paracetamol and an anti-inflammatory (e.g., ibuprofen) for the first 48 hours.

2. **Antibiotics:** Your doctor may prescribe antibiotics at the first visit if:
   - Your child is under 6 months old
   - Your child is under 2 years with a severe or bilateral (both ears) infection
   - Your child has a high fever, severe pain, or a discharging ear
   - There is a perforated eardrum or discharge
   - There are systemic symptoms (e.g., fever)
   - There is no improvement within 48 hours
   - Your child has had 3 or more ear infections in the past 6 months, or 4 or more in 12 months
   - There are concerns about being able to return for review

   If antibiotics are not prescribed immediately, your doctor may give a "back-pocket prescription" to use if symptoms have not improved after 48 hours.

   **Antibiotic options your doctor may consider:**

   *First-line:*
   - **Amoxicillin** 15 mg/kg three times daily for 5 days
   - For severe or recurrent infection, or if a recent antibiotic course was given: **amoxicillin** 30 mg/kg (max 1 g) three times daily for 5–7 days

   *If high-dose amoxicillin has failed:*
   - **Amoxicillin + clavulanic acid** 30 mg/kg (max 625 mg) three times daily for 5–7 days

   *If allergic to penicillin:*
   - **Trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole** (co-trimoxazole): 0.5 mL/kg/dose of oral liquid (40 + 200 mg/5 mL) twice daily for 5–7 days (max 20 mL/dose). Not for infants under 6 weeks.
   - **Erythromycin:** Total daily dose split into 3–4 doses:

     | Weight | Daily dose |
     |--------|-----------|
     | < 5 kg | 30–50 mg/kg/day |
     | 5 to < 7 kg | 200 mg/day |
     | 7 to < 13 kg | 400 mg/day |
     | 13 to < 24 kg | 800 mg/day |
     | 24 to < 45 kg | 1200 mg/day |
     | ≥ 45 kg | 1600 mg/day |

3. **Avoid:** Nasal decongestants, systemic decongestants, and antihistamines — these have not been shown to help.

4. **Reassurance:** Fluid in the middle ear may take 8–12 weeks to fully resolve. This is normal.

---

### AOM With Perforation and Discharge (Eardrum Has Burst)

1. **Oral antibiotics** are prescribed for all children (same antibiotic options as above).

2. **Ear drops** containing antibiotic and steroid may also be prescribed, used 2–3 times daily for 5 days. Your doctor will not prescribe ear drops if there is a perforation without discharge.

   - Note: Ear drops carry a very small risk (1 in 1,000 to 1 in 10,000) of inner ear damage when there is an open pathway to the middle ear. Your doctor will weigh this against the benefit.
   - If ear drops are prescribed, your doctor may advise gentle cleaning of the ear canal beforehand. Do not syringe with water.
   - Advise dry mopping with tissue spears every 6 hours.

3. **Review at 2 weeks:** Your doctor will check whether the eardrum has healed. If discharge continues at 2 weeks, further specialist assessment may be arranged.

---

## Follow-Up

Your doctor may arrange a follow-up appointment at 8 weeks if your child has:
- Multiple previous ear infections
- A history of glue ear (otitis media with effusion)
- Existing hearing or language difficulties

**Reducing risk of further infections:**
- Avoid exposure to secondhand smoke
- Address damp living conditions
- Ensure your child's immunisations are up to date, including pneumococcal vaccine

If your child has 6 or more episodes of AOM within 12 months, or 4 or more within 6 months, your doctor may refer for specialist ENT assessment.

---

## When Your Doctor Will Refer to a Specialist

Your doctor will arrange urgent ENT assessment if:
- Suspected mastoiditis (including abscess behind the ear)
- Facial nerve palsy
- Failure to respond to second-line antibiotic treatment

Your doctor will seek specialist advice if:
- Discharge continues from a perforated eardrum at 2 weeks
- Severe ear canal swelling prevents cleaning or ear drop administration
