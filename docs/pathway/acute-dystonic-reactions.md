# Acute Dystonic Reactions

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the recognition and initial management of acute dystonic reactions — sudden involuntary muscle spasms caused by certain medications.

---

## Background

Acute dystonic reactions are medication-induced movement disorders. The exact cause is uncertain but is thought to involve disruption of dopamine, serotonin, and acetylcholine pathways in the basal ganglia. Most reactions occur within 24 to 48 hours of starting a new medication.

---

## Assessment

### How It Presents

The person appears distressed, often with unusual posturing or difficulty speaking. Not all symptoms occur in every reaction. Presentations include:

- **Oculogyric crisis** — blepharospasm or spasm of the extraorbital muscles, causing the eyes to deviate upwards and outwards
- **Torticollis** — the head is held turned to one side
- **Opisthotonus** — painful forced extension of the neck; when severe, can extend to the back with the person arching off the bed
- **Macroglossia** — the tongue does not swell but protrudes and feels swollen
- **Buccolingual crisis** — may include trismus (jaw lock), risus sardonicus (rictus grin), dysarthria, and grimacing
- **Laryngospasm** — uncommon but frightening
- **Spasticity** — trunk muscles, and less commonly the limbs

Mental status is typically unaffected and vital signs are usually normal.

### Causative Medications

Common medications that can trigger a dystonic reaction:

- Antipsychotics
- Some antiemetics — e.g. metoclopramide, prochlorperazine
- Antidepressants

Less common:

- Antihistamines
- Antiparkinsonian medications
- Anticonvulsants

### Other Causes to Consider (If No Response to Treatment)

If a person does not respond as expected, your doctor will consider alternative diagnoses:

- Hyperventilation — carpopedal spasm is usually more prominent than in dystonic reactions
- Hypocalcaemia or hypomagnesaemia
- Primary neurological causes, e.g. Wilson's disease
- Tetanus or strychnine poisoning
- Neuroleptic malignant syndrome
- Drug toxicity — carbamazepine, anticholinergics, phenytoin, sodium valproate

If in doubt, your doctor will treat as an acute dystonic reaction first, then reassess if there is no response.

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following:

### 1. Benzatropine (previously known as benztropine) — First-line

- **Adults:** 1 to 2 mg intravenous (IV) or intramuscular (IM), followed by oral treatment if required
- **Children over 3 years:** 20 micrograms/kg (maximum 1 mg) IM or IV

Most people respond within 5 minutes and are symptom-free within 15 minutes. If there is no response after 15 minutes, the dose may be repeated (if using the IM route, wait 30 minutes before reassessing). No response to a repeated dose suggests the diagnosis may not be a dystonic reaction.

**Follow-up oral dosing** is important to prevent recurrence — typically benzatropine 1 to 2 mg orally twice a day for 48 to 72 hours, depending on the half-life of the causative drug.

### 2. Other Options

- **Promethazine:** 25 to 50 mg IM (IM is the preferred route — IV promethazine carries a risk of serious tissue injury)
- **Diazepam:** 5 to 10 mg IV — useful when the response to more specific antidotes is incomplete, or when other medications are contraindicated

### 3. Ongoing Management

- If the reaction is medication-related, the causative drug should be avoided going forward. Alternative agents less likely to provoke dystonic reactions should be considered.
- Suspected drug-induced dystonic reactions should be reported to the Centre for Adverse Reactions Monitoring (CARM) / New Zealand Pharmacovigilance Centre.
- If symptoms fail to settle or the diagnosis is in doubt, emergency department assessment may be needed.
