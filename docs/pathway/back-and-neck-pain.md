# Back and Neck Pain

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of back and/or neck pain, with or without limb pain. Most back and neck pain is mechanical in origin and resolves within a few weeks with reassurance and self-management.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

Go to hospital or call your doctor urgently if you have any of the following:

**Cauda equina syndrome** (emergency — may need same-day surgery):
- Altered bladder function (urgency, retention, or incontinence)
- Altered bowel function (constipation or faecal incontinence)
- Saddle numbness (numbness in the groin/perineal area)
- Rapidly progressive leg weakness or difficulty walking
- Sexual dysfunction developing alongside new back pain

**Spinal cord compression:**
- Difficulty with balance or gait
- Progressive arm or leg weakness
- Signs of upper motor neuron involvement (increased reflexes, Hoffman's sign, Babinski sign)

**Spinal infection (osteomyelitis, discitis, epidural abscess):**
- Fever or feeling generally unwell alongside spinal pain
- Risk factors: intravenous drug use, recent spinal surgery, immunodeficiency

**Malignancy:**
- Known history of cancer (the single biggest predictor)
- Unexplained weight loss
- Severe, unrelenting pain that does not improve with rest or is worse at night or when lying down

**Fracture:**
- History of significant trauma
- Minor trauma in someone with osteoporosis, advanced age, or prolonged steroid use

**Vascular emergency:**
- Sudden severe back pain in someone with known aortic aneurysm or strong cardiovascular risk factors — may indicate aortic dissection or rupture

**Additional important symptoms:**
- Fever or malaise
- Chest pain, shortness of breath on exertion, cough, or coughing up blood
- Progressive weakness or numbness at multiple spinal levels
- For neck pain specifically: visual changes, Horner's syndrome (droopy eyelid + small pupil), neck swelling, jaw claudication, difficulty swallowing, or signs of meningism

## Background

Back and neck pain is extremely common:

- Low back pain affects 5–15% of the population each year; about 80% of adults experience it at some point
- Mechanical low back pain accounts for 90–95% of presentations — serious causes are rare
- Neck pain affects 10–20% of the population per year and often recurs
- Most episodes improve within a few weeks; two-thirds of patients recover within 12 weeks
- Investigations (imaging, blood tests) are not usually needed unless red flags are present

Predictors of poor recovery include: fear avoidance behaviour, catastrophising, belief that pain must disappear before resuming activity, depression, anxiety, and poor expectations of recovery.

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

**Take a history including:**
- Location of pain and any radiation into limbs
- Onset (was there a forceful event or trauma?)
- Duration — acute (less than 6 weeks), subacute (6–12 weeks), or chronic (more than 12 weeks)
- Character and severity, including night pain or pain when lying down
- Aggravating and relieving factors
- Red flags and important symptoms (see above)
- Psychosocial factors: impact on work, sleep, and daily life; fear avoidance; past episodes and treatments

**Distinguish between pain types:**

- **Mechanical (somatic/non-specific) pain** — dull, aching, ill-defined borders; dominant in the back or neck but may radiate to buttocks, hips, or limbs; related to movement and position; most common
- **Radicular (neuropathic) pain** — shooting, lancinating, or electric shock-like; radiates in a thin line down the length of a limb; often worse peripherally and at night; suggests nerve root irritation
- **Radiculopathy** — objective nerve conduction changes: numbness, weakness, or absent/reduced reflexes in a dermatomal/myotomal distribution; does not always occur alongside radicular pain

**Examine:**
- Gait and spinal range of movement
- Neurological assessment if limb symptoms: reflexes, motor strength, and sensation
- Provocation tests (e.g., straight leg raise for lumbar nerve root irritation; Spurling's test for cervical nerve root irritation)
- Cardiovascular and abdominal exam if vascular cause suspected

**Likely causes:**
- **Mechanical spinal pain** — most presentations. In the acute/subacute setting, usually non-specific. In the lumbar spine, disc pain is most common in people under 60; facet joint pain is more common in the elderly. For the cervical spine, facet joint pain is the most common in all ages.
- **Radicular syndrome** — 5–10% of presentations
- **Serious pathology** — less than 1% of presentations (fracture, tumour, infection, cauda equina)

## Investigations

Your doctor will consider:

**Blood tests** (only if indicated):
- Suspected infection: FBC, CRP, ESR
- Suspected malignancy: FBC, CRP, calcium, LFTs, myeloma screen, PSA (if relevant)
- Suspected inflammatory disorder: FBC, CRP, calcium, rheumatoid factor, HLA-B27

**X-ray spine** (only if indicated — not routine for uncomplicated back pain):
- Suspected fracture, progressive neurological deficit, moderate-to-high risk of malignancy or infection
- Not useful for disc herniation or most sacral insufficiency fractures

**MRI spine** (if indicated):
- Best for disc herniation, nerve root compression, infection, and occult fracture
- Generally not useful for diagnosing the cause of back or neck pain without radicular features
- Appropriate when serious pathology is suspected or for persistent severe radicular pain being considered for intervention

## Management

### Serious Underlying Conditions

Your doctor will seek urgent hospital assessment if:
- Suspected cauda equina syndrome or spinal cord compression
- Suspected spinal infection (do not wait for test results)
- Rapidly worsening neurological deficit
- Suspected vascular emergency (aortic dissection or rupture)
- Imaging suggests malignant CNS tumour

If bone pain is known to be from cancer or cancer is strongly suspected, your doctor will seek urgent specialist advice.

---

### Uncomplicated Back Pain (Mechanical, Radicular, or Spinal Stenosis)

Most uncomplicated back pain — including mechanical low back pain, radicular pain with radiculopathy, and suspected spinal stenosis — improves with time.

**Key principle:** Back pain is not a sign of damage. Staying active and maintaining usual activities promotes recovery.

#### Advice and Reassurance

Your doctor will:
- Explain that pain does not equal damage, and that normal movement is safe and promotes healing
- Address fear avoidance behaviour and catastrophising
- Reassure you that most back pain resolves within weeks, and ongoing improvement is expected
- Discuss how stress, mood, and anxiety can amplify pain perception

#### Self-Management

Things that can help:
- Stay as active as possible — frequent short walks, changing position regularly, gentle stretches
- Apply heat or ice packs; warm baths or showers
- Use relaxation or distraction techniques: yoga, meditation, mindfulness
- Maintain normal daily activities; use activity pacing if needed
- Remain at work if possible — returning to work supports recovery
- Good sleep, healthy diet, and maintaining a healthy weight
- Self-help resources: *Treat Your Own Back* and *Treat Your Own Neck* by Robin McKenzie; [painTRAINER](https://www.paintrainer.org); [Healthify — Back Pain](https://healthify.nz/health-a-z/b/back-pain#Resources)

Things that may worsen pain:
- Inactivity or prolonged bed rest
- Staying in one position for long periods
- Muscle tension and stress
- Poor sleep and low mood

#### Medications

Your doctor may consider:

**First-line analgesia (stepped approach):**
- NSAIDs — e.g., ibuprofen 200–400 mg three or four times daily (maximum 2400 mg/day), or diclofenac SR 75 mg twice daily (maximum 150 mg/day). NSAIDs are generally more effective than paracetamol for acute low back pain.
- Paracetamol 1 g four times daily

**Muscle spasm:**
- Orphenadrine (Norflex) 100 mg up to twice daily may help. Benzodiazepines should not be used for spinal pain or muscle spasm.

**Severe or neuropathic (radicular) pain**, particularly if disrupting sleep:
- Low-dose tricyclic antidepressants: nortriptyline or amitriptyline 10 mg at night, increasing if needed
- If not tolerated or ineffective: pregabalin or gabapentin (note: pregabalin is no more effective than placebo for radicular leg pain)
- Short course of prednisone may help for cervical radicular pain (e.g., 40 mg for 5 days, then 20 mg for 5 days) — not effective for lumbar radicular pain

**Opioids (with caution):**
- Opioids are not effective for neuropathic pain and are harmful in chronic pain management. They are less effective than simple analgesics in direct comparisons and worsen pain over the longer term.
- If used at all, short-term only with an agreed stop date and clinical review within 2 weeks
- Codeine 30–60 mg up to every 6 hours (maximum 240 mg/day), or tramadol 50–100 mg up to every 4 hours (maximum 400 mg/day)

#### Follow-Up

Your doctor will review you in 1–2 weeks to:
- Check for any new red flags
- Gauge response to management
- Reinforce positive progress and address any ongoing fear avoidance behaviour

Return for review at 6 weeks if there is significant persisting pain, or sooner if symptoms worsen or new symptoms develop.

---

## If Pain Persists Beyond 6 Weeks

Your doctor will:
- Re-examine for red flags and serious underlying conditions
- Consider differential diagnoses: inflammatory spondyloarthropathy, fragility fracture, malignancy, spinal infection
- Arrange further investigations if indicated (imaging, blood tests)
- Review and adjust analgesia
- Consider referral to physiotherapy, musculoskeletal physician, sports medicine specialist, or orthopaedic surgeon
- If severe neuropathic pain: MRI may be appropriate to guide decisions about foraminal steroid injections or surgery
- If suspected inflammatory spondyloarthropathy: rheumatology referral
- If progressive neurological symptoms not typical of radiculopathy (e.g., multilevel or proximal weakness): neurology referral
- If severe, widespread pain with sensitivity to light touch (nociplastic pain): chronic pain specialist referral
