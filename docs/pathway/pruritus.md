# Pruritus (Itching)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of pruritus (itching) in adults — from identifying the cause to topical and systemic treatment options.

---

## Background

Pruritus means itching, and can be associated with a number of disorders, including dry skin, skin disease, hormonal issues, neurological conditions, metabolic disorders, and rarely, cancer.

Like pain, itch can originate anywhere along the neural pathway from the skin to the central nervous system.

Pruritus can be localised or generalised, and with or without a rash.

Constant scratching can cause skin damage (excoriation, lichenification) and may lead to lichen simplex, prurigo, or nodular prurigo.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess whether the itch is localised or generalised, and whether a rash is present, to guide the likely cause.

### Generalised Itch with a Rash

Conditions include but are not limited to:

- Eczema or dermatitis
- Psoriasis
- Urticaria (hives)
- Scabies
- Bullous or pre-bullous pemphigoid

### Localised Itch with a Rash

Conditions include but are not limited to:

- Grover disease (transient acantholytic dermatosis) – typically on the trunk
- Papular urticaria (insect bites – often along the beltline or on the distal limbs)
- Tinea (fungal infection of the foot, groin, body, or hand)
- Head lice or pubic lice
- Pruritus ani or pruritus vulvae
- Lichen simplex chronicus
- Lichen sclerosus (usually genital)
- Lichen planus (rarely generalised)
- Seborrhoeic dermatitis
- Varicose (gravitational) dermatitis (bilateral on lower legs)

### Generalised Itch without a Rash

Conditions include but are not limited to:

- **Dry skin** – very common, especially in the elderly
- **Medications** – a wide range of medicines can cause pruritus, including:
  - Opioids
  - NSAIDs (including aspirin)
  - Antibiotics
  - ACE inhibitors
  - Vildagliptin
- Iron deficiency anaemia
- Liver disease
- Chronic renal failure
- Hyperthyroidism or hypothyroidism
- HIV infection
- Malignancy (e.g. lymphoma, leukaemia, lymphoproliferative disorders)
- Aquagenic pruritus – a severe prickling-like itch triggered by contact with water at any temperature (rainwater, seawater, tap water, sweat)
- Idiopathic (all other causes excluded)

### Localised Itch without a Rash (usually neurological)

Conditions include but are not limited to:

- Brachioradial pruritus
- Notalgia paraesthetica (pigmentation can develop as it progresses)
- Peripheral neuropathy
- Postherpetic neuralgia
- Trigeminal trophic syndrome
- Meralgia paraesthetica

---

## Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- **First-line blood tests:** FBC, CRP, creatinine and electrolytes, LFT, HbA1c, ferritin, TSH
- **Further tests** based on clinical suspicion: LDH, immunoglobulins, protein electrophoresis, calcium, skin autoantibodies, chest X-ray, abdominal ultrasound

---

## Management

### General Measures

Your doctor will advise on managing aggravating factors:

- **Coarse fabrics** – wear light cotton clothing
- **Vasodilators** (e.g. alcohol, caffeine) – reduce intake
- **Extremes of heat and cold** – take short tepid baths or showers; wet dressings can cool the skin
- **Scratching** – gentle rubbing may be substituted if the urge is irresistible

### Skin Care

- Avoid soap; use non-soap alternatives (e.g. cetomacrogol aqueous cream with glycerol)
- Moisturise regularly with an emollient

### Topical Treatments

Your doctor may consider:

- **Emollients** – regular use is a mainstay of management
- **1% menthol in an emollient base** – when tolerated; pharmacies compound this preparation
- **Topical capsaicin cream** – may help for localised neurological itch; initial burning usually settles with continued use; avoid applying to flexures
- **Topical corticosteroids (low or moderate potency)** – if unsuccessful after a few weeks of regular use, potent topical corticosteroids are not indicated

### Systemic Treatments

Your doctor may consider systemic antipruritic medications:

- **Tricyclic antidepressants** (e.g. amitriptyline 10–75 mg at night) – may help reduce pruritus, especially at night; start low and titrate, particularly in older adults
- **Gabapentin or pregabalin** – may help with neuropathic pruritus, pruritus of renal origin, or idiopathic pruritus
- **Antihistamines** – most useful in urticaria; sedating antihistamines (e.g. promethazine) may help at night; avoid topical antihistamines (risk of sensitisation and contact dermatitis)
- **Systemic steroids** – a short trial of prednisone (e.g. 40 mg daily, tapering over 4–6 weeks) may be considered for idiopathic pruritus in the elderly (unapproved indication); underlying causes must be thoroughly investigated first, and risks of systemic steroids considered

### When Your Doctor May Refer You

Referral to a dermatologist may be considered:

- If treatment is unsuccessful after 2 months of adequate trial
- If you are severely distressed by your pruritus
- For phototherapy, where available

**Phototherapy** is most useful for renal pruritus and can also be used for idiopathic pruritus. Suitable patients are generally those who can travel for treatment 2–3 times per week over 2–3 months, can stand unaided in the phototherapy cabinet, and can consent to treatment.
