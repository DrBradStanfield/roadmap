# Herpes Simplex Keratitis / Dendritic Ulcer

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and initial management of herpes simplex keratitis (HSK) and dendritic corneal ulcers — one of the more common causes of a unilateral red eye in adults.

---

## Important Note — Renal Impairment

Aciclovir and valaciclovir doses must be reduced in patients with renal impairment. Aciclovir is 80% eliminated by renal clearance and can accumulate, causing neurotoxicity. Watch for lethargy, confusion, agitation, or hallucinations. Your doctor will ensure adequate hydration and adjust doses accordingly.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Same-Day Ophthalmology

Your doctor will arrange same-day specialist eye assessment if:

- Significantly reduced vision
- Visible corneal opacity
- The patient is a child (especially with vesicles around the eye, photophobia, or abnormal red reflex)

---

## Background

- Herpes simplex keratitis is one of the most common causes of a corneal (epithelial) ulcer, after trauma.
- Primary ocular infection usually occurs in childhood and is mostly self-limiting, affecting the eyelids and conjunctiva.
- Reactivation in adulthood occurs when the latent virus travels down the trigeminal nerve and enters the cornea.
- Virus replication in the corneal epithelium causes ulceration — the characteristic dendritic (branching) ulcer.
- Most ulcers heal without scarring. In some cases, inflammation spreads into the deeper corneal stroma (stromal keratitis), which can cause scarring and significant vision loss.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will check for the following clinical features:

- Red eye, blurred vision, sensitivity to light (photophobia)
- Mild to moderate eye pain, usually lasting up to one week
- Vesicles (small blisters) around the eyelids — seen only in primary HSV infection
- History of previous herpes simplex keratitis or perioral cold sores

Physical examination typically includes:

- **Visual acuity** — may range from normal (if the dendrite is peripheral) to moderately reduced (around 6/48) if the defect involves the central cornea
- **Corneal haze** — can be detected by examining the red reflex at arm's length
- **Fluorescein staining** — a zig-zag or branching (dendritic) pattern of fluorescein uptake is highly specific for HSV

### Differential Diagnosis

Your doctor will consider other causes of a red eye, including:

- **Corneal abrasion** — typically shorter history with a clear traumatic cause; no branching pattern
- **Bacterial keratitis** — no branches; usually a corneal opacity without the dendritic pattern
- **Acute angle-closure glaucoma** — typically nausea and deep visceral pain; no fluorescein staining

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following approach:

**For epithelial keratitis or dendritic ulcer (without red flags):**

- Arrange ophthalmology assessment within a few days
- Swab the inferior conjunctiva for HSV PCR (the tear film will contain HSV if present)
- Treat with **aciclovir 3% eye ointment**, applied 5 times daily for 14 days
- Advise the patient to return promptly if symptoms worsen before specialist review

**If red flags are present (same-day referral):**

- Significantly reduced vision or visible corneal opacity → same-day acute ophthalmology assessment
- Child with photophobia, vesicles, or abnormal red reflex → same-day acute ophthalmology assessment

All management decisions are made by your doctor based on your specific clinical picture.
