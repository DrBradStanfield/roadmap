# Depression in Adults and Older Persons

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: April 2024.*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of depression in adults aged 18 years and over.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Red Flag — Seek Urgent Help

**Suicidality** is a red flag. If you are thinking about suicide, see your doctor the same day, or call or text 1737 (free, any time). If you have a plan or feel you might act on it, call 111 or go to the emergency department.

---

## Background

Depression is common and treatable. Key facts:

- Depression affects about 6% of New Zealanders in any 12-month period, and about 18% of people who attend general practice.
- Lifetime prevalence is around 16%.
- It is 2–3 times more common in people with chronic physical health conditions.
- Depression in older people is common, and often under-detected and untreated. It is never a normal consequence of ageing.
- Effective treatments can reduce suffering and help recovery.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess depression by looking at:

### Screening

Your doctor may use two simple questions (the PHQ-2):

1. During the past month, have you often felt down, depressed, or hopeless?
2. During the past month, have you had little interest or pleasure in doing things?

If the answer to either is yes, they may also ask: *Would you like help with this?* A yes to this third question as well makes depression likely.

If depression is suspected, your doctor will use the **PHQ-9 questionnaire** to gauge severity and to track progress:

| PHQ-9 score | Severity |
|---|---|
| 10–14 | Mild depression |
| 15–19 | Moderate depression |
| 20 to 27 | Severe depression |

The PHQ-9 score is an indicator, not a diagnosis. It does not replace your doctor's clinical judgement. Your doctor will base treatment on how much depression affects your daily life, not on a count of symptoms.

### Targeted Screening

Your doctor may screen more actively if you have:

- Clinical suspicion based on what you describe
- Drug or alcohol misuse
- Pregnancy or recent birth
- Multiple physical symptoms
- Chronic physical health problems (especially diabetes or chronic pain)

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Suicidal thoughts
- Severity and duration of symptoms, and how distressed you feel
- How much your daily life and functioning is affected
- Any past personal or family history of mental health conditions
- Any current or past mental health treatment
- Alcohol and substance use
- Any other mental health conditions, especially anxiety
- Whether you have had mania or hypomania (elevated mood, grandiosity, talkativeness, decreased need for sleep, risk-taking). This matters because starting an antidepressant may trigger a manic episode.
- Any psychotic symptoms

**In older adults:** Symptoms can be subtly different. Older people are less likely to show low mood, worthlessness, or guilt, and more likely to show cognitive changes, physical symptoms, sleep disturbance, agitation, and general loss of interest.

### Possible Causes to Consider

**Medications that may contribute to depression:**

- Anticonvulsants
- Corticosteroids
- Isotretinoin (linked with mood changes, but it has not been proven to cause depression; your doctor will monitor your mood)
- Oral contraceptives
- Some beta-blockers

**Medical conditions that may contribute:**

- Hypothyroidism
- Parkinson's disease
- Sleep disturbance
- Cardiovascular disease
- Diabetes
- Dementia
- Any physical illness that is life-threatening or causes pain or disability

### Investigations

Many people will not need tests. If a physical cause is suspected, your doctor may order blood tests such as full blood count (FBC), thyroid function (TSH), ferritin, liver function, kidney function (creatinine), sodium, potassium, vitamin B12, and folate.

In adults aged over 45 years with sudden-onset depression, your doctor may consider other diagnoses.

**Cognitive screening** may be recommended for patients aged 60 years or older (from age 55 for Māori patients).

## Management

### If You Are at Risk of Harm to Yourself

If you are thinking about suicide, follow the steps in the red flag section above. If you are at significant risk of harming yourself or someone else, call 111 or go to the emergency department. Your doctor may request an acute mental health assessment at hospital.

### Stepped Care — Starting with the Least Intrusive Approach

Your doctor will choose treatment to suit your needs, preferences, and any other health conditions. The general approach is to start with the most effective, least intrusive options first.

### For Everyone — Regardless of Severity

Your doctor may recommend:

**Lifestyle changes:**

- Regular physical activity. Structured group exercise plans have been shown to improve symptoms.
- Healthy eating
- Good sleep habits
- Stopping smoking
- Avoiding recreational drugs
- Daily planning: set achievable goals, keep a regular rising and bed time, include one enjoyable activity each day, and add some achievable exercise

**Psychological support:**

- Counselling or peer support
- Brief intervention therapy through your GP practice or funded programmes
- Online therapy options such as [Just a Thought](https://www.justathought.co.nz) (free CBT courses), [Beating the Blues](https://beatingtheblues.co.nz) (needs a password from your GP), [Back from the Bluez](https://www.cci.health.wa.gov.au/Resources/Looking-After-Yourself/Depression), or [My Journal](https://www.smallsteps.org.nz)

**Engagement and connection:**

- Reconnect with family, friends, and community
- Consider volunteering, men's sheds, group exercise, or local friendship groups
- Return to paid or voluntary work when possible. Regular, meaningful work is strongly associated with improved mental health.

### Mild Depression (PHQ-9: 10–14)

- There is no benefit from antidepressant medicines in mild depression.
- Your doctor may offer brief intervention therapy or other therapies, such as the online options above.
- Your doctor will arrange a follow-up in 2–4 weeks and repeat the PHQ-9.
- If you are improving, care continues as usual with your doctor.
- If there is no improvement, your doctor may step up or change the measures taken so far, and repeat the PHQ-9 after 4–6 weeks.
- If still no improvement, your doctor will manage as moderate depression.

### Moderate Depression (PHQ-9: 15–19)

Your doctor may consider:

1. **Brief intervention therapy first**: counselling, psychological therapy, or work-based EAP programmes.
2. **Antidepressants** as a second option, or in addition to therapy. Your doctor may prefer to defer this decision to a follow-up appointment rather than prescribing at the first visit.

Regular follow-up, by phone and in person, supports good outcomes whatever the treatment.

**If antidepressants are started:**

*First-line: SSRIs:*

All SSRIs are very similar in effectiveness and side effects. Your doctor will choose based on your circumstances:

- For women who could become pregnant, escitalopram and sertraline are safer and easier to stop.
- Citalopram has a lower chance of drug interactions for most people. Like all SSRIs, it has been linked with dose-related QT prolongation (a heart rhythm change), so the maximum dose should not be exceeded.
- A medicine that worked well for you before may be the first choice.

| Medication | Starting dose (days 1–4) | Full dose | Maximum daily dose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sertraline | 25 mg | 50 mg/day | 200 mg |
| Escitalopram | 5 mg | 10 mg/day | 20 mg (10 mg if aged over 65) |
| Citalopram | 10 mg | 20 mg/day | 40 mg (20 mg if aged over 65) |
| Fluoxetine | 10 mg | 20 mg/day | 60 mg |
| Paroxetine | 10 mg | 20 mg/day | 50 mg |

Note: Fluoxetine and paroxetine carry a higher risk of drug interactions and are not recommended first-line. Paroxetine has a higher rate of discontinuation syndrome.

*Second-line:*

- Venlafaxine XR
- Mirtazapine

**In older adults:** Start at half the normal dose and increase slowly to a full adult dose.

**Monitoring if antidepressants are started:**

- **1–2 weeks:** Your doctor will check in (by phone, text, or in person) to repeat the PHQ-9, encourage adherence, and address any side effects. The medicine starts at half dose for the first 4 days, then full dose if tolerated. If not tolerated, your doctor may switch medicines. Suicide risk is monitored closely at this stage.
- **2–4 weeks:** If only a partial response, the dose may be increased. If there is little or no response, or side effects you cannot tolerate, your doctor may change the antidepressant, or change to or add a psychological therapy.
- **4–6 weeks:** PHQ-9 repeated. If responding, continue. If not, the dose may be increased, the medication changed, or a psychological therapy added.
- **6–8 weeks:** If no improvement, a different antidepressant may be tried.
- **After 8–10 weeks of no improvement:** Your doctor may seek specialist advice or refer for a non-urgent specialist mental health assessment.

Continue antidepressants for at least **6 months after remission** to reduce the risk of relapse.

### Severe Depression (PHQ-9: 20+)

Your doctor may consider combination therapy: an antidepressant together with a structured psychological therapy such as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT).

The same antidepressant options and monitoring schedule apply as for moderate depression.

If there is no response after trialling two treatments (e.g., two different antidepressants in a row), or if there is clinical concern at any time, your doctor will refer for specialist mental health assessment.

## After Initial Treatment — Ongoing Monitoring

Your doctor will monitor for potential complications of antidepressant treatment:

**Stopping medication:**

- Antidepressants should be reduced gradually over 4 weeks (not needed for fluoxetine). Paroxetine and venlafaxine need a longer taper due to their shorter half-life.
- You may notice some symptoms while reducing or stopping. These are usually mild and self-limiting, but can be severe, particularly if the medicine is stopped suddenly. Tell your doctor if they are hard to bear.

**Hyponatraemia (low sodium):**

- Your doctor may check sodium levels at the start, at 2 weeks, and at 12 weeks, especially if you are older or frail, female, have low body weight or reduced kidney function, or take other medicines that lower sodium, such as thiazide diuretics.
- If sodium is mildly low (130–135 mmol/L) without symptoms such as dizziness, nausea, lengthy confusion, cramps, or seizures, it may be possible to continue treatment with close monitoring. If sodium is less than 130 mmol/L, the medication will be stopped.

**Serotonin syndrome:**

- This may occur when starting or increasing a serotonergic medicine, or when taking two such medicines together.
- Besides antidepressants, St John's wort, tramadol, pethidine, triptans, lithium, and some recreational drugs can cause it. MAOI antidepressants carry the greatest risk.
- Symptoms include confusion, anxiety, agitation, high blood pressure, high temperature, diarrhoea, sweating, fast heart rate, tremors, overactive reflexes, or muscle stiffness. They usually start within hours to days of starting or increasing the medicine.
- Serotonin syndrome is potentially life-threatening. If you have these symptoms, call 111 or go to the emergency department. Treatment is stopping the medication and supportive care.

**Sexual dysfunction:**

- A common complaint while on antidepressants, which can disrupt arousal and orgasm in men and women.
- Tell your doctor. Strategies include dose reduction, changing the timing of doses or planned breaks, or switching to an antidepressant with lower rates of sexual side effects, such as mirtazapine or bupropion. Viagra or Cialis may help men if medically reasonable.

**QT prolongation:**

- Some antidepressants (citalopram, escitalopram, and tricyclic antidepressants) can affect heart rhythm. Your doctor may arrange an ECG (heart tracing) before starting one of these, or before starting any antidepressant if you have risk factors.
- Risk factors include other medicines that affect the QT interval, heart disease, a history of fainting, a very slow heart rate, kidney failure, low potassium, magnesium, or calcium, and a personal or family history of long QT syndrome or sudden cardiac death.
- If you have a prolonged QT interval or risk factors, your doctor may avoid these antidepressants, repeat ECGs and electrolyte checks, and seek heart specialist advice.

**Bleeding risk:**

- SSRIs are associated with an increased risk of bleeding. If you take NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen, naproxen) or aspirin, your doctor may add a medicine to protect the stomach.
- The bleeding risk is also higher with blood thinners such as warfarin or apixaban.

**Overdose risk:**

- If there is a significant risk of suicide, your doctor will take overdose risk into account when choosing a medicine. Venlafaxine carries a greater risk of death in overdose than other equally effective antidepressants. The greatest risk in overdose is with tricyclic antidepressants.

### Special Considerations for Older Adults

- Always combine antidepressant treatment with non-drug approaches (active support and psychological therapy).
- An SSRI is the first choice antidepressant in older adults.
- In frail older people, start at half the adult dose and increase gradually.
- Monitor carefully for adverse effects and drug interactions, especially with multiple medications. Forgetfulness can make many medicines harder to take as prescribed.
- Antidepressants increase the risk of falls in older adults through sedation, impaired balance, orthostatic hypotension, sleep disturbance, and heart rhythm problems. SSRIs carry a similar falls risk to tricyclic antidepressants. Your doctor should check regularly for sedation, sleep changes, or a change in walking.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor will refer you for specialist mental health assessment, or seek specialist advice, if:

- You are at significant risk of harm to yourself or others. This needs an acute assessment: call 111 or go to the emergency department.
- There is no response after trialling two treatments
- There is clinical concern at any point
- There is no improvement after 8–10 weeks of treatment
