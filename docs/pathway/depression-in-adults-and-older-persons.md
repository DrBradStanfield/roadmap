# Depression in Adults and Older Persons

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of depression in adults aged 18 years and over.

---

## Red Flag — Seek Urgent Help

**Suicidality** is a red flag. If you are having thoughts of ending your life, please seek help immediately. Call or text **1737** (free, 24/7) or go to your nearest emergency department.

---

## Background

Depression is common and treatable. Key facts:

- Depression affects about 6% of New Zealanders in any 12-month period, and about 18% of people who attend general practice.
- Lifetime prevalence is around 16%.
- It is 2–3 times more common in people with chronic physical health conditions.
- Depression is often under-detected and under-treated in older people. It is never a normal consequence of ageing.
- Effective treatments exist and recovery is expected.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess depression by looking at:

### Screening

Your doctor may use two simple questions (the PHQ-2):

1. During the past month, have you often felt down, depressed, or hopeless?
2. During the past month, have you had little interest or pleasure in doing things?

If the answer to either is yes, they may also ask: *Would you like help with this?*

If depression is suspected, your doctor will use the **PHQ-9 questionnaire** to gauge severity:

| PHQ-9 score | Severity |
|---|---|
| 10–14 | Mild depression |
| 15–19 | Moderate depression |
| 20+ | Severe depression |

The PHQ-9 is a guide to support clinical judgement, not a diagnosis on its own.

### Targeted Screening

Your doctor may screen more actively if you have:

- Clinical suspicion based on what you describe
- Drug or alcohol misuse
- Pregnancy or recent birth
- Multiple unexplained physical symptoms
- Chronic physical health problems (especially diabetes or chronic pain)

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Suicidal thoughts
- Severity and duration of symptoms
- How much your daily life and functioning is affected
- Any past personal or family history of mental health conditions
- Any current or past mental health treatment
- Alcohol and substance use
- Any other mental health conditions, especially anxiety
- Whether you have experienced mania or hypomania (elevated mood, decreased need for sleep, grandiosity, risk-taking) — this is important as it affects treatment choices

**In older adults:** Symptoms may look different — less sadness and more cognitive changes, somatic complaints, sleep disturbance, agitation, and general loss of interest.

### Possible Causes to Consider

**Medications that may contribute to depression:**

- Anticonvulsants
- Corticosteroids
- Isotretinoin
- Oral contraceptives
- Some beta-blockers

**Medical conditions that may contribute:**

- Hypothyroidism
- Parkinson's disease
- Sleep disturbance
- Cardiovascular disease
- Diabetes
- Dementia
- Any painful, disabling, or life-threatening illness

### Investigations

Your doctor may order blood tests if a physical cause is suspected — for example, full blood count (FBC), thyroid function (TSH), ferritin, liver function, kidney function, electrolytes, vitamin B12, and folate.

In adults aged over 45 years with sudden-onset depression, other diagnoses will also be considered.

**Cognitive screening** may be recommended for patients aged 60 years or older (from age 55 for Māori patients).

## Management

### If You Are at Risk of Harm to Yourself

If you are suicidal or at significant risk, your doctor will arrange urgent mental health assessment immediately. Do not wait.

### Stepped Care — Starting with the Least Intrusive Approach

Your doctor will tailor treatment to your needs, preferences, and any other health conditions. The general approach is to start with the most effective, least intrusive options first.

### For Everyone — Regardless of Severity

Your doctor may recommend:

**Lifestyle changes:**

- Regular physical activity — structured group exercise has been shown to improve symptoms
- Healthy eating
- Good sleep habits
- Stopping smoking
- Avoiding recreational drugs and alcohol
- Daily planning: set achievable goals, maintain a regular rising and bed time, include one enjoyable activity each day

**Psychological support:**

- Counselling, therapy (including cognitive behavioural therapy), or peer support
- Brief intervention therapy through your GP practice or funded programmes
- Online therapy options such as [Just a Thought](https://www.justathought.co.nz) (free CBT courses), [Beating the Blues](https://beatingtheblues.co.nz), [Back from the Bluez](https://www.cci.health.wa.gov.au/Resources/Looking-After-Yourself/Depression), or [My Journal](https://www.smallsteps.org.nz)

**Engagement and connection:**

- Reconnect with family, friends, and community
- Consider volunteering, group exercise, or local friendship groups
- Return to paid or voluntary work when possible — meaningful work is strongly associated with improved mental health

### Mild Depression (PHQ-9: 10–14)

- Antidepressant medications are **not** recommended for mild depression.
- Your doctor will arrange a follow-up in 2–4 weeks and repeat the PHQ-9.
- If there is no improvement, treatment will be intensified or changed, and reassessed again after 4–6 weeks.
- If still no improvement, your doctor will manage as moderate depression.

### Moderate Depression (PHQ-9: 15–19)

Your doctor may consider:

1. **Brief intervention therapy first** — counselling, psychological therapy, or work-based EAP programmes.
2. **Antidepressants** as a second option, or in addition to therapy. Your doctor may prefer to defer this decision to a follow-up appointment rather than prescribing at the first visit.

**If antidepressants are started:**

*First-line — SSRIs:*

All SSRIs are similar in effectiveness. Your doctor will choose based on your individual circumstances.

| Medication | Starting dose (days 1–4) | Full dose | Maximum daily dose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sertraline | 25 mg | 50 mg/day | 200 mg |
| Escitalopram | 5 mg | 10 mg/day | 20 mg (10 mg if aged over 65) |
| Citalopram | 10 mg | 20 mg/day | 40 mg (20 mg if aged over 65) |
| Fluoxetine | 10 mg | 20 mg/day | 60 mg |
| Paroxetine | 10 mg | 20 mg/day | 50 mg |

Note: Fluoxetine and paroxetine carry a higher risk of drug interactions. Paroxetine has a higher rate of discontinuation syndrome. Your doctor will discuss which option suits you best.

*Second-line:*

- Venlafaxine XR
- Mirtazapine

**In older adults:** Start at half the normal dose and increase slowly.

**Monitoring if antidepressants are started:**

- **1–2 weeks:** Your doctor will check in (by phone, text, or in person) to assess severity, encourage adherence, and address any side effects. Suicide risk is monitored closely at this stage.
- **2–4 weeks:** If only a partial response, the dose may be increased.
- **4–6 weeks:** PHQ-9 repeated. If responding, continue. If not, the dose may be increased, or the medication changed.
- **6–8 weeks:** If no improvement, a different antidepressant may be tried.
- **After 8–10 weeks of no improvement:** Your doctor will refer for specialist mental health assessment.

Continue antidepressants for at least **6 months after remission** to reduce the risk of relapse.

### Severe Depression (PHQ-9: 20+)

Your doctor may consider combination therapy — an antidepressant together with a structured psychological therapy such as cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT).

The same antidepressant options and monitoring schedule apply as for moderate depression.

If there is no response after trialling two treatments (e.g., two different antidepressants), or if there is clinical concern at any time, your doctor will refer for specialist mental health assessment.

## After Initial Treatment — Ongoing Monitoring

Your doctor will monitor for potential complications of antidepressant treatment:

**Stopping medication:**

- Antidepressants should be tapered gradually over at least 4 weeks (not usually needed for fluoxetine). Paroxetine and venlafaxine (venlafaxine is an SNRI, not an SSRI, but follows the same tapering principles) need a longer taper due to their shorter half-life.
- You may experience some withdrawal-type symptoms when reducing — these are usually mild and self-limiting, but tell your doctor if they are severe.

**Hyponatraemia (low sodium):**

- Your doctor may check sodium levels at initiation, 2 weeks, and 12 weeks — especially if you are older, have low body weight, reduced kidney function, or take thiazide diuretics.
- Mild hyponatraemia (sodium 130–135 mmol/L) may be monitored closely; if sodium falls below 130 mmol/L, the medication will be stopped.

**Serotonin syndrome:**

- A rare but potentially serious condition that can occur when starting or increasing a serotonergic medication, or combining two such drugs.
- Symptoms include confusion, anxiety, agitation, high blood pressure, high temperature, diarrhoea, sweating, fast heart rate, tremors, or muscle stiffness.
- Treatment is stopping the medication and supportive care. Tell your doctor immediately if you experience these symptoms.

**Sexual dysfunction:**

- A common side effect of antidepressants that often persists throughout the course of treatment, not just in the early weeks. It is a leading cause of non-adherence. Tell your doctor — strategies include dose reduction, changing the timing of the dose, or switching to a different antidepressant (e.g., mirtazapine or bupropion, which have lower rates of sexual side effects).

**QT prolongation:**

- Some antidepressants (especially citalopram, escitalopram, and tricyclic antidepressants) can affect heart rhythm. Your doctor may arrange an ECG before starting treatment if you have relevant risk factors (e.g., heart disease, other QT-prolonging medications, electrolyte abnormalities).

**Bleeding risk:**

- SSRIs reduce platelet serotonin uptake, which can impair platelet function and increase bleeding risk — particularly gastrointestinal bleeding. This risk is significantly increased when SSRIs are taken together with NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen, naproxen) or anticoagulants (e.g., warfarin, apixaban). Your doctor will advise caution with concurrent NSAID use.

**Overdose risk:**

- If there is a significant risk of suicide, your doctor will take into account that venlafaxine and tricyclic antidepressants carry a higher risk of death in overdose compared with SSRIs. This will influence medication choice.

### Special Considerations for Older Adults

- Always combine antidepressant treatment with non-drug approaches (active support and psychological therapy).
- An SSRI is the first choice antidepressant in older adults.
- Start at half the adult dose and increase gradually.
- Monitor carefully for adverse effects and drug interactions, especially with multiple medications.
- Antidepressants increase the risk of falls in older adults through sedation, impaired balance, orthostatic hypotension, and sleep disturbance. Your doctor will monitor this.

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor will refer you for specialist mental health assessment if:

- You are at significant risk of harm to yourself or others (urgent referral)
- There is no response after trialling two treatments
- There is clinical concern at any point
- There is no improvement after 8–10 weeks of treatment
