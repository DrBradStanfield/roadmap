# Hypertension in Pregnancy and Postpartum

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of high blood pressure during pregnancy and after birth, including pre-eclampsia and related conditions.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Call an ambulance (111) if any of the following occur during pregnancy or postpartum:

- New onset of high blood pressure after 20 weeks of pregnancy
- Severe hypertension — systolic pressure 160 mmHg or greater, or diastolic pressure 110 mmHg or greater
- Seizures (suspected eclampsia)
- Symptoms of pre-eclampsia (see below) combined with high blood pressure

**Symptoms of pre-eclampsia — contact your doctor or midwife urgently if you experience:**
- Severe headache
- Visual disturbances (blurring or flashing lights)
- Right upper quadrant or epigastric (upper stomach) pain
- Shortness of breath or retrosternal pressure/pain
- Nausea or vomiting
- Sudden swelling of the face, hands, or feet

## Background

### What is hypertension in pregnancy?

High blood pressure in pregnancy is defined as a systolic blood pressure (SBP) of 140 mmHg or greater, or diastolic blood pressure (DBP) of 90 mmHg or greater, measured on two or more occasions at least 4 hours apart.

### Types of hypertensive disorders in pregnancy

- **Gestational hypertension** — new onset high blood pressure after 20 weeks of pregnancy, with no features of pre-eclampsia. These patients need close monitoring for development of pre-eclampsia.
- **Pre-eclampsia** — new onset high blood pressure after 20 weeks, combined with involvement of one or more body systems. Signs include proteinuria (protein in urine), deranged liver function, blood count abnormalities, impaired kidney function, or fetal growth restriction.
- **HELLP syndrome** — a severe form of pre-eclampsia. The name stands for **H**aemolysis (breakdown of red blood cells), **E**levated **L**iver enzymes, **L**ow **P**latelet count.
- **Eclampsia** — new onset seizures in association with pre-eclampsia.
- **Chronic (pre-existing) hypertension** — high blood pressure confirmed before conception or before 20 weeks of pregnancy. Associated with a higher risk of pre-eclampsia, preterm birth, and fetal growth restriction.

People who have had pre-eclampsia are at increased risk of cardiovascular disease later in life.

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you at the pre-conception consult and at every antenatal visit. This involves:

### Risk factors your doctor will ask about

**Major risk factors for pre-eclampsia:**
- Pre-existing hypertension (especially poorly controlled)
- Antiphospholipid syndrome
- Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) or lupus nephritis
- Previous pre-eclampsia (especially requiring delivery before 37 weeks)
- Previous HELLP syndrome
- Pre-existing type 1 or 2 diabetes
- Kidney disease
- Oocyte donation or frozen embryo transfer

**Moderate risk factors:**
- Multiple pregnancy (twins or more)
- First-degree relative (mother or sister) with pre-eclampsia
- BMI 35 or greater
- First pregnancy at younger than 18 years or 40 years or older
- Pregnancy interval of 10 years or more
- Assisted reproductive technology or sperm donation
- Blood pressure of 130/80 or higher at the first antenatal visit
- African or Indian ethnicity

### Blood pressure checks

Blood pressure is checked at every antenatal visit. If elevated, readings are repeated 15 minutes apart. Severe hypertension (160/110 or higher) requires urgent assessment.

### Physical examination

If hypertension or symptoms of pre-eclampsia are present, your doctor will typically:
- Perform a cardiovascular examination
- Check weight and look for peripheral swelling
- Check reflexes
- Check fetal fundal height, movements, and heart rate (after 20 weeks)
- Perform a urine dipstick test for protein

### Blood tests (if hypertension or pre-eclampsia suspected)

Your doctor may arrange urgent blood tests including:
- Full blood count (FBC)
- Electrolytes and creatinine
- Liver function tests (LFT)
- Coagulation screen

## Management

This is a complex condition managed in partnership between your GP, midwife, and specialist obstetric team. The information below describes what your doctor may consider — all decisions are made by your medical team.

### Emergency situations

**Eclampsia (seizures):**
Your doctor will call an ambulance immediately and provide emergency management, including:
- Airway protection and ABCDE emergency care
- Magnesium sulphate to control or prevent further seizures (2.5 g into each buttock if actively seizing, total 5 g)
- If magnesium is unavailable: diazepam or midazolam may be considered
- Antihypertensive medication to lower blood pressure below 160/110 mmHg

**Pre-eclampsia or HELLP:**
Your doctor will seek specialist obstetric advice immediately and may give antihypertensive medication to lower blood pressure below 160/110 mmHg, and arrange urgent hospital transfer by ambulance.

**Acute antihypertensive medications your doctor may consider:**

- **Nifedipine (immediate release):** 10 mg oral, repeated after 30–45 minutes if needed (maximum 80 mg/day). Onset 30–45 minutes.
- **Labetalol (IV):** Avoid in asthma or uncontrolled heart failure. 20 mg IV over 2 minutes initially, repeated with 40–80 mg every 10 minutes (maximum 300 mg). Onset 5 minutes.
- **Hydralazine (IV):** 5–10 mg IV over 3–10 minutes (5 mg if fetal compromise), repeated every 20 minutes (maximum 30 mg). Onset 20 minutes.

**Magnesium sulphate** may be given to prevent eclamptic seizures in severe pre-eclampsia, as advised by the specialist team.

---

### Pre-conception (planning a pregnancy)

If you have a history of pre-eclampsia, hypertension in pregnancy, or pre-existing hypertension, your doctor will offer pre-conception counselling and may refer you to a specialist before you conceive.

If you are already on blood pressure medication, your doctor will review whether your current medication is safe in pregnancy. Medications to potentially switch away from before conception include ACE inhibitors, angiotensin II receptor blockers (ARBs), diuretics, and atenolol.

**Antihypertensive medications considered safe in pregnancy that your doctor may consider:**

- **Labetalol** (pregnancy category – low risk): Starting dose 100 mg twice daily with food. Can be increased up to 400 mg twice daily (maximum 2.4 g/day in divided doses). Avoid in asthma.
- **Nifedipine slow-release** (pregnancy category – low risk; unapproved use before 20 weeks): Slow-release 10–20 mg twice daily, or long-acting 30–60 mg once daily. Sublingual nifedipine is not recommended.
- **Methyldopa** (pregnancy category – compatible): Starting dose 250 mg three times daily, up to 500 mg four times daily (maximum 3 g/day). Can affect mood — may be best avoided with a history of depression. Liver function tests monitored monthly. Generally replaced with an alternative agent after delivery.

Your doctor will aim for a blood pressure of 135/85 mmHg or lower throughout pregnancy.

---

### During pregnancy

All patients with hypertension in pregnancy are managed in a shared care model with a specialist obstetric team and lead maternity carer (LMC/midwife).

**Prevention of pre-eclampsia — your doctor may recommend:**

If you have major risk factors for pre-eclampsia:
- **Aspirin 100 mg** (enteric coated) at night, starting from 12 weeks of pregnancy and continuing until around 36 weeks
- **Calcium carbonate 1.25 g twice daily with food** (if dietary calcium intake is low — less than 1 g elemental calcium per day)
- **Vitamin D** (colecalciferol 1.25 mg monthly) if there are risk factors for low vitamin D levels

If you have multiple moderate risk factors, your doctor may seek specialist advice about whether aspirin is indicated.

**Severe hypertension (160/110 or higher at any stage of pregnancy):**
Your doctor will give antihypertensive medication for acute blood pressure lowering and arrange urgent specialist obstetric assessment.

**Chronic hypertension in pregnancy:**
Your doctor will ensure blood pressure medications are safe in pregnancy, aim for a target of 135/85 mmHg, monitor closely, and refer to a specialist if blood pressure is not at target.

**Gestational hypertension:**
Your doctor will seek specialist obstetric advice, check bloods and urine protein, consider starting antihypertensive medication, and arrange urgent outpatient obstetric review. You will be educated about the symptoms of pre-eclampsia to watch for.

---

### Postpartum (after birth)

Blood pressure monitoring continues after delivery. Pre-eclampsia can develop postpartum, including in women who had no complications during pregnancy.

**Your doctor will:**
- Monitor blood pressure and arrange obstetric advice if pre-eclampsia develops postpartum
- Check kidney function and urine protein at 6 weeks postpartum
- Continue antihypertensive medication as needed, reducing gradually if blood pressure improves
- Arrange cardiovascular risk assessment 3–6 months postpartum and regularly thereafter

**Postpartum antihypertensive medications your doctor may consider:**

ACE inhibitors, beta blockers, and calcium channel blockers are all considered safe in breastfeeding. Methyldopa is generally avoided postpartum unless advised by a specialist.

**Future pregnancy planning:**

Your doctor will discuss the increased risk of recurrent gestational hypertension or pre-eclampsia in future pregnancies, as well as longer-term cardiovascular, kidney, and cerebrovascular risks associated with a history of pre-eclampsia.
