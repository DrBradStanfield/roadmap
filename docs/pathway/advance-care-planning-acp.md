# Advance Care Planning (ACP)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway explains what advance care planning is, who should consider it, and what the process typically involves. ACP is a discussion-based process best guided by your doctor or primary care team.

---

## What is Advance Care Planning?

Advance care planning (ACP) is a shared discussion and planning process about future health care. It enables you and your family (whānau) to think about what matters to you and document your values and wishes — before a health crisis occurs.

The key insight: even without formal paperwork, having these conversations is valuable. It prepares everyone for future health care encounters and decision-making.

ACP is relevant across a person's lifetime — whether you are well, in declining health, or approaching the end of life.

ACP may include:

- A serious illness conversation
- Appointing an enduring power of attorney (EPA)
- Writing and updating an advance care plan
- Determining shared goals of care
- Writing advance directives (AD)

---

## Who Should Consider ACP?

Your doctor may consider raising ACP if you:

- Are older than 65 years — whether well, unwell, frail, or needing home care
- Are older than 55 years with greater risk of poor health outcomes or lower life expectancy (e.g., Māori and Pacific peoples, or those living in deprivation)
- Are planning major surgery
- Have a chronic disease that is probably life-limiting
- Have a malignancy, especially if incurable
- Have early cognitive loss
- Have enquired about palliative care or expressed a wish to forego life-sustaining treatment
- Have concerns about who will advocate for you if you become incapacitated
- Already have an existing advance care plan that is due for review
- May be nearing the end of life

Note: Any person who is able to make and discuss decisions about their future healthcare should consider advance care planning — it is not only for older or seriously ill people.

---

## Assessment: Capacity to Participate

Your doctor will consider your ability to participate in ACP. This means assessing whether you can:

- Understand the information relevant to the decision
- Retain that information
- Use or weigh that information when making a decision
- Communicate your decision (by talking, sign language, or any other means)

If there are concerns about capacity, your doctor will liaise with relevant specialists before proceeding.

**For children under 16:** ACP discussions are generally best managed by the wider specialist care team, unless the family specifically requests that the community team be involved.

**Timing:** Immediately after a distressing health event or receiving distressing news may not be the right time to begin ACP. Your doctor may suggest waiting until you have had time to absorb the situation, then booking a follow-up appointment to start the conversation.

---

## Starting the Conversation

If your doctor initiates a serious illness conversation, they may ask questions such as:

1. "What are your priorities if your health does get worse?"
2. "What worries you when you think about your health changing?"
3. "What helps you through the tough times?"
4. "What abilities are so important to you that you can't imagine living without them?"
5. "If your health does get worse, how much are you willing to go through for the possibility of more time?"
6. "How much does your family/whānau know about what is most important to you?"

These questions are designed to open discussion — not to pressure you into any particular decision.

## Key ACP Documents

### 1. Advance Care Plan

An advance care plan is a document that records your values, wishes, and preferences for future care. It belongs to you and is voluntary.

New Zealand's main advance care plan is **My Advance Care Plan** — it can be completed digitally or on paper and covers what matters to you, your goals of care, and any specific treatment wishes.

Much of the plan (sections 1–5 and 7) contains non-clinical personal information and can be discussed with whānau, friends, or health workers. Your doctor will review the entire plan and ensure the goals-of-care section is signed before the plan is shared.

The advance care plan should be reviewed annually or whenever your health situation changes significantly.

### 2. Advance Directive (AD)

An advance directive is a legal document in which you indicate your consent to, or refusal of, specific future medical treatments — to take effect only when you are no longer able to make decisions yourself. It is sometimes referred to as a "living will."

There are two types:

- **Positive AD:** Indicates a preference for or consent to a particular treatment. These do not legally oblige a healthcare provider to provide the treatment — it must also be medically appropriate.
- **Negative AD (anticipatory refusal):** Indicates a refusal of a specific treatment. Where valid, this has the same authority as a current refusal of treatment and is legally binding.

For an advance directive to be valid:
- It must be created by a person with mental capacity to make the directive
- It must be made freely and without undue influence
- The person must intend it to apply to the current circumstances
- The person must understand the benefits, risks, and consequences of consenting to or refusing the specific treatment

Your doctor may suggest using Section 6 of My Advance Care Plan to record treatment wishes — this is a lower-cost alternative to a formal legal advance directive.

### 3. Enduring Power of Attorney (EPA)

An EPA gives a trusted person (your "attorney") the legal authority to make decisions on your behalf if you lose mental capacity.

There are two types:

- **EPA Welfare:** Covers decisions about your personal care and welfare. Not activated until you lose capacity.
- **EPA Property:** Covers decisions about your property and finances. May be activated immediately or on loss of capacity.

Important points:
- An EPA is a legal document arranged through a solicitor or trust company. Your doctor can help identify the need for an EPA and provide the certificate to activate it.
- The attorney for an activated EPA Welfare cannot refuse any standard medical treatment intended to save your life or prevent serious damage to your health.
- If you do not have an EPA, the advance care plan provides a space to name people you would like your healthcare team to involve in decisions — but this does not give them legal authority.

For information and forms, see:
- [Healthify He Puna Waiora — Enduring Power of Attorney](https://healthify.nz/health-a-z/e/enduring-power-of-attorney)
- [Public Trust — What is an Enduring Power of Attorney?](https://www.publictrust.co.nz/personal/enduring-power-of-attorney)

### 4. Shared Goals of Care

A shared goals of care plan is developed between you, your whānau, and your health providers during an episode of care (e.g., a hospital admission or aged residential care). It identifies what the goal of your care is if your condition were to deteriorate.

**Goals of care levels:**

- **Goal A:** Curative or restorative — treatment aims to prolong life. CPR is appropriate.
- **Goal B:** Curative or restorative — treatment aims to prolong life and enhance quality. No CPR. ICU-level care is appropriate.
- **Goal C:** Primarily improving quality of life — treatment focuses on symptom control and well-being. No CPR. ICU care unlikely to be appropriate.
- **Goal D:** Comfort while dying — treatment aims to alleviate suffering. No CPR or ICU referral.

These goals focus on what matters to you — your doctor listens to your priorities and recommends care based on both your wishes and what treatments will best support those goals.

**About CPR:** CPR is chest compressions and rescue breaths. It pushes blood around the body as a bridging measure while the cause of cardiac arrest is treated. CPR works best when the heart stops suddenly from a potentially reversible event (e.g., a heart attack). When the heart finally stops as part of the dying process, CPR is generally not effective. Discussions about CPR are part of shared goals of care planning.

## Assisted Dying and ACP

If you raise the subject of assisted dying, your doctor can discuss it with you. However:

- Assisted dying cannot be requested through an advance directive or by an enduring power of attorney
- The patient must be mentally competent at the time of making the request — it cannot be made in advance
- This is because the End of Life Choice Act takes priority, requiring that the person requesting assisted dying is competent both when requesting it and at the time of the assisted death

You may still wish to record your preference for assisted dying in the free-text section of your advance care plan. This can contribute to end-of-life discussions with your whānau and inform decisions about the ceiling of treatment if you later lose capacity.

## Management: Completing and Sharing Your Plan

Your doctor will:

1. Work with you to complete your advance care plan, booking additional appointments if needed
2. Ensure any advance directive meets validity criteria
3. With your consent, share your completed plan with:
   - Your enduring power of attorney
   - A spouse, loved one, or trusted friend
   - Your hospital
   - Any private specialists involved in your care
4. Advise you to keep a readily accessible copy at home and let whānau and visiting health professionals (including paramedics) know where it is
5. Encourage you to update your plan annually or when your health situation changes

Other documents that may be part of your ACP:
- Shared care plan
- Emergency letter for ambulance services
- Hospice letter

If challenging conversations arise, your doctor may seek specialist ACP clinical support.

### Organ and Body Donation

If you have wishes about organ donation or body bequests, let your doctor know. Discuss any preferences in your advance care plan. Note that age and certain medical conditions may affect eligibility.

Your doctor may also encourage you to make a will as part of broader end-of-life planning.
