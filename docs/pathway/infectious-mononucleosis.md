# Infectious Mononucleosis (Glandular Fever)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of infectious mononucleosis (IM), commonly known as glandular fever.

---

## Background

Infectious mononucleosis is most commonly caused by **Epstein-Barr virus (EBV)** — accounting for 90% of cases. Other causes include CMV, toxoplasmosis, and acute HIV.

- EBV spreads through contact with infected saliva ("the kissing disease")
- Most common in the 15–25 age group
- Young children with EBV often have a mild, unrecognised illness
- Adolescents and adults typically develop a symptomatic illness
- 90% of adults are already seropositive for EBV (have had it in the past)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess for the following:

### Typical Features

- Fever
- Pharyngitis (sore throat), usually with exudate
- Lymphadenopathy — typically posterior cervical lymph nodes
- Fatigue
- Atypical lymphocytosis on blood film

### Less Common Features

- **Hepatomegaly and jaundice** — jaundice occurs in under 10% of young adults but ~30% of older patients; mild liver enzyme elevation is common
- **Rash** — can occur from the virus itself, or as a hypersensitivity reaction (not a true allergy) to beta-lactam antibiotics such as amoxicillin
- Haematological abnormalities, e.g. thrombocytopenia
- Splenomegaly and splenic rupture — rare
- Neurological complications (e.g. Guillain-Barré syndrome, encephalitis) — very rare

### Blood Tests

**In the 12–25 age group:** A clinical diagnosis is usually sufficient. If blood tests are needed:

- Start with **FBC only**. Lymphocytosis with atypical lymphocytes is strongly suggestive — further tests are unlikely to add much.
- EBV serology is more sensitive and specific than the older Paul Bunnell test, but is more expensive. Your doctor will consider it only if the diagnosis is uncertain or symptoms persist beyond 12 weeks.

**EBV serology is recommended if:**

- Diagnosis is uncertain
- Patient is pregnant, immunocompromised, or aged under 12 or over 25
- EBV antibody tests can distinguish between no prior exposure, past infection, and acute/recent infection

**In pregnant women:** An accurate diagnosis is important to exclude other causes (CMV, toxoplasmosis, HIV) that can cause adverse fetal outcomes.

---

## Management

Infectious mononucleosis is a self-limiting viral illness. There is no specific antiviral treatment. Your doctor will focus on symptom relief and safety advice.

### Symptom Relief

- **Analgesia** — paracetamol or ibuprofen for fever and sore throat pain
- **Adequate fluid intake**
- **Rest** — return to school, university, or work when feeling well; if fatigued, gradually increase normal activities

### What to Avoid

- **Amoxicillin** (and ampicillin) — avoid during the acute phase as it commonly causes a widespread rash in people with IM (this is a drug reaction, not a true penicillin allergy)
- **Alcohol** — avoid during the illness

### Expected Time Course

- Fever and sore throat typically resolve within **1 month**
- Fatigue may persist for **2–3 months**
- Chronic or persistent EBV infection lasting longer than 6 months is rare

### Exercise and Sport

Your doctor will advise caution about physical activity:

- Avoid strenuous exercise and contact sports for at least **3–4 weeks** from symptom onset due to the risk of splenic rupture
- Splenomegaly occurs in approximately 50–60% of patients in the first 2 weeks — splenic rupture itself is rare
- A **gradual return to exercise** is recommended
- The evidence base for exact return-to-sport timing is limited — your doctor will guide this based on your individual situation

### Post-Infectious Fatigue

Chronic fatigue syndrome following infectious mononucleosis is uncommon:

- Reported in approximately 7% of adolescents at 12 months post-illness
- Approximately 4% at 24 months

If fatigue is prolonged or severe, discuss with your doctor.

### Corticosteroids

Corticosteroids are **not** routinely recommended — they have not been shown to reduce complications or shorten illness duration. They may be used only in life-threatening situations such as acute upper airway obstruction.

---

## When to See Your Doctor

Seek medical review if you experience:

- Worsening or very severe sore throat (may need throat swab to rule out bacterial infection)
- Sudden severe abdominal pain (could indicate splenic rupture — seek emergency care)
- Difficulty breathing or stridor
- Jaundice (yellowing of the skin or eyes)
- Symptoms persisting beyond 3–4 weeks without improvement
- You are pregnant or immunocompromised and think you may have IM

> This information is a general guide. Always discuss your symptoms and test results with your doctor, who can tailor advice to your specific situation.
