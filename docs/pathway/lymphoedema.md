# Lymphoedema

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of lymphoedema — a progressive, chronic condition caused by accumulation of fluid in tissue spaces due to a blockage or defect in the lymphatic system.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Medical Care

See your doctor promptly if you develop:

- Signs of **cellulitis** (skin that is red, warm, swollen, or painful — particularly if spreading or associated with fever)

People with lymphoedema are at greater risk of developing cellulitis and should present for medical advice early if symptoms develop.

---

## Background

- Lymphoedema causes intermittent or persistent swelling, most often in a limb.
- **Early diagnosis and treatment** helps prognosis — recognising early warning signs is important.
- Lymphoedema increases the risk of tissue infection (e.g., cellulitis).
- **Primary lymphoedema** is caused by abnormal development of the lymphatic vessels. Swelling may appear at birth or develop during puberty, pregnancy, or adulthood.
- **Secondary lymphoedema** is most commonly caused by surgery, radiotherapy, or trauma, and can occur at any time after the causative event.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you by taking a history, examining the affected limb, and considering other possible causes of swelling.

### Risk Factors Your Doctor Will Ask About

- Previous trauma to the lymphatic system (surgery, radiotherapy, infection)
- Lymph node surgery — even removal of a single node creates a lifelong risk in the affected limb
- Immobility or obesity
- Advanced cancer
- Family history of chronic oedema
- Venous disease or inflammatory arthritis
- Recurrent cellulitis
- Filariasis (roundworm infection, most common in tropical regions)

### Early Warning Signs

- Transient swelling following exercise or physical activity
- Feelings of heaviness in the affected limb or body part
- Discomfort, tension, or a bursting/fullness sensation in the affected area
- Clothing or jewellery becoming tighter on the at-risk limb

### Symptoms to Discuss

Tell your doctor about:

- Swelling (including genital swelling if lower limb swelling is present — this can be a symptom of lymphoedema or of metastatic disease)
- Skin changes
- Heaviness or discomfort
- Restricted movement due to heaviness of the limb

It is common for people to feel embarrassed about swelling — your doctor will ask directly.

### Examination of the Affected Limb

Your doctor will assess:

- Shape and severity of swelling, including circumference compared with the unaffected limb
- Type of oedema — pitting or non-pitting
- Complicating factors: open wounds, ischaemia, skin lesions, cellulitis, venous insufficiency
- Lymphorrhoea (leakage of lymph fluid from the skin surface)
- Skin changes: wounds, hyperkeratosis, dryness, cracks, infection, bruising, thickened skin folds
- Stemmer's sign — if a fold of skin at the base of the second toe or middle finger cannot be pinched and raised, this may indicate chronic oedema

### Mobility and Function

Your doctor may assess:

- Effect of lymphoedema on daily activities
- Falls risk (particularly in older adults)
- Range of movement
- For arm swelling: ability to manage buttons, bra fastenings, hand grip and pincer movement
- For leg swelling: ability to get up from sitting, walking pattern, ability to lift each leg, posture, and safety of footwear

If you have significant leg swelling and drive, your doctor may assess whether it is safe for you to continue driving.

### BMI

Obesity is a contributing risk factor for lymphoedema developing or worsening, so your doctor will measure height and weight.

### Other Causes of Swelling

Your doctor will also consider other causes:

- **Unilateral (one-sided) swelling:** DVT or post-thrombotic syndrome, chronic venous insufficiency, arthritis, Baker's cyst, malignancy (pelvic or abdominal)
- **Symmetrical swelling:** heart failure, chronic venous insufficiency, dependent oedema, renal or liver dysfunction, hypoproteinaemia, hypothyroidism, drug-induced (e.g., calcium-channel blockers, steroids, NSAIDs), lipoedema

## Management

Early intervention and education can significantly reduce the risk of developing lymphoedema, and reduce its severity.

### Cellulitis Treatment

If you have signs of acute cellulitis, your doctor may consider:

- Urgent hospital assessment if there are signs of sepsis or you are systemically unwell
- A prolonged course of oral **flucloxacillin** 500 mg three to four times a day for 14 days (if systemically well)
- **Erythromycin** 400 mg four times daily for 14 days (if penicillin allergy)
- If you have more than 2 episodes of cellulitis per year, your doctor may discuss prophylactic antibiotics with an infectious diseases specialist

### Skin Care

Your doctor will advise:

- Apply a moisturiser at least once a day — healthy skin acts as a barrier to infection
- Avoid constrictions on the affected limb (jewellery, tight clothing)
- Use insect repellent and high-SPF sunscreen
- Clean and dry feet daily
- Protect skin from injury; promptly treat any wound or infection (including fungal)

### Physical Activity

Physical activity is encouraged. Your doctor may refer you for exercise support. Avoid strenuous activity in hot weather. Hot baths, spas, and saunas may worsen swelling.

### Weight Management

If your BMI is above 30, your doctor will discuss weight management — excess body weight can slow lymphatic and venous flow.

### Compression Garments

- Correct fit is essential — incorrect fitting can worsen lymphoedema
- Decongestion therapy should be completed first, and the garment measured by a lymphoedema therapist
- The benefit of compression garments for air travel or long road trips (4+ hours) is uncertain and is assessed individually

### Precautions for At-Risk Limbs

Your doctor will advise you to avoid — where possible — blood pressure readings, injections, and IV drips on the at-risk or affected limb. If this is unavoidable, discuss risks, benefits, and alternatives with the treating clinician.

### Breast Cancer

If you have breast cancer, lymphoedema information and potential funding for lymphoedema therapy may be available through the Breast Cancer Foundation NZ (for those with a BRCA diagnosis within the last 2 years).

### Diuretics

Diuretics can partially treat multifactorial leg swelling (e.g., lymphovenous oedema) but are ineffective in isolated lymphoedema.

### When Your Doctor May Request Specialist Input

Your doctor may refer you for **lymphoedema therapy** if:

- Swelling is related to cancer treatment (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, or node resection surgery)
- You have primary lymphoedema
- Lymphoedema is developing or worsening

Your doctor may also request:

- Specialist wound management nursing (if established lower limb lymphoedema with complicating factors such as open wounds, ischaemia, skin lesions, lymphorrhoea, cellulitis, or venous insufficiency)
- Physical activity support
