# Vitamin D Deficiency in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: November 2025.*

This pathway covers assessment and management of vitamin D deficiency in children, including risk factor identification, investigations, age-specific dosing, and when specialist assessment is needed.

> This is general information from a clinical pathway written for health professionals. Always discuss your child's care with their doctor.

---

## Background

Vitamin D supports bone health and metabolism across all age groups. There is increasing evidence for its role in immune modulation and anti-inflammatory properties.

If left untreated, vitamin D deficiency can result in rickets and hypocalcaemia.

80 to 90% of vitamin D is produced through skin exposure to UVB, and 10 to 20% through diet.

**Sun exposure:**
- Limited in winter and spring, more so in southern latitudes
- Optimum duration of sun exposure is not well established
- Direct sun exposure is not recommended for young infants

**Dietary sources:**
- A term infant of a vitamin D-replete mother has adequate vitamin D stores for 8 to 12 weeks
- Breast milk is the best food for babies, but is a poor source of vitamin D (25 units/L) unless mothers are receiving high-dose supplementation
- Infant formula is fortified with vitamin D (400 units/L)
- Vitamin D3 is found in small quantities in fatty fish (salmon, tuna, sardines, mackerel), and in very small quantities in liver, eggs, and fortified foods such as some margarines, dairy- and plant-based milk products, or yoghurt
- The recommended dietary intake of calcium varies with age, from 200 mg/day in infants to 1300 mg/day in adolescents

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Low calcium (hypocalcaemia) is life-threatening and can get worse quickly. Signs of **symptomatic hypocalcaemia** include:

- Seizures
- Tetany or carpopedal spasm
- Laryngospasm, stridor, wheeze, or apnoea (in babies younger than 6 months)
- Hypotonia, weakness, or muscle cramps
- Hyperreflexia

If your child has any of these signs, call 111 or go to the emergency department.

If your child's blood test shows corrected calcium < 2 mmol/L, or confirms vitamin B12 deficiency, call 111 or go to the emergency department, even if your child has no symptoms.

---

## Risk Factors

Your healthcare provider may consider targeted screening for children with risk factors. Those with multiple risk factors are at greater risk.

**Individual factors:**
- Darker skin pigmentation, particularly children with South Asian or African ethnicity
- Prematurity (under 37 weeks) or low birth weight (under 2500 g)
- Disability, which often leads to reduced sun exposure, mobility problems, dietary deficiencies, and use of medications that reduce vitamin D
- Reduced sun exposure due to health, social, religious, or environmental factors

**Diet factors:**
- Exclusive breastfeeding, or mixed feeding (breast milk and formula combined)
- Delayed introduction of solids in exclusively breastfed infants
- Low vitamin D diet, such as fussy eaters or vegan diets, especially in older children

**Family factors:**
- Born to a vitamin D-deficient mother, which is more common in dark-skinned, veiled, or recently arrived migrant mothers
- Sibling of a vitamin D-deficient child

**Chronic disease:**
- Malabsorption syndromes (e.g., coeliac disease, pancreatic insufficiency)
- Chronic liver or renal disease

**Medications that can affect vitamin D metabolism or bone health:**
- Oral steroids
- Some anticonvulsants: sodium valproate, carbamazepine, phenytoin
- Antiretrovirals, rifampicin, and some chemotherapy agents

## Assessment

Your healthcare provider will take a history and look for signs of hypocalcaemia or rickets. Hypocalcaemia is more likely during periods of rapid growth (breastfed infants, adolescents) and may come before bony deformity and rickets.

### Rickets

More likely in children older than 6 months. It is indicated by a 25(OH)D level less than 25 nmol/L together with signs and symptoms such as:

**Skeletal changes (bony deformities at growth plates):**
- Swelling of wrists or ankles
- Leg deformities (genu varum or valgum)
- Rachitic rosary (enlarged costochondral joints)
- Delayed anterior fontanelle closure (normally closed by age 2 years)
- Frontal bossing
- Bone pain

**Non-skeletal manifestations:**
- Delayed tooth eruption and dental caries
- Irritability, muscle pain, fatigue
- Gross motor delay, waddling gait, or delayed walking
- Poor growth (height suppressed more than weight)
- Increased susceptibility to infections, especially respiratory

Note: there are other forms of rickets not related to vitamin D deficiency (e.g., hypophosphataemic rickets). Specialist assessment is indicated in all suspected rickets cases.

---

## Investigations

Your healthcare provider may arrange blood tests if your child has symptoms of hypocalcaemia or clinical signs of rickets, or has multiple risk factors with concern about severe deficiency or other deficiencies.

**Blood tests typically include:**
- 25(OH)D (vitamin D level)
- Calcium, phosphate, albumin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), creatinine
- FBC, ferritin, and CRP, because iron deficiency often coexists with vitamin D deficiency
- Vitamin B12, only for breastfed infants of vegan mothers who are not taking B12 supplementation or regularly eating B12-fortified foods

Note: investigations are not needed if your child has only one or two risk factors and no symptoms. Some recently arrived migrant children with risk factors may have normal vitamin D levels on arrival in New Zealand.

### Interpreting 25(OH)D Results

| Vitamin D status | 25(OH)D level |
|---|---|
| Sufficient | ≥ 50 nmol/L |
| Mild deficiency | 26 to 49 nmol/L |
| Moderate deficiency | 12.5 to 25 nmol/L |
| Severe deficiency | < 12.5 nmol/L |

Vitamin D deficiency is 7 times more likely to be detected when tested in winter compared to summer.

## Management

### Early Supplementation for Breastfed Infants

Exclusively or partially breastfed infants (receiving less than 500 mL of formula daily) are at high risk of deficiency. Your healthcare provider may recommend starting vitamin D supplementation before 4 weeks of age, continuing until at least age 1 year. Supplementing the breastfeeding mother does not significantly increase vitamin D levels in breast milk and is not a substitute for supplementing the infant.

---

### Prophylaxis (One or Two Risk Factors, Asymptomatic)

If your child has one or two risk factors and no symptoms, your healthcare provider may start prophylaxis without testing. If your child has multiple risk factors and their vitamin D level has not been checked, the same prophylaxis may be used.

Your doctor will discuss the options with you. A single dose once a year in autumn is generally preferred, because giving daily doses long term can be hard to keep up.

**Under 12 months:**
- A yearly single dose is not recommended
- Vitamin D drops (colecalciferol) 400 units (1 drop) daily

**Aged 1 to 2 years, either:**
- 150,000 units as a single dose once a year in autumn
- Or vitamin D drops 400 to 800 international units (1 to 2 drops) daily

**Aged 2 to 5 years, either:**
- 300,000 units as a single dose once a year in autumn
- Or vitamin D drops 400 to 800 international units (1 to 2 drops) daily

**Aged over 5 years, either:**
- 600,000 units as a single dose once a year in autumn
- Or vitamin D drops 400 to 800 international units (1 to 2 drops) daily

*Note on colecalciferol capsules: they contain soya oil (not suitable for soya or peanut allergy) and animal gelatin, so they may be unsuitable for some people for ethical, dietary, or religious reasons. The current funded capsule is derived from beef, not pork. For children unable to swallow capsules, the capsules can be softened in a small amount of hot water and the oily contents squeezed out, then the whole water-and-oil mixture added to a few spoonfuls of soft food such as yoghurt or puree.*

*Tips for drops: hold the bottle at a 45-degree angle and wait 10 seconds for the drop to form. Do not shake the bottle. The drop can go directly into the mouth, onto a dummy, or onto the nipple when breastfeeding.*

---

### Treatment Protocol (Confirmed Deficiency, Normal Calcium and Phosphate)

If vitamin D deficiency is confirmed but calcium and phosphate are normal, your healthcare provider will treat based on age and vitamin D level, and discuss the options with you. A follow-up appointment is typically recommended at 3 months.

**Younger than 3 months (a single high dose is not recommended):**
- If 25(OH)D 25 to 50 nmol/L: vitamin D drops 400 units (1 drop) daily for 3 months
- If 25(OH)D less than 25 nmol/L: vitamin D drops 800 units (2 drops) daily for 3 months

**Aged 3 months to 2 years (25(OH)D less than 50 nmol/L), either:**
- 150,000 units as a single dose
- Or vitamin D drops 800 units (2 drops) daily for 3 months

**Aged 2 to 5 years (25(OH)D less than 50 nmol/L), either:**
- 300,000 units as a single dose
- Or vitamin D drops 2000 units (5 drops) daily for 3 months

**Aged 5 years or older (25(OH)D less than 50 nmol/L), either:**
- 600,000 units as a single dose
- Or vitamin D drops 2000 units (5 drops) daily for 3 months

---

### Managing Associated Deficiencies

**Hypocalcaemia:**
- If corrected calcium is > 2 mmol/L, your doctor will seek urgent paediatric advice on whether hospital admission or other urgent tests are needed. The specialist will advise whether calcium supplementation is needed.
- If corrected calcium is < 2 mmol/L, or if your child has symptomatic hypocalcaemia, call 111 or go to the emergency department, whether or not your child has symptoms. Hypocalcaemia is life-threatening and can deteriorate rapidly. Low calcium may cause no symptoms.

**Hypophosphataemia (low phosphate):**
- If it occurs together with hypocalcaemia, it is managed as for hypocalcaemia
- Low phosphate on its own, or with vitamin D deficiency, needs a non-urgent paediatric assessment

**Iron deficiency:**
- Commonly coexists with vitamin D deficiency, especially in infants with prolonged breastfeeding and delayed solids
- Your healthcare provider may recommend an iron supplement (ferrous sulfate solution). Common side effects include dark stools, constipation, and gastrointestinal upset. Using a straw helps prevent tooth staining.
- Iron supplements should be stored securely, as they can be toxic in overdose

**Vitamin B12 deficiency:**
- If vitamin B12 deficiency is confirmed, call 111 or go to the emergency department, even if your child has no symptoms

---

## Follow-Up

Children treated for vitamin D deficiency are followed up at 3 months. Your doctor will advise on ongoing management based on your child's risk factors and response to treatment:

- Repeat testing is not needed for mild vitamin D deficiency
- Children with moderate to severe deficiency should have follow-up blood tests (25(OH)D, calcium, phosphate, ALP) after treatment to confirm response
- If ongoing risk factors persist, your healthcare provider may recommend continued prophylaxis (a single dose each autumn or daily drops). It is safe to repeat a single-dose treatment after 6 months if this coincides with autumn.

**Vitamin D toxicity:** Uncommon, but can occur with excessive supplementation. Risk is higher if your child also takes over-the-counter supplements containing high levels of vitamin D (e.g., cod liver oil). Always discuss all supplements with your doctor.

---

## When Specialist Assessment Is Needed

Your healthcare provider will arrange a specialist paediatric assessment if:

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department:**
- Corrected calcium < 2 mmol/L, even if your child has no symptoms, or symptomatic hypocalcaemia
- Confirmed vitamin B12 deficiency, even if your child has no symptoms

**Urgent specialist advice:**
- Hypocalcaemia with corrected calcium > 2 mmol/L needs urgent specialist advice on whether admission is needed

If your child has signs of low calcium, such as seizures, spasms, or breathing problems, call 111 or go to the emergency department.

**Non-urgent (routine paediatric referral):**
- Clinical concern about rickets
- Vitamin D deficiency that does not respond to treatment
- Low phosphate (hypophosphataemia)
- Iron deficiency not tolerated with oral therapy (may need iron infusion)
