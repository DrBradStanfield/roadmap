# Vitamin D Deficiency in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of vitamin D deficiency in children, including risk factor identification, investigations, age-specific dosing, and when specialist assessment is needed.

---

## Background

Vitamin D is important for bone health and metabolism across all age groups. There is increasing evidence for its role in immune modulation and anti-inflammatory properties.

If left untreated, vitamin D deficiency can result in rickets and hypocalcaemia.

90% of vitamin D is produced through skin exposure to UVB, and 10% through diet.

**Sun exposure:**
- Limited in winter and spring, more so at higher latitudes
- Optimum duration of sun exposure is not well established
- Direct sun exposure is not recommended for young infants

**Dietary sources:**
- A term infant of a vitamin D-replete mother has adequate vitamin D stores for 8–12 weeks
- Breast milk is the best food for babies, but is a poor source of vitamin D (~25 units/L) unless mothers are receiving high-dose supplementation
- Infant formula is fortified with vitamin D (400 units/L)
- Vitamin D3 is found in small quantities in fatty fish (salmon, herring, mackerel), and in very small quantities in liver, eggs, and fortified foods such as some dairy products
- The recommended daily intake of vitamin D is 400 units per day

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Your child should be assessed urgently if they have symptoms of **symptomatic hypocalcaemia**:

- Seizures
- Tetany or carpopedal spasm
- Laryngospasm, stridor, wheeze, or apnoea (especially under 6 months)
- Hypotonia, weakness, or muscle cramps
- Hyperreflexia

These are urgent — discuss with your doctor or seek emergency care without delay.

---

## Risk Factors

Your healthcare provider may consider targeted screening for children with risk factors. Those with multiple risk factors are at greater risk.

**Individual factors:**
- Darker skin pigmentation, particularly children with South Asian or African ethnicity
- Prematurity (under 37 weeks) or low birth weight (under 2500 g)
- Disability — often leads to reduced sun exposure, mobility problems, dietary deficiencies, and use of medications that reduce vitamin D
- Reduced sun exposure due to health, social, religious, or environmental factors

**Diet factors:**
- Exclusive breastfeeding, or mixed feeding (breast milk and formula combined)
- Delayed introduction of solids in exclusively breastfed infants
- Low vitamin D diet — fussy eater, vegetarian, or vegan, especially in older children

**Family factors:**
- Born to a vitamin D-deficient mother — more common in dark-skinned, veiled, or recently arrived migrant mothers
- Sibling of a vitamin D-deficient child

**Chronic disease:**
- Malabsorption syndromes (e.g., coeliac disease, pancreatic insufficiency)
- Chronic liver or renal disease

**Medications that can affect vitamin D metabolism or bone health:**
- Oral steroids
- Some anticonvulsants — sodium valproate, carbamazepine, phenytoin
- Antiretrovirals, rifampicin, and some chemotherapy agents

## Assessment

Your healthcare provider will take a history and examine for signs of rickets or hypocalcaemia, which are more likely during periods of rapid growth (breastfed infants, adolescents).

### Rickets

More likely in children older than 6 months, when 25(OH)D is less than 25 nmol/L. Signs and symptoms include:

**Skeletal changes (bony deformities at growth plates):**
- Swelling of wrists or ankles
- Leg deformities (genu varum or valgum)
- Rachitic rosary — enlarged costochondral joints
- Delayed anterior fontanelle closure (normally closed by age 2 years)
- Frontal bossing
- Bone pain

**Non-skeletal manifestations:**
- Delayed tooth eruption and dental caries
- Irritability, muscle pain, fatigue
- Gross motor delay, waddling gait, or delayed walking
- Poor growth (height suppressed more than weight)
- Increased susceptibility to infections, especially respiratory

Note: there are other forms of rickets not related to vitamin D deficiency (e.g., hypophosphataemic rickets). Specialist assessment is indicated in all suspected rickets cases.

---

## Investigations

Your healthcare provider may arrange blood tests if your child has symptoms of hypocalcaemia, clinical signs of rickets, or multiple risk factors with concern about severe deficiency.

**Blood tests typically include:**
- 25(OH)D (vitamin D level)
- Calcium, phosphate, albumin, alkaline phosphatase (ALP), creatinine
- FBC, ferritin, and CRP — iron deficiency often coexists with vitamin D deficiency
- Vitamin B12 — only for breastfed infants of vegan mothers not taking B12 supplementation

Note: investigations are generally not needed if your child has only one or two risk factors and no symptoms.

### Interpreting 25(OH)D Results

| Vitamin D status | 25(OH)D level |
|---|---|
| Sufficient | ≥ 50 nmol/L |
| Mild deficiency | 26–49 nmol/L |
| Moderate deficiency | 12.5–25 nmol/L |
| Severe deficiency | < 12.5 nmol/L |

Vitamin D deficiency is approximately 7 times more likely to be detected when tested in winter compared to summer.

## Management

### Early Supplementation for Breastfed Infants

Exclusively or partially breastfed infants (receiving less than 500 mL of formula daily) are at high risk of deficiency. Your healthcare provider may recommend starting vitamin D supplementation before 4 weeks of age, continuing until at least age 1 year. Supplementing the breastfeeding mother does not significantly increase vitamin D levels in breast milk and is not a substitute for supplementing the infant.

---

### Prophylaxis (One or Two Risk Factors, Asymptomatic)

If your child has one or two risk factors and no symptoms, your healthcare provider may start prophylaxis without testing:

**Under 12 months:**
- Vitamin D drops (colecalciferol) 400 units (1 drop) daily until age 1 year
- If breastfeeding, the mother's own vitamin D status should be discussed with her doctor

**Aged 1–2 years — either:**
- Annual prophylactic dose in autumn (STOSS therapy): 150,000 units as a single dose
- Or vitamin D drops 400 units (1 drop) daily throughout the year if daily adherence is reliable

**Aged 2–5 years — either:**
- Annual STOSS therapy: 300,000 units as a single dose
- Or vitamin D drops 400 units (1 drop) daily

**Aged over 5 years — either:**
- Annual STOSS therapy: 600,000 units as a single dose
- Or vitamin D drops 400 units (1 drop) daily

*Note on STOSS capsules: they contain soya oil (not suitable for soya or peanut allergy) and animal gelatin. For children unable to swallow capsules, the contents can be mixed with a small amount of hot water and stirred into soft food such as yoghurt or puree.*

---

### Treatment Protocol (Confirmed Deficiency, Normal Calcium and Phosphate)

If vitamin D deficiency is confirmed but calcium and phosphate are normal, your healthcare provider will treat based on age. A follow-up appointment is typically recommended at 3 months.

**Under 6 months:**
- STOSS therapy is not used in this age group
- If 25(OH)D 30–50 nmol/L: vitamin D drops 400 units (1 drop) daily for 3 months
- If 25(OH)D < 30 nmol/L: vitamin D drops 2000 units (5 drops) daily for 3 months
- If breastfeeding, maternal vitamin D status should also be addressed

**Aged 6 months to 2 years — either:**
- STOSS therapy: 150,000 units as a single dose
- Or vitamin D drops 2000 units (5 drops) daily for 3 months
- Annual autumn STOSS therapy may be considered ongoing (safe to repeat from 6 months)

**Aged 2–5 years — either:**
- STOSS therapy: 300,000 units as a single dose
- Or vitamin D drops 2000 units (5 drops) daily for 3 months
- Annual autumn STOSS therapy may be considered ongoing

**Aged over 5 years — either:**
- STOSS therapy: 600,000 units as a single dose
- Or vitamin D drops 2000 units (5 drops) daily for 3 months
- Annual autumn STOSS therapy may be considered ongoing

---

### Managing Associated Deficiencies

**Hypocalcaemia:**
- If corrected calcium is > 2 mmol/L, your doctor may recommend calcium supplementation and will assess whether further investigations or specialist advice are needed
- If corrected calcium is < 2 mmol/L, or if your child has symptomatic hypocalcaemia, urgent hospital assessment is required — hypocalcaemia can deteriorate rapidly

**Iron deficiency:**
- Commonly coexists with vitamin D deficiency, especially in infants with prolonged breastfeeding and delayed solids
- Your healthcare provider may recommend an iron supplement (ferrous sulfate solution). Common side effects include dark stools, constipation, and gastrointestinal upset. Using a straw helps prevent tooth staining.
- Iron supplements should be stored securely — overdose is dangerous

**Vitamin B12 deficiency:**
- Requires urgent specialist assessment

---

## Follow-Up

Your doctor will advise on ongoing management based on your child's risk factors and response to treatment:

- Repeat testing is not needed for mild vitamin D deficiency
- Children with moderate to severe deficiency should have follow-up blood tests (25(OH)D, calcium, phosphate, ALP) after treatment to confirm response
- If ongoing risk factors persist, your healthcare provider may recommend continued prophylaxis (annual STOSS therapy or daily drops)

**Vitamin D toxicity:** Uncommon, but can occur with excessive supplementation. Risk is higher if your child also takes over-the-counter supplements containing vitamin D (e.g., cod liver oil). Always discuss all supplements with your doctor.

---

## When Specialist Assessment Is Needed

Your healthcare provider will arrange a specialist paediatric assessment if:

**Urgent (acute assessment):**
- Symptomatic hypocalcaemia, or corrected calcium < 2 mmol/L
- Confirmed vitamin B12 deficiency

**Non-urgent (routine paediatric referral):**
- Vitamin D deficiency without hypocalcaemia or hypophosphataemia, and multiple risk factors
- Clinical concern about rickets
- Vitamin D deficiency that does not respond to treatment
- Isolated or concurrent hypophosphataemia
- Iron deficiency not tolerated with oral therapy (may need iron infusion)
