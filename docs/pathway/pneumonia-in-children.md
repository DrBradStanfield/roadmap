# Pneumonia in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers how pneumonia in children is assessed, treated, and followed up in primary care.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

A child needs urgent medical assessment if they have:

- Chronic lung disease
- A weakened immune system (immunocompromised)
- Age under 3 months
- Severe illness
- Symptoms that are getting worse despite taking oral antibiotics

---

## Background

Pneumonia is an infection of the lungs. In children, it can be caused by bacteria or viruses. Most children with mild or moderate pneumonia can be safely managed at home with oral antibiotics and close follow-up.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider pneumonia in children who present with:

- Acute illness with persistent fever
- Respiratory distress — including chest wall indrawing and fast breathing (tachypnoea)
- Irritability, lethargy, or difficulty feeding
- Cough (less common in infants)
- Crackles heard through a stethoscope

### Risk factors your doctor will check

**Environmental:**
- Tobacco smoke exposure
- Living in cold, damp, or overcrowded housing
- Not immunised

**Personal:**
- Premature birth
- Poor nutrition
- Breastfed for less than 2 months
- Underlying conditions such as chronic lung disease, cystic fibrosis, primary immunodeficiency, or chronic aspiration

### Physical examination

Your doctor will check your child's general appearance, alertness, and hydration. They will also check vital signs — including temperature, heart rate, breathing rate, and oxygen levels (a reading below 93% on room air is concerning).

### Features suggesting serious illness

Your doctor will look for signs that may indicate significant underlying pathology:

- Cyanosis (bluish skin colour)
- Apnoea (pauses in breathing)
- Absent breath sounds with dull percussion (suggests fluid around the lung)
- Stridor, wheeze, or crackles
- Reduced air entry
- Breathing rate above 60 per minute
- Marked chest wall indrawing, retractions, grunting, or nasal flaring

### Other conditions your doctor will consider

- Asthma
- Croup
- Inhaled foreign body
- Pertussis (whooping cough)
- Simple upper respiratory tract viral infection
- Viral bronchiolitis

### Chest X-ray

Most children with pneumonia can be diagnosed and managed on clinical grounds alone. A chest X-ray does not reliably distinguish bacterial from viral pneumonia. Your doctor may request one if there is diagnostic uncertainty, suspected complications, an atypical presentation, or a history of underlying respiratory disease.

### Severity assessment

Your doctor will assess how unwell your child is:

| Feature | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---------|------|----------|--------|
| Behaviour | Normal | Some irritability | Increasing irritability, lethargy, or fatigue |
| Respiratory rate | Normal to mildly elevated | Increased | Markedly increased or decreased |
| Chest wall retractions | None to mild | Moderate, with nasal flaring | Marked, with nasal flaring and tracheal tug |
| Oxygen saturation | Above 92% (room air) | 90–92% (room air) | Below 90% (room air) |
| Apnoea | None | Brief episodes | Frequent or prolonged episodes |
| Feeding | Normal | Some difficulty or reduced | Reluctant or unable to feed |

## Management

### When hospital assessment is needed

Your doctor will arrange urgent medical assessment if your child:

- Has chronic lung disease
- Is immunocompromised
- Is under 3 months of age
- Is severely unwell
- Is getting worse while taking oral antibiotics

### Mild or moderate pneumonia (child aged 3 months or older)

Your doctor may consider managing your child at home with:

**Oral antibiotics** — the first-line treatment is amoxicillin 25–30 mg/kg three times a day for seven days.

- If your child has a penicillin allergy or treatment is not working, your doctor may consider:
  - Erythromycin 10–12.5 mg/kg four times a day for seven days
  - Roxithromycin — dose depends on your child's weight:
    - Under 40 kg: 2.5–4 mg/kg (maximum 150 mg) twice a day for seven days
    - Over 40 kg: 150 mg twice a day for seven days
- If an atypical infection is suspected in a child aged 5 years or older, erythromycin 10 mg/kg four times a day for seven days may be used. Atypical infections are uncommon in children under 5 years.

Your doctor will arrange a follow-up phone call or review after 48 hours to check how your child is responding.

### When to return sooner

Bring your child back for review immediately if they have:

- Persistent or worsening symptoms
- Decreased feeding or fewer wet nappies — this suggests your child may not be taking in enough fluids

---

## Follow-up

1. **6-week review** — your doctor will check for unresolved symptoms such as a chronic moist cough or persistent chest signs.
2. **Annual flu vaccination** — if your child is under 5, your doctor may set a recall for annual funded influenza vaccination.
3. **Hand hygiene** — frequent hand washing by all family members helps during respiratory viral season.
4. **Smoking cessation** — your doctor may offer support for any household members who smoke.
