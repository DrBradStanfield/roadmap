# Hiccups in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of hiccups in adults with advanced illness. Hiccups lasting more than 48 hours are not uncommon in patients with advanced cancer and can be very distressing. Management should always be guided by your doctor.

---

## When Treatment Is Needed

Hiccups often stop spontaneously. Treatment is only required if persistent or causing distress, for example disrupting sleep, communication, or activity.

Your doctor will determine whether treatment is necessary and guide the approach.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider possible underlying causes, including:

- Gastric stasis and distension (the most common cause)
- Gastro-oesophageal reflux
- Metabolic disturbances e.g., uraemia, hypercalcaemia, hyponatraemia
- Irritation of the diaphragm, phrenic nerve, or vagus nerve
- Hepatic disease or hepatomegaly
- Cerebral causes e.g., brain stem infarct, tumour, metastases

---

## Management

Your doctor will manage hiccups in a stepwise approach:

### Step 1 — Treat reversible causes

Where possible, your doctor will address underlying causes and consider stopping medications with anticholinergic activity (e.g., tricyclic antidepressants, cyclizine) if these may be contributing.

### Step 2 — Non-pharmacological measures

Simple measures that have previously worked should be tried first, such as:

- Sipping iced water
- Swallowing crushed ice
- Stimulating the soft palate with a swab (pharyngeal stimulation)

### Step 3 — Medications (if needed)

Your doctor may trial medications. Drugs are typically trialled for 3 days (dexamethasone for 7 days). The choice depends on the likely cause and individual circumstances:

| Medication | How it may help |
|-----------|----------------|
| Chlorpromazine | The only medication formally approved for intractable hiccups; can suppress the hiccup reflex centrally. Note: sedating; cannot be given via the subcutaneous route; avoid if blood pressure is low. |
| Baclofen | Skeletal muscle relaxant; may also suppress the central hiccup reflex. |
| Dexamethasone | Reduces compression or irritation from hepatic or cerebral tumours. Stopped if no benefit after 7 days. |
| Gabapentin | May suppress the central hiccup reflex. |
| Haloperidol | May suppress the central hiccup reflex. |
| Metoclopramide | Reduces gastric distension; may also suppress the central hiccup reflex. |
| Midazolam | Muscle relaxant and sedative; used for hiccups that do not respond to other treatments. |
| Nifedipine | Smooth muscle relaxant. Immediate-release preparation used (unapproved indication). Avoid if blood pressure is low. |
| Simeticone | Antifoaming agent — reduces gastric distension. |

*Most of these are unapproved indications. Your doctor will select the most appropriate option and monitor response.*

### Step 4 — Specialist palliative care advice

If early measures are ineffective, your doctor will seek adult palliative care support and advice. Hiccups can be difficult to manage and are very distressing.

---

## When to Contact Your Doctor or Care Team

Contact your doctor or palliative care team promptly if:

- Hiccups are persistent and causing significant distress
- Hiccups are disrupting sleep, eating, or communication
- Treatments tried at home are not helping
