# Ovarian Cancer Symptoms

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway is for women with new and persisting (more than 1 month) abdominal or pelvic symptoms. The information below is a guide — your doctor will assess your individual situation and decide on next steps.

---

## Red Flags — Discuss With Your Doctor Promptly

Tell your doctor as soon as possible if you have:

- Genetic risk — strong family history of ovarian or breast cancer, known Lynch syndrome (HNPCC), or a BRCA gene mutation

These features mean your doctor will want to assess you sooner.

---

## Background

- Ovarian cancer is more common in postmenopausal women. The mean age of diagnosis is 65 years.
- The lifetime incidence for women is approximately 1.6%.
- In premenopausal women, ovarian cancer is uncommon but more likely if there is a strong family history or known BRCA or HNPCC mutations.
- Around 10% of ovarian cancer is caused by hereditary cancer syndromes.
- Symptoms are often non-specific and not obviously gynaecological, which can make diagnosis difficult.
- A physical examination is important — there may be a mass or signs of abdominal disease.
- Women with one first- or second-degree relative diagnosed with ovarian cancer after age 50 have approximately a 5% lifetime risk (slightly higher than the general population risk of 1.6%). Women with a known BRCA mutation have a much higher risk.
- There is currently no proven benefit to CA125 or ultrasound screening in women without symptoms, as these tests can produce false positives and lead to unnecessary procedures.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

### Possible Symptoms of Ovarian Cancer

- Persistent abdominal distension or bloating
- Early satiety (feeling full quickly) or loss of appetite
- Pelvic or abdominal pain without another clear cause
- Increased urinary urgency or frequency
- Irritable bowel symptoms, especially if new onset in women aged over 50
- Unexplained weight loss or fatigue
- Postmenopausal bleeding

Symptoms can be persistent or come and go, and are often vague and generalised. Your doctor may ask you to keep a symptom diary if the pattern is unclear.

### Risk Factors

- Age over 50 years
- Genetic risk: Lynch syndrome (hereditary nonpolyposis colorectal cancer — HNPCC), BRCA mutation
- Other possible risk factors:
  - First-degree family history of ovarian cancer
  - Long-term use of menopausal hormone therapy (MHT)
  - Endometriosis
- Factors associated with mildly increased risk of some types of ovarian cancer:
  - High number of ovulations (early menarche, not having children, late menopause)
  - Previous cancer diagnosis, particularly breast or bowel cancer
  - Diabetes
  - Obesity
  - Smoking

### Protective Factors

- Ovulatory suppression: menopause before age 45, childbearing, breastfeeding, combined oral contraceptives
- Tubal ligation
- Hysterectomy

### Other Causes Your Doctor Will Consider

Pelvic pain can have many causes. Your doctor may also consider:

**Gynaecological:** endometriosis, adenomyosis, chronic pelvic inflammatory disease (PID), vulvodynia, pelvic congestion syndrome

**Adhesions:** from endometriosis, previous surgery, or pelvic infection

**Urological:** interstitial cystitis (bladder pain syndrome), recurrent urinary tract infections

**Gastrointestinal:** irritable bowel syndrome, diverticular disease, coeliac disease, inflammatory bowel disease, constipation, bowel cancer

**Musculoskeletal:** pelvic floor muscle pain, coccydynia, chronic abdominal wall pain, referred pain from the back

**Neurological:** neuropathic pain (possibly related to previous surgery), pudendal neuralgia

**Psychological:** depression, anxiety, sexual dysfunction, somatisation

**Coexisting pain conditions:** fibromyalgia, migraine, myalgic encephalomyelitis/chronic fatigue syndrome (ME/CFS)

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will perform a physical examination, which typically includes:

**Abdominal examination:**
- Feeling for tenderness, masses, or enlarged organs
- Checking for ascites (fluid in the abdomen) by percussion and palpation

**Pelvic examination:**
- Inspection of the vulva
- Speculum examination to view the cervix and vagina (unless not sexually active)
- Bimanual examination to feel for adnexal masses, nodularity, or tenderness (unless not sexually active)
- Rectal examination to exclude a mass or constipation

Your doctor may also check spine and hip mobility to rule out referred pain.

### Investigations Your Doctor May Arrange

- **Pregnancy test** — for all premenopausal women
- **Urine test (MSU)** — for all postmenopausal women, and premenopausal women with urinary symptoms
- **Blood tests:** CA125, liver function tests (LFT), full blood count (FBC), CRP, calcium, creatinine, and electrolytes

**About CA125:**
- CA125 is a blood marker raised in 85% of advanced ovarian cancers, but only 50% of early-stage (stage 1) cancers
- It can also be elevated in benign conditions including endometriosis, uterine fibroids, cirrhosis, pelvic inflammatory disease, and other cancers
- It is most useful in postmenopausal women where there is less risk of a false-positive result
- It is not reliable enough to use as a screening test in women without symptoms

- **Screening for coeliac disease** — if gastrointestinal symptoms are present (requires at least 4 weeks of regular gluten intake before testing)
- **Urgent pelvic ultrasound (within 2 weeks)** — arranged if:
  - There are signs of a pelvic or abdominal mass, or ascites
  - Premenopausal and CA125 is greater than 200 units/mL
  - Postmenopausal and CA125 is greater than 35 units/mL

For women with a known genetic risk (e.g., BRCA mutation or HNPCC), your doctor will follow recommendations from genetic services.

## Management

Your doctor will consider the following approach based on examination findings, CA125 result, and menopausal status.

### If Ultrasound Has Been Done — Referral Criteria

Your doctor will refer you to a gynaecologist (marked as "high suspicion of cancer") if any of the following apply:

- Malignant features on ultrasound:
  - Abnormal blood flow
  - Multiloculated cyst
  - Solid areas
  - Papillary areas
  - Irregular cyst
  - Increasing size
  - Bilateral lesions
  - Ascites
  - Signs of spread (metastasis)
- CA125 greater than 200 units/mL (premenopausal)
- CA125 greater than 35 units/mL (postmenopausal)

Additional blood tests your doctor may request before referral:
- For women aged under 40: CEA, CA19-9, AFP, HCG, and LDH
- For women aged 40 or older: CEA and CA19-9

### If No Clinical Signs — Management by Menopausal Status

**Premenopausal women** (benign causes are the most common reason for elevated CA125 in this group):

- *CA125 less than 35 units/mL:* Your doctor will assess for other causes of your symptoms. If no other cause is found, you will be advised to return if symptoms increase or persist at 3 months. If symptoms continue or worsen, your doctor may arrange a pelvic ultrasound or seek gynaecology advice.

- *CA125 between 35 and 200 units/mL:* Your doctor will repeat the CA125 in 6 weeks. If it is rising or remains high, a pelvic ultrasound will be arranged and a gynaecology referral made. If the CA125 decreases, your doctor will reassure you and advise follow-up in 3 months if symptoms persist.

- *CA125 greater than 200 units/mL:* Your doctor will arrange further blood tests and a pelvic ultrasound, then refer you to gynaecology.

**Postmenopausal women:**

- *CA125 less than 35 units/mL:* Your doctor will assess for and treat other causes of your symptoms. If no other cause is found, you will be advised to return if symptoms increase or persist at 6 weeks. If symptoms continue or worsen, your doctor may arrange a pelvic ultrasound or seek gynaecology advice.

- *CA125 greater than 35 units/mL:* Your doctor will arrange further blood tests and a pelvic ultrasound, then refer you to gynaecology.

### If Ultrasound Suggests a Non-Malignant Ovarian Cyst

Your doctor will manage this according to the diagnosed ovarian cyst pathway.

### Women With Known Genetic Risk

If you have a known genetic risk but do not meet the criteria for referral, your doctor may seek gynaecology advice on your behalf.
