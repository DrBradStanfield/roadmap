# Sleep Disturbances in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Sleep problems are common in palliative care. This pathway covers the assessment and management of sleep disturbances in adults — including insomnia (difficulty sleeping) and hypersomnia (excessive sleepiness). Your doctor can assess which factors are contributing and what management approach is most appropriate for your situation.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

**Sleep history:**
- Difficulty falling or staying asleep
- Fear of sleeping or other anxieties
- Number and length of awakenings
- Dreams and nightmares
- Daytime sleepiness and naps

**Contributing factors your doctor will explore:**
- Pre-existing insomnia
- Caffeine intake
- Restless legs syndrome
- Cognitive impairment
- Physical symptoms such as pain, nausea, breathlessness, cough, itch, cramps, or continence issues (including nocturia and urinary retention — especially in the terminal phase)
- Psychological and emotional issues, including anxiety, depression, fear, or spiritual distress. Existential distress (questioning one's beliefs or the meaning of life) is common as illness progresses and can make physical symptoms harder to manage.
- Environmental factors: unfamiliar surroundings (hospital or hospice), disturbance by others, bed discomfort

**Medication review** — your doctor will check:
- Medications that may disrupt night-time sleep: diuretics or corticosteroids taken after midday, theophylline, methylphenidate, beta blockers, sedative or hypnotic withdrawal
- Medications that may cause daytime drowsiness: sedatives, opioids, hypnotics, anxiolytics, antihistamines

---

## Management

### Non-pharmacological approaches (first-line)

Your doctor will discuss expectations around sleep and acknowledge that sleep needs vary.

**Good sleep habits:**
- Go to bed and get up at the same time every day
- Avoid napping during the day if possible. If unavoidable, nap before 3 pm for no more than 30 minutes
- Engage in relaxing activities before bed (e.g., listening to music, a warm bath)
- Write things down to let go of worries and allow the mind to relax
- Avoid caffeine and alcohol before bed

**Stimulus control:**
- Avoid caffeine and energy drinks for several hours before bed
- Avoid alcohol near bedtime
- Keep the bedroom for sleeping only — no TV, electronic devices, or reading in bed
- If unable to sleep after 20 minutes in bed, get up and do something non-stimulating (reading, quiet chores, or relaxation), then return when tired
- Keep the bedroom dark and at a comfortable temperature
- Restrict noise or use earplugs
- Avoid gadgets or screens for at least 2 hours before sleep
- Engage in regular gentle daytime physical activity

**Relaxation techniques:**
- Yoga, t'ai chi
- Breathing and distraction techniques
- Progressive muscle relaxation
- Mindfulness and meditation

Treating any physical symptoms and addressing unresolved psychological, social, emotional, or spiritual issues is an important part of improving sleep.

---

### Medications (if non-pharmacological measures are insufficient)

Discuss all medication options with your doctor before starting anything. In palliative care, the approach to medications is tailored to the individual's prognosis, symptoms, and overall situation.

**Short-acting options (for difficulty falling asleep):**
- Zopiclone 3.75–7.5 mg at night
- Temazepam 10–20 mg at night
- Both have a duration of effect of about 4 hours. If the patient wakes before 2 am, a split dose (half or one additional tablet) may be considered.

**Longer-acting option (for difficulty staying asleep):**
- Clonazepam 0.5–1 mg at night — may cause morning drowsiness

**Melatonin** (for sleep-wake cycle disorders, e.g., in the setting of brain tumours):
- 2–3 mg taken 2 hours before sleep
- Note: melatonin is not funded in New Zealand

**Tricyclic antidepressants** (e.g., amitriptyline or nortriptyline 10–25 mg at night):
- Multiple side effects; may be useful in patients with co-existing neuropathic pain

**Sedating antipsychotics** (e.g., quetiapine) may be considered if anxiety is a significant contributor and other measures have not helped — discuss with your palliative care team.

---

## When to Seek Specialist Input

Your doctor will ensure close collaboration between all palliative care providers. Specialist palliative care support is sought for:

- Anticipatory planning or acute management
- Arranging a joint visit
- Considering admission to hospice
- Uncertainty about prescribing sedating antipsychotics

Children's palliative care involves a separate specialist service — your doctor will make the appropriate referral.

---

*Always discuss your symptoms and any changes to medications with your doctor. Palliative care decisions are made together with the patient, family/whānau, and the care team.*
