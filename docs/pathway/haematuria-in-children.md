# Haematuria in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to finding blood in urine in children, from causes other than urinary tract infection.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

Your doctor will arrange urgent assessment if any of the following are present:

- Large proteinuria (2+ or more on dipstick, or protein:creatinine ratio (PCR) over 100 mg/mmol)
- Hypertension (high blood pressure)
- Impaired renal function
- Decreased urine output
- Fluid overload or oedema (swelling)

---

## Background

Haematuria is defined as the presence of red blood cells in the urine (more than 5 × 10⁶ red cells, or 1+ on dipstick). False positives are common.

In the majority of cases, haematuria represents a benign incidental finding. However, it can be a sign of significant renal disease, especially when associated with proteinuria or a family history of chronic kidney disease.

Key points:
- Most cases of microscopic haematuria in well children resolve within 6 months
- Asymptomatic microscopic haematuria is common — a single test finds it in approximately 4% of school children; only 0.5% have it on repeat testing 2–3 weeks apart
- The presence of haematuria with proteinuria strongly suggests renal disease; the more protein present, the higher the likelihood

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history, including:

- Urinary symptoms
- Colour of the urine — dark or cola-coloured urine is more likely to come from the kidney; bright red is more likely from the lower urinary tract
- Change in urine output
- Pain (which may suggest kidney stones, infection, or obstruction)
- Recent exercise (can cause microscopic haematuria)
- Preceding or concurrent viral symptoms (rashes, sore throat, coryza, cough, diarrhoea)
- Other illnesses and known medical conditions
- Trauma
- Systemic symptoms (fatigue, weakness, weight loss, failure to thrive)
- Medications
- Other conditions that may cause discoloured or blood-stained urine, or be an alternative source of blood, including:
  - Vulvovaginitis or balanitis
  - Local rashes or irritation near the urethra
  - Urinary tract infection
  - Menstrual blood
  - Haemoglobinuria or myoglobinuria
  - Substances that discolour urine (beetroot, blackberries, rifampicin)
  - Urate crystals in newborns (produce a reddish "brick dust" appearance in the nappy — sometimes mistaken for blood)
- Risk factors for renal disease, including:
  - Prematurity or low birth weight
  - History of renal insult (e.g., acute kidney injury, trauma)
  - Known renal or urinary tract anomalies
  - Family history of haematuria, renal disease (dialysis or transplant), kidney stones, or deafness (Alport's syndrome)

Physical examination will typically include:

- Urine dipstick (performed or repeated)
- Height and weight, plotted on a growth chart
- Blood pressure measurement using an age-appropriate cuff size
- Abdominal examination
- Examination of the genitalia and perineum for rashes and irritation
- Assessment for oedema and volume overload
- Check for generalised rashes, bruising, and joint swelling

Initial investigations will typically include:

- Urine for microscopy and culture (dysmorphic red cells or red cell casts indicate a glomerular source)
- If no cells are found on a promptly examined urine sample, your doctor may consider haemoglobinuria or myoglobinuria and check serum creatinine kinase and myoglobulin

## Management

Your doctor will manage haematuria based on the specific findings and whether it is microscopic or macroscopic, and whether proteinuria is also present.

### If Red Flags Are Present or the Child Is Unwell

Your doctor will arrange urgent paediatric medical assessment. If glomerulonephritis is suspected (haematuria, proteinuria, hypertension, volume overload, or low urine output), urgent assessment is also required.

### If Macroscopic Haematuria and Pain

Your doctor will arrange urgent paediatric surgical assessment.

### If Other Causes Are Found

Your doctor will manage any alternative source of blood (e.g., vulvovaginitis, UTI) and recheck for haematuria once treated.

---

### Isolated Microscopic Haematuria (No Proteinuria)

Your doctor may consider:

1. **Repeat first morning dipstick on two occasions** when the child is well. If no further abnormality, reassurance is appropriate.
2. **If further microscopic haematuria is detected:**
   - Confirm whether it is persistent by checking first morning dipstick 3–4 times over one year
   - Test parents' urine for haematuria (there are familial causes, including hypercalciuria, Alport's syndrome, IgA nephropathy)
   - If proteinuria develops, manage as per the microscopic haematuria with proteinuria pathway below
   - Refer to paediatric medicine if there are underlying risk factors for renal disease
3. **If persistent or recurrent for more than 1 year**, your doctor will arrange:
   - Urine calcium:creatinine ratio for hypercalciuria (upper limits vary by age — see table below)
   - Renal function tests
   - Non-urgent paediatric medical assessment, with all test results

**Urine calcium:creatinine ratio upper limits by age:**

| Age (years) | Calcium/creatinine upper limit (mmol/mmol) |
|-------------|---------------------------------------------|
| 0–1 | 2.0 |
| 1–3 | 1.5 |
| 3–5 | 1.1 |
| 5–7 | 0.8 |
| >7 | 0.6 |

If hypercalciuria is confirmed (by repeat spot samples or 24-hour urine collection), your doctor will arrange a renal tract ultrasound looking for renal tract calculi and tumours.

---

### Microscopic Haematuria with Proteinuria

Your doctor may consider:

1. **Repeat first morning dipstick on 3 different occasions** to confirm persistent haematuria and proteinuria, and recheck blood pressure.
2. **If persistent, child remains well, and no obvious source** (such as skin source or UTI), your doctor will:
   - Review family history
   - Arrange blood tests including: early morning urine protein:creatinine ratio (PCR), FBC, electrolytes, urea, creatinine, albumin, anti-nuclear antibody (ANA), C3, C4, streptococcal serology, and lipids
   - Refer for non-urgent paediatric medical assessment

---

### Macroscopic Haematuria

**If fresh (red) blood** — a lower urinary tract or surgical cause is more likely. Your doctor may:
- Exclude UTI or viral cystitis with urine microscopy and culture
- Check urine calcium:creatinine ratio for hypercalciuria
- Arrange a renal tract ultrasound (looking for stones, PUJ obstruction, polycystic kidney disease, tumours)
- Refer to the appropriate specialty based on findings
- If no cause found for painless macroscopic haematuria, refer for non-urgent paediatric medical assessment

**If altered (dark, cola-coloured) blood** — an upper renal tract or medical cause is more likely. Your doctor may:
- Check for onset in relation to pharyngitis or viral illness (possible post-infectious glomerulonephritis)
- Review family history and recheck blood pressure
- Check urine microscopy for dysmorphic cells
- Check electrolytes, creatinine, urea, and albumin
- Consider glomerulonephritis investigations: C3, C4, streptococcal serology, anti-nuclear antibody (ANA)
- Arrange renal tract ultrasound and refer for non-urgent paediatric medical assessment

**Always follow up** after an episode of macroscopic haematuria to check there is no persistent microscopic haematuria.
