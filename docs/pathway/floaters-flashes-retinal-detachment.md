# Floaters, Flashes, and Retinal Detachment

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of floaters, flashes, and suspected retinal detachment — helping determine whether urgent specialist review is needed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

Contact your doctor or seek urgent assessment if you have any of the following:

- Significant floaters of recent onset, pinpointed to within the last 1 to 2 weeks
- Decreased vision or a visual field defect (e.g., a shadow or missing area in your vision)
- Significant short-sightedness (more than minus 5) or a previous retinal detachment
- Recent ocular surgery (cataract, intravitreal injection) within the last 3 months
- Direct eye trauma with onset of symptoms

---

## Background

### Floaters and Flashes

- Floaters are common after age 55, though short-sighted people may experience them earlier.
- They can appear as cobweb-like strands, spots, or shapes drifting in the vision.
- Flashes and floaters can be a sign of retinal detachment, which occurs in about 1 in 10,000 people per year.
- Long-standing floaters (present for months) are not typically a sign of retinal detachment — they are more likely due to normal, age-related changes in the eye.

### Posterior Vitreous Detachment (PVD)

- PVD is the most common cause of sudden-onset flashes and floaters.
- It is a normal part of ageing — the vitreous gel inside the eye changes consistency and pulls away from the retina, causing collagen condensations that cast shadows (floaters).
- The vitreous pulling on the retina can also stimulate bright flashes of light.

### Retinal Tear and Retinal Detachment

- In some cases, PVD leads to a retinal tear. About 50% of retinal tears progress to retinal detachment.
- A retinal tear may have minimal symptoms, or may cause sudden floaters, flashes, and reduced vision.
- Retinal detachment tends to present with recent-onset, more prominent symptoms — large floaters, obvious daytime flashes, and often a visual field defect (shadow in vision).
- A dilated eye examination by an optometrist or ophthalmologist is needed to tell the difference between uncomplicated PVD and a retinal tear or detachment.

## Assessment

Your doctor will typically:

**Take a history, asking about:**
- Type of symptoms — flashes, floaters, decreased vision, or a growing shadow in vision
- Duration and timing of symptoms
- Any recent direct eye trauma
- Medical history including diabetes, short-sightedness, previous retinal tear treated with laser, cataract surgery, or previous retinal detachment
- Family history of retinal detachment
- Migraine or headaches (migrainous flashes typically evolve over 30 minutes, then resolve with headache onset, and visual acuity remains normal)

**Examine the eye, including:**
- Visual acuity (with glasses and pinhole)
- Red reflex and assessment for cataract
- Relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD) — swinging flashlight test
- Visual fields to confrontation (checking all four quadrants of vision in each eye)
- Fundus examination — optic disc, vessels, peripheral retina, and macula

---

## Possible Causes

### Posterior Vitreous Detachment (PVD)

The most common cause of acute floaters and flashes. A normal age-related process — the vitreous shrinks and separates from the retina. Usually benign, but needs dilated eye examination to rule out a retinal tear.

### Retinal Tear

- Sudden appearance of floaters, flashes, and possibly reduced vision
- A precursor to retinal detachment — about 50% of retinal tears progress if untreated
- Threshold for referral is lower if there is a past history of retinal detachment, family history, significant short-sightedness, or eye trauma

### Retinal Detachment

- Recent onset of prominent symptoms: large floaters, obvious daytime flashes, visual field defect
- If a patient has both floaters and a visual field defect, retinal detachment is likely

### Floaters Alone — Other Causes

- **Diabetic vitreous haemorrhage:** Diabetic patients with known retinopathy may develop vitreous haemorrhage from proliferative vessels, causing increased floaters
- **Inflammatory (uveitis):** Tends to have a longer history, may be bilateral, and vision is often decreased

### Flashes Alone

- **Migraine:** Flashes evolve over approximately 30 minutes, resolve with headache onset, and visual acuity remains normal

## Management

Your doctor will assess urgency based on your symptoms and examination findings:

**Urgent ophthalmology referral is typically needed if you have:**
- Significant floaters of recent onset (within 1–2 weeks)
- Flashes and/or floaters with any of: decreased vision, visual field defect, significant short-sightedness (more than minus 5), previous retinal detachment, recent ocular surgery, or eye trauma

**Non-urgent ophthalmology referral may be needed if:**
- Floaters are increasing over time and are associated with reduced vision
- You have diabetes and new floaters

**Optometrist review is appropriate if:**
- New onset floaters with no other symptoms
- Isolated flashes with no floaters and normal vision
- Mild floaters and flashes with normal vision — dilated retinal examination is needed to distinguish uncomplicated PVD from a tear or detachment

**For floaters present for more than 1 month:**
- Urgent assessment is generally not required
- Your doctor may provide reassurance and education
- You should seek review if vision decreases, new floaters appear, or any other change occurs
- Your doctor may consider an optometrist review if vision improves with pinhole testing
