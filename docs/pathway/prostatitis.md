# Prostatitis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of prostatitis — inflammation of the prostate gland — which ranges from acute bacterial infection to chronic non-bacterial pain syndromes.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if you have:

- Fever with rigors (shaking chills) and urinary symptoms suggesting severe infection or sepsis
- Inability to urinate (urinary retention)
- Signs of sepsis: very high or low temperature, rapid heart rate, low blood pressure, confusion

---

## Background

There are three main types of prostatitis:

**Acute bacterial prostatitis**
- Makes up about 5% of prostatitis cases
- Is a complicated urinary tract infection, usually caused by bacteria such as *E. coli*
- Can be complicated by urinary retention, abscess, or life-threatening septicaemia

**Chronic bacterial prostatitis**
- A persistent prostatic infection; only about 5% of chronic prostatitis cases have a proven bacterial cause (75–80% due to *E. coli*)
- Can be acquired via ascending urethral infection or spread through the lymphatic or blood stream

**Chronic non-bacterial prostatitis (chronic pelvic pain syndrome)**
- The most common form, accounting for 90–95% of chronic prostatitis cases
- The prostate is inflamed but no bacterial infection is found
- Symptoms are highly variable; all patients report genitourinary pain or discomfort
- Symptoms may resolve and recur; psychological stress may be a contributing factor

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history looking for:

- Urinary symptoms: dysuria (pain passing urine), urinary frequency, slow urine flow
- Fever or rigors, perineal or lower abdominal pain
- Chronic prostatic pain and recurrent lower urinary tract symptoms (suggesting chronic prostatitis)
- Risk factors: urinary catheter use, diabetes, recent prostate biopsy, or immunosuppression

Physical examination will typically include:

- Vital signs to assess for sepsis
- Digital rectal examination to assess for prostate tenderness (note: vigorous examination is avoided in acute prostatitis as it may worsen infection)
- Examination may be normal in chronic non-bacterial prostatitis

Investigations your doctor may arrange:

- **Midstream urine (MSU)** for culture and sensitivity — the key first test
- **STI testing** (first-catch urine for chlamydia and gonorrhoea PCR) if sexually active and at risk
- **Pre- and post-prostatic massage urine samples** — considered if chronic bacterial prostatitis is suspected (not performed in acute prostatitis)

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following:

### Pain Relief

- Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) such as ibuprofen for analgesia and to help reduce inflammation

### Antibiotic Treatment

**Mild to moderate acute prostatitis:**
- Empiric antibiotics are started while awaiting culture results
- First choice: **co-trimoxazole** 960 mg twice daily for 4 weeks
- If co-trimoxazole is contraindicated: **norfloxacin** 400 mg twice daily for 4 weeks
- Beta-lactam antibiotics are an alternative if the above are not suitable (they achieve lower prostate concentrations but may still be effective)
- **Nitrofurantoin should not be used** — it does not reach therapeutic concentrations in prostate tissue

**Severe acute prostatitis (or not tolerating oral therapy):**
- Emergency hospital assessment is arranged — intravenous antibiotics and assessment for prostate abscess may be required

**Chronic bacterial prostatitis (based on culture results):**
- First choice: **co-trimoxazole** 960 mg twice daily for 4 weeks
- Second choice: **norfloxacin** 400 mg twice daily for 4 weeks
- If chlamydia or ureaplasma: **doxycycline** 100 mg twice daily for 4 weeks
- Relapse is common even after prolonged antibiotic courses

### Additional Treatments for Chronic Prostatitis

- **Alpha-1-blockers** (e.g., doxazosin, tamsulosin) may be considered to reduce obstructive urinary symptoms and the chance of recurrence
- NSAIDs for ongoing pain management
- Evidence for other treatments in chronic non-bacterial prostatitis is limited

### If Not Responding to Treatment

- If acute bacterial prostatitis is not responding to antibiotics, your doctor will arrange urgent urology assessment to exclude abscess or other complications
- If chronic prostatitis symptoms are refractory to therapy, or if prostatic calculi (stones) are present, non-urgent urology assessment will be arranged — these patients often require individual symptom management
