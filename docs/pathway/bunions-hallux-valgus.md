# Bunions (Hallux Valgus)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of bunions (hallux valgus), including conservative treatment and when surgical referral may be appropriate.

---

## Background

A bunion is the medial prominence of the head of the first metatarsal.

Hallux valgus involves medial deviation of the first metatarsal at the tarsometatarsal joint and lateral deviation of the great toe at the metatarsophalangeal joint (MTPJ).

The lesser toes can be affected by overriding of the second toe, metatarsalgia, and hammer or claw deformities.

The cause is likely multifactorial, including shoe wearing, family history, general ligamentous laxity, and congenital anatomical factors. Factors that may worsen the condition include amputation of the second toe, short Achilles tendons, and flat feet.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- **Pain** — usually localised to the medial eminence of the bunion, often due to pressure from shoes. May spread to the second and third MTPJs as weight distribution changes.
- Functional impairment — walking distance, sport, and daily activities
- Footwear issues — difficulties purchasing and wearing footwear
- Co-morbidities — diabetes, smoking, circulatory problems, arthritis (especially rheumatoid arthritis)
- Occupation — active, standing, or sedentary work

Physical examination typically includes:

- Observing gait — looking for abnormal toe-off caused by restricted dorsiflexion
- Examining footwear for suitability, pressure points, and wear patterns
- Foot examination:
  - Standing — looking for bunion, overlying skin changes, hallux valgus, clawing, hammer toes
  - Supine — checking for callosities under the second and third metatarsal heads
  - Range of motion in ankle and subtalar joint; Achilles tightness
  - First MTPJ — tenderness, pain on movement, and whether the deviation is stiff or correctable
  - Lesser toes — whether deformities are correctable; tenderness with MTPJ motion (suggesting bursitis or Morton's neuroma)
- Neurovascular examination for peripheral neuropathy and peripheral vascular disease

If surgery is being considered, weight-bearing foot X-rays are usually arranged.

---

## Management

Most people with bunions retain good mobility with conservative management. Surgery is only considered in more severe cases.

### Conservative Management

Your doctor may consider:

- **Footwear advice** — wearing wide or large toe-box shoes, open shoes, or running shoes with a rigid heel counter. Avoiding high heels, narrow, pointed, or thin-soled shoes. Stretching shoe areas that cause pressure. Buying shoes for comfort rather than fashion.
- **Bunion protector pads, toe spacers, and toe tubes** — helpful for mild symptoms, especially if the toe is rubbing on footwear or skin irritation is present. Available from pharmacies or podiatry clinics.
- **Pain relief** — paracetamol or nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), if tolerated
- **Callus management**
- **Podiatry assessment** — for orthotics, gait analysis, footwear advice, callus management, and management of associated conditions such as metatarsalgia. Orthotics can provide good symptomatic relief by offloading the joint and reducing rotational forces on toe-off; options include longitudinal arch support, pre-metatarsal dome, and toe spacers.

### When Surgery May Be Considered

Surgery may be considered for patients with:

- Failed conservative treatment
- Persistent pain
- Inability to find comfortable footwear
- Willingness to proceed with surgery

What to know about surgery:

- Surgery may reduce pain and deformity, and may help with lesser toe problems including pain, skin issues, and ulceration
- Surgery does not stop progression of the condition
- Most patients can wear fashionable shoes after surgery
- The vast majority of patients do well after surgery; a small minority have ongoing pain, deformity, or stiffness
- Complication rate (including infection and DVT) is low
- The Scarf osteotomy (osteotomy of the first metatarsal with a soft tissue procedure) is commonly used
- Recovery involves 6 weeks in a special postoperative shoe, then a further 6 weeks in a soft shoe
- Return to work may be around 6 weeks; if standing work is required, up to 3 months off work may be needed
- Bilateral surgery is possible with adequate social support

Contraindications to surgery include poor peripheral arterial circulation, current infection, uncontrolled diabetes, and significant peripheral neuropathy.

Your doctor may refer you for an orthopaedic foot and ankle assessment if there is severe functional impairment with ulceration, or if you are requesting surgery and conservative treatment has failed.
