# Suspected B12 Deficiency

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers when to test for B12 deficiency, how to interpret results, and how treatment is guided by the underlying cause and symptoms present.

---

## Key Principle — Only Test if Deficiency is Suspected

B12 testing should not be part of routine wellness screening. Your doctor will only test if there is a clinical reason to suspect deficiency.

---

## Background

Vitamin B12 is found in animal products — fish, meat, poultry, eggs, milk, and dairy. It is absorbed in the terminal ileum and requires intrinsic factor produced by the stomach. Deficiency can result from:

- **Pernicious anaemia** (the most common cause, ~75%) — an autoimmune condition that destroys intrinsic factor-producing cells
- **Malabsorption** from gastrointestinal disease (Crohn's disease, coeliac disease, gastrectomy, ileal resection)
- **Medications** — prolonged proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) or metformin
- **Dietary insufficiency** — vegans, chronic alcoholics, older adults with poor diet

Serum B12 is an imperfect measure of body stores — levels don't stay in a narrow range, making interpretation difficult. A low level doesn't always mean true deficiency, and a normal level doesn't always rule it out.

---

## When Your Doctor Will Test

**Common indications for B12 testing:**

- Unexplained anaemia or macrocytosis (large red blood cells)
- Neurological or neuropsychiatric symptoms — tingling, numbness, weakness, peripheral neuropathy, unexplained dementia
- Gastrointestinal conditions — glossitis, Crohn's disease, coeliac disease, previous gastric surgery
- Long-term vegan or vegetarian diet
- Prolonged use of metformin, PPIs, or H2-receptor antagonists
- Age over 75 years
- History of autoimmune disease (thyroid disease, type 1 diabetes, coeliac disease)
- Pregnancy or breastfeeding where deficiency is suspected
- 6 months after starting B12 replacement (to confirm adequacy)

**Testing is avoided:**

- In asymptomatic patients or those with only non-specific symptoms
- More often than every 6 months
- In patients on long-term B12 supplementation (unless compliance is in question)
- During acute illness or acute liver disease (can cause falsely elevated levels)

---

## Investigations Your Doctor May Arrange

- **Serum vitamin B12**
- **Full blood count (FBC) with blood film** — to look for haematological signs of deficiency:
  - Macrocytosis (may appear before anaemia)
  - Macrocytic anaemia
  - Hypersegmented neutrophils
  - Pancytopenia (in severe cases)
- **Serum folate** — only if specific indications are present (malnutrition, malabsorption, certain medications, unexplained macrocytosis)
- **Additional tests as indicated:** liver function tests, iron studies, TSH

---

## Interpreting B12 Results

| Level (pmol/L) | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Less than 110 | Deficiency likely — FBC and blood film should be checked |
| 110–170 | Low-normal / inconclusive — clinical correlation required; may or may not represent true deficiency |
| Greater than 170 | Normal or raised — if deficiency still strongly suspected, specialist advice needed |

A level between 110 and 170 is common and does not confirm deficiency on its own. Your doctor will consider your symptoms, diet, and other test results together.

---

## What Your Doctor Will Ask About

- **Symptoms:** fatigue, tingling or numbness in hands/feet, weakness, memory problems, depression, glossitis (sore tongue), loss of taste or smell, dizziness on standing
- **Diet:** animal product intake, vegan/vegetarian status, alcohol use
- **Medications:** metformin, PPIs, H2-blockers, combined oral contraceptive pill, nitrous oxide exposure (recreational)
- **Medical history:** autoimmune conditions, gastrointestinal disease or surgery, family history of B12 deficiency or malabsorption
- **Pregnancy/breastfeeding status**

---

## Causes to Be Aware Of

### Pernicious Anaemia

An autoimmune condition causing failure of intrinsic factor production. More common in European and Indian populations. About half of affected patients test positive for intrinsic factor antibody (which, if positive, is virtually diagnostic). Patients have a higher lifetime risk of gastric cancer and thyroid failure.

Your doctor may suspect pernicious anaemia if:
- B12 is low despite adequate dietary intake
- You have a personal or family history of autoimmune conditions (thyroid disease, vitiligo, type 1 diabetes)

### Medication-related

- **PPIs and H2-blockers:** Reduce stomach acid, impairing B12 release from food
- **Metformin:** May reduce B12 absorption
- **Combined oral contraceptive pill:** Can cause falsely low B12 (usually not clinically significant)
- **Nitrous oxide (recreational):** Inactivates B12 — can cause rapid-onset functional B12 deficiency, megaloblastic anaemia, and peripheral neuropathy. Should be considered in young people with rapidly developing B12 deficiency signs

### Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

Infant B12 deficiency is a serious risk in vegan or vegetarian mothers. Infant tolerance to B12 deficiency is much lower than in adults. Breastfed infants of vegans are at significant risk even if the mother appears well.

---

## Further Tests Your Doctor May Arrange

- **Intrinsic factor antibody** — low sensitivity (50–60%) but high specificity; a positive result is virtually diagnostic of pernicious anaemia. Checked if anaemia, neuropathy, or glossitis is present, or if low/borderline B12 with no other identified cause
- **Parietal cell antibodies** — not routinely requested (less specific; a positive result alone is not diagnostic)
- **Functional markers** (methylmalonic acid, holotranscobalamin, homocysteine) — specialist-guided testing for complex or inconclusive cases

---

## Management

Treatment depends on the severity of symptoms and the underlying cause. Your doctor will direct the appropriate approach.

### With Neurological Symptoms

- **Loading:** Weekly hydroxocobalamin 1 mg injections (intramuscular) for 4–5 weeks
- **Expected response:** Improvement in how you feel within 1–2 days; FBC normalises within 8 weeks; neurological symptoms should improve within 6–12 weeks. If there is no improvement, B12 deficiency is unlikely to be the cause
- **Maintenance** (if cause is irreversible): Lifelong hydroxocobalamin 1 mg IM every 2–3 months

### With Non-Neurological Symptoms

- **Trial of treatment:** Two hydroxocobalamin 1 mg IM injections, 1–2 weeks apart; response assessed at 4–8 weeks
- **Maintenance** if there is a clear response and an irreversible underlying cause

### Without Symptoms (Confirmed Deficiency)

- **Treat the underlying cause** first
- **Consider maintenance treatment** if the cause cannot be corrected

### Oral B12 as an Alternative

Oral vitamin B12 (cyanocobalamin) may be considered as an alternative to injections in patients with good compliance and a functioning gastrointestinal tract. Approximately 1% of B12 is absorbed passively (without intrinsic factor).

- **Pernicious anaemia:** 1000 micrograms daily
- **Dietary deficiency:** 50–200 micrograms daily
- Oral B12 is not subsidised in New Zealand but is available over the counter
- Vitamin B complex tablets do **not** contain B12

---

## Addressing the Underlying Cause

- **Dietary:** Advise on B12 sources (animal products, Marmite, fortified plant milks). If vegan/vegetarian, oral supplementation is recommended
- **Pernicious anaemia:** Lifelong maintenance injections; gastroscopy referral for surveillance; monitor for iron deficiency and thyroid disease
- **Malabsorption (coeliac, Crohn's, bariatric surgery):** Lifelong monitoring may be needed; check iron and folate; address the gastrointestinal condition
- **Medications:** Consider stopping or switching the contributing medication where possible (e.g. reviewing PPI need, switching metformin)

---

## Monitoring After Treatment

- **Re-test B12** 6 months after starting oral or parenteral replacement to confirm adequacy
- **Re-test if no improvement** 12 weeks after an IM loading course
- Do not re-test more frequently than every 6 months (stores take years to deplete; a less than 20% change between tests is likely biological variation)
- Routine ongoing monitoring is not needed on stable long-term replacement unless there are compliance concerns

---

## When to Seek Specialist Input

Your doctor will seek specialist advice if:

- Severe neurological symptoms are present
- B12 status remains inconclusive (normal-low level with suggestive symptoms)
- Pernicious anaemia is suspected but intrinsic factor antibody is negative
- Functional marker testing (methylmalonic acid, holoTC, homocysteine) is needed
- Suspected malabsorption or gastrointestinal pathology requiring further investigation

Specialist referrals may include gastroenterology (for gastroscopy in pernicious anaemia, or malabsorption workup), neurology, haematology, or dietitian.
