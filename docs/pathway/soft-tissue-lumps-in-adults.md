# Soft Tissue Lumps in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to soft tissue lumps in the trunk and limbs in adults aged 15 years and over. The key aim is to detect soft tissue sarcoma early, as delayed diagnosis is common and early treatment improves outcomes.

---

## Red Flags — See a Doctor Promptly

A new, unexplained soft tissue mass with any of the following features requires urgent medical assessment to rule out sarcoma:

- Deep to deep fascia (i.e., fixed or tethered), hard, or irregular — of any size
- Larger than 5 cm
- Changing or growing rapidly
- Painful
- Recurring after a previous sarcoma excision
- Increasing abdominal girth, fullness, or lump (may suggest retroperitoneal sarcoma)

**Important:** Most sarcomas are painless. Pain may suggest nerve or bone involvement but its absence does not rule out malignancy.

---

## Background

- Most soft tissue lumps in general practice are benign — lipomas are the most common type.
- Less than 1% of subcutaneous lumps seen in general practice are malignant. If red flags are present, about 10% are malignant.
- Soft tissue sarcomas are uncommon (less than 1% of all malignancies), but a doctor may only see 1–2 cases every 20 years — so maintaining a high index of suspicion is essential.
- Sarcomas occur throughout the body, but 46% are found in the thigh, buttock, and groin area.
- Delayed diagnosis of sarcomas is common. Early detection and referral are crucial to achieving the best prognosis.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will typically:

1. **Take a history** — including how long the lump has been present, how fast it has grown, and any associated symptoms. Most sarcomas are asymptomatic, but local pain or pressure symptoms can occur as the tumour expands.

2. **Check risk factors for sarcoma** — most patients who develop sarcoma have no identifiable risk factors, but risk is increased with:
   - Past history of malignancy
   - Previous radiotherapy
   - Exposure to certain chemicals (e.g., arsenic, vinyl chloride, phenoxy herbicides, chlorophenol-containing wood preservatives)
   - Neurofibromatosis
   - HIV

3. **Examine the lump:**
   - Record location, size, and texture
   - Assess whether the lump is superficial or deep to fascia — your doctor may ask you to tense and relax the muscles to check if the lump moves freely or becomes fixed
   - Check for surrounding swelling or signs of nerve involvement

4. **Consider possible causes**, including:
   - Lipoma (most common)
   - Haematoma — note that many sarcoma patients recall a prior trauma that was likely unrelated to the lump
   - Abscess or reactive/infective cause
   - Ganglion cyst
   - Baker's cyst (behind the knee)
   - Reactive lymphadenopathy
   - Epidermoid cyst
   - Hernia

5. **Investigations:**
   - If red flags are present, or any previous imaging suggests malignancy, your doctor will arrange both X-ray and ultrasound urgently, and refer to orthopaedics without waiting for results.
   - **Do not arrange FNA (fine needle aspiration) for suspected sarcoma.** FNA cannot diagnose sarcoma — diagnosis always requires guided core biopsy performed by a tumour surgeon.
   - Ultrasound cannot confirm or rule out sarcoma (it cannot distinguish benign from malignant). MRI is the preferred imaging when detailed characterisation is needed. Ultrasound may be useful to determine whether a lump is present, superficial or deep to fascia, or solid versus cystic (e.g., ganglion).
   - If the lump is superficial, mobile, and sarcoma is unlikely, your doctor may consider excision biopsy to give a definitive tissue diagnosis.

---

## Management

### If Red Flags Are Present or Imaging Raises Concern

Your doctor will:
- Arrange urgent X-ray and ultrasound concurrently
- Refer urgently to orthopaedics (does not wait for imaging results)
- Not arrange FNA, biopsy, or other imaging — the orthopaedic service determines further investigations and management

If an ultrasound shows any of the following, urgent orthopaedic referral is arranged:
- Mass deep to fascia or involving fascia
- Any mass with vascularity or solid components
- Any concerning radiological features
- Recommendation from the radiologist to refer

### If No Concerning Features

Your doctor may consider:
- **Excision biopsy** if the lump is superficial and mobile — this gives a definitive tissue diagnosis
- **Routine ultrasound** only if accepted criteria are met:
  - Superficial, mobile mass >5 cm with uncertain diagnosis, or
  - Mass <5 cm with uncertainty about whether it is deep to fascia
- **Watchful waiting** — if not being investigated or referred, you should return immediately if the lump grows, becomes painful, or if you have other concerns. A review at 3 months is recommended if there is any concern.

### Lymph Nodes

- If a malignant lymph node is suspected (and sarcoma is not a concern), your doctor may request open biopsy via a general surgery referral.
- If you have known cancer and a metastatic node is suspected, your doctor will arrange FNA by a pathologist and refer to the relevant specialist.
