# Infectious Keratitis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Infectious keratitis is a serious, sight-threatening infection or inflammation of the cornea. It is the most common reason for ophthalmology hospital admission. This condition always requires urgent specialist assessment — your doctor will arrange this.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

Tell your doctor immediately or go to an emergency department if you have:

- A painful, red eye and you wear contact lenses
- A painful, red eye with corneal cloudiness, haze, or a white spot on the eye
- Severe eye pain that seems out of proportion to how the eye looks (especially in contact lens wearers)
- Significantly reduced or blurred vision

---

## Background

Infectious keratitis is inflammation or infection of the cornea (the clear front surface of the eye). It is often called a "corneal ulcer," though strictly speaking keratitis refers to the infection or inflammation itself, while an ulcer means there is actual loss of corneal tissue.

**Common causes include:**

- Bacterial infection — most often related to contact lens use. *Pseudomonas aeruginosa* is common in contact lens wearers.
- Viral infection (e.g. herpes simplex — see also Herpes Simplex Keratitis)
- Fungal or protozoal infection (e.g. *Acanthamoeba*, which can cause severe pain out of proportion to visible signs)
- *Neisseria gonorrhoea* and *Haemophilus influenzae* are unusual in that they can infect even healthy, intact corneal tissue

The condition is usually triggered by something that disrupts the corneal surface — trauma, contact lens wear, tear film problems, or eyelid disease.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Onset and progression of symptoms — typically a 1 to 3 day acute history starting with sharp pain, redness, and light sensitivity, progressing to severe pain and often decreasing vision
- Whether you wear contact lenses or have had any recent eye trauma
- Use of over-the-counter eye drops, including any antibiotic eye drops
- History of previous corneal disease, rosacea, or recent use of steroid drops or creams near the eye (e.g. for facial eczema)

Physical examination typically includes:

- **Visual acuity** — measured in each eye separately. Central corneal involvement usually reduces vision; peripheral changes may leave vision intact
- Full eye assessment including examining the lids and external eye
- Looking for signs of keratitis:
  - Severely red eye (ciliary flush), swollen eyelids, and discharge
  - Corneal haze, opacity, or thickening — examined with a slit lamp or direct ophthalmoscope
  - A corneal infiltrate with a matching area of fluorescein staining over it
  - In severe cases, a level of pus inside the eye (hypopyon) may be visible

---

## Management

Infectious keratitis is a specialist emergency — your doctor will arrange urgent ophthalmology assessment for all suspected cases.

**While awaiting specialist review, your doctor may:**

- Advise you to wear dark glasses to reduce light sensitivity
- Provide pain relief — oral or a short-acting topical anaesthetic eye drop to allow examination (topical anaesthetics are only used to aid examination and are never prescribed for ongoing use at home)

**Important steps your doctor will take:**

- **No antibiotics will be started** before specialist review — this is deliberate, as starting antibiotics prevents a useful culture from being taken
- **No eye patching** — pressure patching can worsen *Pseudomonas* infection
- If you wear contact lenses: lenses will be removed and you must not reinsert them. Do not clean or throw away your contact lenses — keep them for the ophthalmologist, as they may wish to send them for culture

**After specialist assessment, further management may include:**

- A corneal scrape if an ulcer is present (to identify the organism)
- Avoiding contact lens use for at least 7–10 days
- Topical antibiotic eye drops, initially given very frequently (e.g. every hour, then every 2 hours)

**If you wear contact lenses with significant pain but no visible corneal changes**, your doctor will discuss *Acanthamoeba* keratitis with the on-call ophthalmology registrar, as this infection can cause severe pain with few early signs.

**If you wear contact lenses with only mild symptoms and no corneal infiltrate or staining**, your doctor may advise:

- Stop contact lens use immediately and discard any opened lens solutions and cases
- See an optometrist as soon as possible for a slit lamp examination
