# Preterm and Low-Birth-Weight Infants

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the ongoing care of preterm (born before 37 weeks) and low-birth-weight (under 2500 g) infants in the community after hospital discharge.

---

## Background

- **Preterm** infants are born before 37 completed weeks of gestation:
  - *Late preterm* — 32 to 36+6 weeks
  - *Very preterm* — 28 to 31+6 weeks
  - *Extremely preterm* — before 28 weeks
- Preterm infants are at increased risk of poor physical and neurodevelopmental outcomes, including blindness, deafness, and cerebral palsy.
- **Low birth weight** is defined as under 2500 g at birth. An infant can be low birth weight due to preterm birth, small for gestational age (birth weight at or below the 10th percentile), or both.
- Risk factors for low birth weight include: Māori ethnicity (disproportionately higher preterm birth rates), low socio-economic status, maternal age under 20 or over 35 years, and smoking.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

Contact your doctor or seek emergency care if your baby shows:

- Developmental regression (losing skills they previously had)
- Any symptoms suggesting a serious underlying medical condition
- Feeding problems or poor growth associated with signs of dehydration (dry mouth, sunken fontanelle, fewer than 4 wet nappies per day, no tears)
- New visual symptoms or squinting (strabismus) in any infant with a history of retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) or who was born before 30 weeks or under 1250 g

## Assessment

### Corrected Age

Your doctor will use **corrected age** (not chronological age) when plotting growth and reviewing developmental milestones for infants born before 37 weeks.

Corrected age = chronological age minus the number of weeks born early.

*Example: an infant who is 20 weeks since birth, born at 32 weeks gestation (8 weeks early), has a corrected age of 12 weeks.*

Corrected age is used for growth plotting until 1 year of age (for infants born before 37 weeks).

### Growth

Your doctor will plot weight, height, and head circumference at each visit. Key points:

- After the expected birth date (42 weeks), growth is plotted on standard charts using corrected age.
- If weight is falling and crossing more than one centile line, your doctor will review feeding and look for causes of faltering growth.
- Catch-up growth normally occurs within the first year. If rapid weight gain occurs after the first year, your doctor will monitor for increased risk of diabetes later in life.

### Feeding

Your doctor will ask about:

- Timing, frequency, and tolerance of feeds
- Breastfeeding latch (if breastfed) and whether growth is adequate
- If bottle fed: volume (standard is 150 mL/kg per day at term; growth-restricted infants may need up to 180 mL/kg per day) and whether formula is being prepared correctly
- Use of breast milk fortifier (sometimes started in hospital)

Signs that may suggest gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD) — which is more common in preterm infants:

- Refusal to feed
- Weight loss or crossing percentiles
- Blood in vomit
- Swallowing difficulties
- Chronic cough or wheeze
- Apnoea episodes
- Significant distress after feeds, with back arching

### Development

Your doctor will look for early signs of developmental problems, including:

- Delays in developmental milestones (using corrected age)
- Problems with gaze fixing or following — from term to 4 weeks corrected age, infants fix on faces and startle at noise; from 4 to 8 weeks corrected age, they smile, vocalise, fix and follow with their eyes, and briefly raise their head when prone
- Persistent feeding problems (including texture hypersensitivity)
- Abnormal muscle tone (too stiff or too floppy)

Some infants who were in the Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) will continue to have follow-up with a paediatrician or multidisciplinary team.

### Sleep

Your doctor will ask about the sleep environment to check parents are aware of safe sleeping practices. Preterm infants have shorter sleep cycles and need time to learn to self-settle.

### Bowels

Your doctor will review stool frequency and consistency. Note that iron supplements and Gaviscon can both make stools darker and firmer.

Premature infants are at higher risk of necrotising enterocolitis (NEC). Infants who had NEC as a neonate are at increased risk of under-nutrition, poor growth, gastrointestinal complications, and recurrent hospitalisation.

### Vision

Retinopathy of prematurity (ROP) is an eye problem that occurs in some premature infants. It is more common in infants with birth weight under 1250 g or born before 30 weeks.

Your doctor will check that an eye examination has been arranged. Some cases resolve on their own; severe cases can threaten sight and require laser treatment.

### Hearing and Undescended Testes

Your doctor will check that newborn hearing screening has been completed and will assess for undescended testes in male infants.

### Social History

Your doctor will ask about social and financial support, the home environment (including household smoking, overcrowding, cold or damp housing), and screen for family violence where appropriate. Screening for postnatal depression is also recommended.

## Management

### Medications

Infants born before 37 weeks or under 2500 g are often discharged on medications that require ongoing prescribing by your doctor:

- **Iron (ferrous sulfate oral liquid):** 0.5–1 mL/kg/day (approximately 3 mg/kg/day of elemental iron), usually in two divided doses. Continued until 12 months corrected age. Iron can make stools darker and firmer — this is normal.
- **Vitamin D:** 1 drop per day, continued until 12 months of age.

Your doctor will adjust doses with weight checks as your baby grows.

### Feeding and Growth

Your doctor may consider:

- Introducing solids between 3 months corrected and 7 months uncorrected age — similar to full-term infants. Early introduction of iron-rich foods is important as preterm infants have lower iron stores.
- Addressing lactation insufficiency or other breastfeeding problems if breastfeeding and growth is not optimal
- Ensuring formula volume and preparation are correct if bottle fed
- Managing GORD if present

If there are significant feeding problems, faltering growth, or suspected swallowing difficulties, your doctor will refer to a paediatrician, speech language therapist, or dietitian as appropriate.

### Sleep Safety

Your doctor may advise the following safe sleeping practices (PEPE guidelines):

- Place baby in their own baby bed in the same room as the parent or caregiver
- Keep the sleeping environment smokefree
- Position baby flat on their back to sleep, with face clear of bedding
- Encourage and support exclusive breastfeeding

If there are concerns about the sleep environment in a high-risk family, your doctor may refer to a safe sleep coordinator.

### Constipation

Your doctor may manage constipation using the standard approach for infants, taking into account the effect of iron supplements on bowel habits.

### Immunisations

Immunisations are given based on **chronological age** (not corrected age):

- All infants start immunisations at 6 weeks of age as per the national schedule.
- There are no weight or gestation criteria to delay immunisation (except BCG, which requires 34 weeks post-conceptual age).
- Preterm infants are more vulnerable to infections and their complications — keeping immunisations on schedule is particularly important.
- Preterm infants receive the same vaccine dose as term infants.

Additional immunisations your doctor may arrange:

- **Pertussis (whooping cough) vaccine** for parents and primary caregivers of infants admitted to NICU or SCBU for more than 3 days (if the mother was not vaccinated at least 14 days before birth)
- **Extended pneumococcal programme** for infants born before 28 weeks: 3-dose primary series at 6 weeks, 3 months, and 5 months, with a booster at 12 months
- **23PPV pneumococcal vaccine** for children over 2 years who were born before 28 weeks: two doses 5 years apart
- **Influenza vaccination** for parents and primary caregivers is encouraged

### Vision Follow-Up

- At 2.5 years of age, children born under 1250 g or before 30 weeks who did not have ROP will have a prearranged ophthalmology review, as some will develop squinting (strabismus) or other visual problems.
- If new visual symptoms or strabismus develop in any child with a history of ROP or preterm/low birth weight, seek urgent ophthalmology assessment.

### Developmental Follow-Up

- Infants born **after 32 weeks** are usually followed up in general practice according to the routine Well Child health check schedule.
- Infants born **before 32 weeks** are usually followed up by a paediatrician or multidisciplinary team.
- Your doctor will refer for urgent paediatric assessment if there is developmental regression or symptoms suggesting a serious medical condition.
- Your doctor will refer for non-acute paediatric assessment if new developmental concerns arise.

### Support for Parents

- If postnatal depression is identified, your doctor will follow the perinatal mental health pathway.
- Your doctor may provide information about normal emotional responses after having a preterm infant and coping strategies.
