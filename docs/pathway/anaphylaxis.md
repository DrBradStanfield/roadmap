# Anaphylaxis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the diagnosis and emergency management of anaphylaxis in adults and children.

> **Always discuss with your doctor or call emergency services (111) if you suspect anaphylaxis.** Anaphylaxis is a life-threatening emergency. The information below is for educational understanding only.

---

## Recognising Anaphylaxis — When to Call an Ambulance

Call 111 immediately if there is sudden onset and rapid progression of:

**Airway problems (most urgent):**
- Stridor (high-pitched noise when breathing)
- Significant tongue or upper airway swelling (angioedema)
- Hoarse voice
- Feeling as though the throat is closing up

**Breathing problems:**
- Difficulty breathing
- Wheeze or bronchospasm
- Low oxygen levels
- Confusion

**Circulation problems:**
- Low blood pressure (in adults, usually systolic BP < 80 mmHg)
- Collapse or fainting
- Pale, clammy skin
- Fast heart rate

**Other features:**
- Abdominal cramps or pain, vomiting, diarrhoea
- Skin or mucosal changes: itching, urticaria (hives), flushing, angio-oedema

> Note: Skin changes alone are not sufficient for an anaphylaxis diagnosis, and can be subtle or absent in up to 20% of reactions.

---

## Distinguishing Anaphylaxis from Mild-to-Moderate Allergic Reaction

Skin, oral, and gastrointestinal symptoms (generalised itch, redness, hives, lip/eye swelling, tingling mouth, abdominal cramps, vomiting) are:
- **Often present in anaphylaxis**, but not required for the diagnosis
- **Likely a mild-to-moderate allergic reaction** (not anaphylaxis) if: not triggered by an insect sting or injected medication, and there is no respiratory or circulatory involvement

Other diagnoses to consider: vasovagal episode, severe asthma, panic attack, heart failure, hereditary angio-oedema.

---

## Immediate Management

> **This is emergency medicine. Your doctor will manage this. The information below explains what treatment involves.**

### The Most Important Drug: IM Adrenaline

The most important drug for anaphylaxis is **intramuscular (IM) adrenaline**. It acts within 1–2 minutes. Antihistamines and steroids are second-line only.

Adrenaline is given into the **mid-anterolateral thigh** (outer thigh). The buttocks should not be used.

**IM Adrenaline Dose (using 1:1000 formulation, i.e., 1 mg/mL):**

Dose is 0.01 mL/kg (minimum 0.1 mL, maximum 0.5 mL regardless of age).

By age (if weight unknown or child under 10 kg):

| Age | Volume of 1:1000 adrenaline |
|---|---|
| Under 2 years | 0.1 mL |
| 2–4 years | 0.2 mL |
| 5–11 years | 0.3 mL |
| 12 years and over | 0.5 mL* |
| Adult | 0.5 mL |

*Use 0.3 mL if small or prepubertal child.

Repeat every 5–10 minutes if breathing or circulation problems continue.

### Adrenaline Auto-injectors (EpiPen)

If an auto-injector is available:

| Weight | Device |
|---|---|
| 10–20 kg | EpiPen Jr — 150 micrograms (green label) |
| 20 kg or more | EpiPen — 300 micrograms (yellow label) |

EpiPen Junior can also be used for infants from 7.5 kg.

### IV Fluids

If there is circulatory compromise or shock, IV fluid is given:
- Adults: 500–1000 mL normal saline
- Children: 20 mL/kg

### Second-Line Medications

After adrenaline and IV fluids, your doctor may add:

- **Antihistamines** (cetirizine or loratadine) — for itch, hives, or angio-oedema. Adult dose: 20 mg daily for 3 days.
- **Steroids** (oral prednisone 2 mg/kg, maximum 40 mg, for 3 days) — may help prevent or shorten a prolonged reaction; also useful for bronchospasm.
- **Bronchodilators** — for persistent wheezing or bronchospasm.

### Monitoring

At least 4 hours of monitoring is required after anaphylaxis. Biphasic reactions (return of symptoms 6–24 hours after the initial attack) can occur in a minority of patients. Most patients are discharged with a short course of oral antihistamines and steroids.

Your doctor may collect blood to measure **tryptase** (released by activated mast cells). Peak levels occur 1–2 hours after anaphylaxis onset, returning to normal by 6–8 hours. This helps confirm the diagnosis if uncertain.

## Prevention and Long-Term Follow-up

Your doctor will assess whether you need:

### Adrenaline Auto-injector (EpiPen) Prescription

EpiPen is considered when the trigger is unknown or cannot be reliably avoided (food allergy, venom allergy). It is generally not prescribed for drug-induced anaphylaxis, where avoidance is the strategy.

EpiPen is a restricted medication in New Zealand, funded with a special authority prescription. It can also be purchased over the counter at pharmacies.

Your doctor or nurse will teach you how to use the EpiPen correctly with a trainer device.

### Identifying the Trigger

Your doctor will refer you to an allergy or immunology specialist to confirm the trigger through testing. Note: skin testing may be falsely negative within 4 weeks of a reaction — testing may be delayed, but referral should not be.

### If the Cause Was a Medication

- The reaction will be recorded in your medical file
- You should avoid all medications in the same class
- Be aware of potentially cross-reactive drugs (e.g., cephalosporins if penicillin-allergic)
- Your doctor will notify the NZ Pharmacovigilance Centre

### Ongoing Precautions

- Carry your EpiPen at all times
- Consider a **MedicAlert bracelet**
- Follow a personalised ASCIA Anaphylaxis Action Plan (your doctor will provide this; children should have a copy at school or childcare)
- If the trigger is food: read labels carefully, avoid only the specific allergen (not broader food families unnecessarily)
- If the trigger is insect venom: venom desensitisation (immunotherapy) may be available — discuss with your specialist
