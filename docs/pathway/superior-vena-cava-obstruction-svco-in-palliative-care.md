# Superior Vena Cava Obstruction (SVCO) in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway applies to adult oncology and palliative care patients. SVCO is a medical emergency. Always discuss assessment and management with your doctor or palliative care team.

---

## What Is SVCO?

Superior vena cava obstruction (SVCO) occurs when blood flow through the superior vena cava is blocked by external compression, thrombosis, or direct tumour invasion. It may come on suddenly or develop gradually as worsening breathlessness.

The most common cause is lung cancer. Less common causes include lymphoma and other malignancies.

---

## Symptoms — Seek Emergency Care

SVCO should be treated as a medical emergency. Tell your doctor or call for help immediately if you notice:

- Breathlessness (most common)
- Swelling of the face, neck, or arms
- Headache or head fullness — worse when bending forward, stooping, or lying down
- Distended veins in the neck or chest
- Visual changes or dizziness
- Cough, chest pain, or difficulty swallowing
- Stridor (noisy breathing), cyanosis (blue discolouration), or rapidly increasing breathing rate

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess for symptoms and signs of SVCO, including:

- Facial oedema and distended neck or chest veins (most common signs)
- Conjunctival or periorbital oedema
- Facial plethora (redness/fullness)
- Swelling of the hands or arms
- Dilated collateral veins in the arms

---

## Management

Management depends on whether the goal is active treatment or comfort care.

### If active treatment is appropriate

Your doctor will treat this as an acute medical emergency and arrange urgent hospital assessment. Urgent stenting and radiotherapy may be needed to prevent respiratory failure.

While awaiting hospital transfer, your doctor may:

- Advise loosening restrictive clothing and resting upper arms on pillows
- If the tumour is steroid-sensitive (e.g. lymphoma) or laryngeal oedema is present, prescribe steroids:
  - **Dexamethasone 16 mg** immediately — given orally, or by injection if tablets cannot be swallowed. Given subcutaneously in 2 separate boluses at separate sites (administered slowly, as it can sting).
  - Alternatively: intravenous hydrocortisone 400 mg, or oral prednisone 100 mg.
  - Note: in palliative care, some doses and routes may be used in ways not officially approved by the medicines regulator.
- Offer benzodiazepines and opioids for comfort as for other acute palliative care crises.

### If the patient is in the last days of life and no further intervention is wanted

Your doctor will focus on comfort and symptom relief, and will seek specialist palliative care support and advice. The emphasis shifts to managing distressing symptoms — breathlessness, anxiety, and pain — rather than disease-directed treatment.

---

## Working with Your Doctor

SVCO is a specialist-managed emergency. Your doctor will arrange urgent oncology or general medicine assessment, or contact the palliative care team depending on the patient's goals. Decisions about treatment versus comfort care should be made in discussion with the patient and their family/whānau.

---

## Key Points

- SVCO is an oncological emergency — it can cause respiratory failure if untreated
- Most common cause is lung cancer; lymphoma is also common and is steroid-sensitive
- If the patient is in their last days of life and wishes no further intervention, the focus shifts to comfort care with palliative support
- Always discuss SVCO urgently with your doctor
