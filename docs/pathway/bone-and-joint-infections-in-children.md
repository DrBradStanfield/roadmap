# Bone and Joint Infections in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to possible bone or joint infections in children, including septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, and important differential diagnoses such as acute rheumatic fever.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

Take your child to the emergency department or call an ambulance if they have any of the following:

- Severe limb pain
- Refusal to bear weight or move a limb
- In a younger child, refusal to use a joint (pseudoparalysis — the limb hangs limp as if paralysed)
- Toxic or very unwell appearance

**Do not wait for blood tests or X-rays if red flags are present. Do not start antibiotics before specialist assessment.**

---

## Background

### Septic Arthritis

- Affects the lower limb joints in 80% of cases, especially the hip and knee
- Presentation varies by age:
  - **Infants:** May present with sepsis (irritability, poor feeding) or pseudoparalysis
  - **Toddlers:** May have only a limp and mild fever
  - **Older children:** More likely to have localised symptoms
- Typical presentation: acute onset of limp, not bearing weight, or refusing to use the limb, with pain on movement and at rest
- May have soft tissue redness or swelling over joints (but not usually visible over the hip)
- Pain and fever worsen over time

### Osteomyelitis

- Often clinically indistinguishable from septic arthritis
- Ranges from an acutely unwell child with high fever, to an afebrile child with a low-grade fever and gradual onset of limp or refusal to use a limb
- Spondylodiscitis (spinal osteomyelitis) is uncommon — presents in infants and toddlers with deteriorating mobility, pain on handling, and low-grade fevers

### Acute Rheumatic Fever

An important differential diagnosis — especially in New Zealand for children of Māori or Pacific Island ethnicity.

| Disease | Māori (per 100,000) | Pacific Island (per 100,000) | Other (per 100,000) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acute rheumatic fever | 40.2 | 81.2 | 2.1 |
| Septic arthritis | 23.25 | 11.41 | 6.25 |

Consider acute rheumatic fever in any child with acute onset limp, refusal to use a limb, or joint pain or swelling — particularly if Māori or Pacific Island ethnicity.

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, or acute rheumatic fever in a child who presents with:

- A limp
- Single, painful, or swollen limb
- Unable to bear weight or needing to be carried
- Unable to move a limb normally (in infants not yet mobile) — i.e., pseudoparalysis
- Unwell with a fever but no obvious cause
- Skin infection that is slow to resolve, especially if over a joint

**Note:** It is not important to distinguish between septic arthritis, osteomyelitis, and rheumatic fever in general practice. Acute specialist assessment is required regardless.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Sudden or gradual onset
- Any preceding trauma (most children will have had some recent minor trauma — it may or may not be responsible)
- Night waking with pain:
  - Growing pains occur at night after activity, but the child is well during the day without a limp; pains settle in 30–40 minutes
  - With septic arthritis or osteomyelitis, pain wakes the child and also causes significant daytime pain that interferes with activities
- Pain at rest
- Other painful or swollen joints
- Recent sore throat (especially important if the child is Māori or Pacific Island ethnicity, given the risk of rheumatic fever)
- Family history of rheumatic fever or rheumatic heart disease

### Examination

Your doctor will:

- Look for fever (significant if above 38°C), rash, irritability, and tachycardia — and check all joints
  - Note: absence of fever does not exclude septic arthritis or osteomyelitis
- Examine the affected joint or limb for swelling and warmth, comparing both sides, and check active and passive movement

### Investigations

Your doctor will usually make the diagnosis clinically — routine blood tests or X-rays are not arranged first, as this may cause unsafe delays.

- If there is a clear history of trauma, no fever, and a fracture is the most likely diagnosis, an X-ray may be arranged
- If blood tests have already been done, an elevated FBC, CRP, or ESR supports the diagnosis
- If an X-ray has been done, it may be normal, or may show a joint effusion (suggesting septic arthritis) or bony erosion (a late sign of osteomyelitis)
- A normal X-ray or blood test does not exclude septic arthritis or osteomyelitis
- Further investigations (joint ultrasound, diagnostic aspiration) are done in hospital

### Differential Diagnoses

Your doctor will also consider:

- Malignancy (leukaemia, osteosarcoma)
- Acute rheumatic fever
- Slipped upper femoral epiphysis (SUFE)
- Acute juvenile arthritis

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following:

- **If sepsis is suspected:** Managed as a medical emergency — see the Sepsis in Children pathway
- **If red flags are present, or septic arthritis or osteomyelitis is suspected, or there is acute onset of a swollen joint:** Urgent paediatric orthopaedic specialist assessment is needed. If there has been any contact with methicillin-resistant *Staphylococcus aureus* (MRSA), this should be included in the referral as it affects antibiotic choice
- **If rheumatic fever is suspected:** Urgent paediatric medical assessment is needed. NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen or naproxen) should NOT be given to children at high risk of rheumatic fever (Māori or Pacific Island ethnicity), as these medications may mask the evolution of joint symptoms
- **If other conditions are suspected** (e.g., acute juvenile arthritis, leukaemia): Urgent paediatric medical assessment is needed
- **If osteosarcoma is suggested** by clinical presentation or X-ray: Urgent paediatric orthopaedic assessment is needed
- **If unsure about the diagnosis:** Emergency department assessment and investigation

**Important:** Do not delay specialist assessment by arranging blood tests or X-rays, and do not start oral antibiotics before assessment if red flags are present.
