# Nasal Fracture

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to a broken nose — assessing severity, identifying complications, and determining next steps.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if:

- Compound nasal fracture (bone breaking through the skin)
- Other facial fractures (cheekbone or upper jaw)
- Suspicion of septal haematoma (boggy or cherry-red swelling inside the nose on both sides of the dividing wall)
- Clear watery fluid running from the nose (may indicate a skull base fracture)
- Nosebleed that does not stop with simple first aid measures

---

## Background

- Nasal fractures are usually caused by a direct blow to the nose.
- Symptoms include pain, nosebleed, swelling, a crunching sensation, deformity, and nose mobility.
- Swelling may worsen up to day 5 then gradually improve over the following 10 days — deformity may not be obvious until swelling settles.
- If the nose remains obviously deviated or a nasal passage stays blocked after swelling resolves, a procedure called manipulation under anaesthetic may be needed.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you by:

1. **Controlling any bleeding** — firmly pinching the fleshy part of the nose just below the nasal bone; a cold pack on the bridge of the nose can also help.

2. **Taking a history**, including:
   - How the injury happened and when
   - Whether there was immediate deformity before swelling developed
   - Any new blockage of one or both nostrils
   - Any previous nose injury or pre-existing deformity
   - Use of blood-thinning medications (anticoagulants)
   - Date of last tetanus vaccination

3. **Examining for:**
   - Septal haematoma (a blood clot inside the nose that, if untreated, can destroy the nasal cartilage and cause the nose to collapse)
   - Signs of airway compromise
   - Other facial fractures (cheekbone or upper jaw fractures)
   - Air trapped under the skin (subcutaneous emphysema)
   - Clear watery nasal discharge
   - Persistent nosebleed
   - Obvious bony deviation — best assessed immediately or after 48 hours once early swelling has settled

4. **X-rays are not routinely needed** — undisplaced fractures require no specific treatment, and displaced fractures are usually obvious on clinical examination.

---

## Management

Management depends on whether there are red flags, deformity, or nasal obstruction:

- **Red flags present (septal haematoma, compound fracture, other facial fractures, CSF leak, uncontrolled nosebleed):** Your doctor will arrange urgent specialist assessment — this typically means ear, nose and throat (ORL/ENT) or oral and maxillofacial surgery.

- **Nasal fracture with external deformity or new unilateral nasal blockage (within 14 days of injury):** Your doctor may seek specialist advice. Most nasal fractures are best assessed 7–10 days after injury once swelling has reduced. Depending on the situation, a specialist may recommend manipulation under local or general anaesthetic.

- **Nasal fracture with external deformity or new unilateral nasal blockage (more than 14 days after injury):** The bones are less likely to move with manipulation at this stage. Your doctor may consider a referral for possible septoplasty or rhinoplasty.

- **Nasal bones acceptably straight, no red flags:** Your doctor will manage this conservatively. A review at 48 hours is recommended, as deformity can become more apparent as swelling reduces.
