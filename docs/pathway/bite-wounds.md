# Bite Wounds

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers wounds caused by human or animal bites — assessing risk, managing the wound, and deciding when specialist care is needed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Prompt Medical Attention

See a doctor urgently if:

- Bite was inflicted while overseas (rabies risk)
- Bite may be related to self-harm or non-accidental (inflicted) injury

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. Take a history of how the wound occurred
2. Assess the wound and consider factors that may affect healing, including:
   - Arterial or venous disease
   - Comorbidities such as diabetes, peripheral vascular disease, or rheumatoid arthritis
   - Medications that affect healing (steroids, NSAIDs, anticoagulants)
   - Nutrition, mobility, smoking, and sleep

3. Consider risks specific to the type of bite:
   - **Bite overseas** — possible rabies exposure
   - **Human bite** — possible blood-borne virus exposure (hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV)
   - **Monkey bite** — possible herpes B virus exposure
   - **Unusual or marine animal bite** — specialist infectious diseases advice needed

4. Consider whether the injury may be non-accidental. Indicators include:
   - Delay in seeking help
   - Accounts of events that differ over time
   - History inconsistent with the wound
   - Past history of inflicted injury
   - Inappropriate behaviour by the patient or caregivers

5. Check tetanus immunisation status

6. Ask about stress or anxiety related to the bite and whether the patient is at risk of further injury from the same source

7. Arrange investigations if needed:
   - **Wound swab** if signs of infection are present
   - **Blood tests** for blood-borne viruses if it is a human bite
   - **X-ray** for clenched fist injuries, bites near a bone or joint, penetrating wounds to the scalp, crush injuries, possible fractures, or possible foreign body (e.g., teeth)

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following steps:

### Transmissible Infection Risk

If there is suspected exposure to rabies, herpes B virus, HIV, hepatitis B, or hepatitis C, or if there are concerns about an unusual or marine animal bite, your doctor will seek urgent infectious diseases advice.

### Specialist Referral

Your doctor will consider referring to a specialist if:
- The bite involves tendons, arteries, nerves, muscles, joints, or bones
- There is a penetrating bite to the hands or feet
- There are facial wounds (unless very minor)
- A foreign body may be in the wound
- The wound requires extensive debridement
- The severity of injury is difficult to assess

### Wound Care

- The wound will be thoroughly irrigated with sterile saline or clean water
- Your doctor will weigh up whether to close the wound or allow it to heal by secondary intention:
  - **Dog bites** — an experienced clinician may close the wound if it is less than 12 hours old, shows no signs of infection, is not on the hand or foot, and the patient is not immunocompromised
  - **Cat and human bites** — most are left to heal on their own, unless on the face
- A dressing will be applied. First dressing change is typically at 72 hours; subsequent dressings every 48–72 hours depending on healing
- Tetanus cover will be arranged if needed

### Pain Relief

Your doctor will arrange appropriate analgesia.

### Prophylactic Antibiotics

Antibiotics to prevent infection are recommended for:
- All cat bites
- All human bites less than 72 hours old
- Dog bites to the face, hand, foot, or genitals, or those requiring surgical debridement
- Bites affecting underlying structures (tendons, ligaments, joints, or bones)
- Wounds that have been primarily closed
- Patients at higher risk of infection (e.g., diabetes, immunosuppressant medications)

**Standard regimen (3 days):**
- Adults (15 years and older): amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 625 mg three times daily for 3 days
- Children (14 years and younger): amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 30 mg/kg/dose (max 625 mg) three times daily for 3 days

**If penicillin allergy — adults and children 12 years and older:**
- Doxycycline 200 mg on day 1, then 100 mg once daily on days 2–3 (avoid in pregnancy and breastfeeding), AND
- Metronidazole 400 mg twice daily for 3 days

**If penicillin allergy — children under 12 years:**
- Trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole 24 mg/kg (max 960 mg) every 12 hours for 3 days (avoid in infants under 6 weeks), AND
- Metronidazole 7.5 mg/kg (max 400 mg) three times daily for 3 days

### Treating Established Infection

If the wound is already infected, treatment depends on severity:

**Mild infection:**
- Adults: amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 625 mg three times daily for 7 days
- Children: amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 30 mg/kg/dose (max 625 mg) three times daily for at least 5 days
- If penicillin allergy in adults or children 12+: doxycycline + metronidazole for 7 days
- If penicillin allergy in children under 12: trimethoprim + sulfamethoxazole + metronidazole for 5 days

**Moderate infection:**
- If a systemically well adult who does not need surgery, your doctor may prescribe higher-dose oral antibiotics
- If moderate infection in a child, or if the adult is unwell or requires surgery, specialist assessment will be arranged

**Severe infection:**
- Usually requires specialist care and hospitalisation

### Follow-Up and Return

Return earlier for wound review if you develop:
- Fever
- Nausea, vomiting, or lethargy
- Increasing pain, swelling, or redness at the wound that spreads beyond the dressing

### Anxiety and Safety

Your doctor may address any anxiety, flashbacks, or stress related to the bite, and will ensure you are not at risk of further injury from the same source.
