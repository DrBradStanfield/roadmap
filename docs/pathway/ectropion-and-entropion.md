# Ectropion and Entropion

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of ectropion (eyelid turning outward) and entropion (eyelid turning inward).

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

- Corneal fluorescein staining in an elderly patient

If you have this finding, your doctor will arrange urgent assessment.

---

## Background

### Ectropion

Ectropion is outward rotation of the eyelid away from the eye. It may be caused by age-related changes, a lump on the eyelid (including underlying malignancy), or paralysis of the facial nerve (seventh nerve palsy).

Symptoms include:
- Irritation from conjunctival thickening and drying
- Watering of the eye (epiphora), due to outward rotation of the eyelid and punctum

### Entropion

Entropion is inward rotation of the lower eyelid, causing the eyelashes to rub against the eye surface. It is usually caused by age-related changes of the orbicularis muscle and loss of eyelid support. It may also occur after eye surgery or trauma.

Symptoms include:
- Discomfort on blinking
- Pain
- Watering of the eye due to irritation of the eye surface

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. Ask about how long symptoms have been present
2. Check for:
   - Changes to vision, including visual acuity (sharpness of vision)
   - Conjunctival irritation, thickening, or signs of infection
   - Redness of the eye
   - Excessive eye watering (epiphora)
   - Corneal abrasions, ulceration, or scarring — assessed using fluorescein stain (a dye that highlights damage to the eye surface)
   - Signs of infective keratitis (infection of the cornea)
   - Underlying causes such as eyelid lumps, malignancy, or seventh nerve palsy — the eyelid will be palpated

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following:

1. **Artificial tears** — prescribed for symptomatic relief of irritation
2. **Eye ointment** (e.g., paraffin liquid + paraffin soft white such as Refresh Night Time, or paraffin liquid + lanolin such as Poly Visc) — if there is chronic corneal irritation, poor blink, seventh nerve palsy, or corneal anaesthesia from previous shingles
3. **Antibiotic eye drops** (e.g., chloramphenicol) four times a day — if fluorescein staining shows an area of uptake on the cornea. Your doctor will review after 1–2 weeks of treatment
4. **Lid taping** — to provide extra support for paralytic ectropion, or to raise, evert, and laterally tighten the lower eyelid if there is corneal injury from entropion
5. **Referral to ophthalmology (eye specialist)** — considered for:
   - Entropion or ectropion with signs of infective keratitis (urgent assessment)
   - A corneal defect not healing after antibiotic treatment
   - Ectropion with concerning eyelid changes or an associated eyelid mass
   - Persistent entropion, due to the risk of progression over time
   - Ectropion causing ongoing irritation or excessive watering without corneal staining, where surgical correction may be considered
