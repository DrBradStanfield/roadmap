# Dyspnoea in MND

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of breathlessness in people with motor neurone disease (MND). Dyspnoea in MND is progressive and requires early planning — always discuss management with your doctor or specialist team.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a clinical history including:

- Exertional breathlessness
- Orthopnoea (breathlessness when lying flat)
- Sleep disturbance — nightmares and headaches on waking
- Fatigue and daytime sleepiness or naps
- Fear, anxiety, and panic
- Symptoms of stridor or laryngeal closure
- Secretion volume, consistency, and ease of clearance

Physical examination typically includes:

- **Breath-counting test:** Taking a deep breath and counting numbers in sequence on a single breath — each number counted roughly equates to ~100 mL of FVC
- Voice strength
- Stridor
- Cough strength
- Paradoxical abdominal movement with respiration

Your doctor will also look for reversible causes of breathlessness:

- Heart failure
- COPD or asthma
- Pleural or pericardial effusion
- Mechanical airway obstruction
- Anaemia
- Anxiety
- Superior vena cava obstruction (SVCO)
- Pulmonary embolism

## Management

### Non-Pharmacological Measures

Your doctor may recommend the following approaches, often starting with these before medications:

- **Fresh air:** A fan directed at the face, or opening a door or window
- **Relaxation techniques**
- **Breathing control techniques**
- **Distraction techniques**
- **Music therapy**
- **Positioning advice**
- **Energy conservation** — for example, using a walking frame to reduce effort

Physiotherapy can provide advice on breathing control and help with clearing sputum if coughing is impaired. Occupational therapy can assist with adaptive equipment, activity pacing, and changes to the home environment.

### Lung Function Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- Cough peak flow measurement
- Spirometry (via mouthpiece or face mask)
- Overnight oximetry
- Arterial blood gas testing is not routinely required — oxygen saturations are usually preserved when awake

### Non-Invasive Ventilation

If sleep-disordered breathing is suspected, your doctor may consider a trial of non-invasive ventilation. This may include overnight bi-level positive airway pressure (BiPAP) — a mask-based device that can give significant symptom relief, though it can be cumbersome to use.

### Breathlessness Management Plan

If you require a combination of interventions, your doctor may put together a breathlessness management plan. This typically starts with non-pharmacological measures, followed by stepwise instructions for medication use. It will include contact details for who to call if the plan is not working.

### Planning for Acute Episodes

Your doctor will help plan ahead for episodes of acute breathlessness or chest infections. This may include:

- Establishing out-of-hours medical or nursing contact
- Reinforcing chest clearance techniques
- Ensuring medications are available at home
- Antibiotic therapy instructions (e.g., amoxicillin kept at home in powdered form if you have a PEG tube)
- Discussing and documenting your wishes about ventilation and other end-of-life decisions

## Medications Your Doctor May Consider

All medications below are prescribed and managed by your doctor or palliative care team. Do not adjust doses without medical guidance.

### Morphine for Breathlessness

Morphine reduces breathlessness by reducing respiratory effort. Lower doses are used than for pain relief:

- A one-off **2.5 mg subcutaneous** dose may be used in an acute distress crisis
- **2.5 to 5 mg oral liquid** every four hours, or as required — lower doses for older patients
- The goal is comfort, not complete resolution of breathlessness
- If more than 2 doses per day are regularly needed, your doctor may convert to low-dose slow-release morphine capsules (e.g., 10 mg twice a day) or a continuous subcutaneous infusion (10 mg/24 hours), with as-required morphine liquid 2.5 mg up to four times a day available alongside
- 30 mg per day of oral morphine is generally sufficient when used for breathlessness alone
- If kidney function is reduced (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m²), your doctor will seek palliative care advice on alternatives

### Benzodiazepines (for Anxiety Component)

Benzodiazepines are useful only when anxiety is contributing to breathlessness. Your doctor will be cautious about excessive sedation.

**Clonazepam:**
- Can be very sedating — usually reserved unless in the terminal stage
- 0.5 to 1 mg at night is the most common dose; may be given twice daily if needed
- Given as oral drops (1 drop = 0.1 mg); recommended dose is five to ten drops (0.5–1 mg) of the 2.5 mg/mL solution
- Drops should be placed on a spoon first — do not administer directly into the mouth
- Your doctor will prescribe as a number of drops rather than mL to avoid dosing errors

**Lorazepam:**
- 0.5 mg to 1 mg orally, every four to six hours as required

**Midazolam (for crisis or severe distress):**
- 2.5 mg given buccally (inside the cheek) or sublingually — or 2.5 to 5 mg subcutaneously up to every 15 minutes in a crisis, then every two hours as required once settled
- Maximum 15 mg per day — if more is needed, palliative care input is sought

### Nebulisers

May be useful if there is some degree of airflow obstruction. Not used more often than every 4 hours. Nebulised saline can help with thick secretions.

---

## End-of-Life Care

If nearing end of life, your doctor will consider involving the palliative care team for parenteral opioids and sedation:

- **Morphine:** 15 mg per 24 hours subcutaneously (or half the daily oral dose if already on regular morphine)
- **Midazolam via subcutaneous infusion:** typically 10 to 20 mg over 24 hours as a starting dose; higher doses may be needed in consultation with a palliative care specialist
