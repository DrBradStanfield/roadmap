# Gonorrhoea

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of gonorrhoea — including who to test, specimen collection, antibiotic treatment, contact tracing, and when specialist sexual health advice is needed.

> **Note:** This pathway uses the terms male, female, men, and women in a cis-gendered way to refer to genital anatomy. It recognises that gender identity may not match the sex assigned at birth.

---

## Background

- Gonorrhoea is caused by the bacterium *Neisseria gonorrhoeae*. It infects the anogenital region (endocervix, urethra, rectum), the pharynx, and occasionally the eye. In disseminated infection, there may be joint and skin involvement.
- The incubation period is typically 1–14 days but can be longer.
- Transmitted by contact with infected genital or anal secretions.
- Often **asymptomatic**, especially in the rectum, pharynx, and endocervix. Transmission occurs in both symptomatic and asymptomatic infection.
- Gonorrhoea is **increasing in prevalence** in the general population, especially in women of reproductive age.
- **Reduced susceptibility to first-line treatment is emerging globally.** Reinfection is common.

### Complications of Untreated Gonorrhoea

Your doctor will be aware of these potential complications:

- Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID) → chronic pelvic pain, hydropyosalpinx, subfertility (fallopian tube scarring), ectopic pregnancy
- Adverse pregnancy outcomes (chorioamnionitis, premature rupture of membranes, neonatal conjunctivitis)
- Bartholin gland abscess
- Epididymo-orchitis
- Prostatitis (rarely)
- Reactive arthritis
- Disseminated disease (rare) — arthritis, skin lesions, endocarditis, meningitis
- Fitz-Hugh Curtis syndrome (perihepatitis), typically in patients with PID

## Who Should Be Tested

Your doctor will offer gonorrhoea testing to:

- People at **increased risk** of STI — risk behaviours are more important than group membership:
  - Young people; men who have sex with men (MSM); Māori; Pacific peoples
  - Multiple sexual contacts; recent change in sexual contact; a sexual partner with an STI
  - Sexually active patients younger than 30 years
- Anyone with **signs or symptoms** suggestive of gonorrhoea
- Before **termination of pregnancy (TOP)**
- Before **IUD insertion** in people at risk of STIs
- With suspected **epididymo-orchitis** or **pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)**
- With a history of **sexual assault** or intimate partner violence
- Opportunistically with cervical screening, antenatal care, contraception advice, and travel medicine

### Gonorrhoea Symptoms

Your doctor will assess for — noting the patient may be asymptomatic:

- **Urethral:** penile urethral discharge (~90% symptomatic), dysuria
- **Cervical** (~80% have no symptoms): vaginal discharge, intermenstrual or postcoital bleeding, dyspareunia
- **Anorectal** (usually absent): anal pain, bleeding, or discharge; irritation; change in bowel function; painful defecation
- **Ocular:** conjunctivitis and eye inflammation — can be purulent and sight-threatening
- **Pharyngeal:** usually absent
- Unilateral scrotal pain and swelling

## Testing / Specimen Collection

If an STI is suspected, your doctor will complete a full sexual health check at the same consultation.

### Recommended Testing Intervals (if asymptomatic, concern about specific exposure)

- Chlamydia, gonorrhoea, trichomoniasis — test 2 weeks after last unprotected sexual activity
- HIV and syphilis — test 3 months after last unprotected sexual activity
- High-risk HIV exposure — test 6 weeks after the event and again at 3 months

### PCR/NAAT Testing by Patient Type

**Patients with a vagina:**
- Self-collected vaginal swab (preferred — more sensitive), or first-catch urine if vaginal swab declined
  - **First-catch urine:** first 20–30 mL of urinary stream, preferably ≥ 1 hour after last void; include clinical details
  - **Vaginal swab:** insert 4 cm into vagina, rotate for count of 5; snap off at black line, seal; include clinical details
- If being examined: take swab before speculum insertion

**Patients without a vagina (people with a penis):**
- First-catch urine (first 20–30 mL, preferably ≥ 1 hour after last void)

**Any patient with anal sex or anorectal symptoms:**
- Clinician-collected or self-collected anorectal PCR/NAAT swab
  - Insert swab 4 cm into anal canal, rotate, replace in container; include clinical details

**Men who have sex with men (MSM)** — asymptomatic infection is common; offer all three regardless of reported practices or condom use:
- First-catch urine
- Oropharyngeal PCR/NAAT swab
- Anorectal PCR/NAAT swab

**Suspected gonorrhoea conjunctivitis:** eye PCR/NAAT swab

**Suspected gonorrhoea pharyngitis:** throat PCR/NAAT swab

### Culture Swab (in addition to PCR, not as alternative)

Your doctor will take a culture swab (purple Amies swab) if:
- Patient is a contact of gonorrhoea
- Gonorrhoea clinically suspected (e.g. profuse discharge)
- PID is suspected

Culture permits antimicrobial susceptibility testing. Sites:
- **Endocervical** — known contact of gonorrhoea, suspected PID, or profuse cervical discharge
- **Urethral** — urethral discharge (no need to insert swab into urethra)
- **Anal** — anal discharge

## Treatment

### Standard Treatment: Asymptomatic or Uncomplicated Genital, Pharyngeal, or Eye Infection

*(Including patients who are breastfeeding. Dual therapy is recommended to reduce resistance to ceftriaxone.)*

Your doctor will prescribe **both**:
1. **Ceftriaxone 500 mg in 2 mL of 1% lignocaine IM — single dose**
2. **Azithromycin 1 g orally — single dose (immediately)**

Alternative treatments are **not recommended** due to high levels of resistance.

**If allergy or contraindications:**
- To azithromycin → ceftriaxone 1 g in 3.5 mL of 1% lignocaine IM as a single dose
- To ceftriaxone → seek sexual health advice (alternatives not recommended due to resistance)

---

### Anorectal Infection

**Asymptomatic** — prescribe both (same as above): ceftriaxone 500 mg IM + azithromycin 1 g orally.

**Symptomatic** — management is complex:
- Seek sexual health advice or request acute sexual health assessment
- See Aotearoa New Zealand STI Management Guidelines — Anorectal Syndromes

---

### Pregnancy

Prescribe **both**:
1. **Ceftriaxone 500 mg in 2 mL of 1% lignocaine IM — single dose**
2. **Azithromycin 1 g orally — single dose**

Allergy/contraindications managed same as above.

---

### Co-infection (Gonorrhoea + Chlamydia)

**Ceftriaxone 1 g in 3.5 mL of 1% lidocaine IM (single dose) + doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 7 days.**

If symptomatic rectal co-infection → seek sexual health advice or request acute sexual health assessment.

---

### Associated Conditions

See relevant pathways for:
- Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)
- Epididymo-orchitis
- Urethritis

## After Treatment

### Screen for Other STIs

Your doctor will screen for other STIs — especially herpes simplex virus if the patient has genital sores/ulcers or proctitis.

### Notification

Gonorrhoea is a **notifiable disease**. Your doctor will:
- Check the Health New Zealand | Te Whatu Ora case definition for gonorrhoea
- Complete the online gonorrhoea questionnaire (web-based, one-time notification per link use)

### Preventing Reinfection and Further Transmission

**Sexual abstinence advice:** Your doctor will advise the patient to abstain from sex (including oral sex) or use condoms:
- Until 7 days after their own treatment
- Until 7 days after their sexual contacts have been treated

**Contact tracing:**
- All sexual contacts in the last **3 months** should be notified
- Most patients choose to notify contacts themselves; written information helps
- Notifying all contacts may not be possible (insufficient information, safety concerns)

**Managing sexual contacts:**
- Recommend a sexual health check for all sexual contacts
- Immediate treatment of contacts is not recommended unless: last sexual contact with the index case was within the past 2 weeks, the contact is symptomatic, or unlikely to await results or return for treatment
- Advise contacts to abstain from sex or use condoms until results are available and for 1 week from start of treatment
- **Patient-delivered partner therapy is not legal in Aotearoa New Zealand**

### Follow-up at 1 Week

Your doctor will review the patient to:
- Discuss test results
- Check symptoms (if any) have resolved
- Ask whether there has been condomless sex in the week post-treatment
- Confirm medication was completed and tolerated
- Ensure notifiable contacts have been informed
- Assess risk of reinfection (re-treatment is necessary if re-exposed to an untreated contact)

### Test of Cure

Required if the patient is **pregnant** or has had **pharyngeal gonorrhoea**:
- Performed at least 4 weeks after treatment is completed
- Self-collected PCR/NAAT swab recommended

### Ongoing Screening

- Re-screen in the **third trimester of pregnancy** and **3 months after any infection** to detect reinfection
- Consider testing for other STIs or retesting at the recommended interval

## Doxy-PEP (Doxycycline Post-Exposure Prophylaxis)

Your doctor may discuss doxy-PEP for eligible patients. **Doxy-PEP reduces the risk of syphilis and chlamydia — but is NOT suitable for preventing gonorrhoea in New Zealand due to local resistance levels.**

**Eligibility (discuss proactively):**
- Assigned male at birth (includes cisgender men, transgender women, non-binary people), AND
- Has sex with men, AND
- Diagnosed in the past 12 months with syphilis, OR two bacterial STIs (chlamydia, gonorrhoea)

**Also consider for people assigned male at birth who have sex with men if they:**
- Have an upcoming period of high STI risk
- Have concurrent male partners and partners at risk of pregnancy
- Are Māori or Pacific (due to inequities in syphilis rates)

**Not suitable for:**
- Prevention of gonorrhoea (NZ resistance levels)
- Patients presenting as a contact of syphilis, gonorrhoea, or chlamydia — use the relevant pathway

**If prescribed:** doxycycline 200 mg within 72 hours of unprotected sex, maximum 3 doses per week; maximum 72 × 100 mg tablets per prescription. 3-monthly follow-ups required.

## When to Seek Sexual Health Advice

Your doctor will seek sexual health specialist advice if:
- Symptoms persist after correct management
- Complicated clinical situation
- Allergy or standard treatment options are contraindicated
- Symptomatic anorectal infection
