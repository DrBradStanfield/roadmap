# Genitourinary System in Spinal Cord Impairment (SCI)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers urinary tract management in adults with spinal cord impairment (SCI) — including UTI recognition and treatment, catheter management, and bladder monitoring. SCI urinary management is complex and this information is a guide only. Always discuss your situation with your doctor.

---

## Background

Management of the lower urinary tract in SCI depends on the degree of neurologic urinary dysfunction, the person's mobility, and hand function.

Patients with SCI-related neurogenic bladders require regular screening to prevent renal failure. Annual screening includes:

- Renal ultrasound
- Kidney, ureter, bladder X-ray (KUB)
- Serum creatinine
- Urine microalbumin

Urinary tract infection is more common in SCI due to:

- Altered voiding dynamics (e.g., detrusor sphincter dyssynergia, low flow rate)
- Increased post-void residuals
- Use of catheters for drainage
- Increased incidence of urinary calculi
- Increased intravesical (bladder) pressure
- Urinary reflux

Recurrent UTIs, high bladder pressure, reflux, and inadequate drainage can all contribute to renal failure. There is an increased incidence of urinary tract stones in the first 2 years following SCI.

**Important:** Colonisation of the bladder does not necessarily mean a UTI requiring antibiotics. Your doctor will assess whether treatment is needed.

---

## Recognising a UTI in SCI

People with SCI may not present with classic UTI symptoms. Your doctor will look for:

**Possible signs of UTI in SCI:**
- Debris or change in colour of urine
- Increased leakage around the catheter (urethral or suprapubic site)
- Increased bladder spasms or new incontinence
- Difficulty passing urine or incomplete emptying
- Increase in spasms or spasticity
- Autonomic dysreflexia (see below)
- Malaise, lethargy, fever, or chills
- Pain or discomfort over the bladder (suprapubic region) or flanks

Patients often recognise correctly when their symptoms are due to urinary infection, retention, or catheter blockage.

---

## UTI Management

Your doctor will:

- Wait for laboratory culture results before prescribing antibiotics where possible — infections in SCI are often caused by resistant bacteria.
- Treat symptomatic UTIs with antibiotics (usually for 7 days), guided by recent cultures.
- **Not** treat asymptomatic patients or those whose only symptom is smelly urine, as most will have bacterial colonisation rather than true infection.
- Ensure adequate hydration — dilute urine supports the natural immune response and antibiotic treatment.
- Change catheters after at least 1 dose of antibiotics, preferably after 24 hours of treatment.

**Note:** If you are acutely unwell, your doctor will arrange urgent hospital assessment. SCI patients with pyelonephritis (kidney infection) need hospital management.

**Prophylactic antibiotics** are controversial in SCI, especially with long-term catheter use. Your doctor will advise if they are appropriate for your situation.

## Catheter Blockages and Autonomic Dysreflexia

A blocked catheter can be very distressing and can trigger **autonomic dysreflexia (AD)** — a potentially dangerous hypertensive crisis in people with SCI, especially at or above T6. This is a medical emergency.

**Signs of autonomic dysreflexia include:** severe headache, flushing above the injury level, sweating, and high blood pressure. Seek emergency care immediately.

Your doctor will:

- Urgently change the urethral catheter if AD is suspected due to a blocked catheter.
- If a suprapubic catheter is blocked: insert a urethral catheter — **do not remove** the suprapubic catheter.
- Follow with a bladder washout after catheter change to remove debris.

---

## Suprapubic Catheter Issues

A suprapubic catheter (SPC) sits in the bladder through the abdominal wall and is held in place by a balloon. It requires regular changing.

**If the SPC falls out (with a healed track):** Reinsertion within 30 minutes using the same tract is sometimes possible. After that, tract closure begins and reinsertion in the community may not be possible. If unable to reinsert, a urethral catheter should be inserted immediately and urgent specialist review arranged.

**Important:** If the SPC has fallen out and the tract is not patent, your doctor will insert a urethral catheter immediately and monitor for autonomic dysreflexia.

**If the SPC was placed within the last 6 weeks** (unhealed track), it must be replaced by a urologist — do not attempt community reinsertion.

## Intermittent Self-Catheterisation (ISC)

ISC is performed by the patient (if hand function allows) or by a care provider. It involves inserting a catheter to empty the bladder at timed intervals (or by sensation if the patient is sensate), then removing it.

Benefits of ISC over indwelling catheters:

- Greater independence
- Decreased risk of infection when performed correctly and regularly
- Improved social participation and quality of life
- No ongoing need for drainage bags

**Leakage between catheterisations** may indicate: infection, change in bladder or renal function, need for more frequent catheterisation, or other medical concerns. Your doctor will investigate with a urine sample and, if negative, arrange further tests including renal ultrasound, abdominal X-ray (KUB), serum creatinine, and urine albumin:creatinine ratio.

---

## When to Seek Help

Contact your doctor or seek urgent care if:

- You feel acutely unwell
- You have signs of autonomic dysreflexia (severe headache, high blood pressure, flushing, sweating)
- You have a blocked or dislodged catheter you cannot manage
- You have recurrent UTIs
- There is a change in your bladder or renal function
- You have leakage between catheterisations that is not due to infection

**Your doctor will discuss** specialist referral for: recurrent UTIs, suprapubic catheter replacement, changes in bladder or renal function, or complex catheter issues.
