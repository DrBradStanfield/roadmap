## What is Obstructive Sleep Apnoea (OSA)?

OSA is the most common form of sleep-disordered breathing, with an estimated prevalence of 9% to 38% in New Zealand. Most cases are undiagnosed. It is part of a spectrum of sleep-disordered breathing in which increased resistance in the upper airway disturbs sleep. It is associated with excessive daytime sleepiness, but some people have no symptoms.

**Why it matters:**
- OSA is a significant risk factor for high blood pressure and cardiovascular disease. Untreated OSA confers a 2 to 4-fold increase in the risk of a cardiovascular event.
- People with OSA have a high chance of having high blood pressure. Among people with high blood pressure, OSA prevalence ranges from 40%, and is as high as 80% in older people with resistant hypertension.
- More severe OSA is associated with higher vascular risk, more work and motor vehicle accidents, and cognitive decline.

## Who is at risk?

**Main risk factors:**
- Overweight or obesity (the main risk factor, thought to account for up to 50% of the risk of OSA)
- Male sex (twice the incidence of females)
- Post-menopausal women (similar prevalence to men)
- Age over 50 years
- Neck circumference >42 cm (male) or >41 cm (female)
- Excessive alcohol consumption
- Sedative medications including opioids
- Enlarged tonsils (especially in young adults)
- Craniofacial abnormalities (e.g., retrognathia, where the lower jaw sits back, or a soft palate and uvula positioned further back)
- Family history of OSA

**Ethnic risk note:** Rates of OSA are higher in Māori and Pacific peoples, who have about twice the prevalence of other groups and tend to have more severe OSA and co-morbidities. Māori are 4.3 times more likely to have moderate-to-severe OSA than non-Māori (after controlling for age and sex). Asian people may develop OSA at a lower BMI than other groups, so doctors should have a lower threshold for considering OSA in non-obese Asian people with OSA symptoms.

## Symptoms

### Night-time symptoms
- Loud, habitual snoring, often bothersome to others
- Witnessed pauses in breathing (apnoeas), often interrupting the snoring and ending with a snort
- Waking to urinate (nocturia)
- Restless sleep with frequent arousals and tossing or turning
- Nocturnal sweating

### Daytime symptoms
- Non-restorative sleep (waking as tired as when going to bed)
- Excessive daytime sleepiness
- Morning headache
- Morning confusion
- Difficulty concentrating, poor attention span, poor memory
- Irritability, personality and mood changes, including depression or anxiety
- Sexual dysfunction, including impotence and decreased libido

### Presentation in women (important)
Women may not present with classic symptoms:
- May have excessive daytime sleepiness but not score highly on the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS was validated for male subjects only)
- 40% of women may not have classical OSA symptoms (like those on STOP-BANG)
- Women may instead present with insomnia, restless legs, depression, nightmares, palpitations, or hallucinations

## Clinical assessment tools

### Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS)
A subjective measure of daytime sleepiness. Questionnaire: https://www.sleepprimarycareresources.org.au/questionnaires/ess
- Score **>10** is considered abnormal
- Score **>16** raises concern for high risk of motor vehicle accident (MVA)
- Score **18 or higher** is a high-risk feature, and your doctor may refer you for specialist sleep assessment

> Your doctor may use the ESS to measure how sleepy you are during the day. Scores above 10 are considered abnormal.

### STOP-BANG Questionnaire
Used to estimate the pre-test likelihood of OSA.

Calculator: https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/3992/stop-bang-score-obstructive-sleep-apnea

> Your doctor may use the STOP-BANG questionnaire to assess how likely you are to have OSA before arranging any tests.

## Driving safety

More severe OSA is associated with more motor vehicle and work accidents. **High risk of MVA** if any of:
- Patient reports falling asleep at the wheel
- ESS score above 16
- History of accidents or near-misses related to inattention or sleepiness

**If your symptoms suggest you are not fit to hold an unconditional licence:** you should not drive until you have been seen in a sleep clinic. Your doctor will request a specialist sleep assessment.

**If fit to drive, self-management advice:**
- Minimise unnecessary driving
- Take regular rest breaks and share the driving, especially if sleepy
- Pull over and stop as soon as you notice any warning sign of sleepiness (head nods, blinking, yawning, feeling warm, lane drifting)
- Avoid driving at times you would normally be asleep, when sleep-deprived, or long distances or for longer than one hour without a break, particularly on high-speed roads
- Avoid alcohol or sedative medications

Think about how OSA affects your work too (for example, operating machinery). OSA can significantly increase the risk of workplace accidents.

> If you are concerned about falling asleep at the wheel, speak to your doctor before driving again. This is a patient safety issue that your doctor needs to know about.

## Investigation: Sleep testing

### Community sleep test (overnight oximetry)
A basic overnight breathing study, available through some pharmacies or private clinics. This test is not funded.

**Appropriate for:** Snorers with moderate-to-severe daytime sleepiness, with no other explanation for tiredness.

**NOT appropriate if any of these high-risk features apply:**
- Sleepy-driver episode (collision or near-miss linked to sleepiness, or falling asleep while driving)
- High-risk occupation (heavy machinery operator, professional driver)
- Surgical patients with significant perioperative risk
- Significant cardiovascular comorbidities (severe heart failure, uncontrollable hypertension)
- Significant respiratory or neurological condition
- Risk of obesity hypoventilation: extreme obesity with BMI >50, especially if daytime oxygen saturation ≤95%
- ESS score 18 or higher

**Also avoid** if the patient snores but is not sleepy, has insomnia (results can be confusing), is currently undergoing sleep restriction CBT, or already meets the criteria for specialist sleep assessment.

Your doctor may also consider tests for other causes of tiredness.

> A community sleep test gives basic overnight breathing information. Your doctor will decide whether it's the right test for your situation, or whether you need a more specialised assessment.

## Treatment options

### 1. Lifestyle measures (all patients benefit)
All people with snoring and OSA are likely to benefit from lifestyle measures, alone or combined with more specific therapy. For mild-to-moderate OSA without complications, this may be the only treatment needed.

**Weight management:**
- Weight loss in overweight people may reduce OSA severity and improve symptoms
- Sufficient weight loss can be curative
- Weight gain is a strong factor for developing or worsening snoring and OSA

**Smoking cessation:**
- Smoking causes irritation and swelling of the pharyngeal space, making snoring and OSA more likely

**Alcohol and sedative medications:**
- Alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, anti-epileptics, antidepressants, antihistamines, and narcotics may worsen OSA, though a causative link is unproven. Sedatives relax the throat muscles, so the airway walls collapse more easily.
- Avoid alcohol before bed; avoid medications that cause weight gain or sedation

**Exercise:**
- Regular exercise, even without weight loss, has been shown to reduce snoring and OSA severity

**Sleep hygiene:**
- Go to bed and get up at the same time every day
- Get into bed when feeling drowsy, tired and ready to sleep
- Prioritise a consistent wake-up time (increases exposure to bright morning light)
- Avoid napping during the day if possible; if unavoidable, nap before 3:00 pm and only for 30 minutes
- Avoid caffeine and alcohol before bed
- Engage in relaxing activities before sleep (music, a bath that is not too hot)
- Write things down to let your mind relax

**Positional therapy:**
- Avoid sleeping on your back: try using pillows, raising the head of the bed, a tennis ball in a sock sewn onto the back of your pyjamas, or alarms with positional sensors

### 2. CPAP therapy (Continuous Positive Airway Pressure)

**Benefits:**
- Reduces the risk of hypertension
- May reduce cardiometabolic disease severity
- May reduce motor vehicle accidents due to sleepiness

**Common side effects and solutions:**
- Mask discomfort: pay proper attention to mask fit
- Dry mouth: adding a humidification circuit helps
- Nasal congestion: usually responds to nasal corticosteroids

> Managing side effects early can reduce the chance of stopping CPAP. Speak to your doctor or sleep clinic if you're having trouble with it. See Sleep Health Foundation: [CPAP: Making it Work for You](https://www.sleephealthfoundation.org.au/sleep-disorders/cpap-making-it-work-for-you).

### 3. Oral appliances (Mandibular Advancement Splint — MAS)
- Can be an effective second-line treatment for OSA
- Often used for less severe disease, in people who are not obese, or when CPAP is not tolerated
- Less effective than CPAP at reducing severity, but compliance and tolerance may be better, particularly in mild disease
- Requires your own teeth in good condition and no jaw joint (TMJ) problems
- Not provided through the regional sleep services; your usual dentist can advise

### 4. Positional devices (for positional OSA)
For people whose OSA occurs mainly when sleeping on their back:
- Lumbar or abdominal binders
- Backpacks
- Full-length pillows
- Tennis balls attached to back of nightwear
- Alarms with positional sensors

### 5. Surgical options
Surgery to the upper airway benefits people with sleep-disordered breathing, particularly where there is a clearly defined upper airway abnormality. Your doctor may consider a surgical assessment for nasal obstruction, deviated septum, or craniofacial abnormalities, especially with a normal neck circumference:
- **Tonsillectomy:** Benefits adults with large tonsils and few other airway abnormalities
- **Nasal surgery:** Less benefit for OSA unless there is severe nasal obstruction; can help CPAP compliance
- **Bariatric surgery:** May be discussed if BMI is over 35

### Snoring without other features of OSA
If you snore but have no other features of OSA, your doctor can manage this: treat any underlying conditions and use the lifestyle measures above. The type of snoring may guide treatment:
- **Nasal snoring:**
  - Normal saline douching
  - Otrivine, 1 to 2 sprays at night, maximum duration 1 week to avoid rebound congestion
  - A topical nasal steroid, 1 to 2 sprays each morning, for at least a 6 week course
- **Tongue base snoring:** a mandibular advancement splint from a private dentist, preferably with experience in dental sleep medicine
- **Soft palate snoring:** if conservative management fails, surgical options in the private sector

## Managing co-morbidities

Your doctor may improve management of other conditions linked to OSA and address cardiovascular risk:
- **Cardiovascular:** Hypertension, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, arrhythmias
- **Endocrine:** Diabetes, impaired glucose tolerance, thyroid disease
- **Respiratory:** COPD, asthma, allergic rhinitis
- **Neuromuscular diseases**
- **Sinus disease and reflux** can worsen OSA, so these are treated too

## When to see a specialist

Your doctor may refer you for specialist sleep assessment if:
- You have suspected OSA with any high-risk features (see above)
- A community sleep test shows at least moderate sleep apnoea with excessive daytime sleepiness
- Symptoms suggest another sleep disorder needing more assessment (narcolepsy, parasomnia, periodic limb movement disorder)
- The cause of your excessive sleepiness remains unknown
- Symptoms suggest you are not fit to hold an unconditional driving licence
- Your symptoms persist or recur after other causes are addressed (not using therapy as advised, other medical conditions, or not enough sleep)

> If you have any high-risk features, your doctor may refer you to a specialist sleep service rather than manage this in the community. Don't delay in speaking to your doctor if you're concerned.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q: Can OSA be cured?**
Sufficient weight loss can be curative. For mild-to-moderate OSA without complications, lifestyle measures may be the only treatment needed. Other options include CPAP, oral appliances, positional devices and, in selected people, surgery.

**Q: Is snoring the same as OSA?**
No. Some people snore without any other features of OSA, and their doctor can manage the snoring. Loud, habitual snoring is a night-time symptom of OSA, so tell your doctor about it, especially if you also have daytime sleepiness or someone has seen you stop breathing in your sleep.

**Q: Do I have to use CPAP forever?**
Sufficient weight loss can be curative, but whether you still need CPAP is a question for your doctor or sleep clinic. Talk to them before making any changes.

**Q: Can women have OSA?**
Yes. Post-menopausal women have a similar prevalence to men. Women's symptoms are often different from the classic pattern, and many may not have classical symptoms. They may instead present with insomnia, restless legs, depression, nightmares, palpitations, or hallucinations.

**Q: Will OSA affect my driving licence?**
Potentially. If your symptoms suggest you are not fit to hold an unconditional licence, you should not drive until you have been seen in a sleep clinic, and your doctor will request a specialist sleep assessment. Speak to your doctor.
