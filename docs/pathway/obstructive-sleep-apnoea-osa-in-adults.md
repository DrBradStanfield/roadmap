## What is Obstructive Sleep Apnoea (OSA)?

OSA is the most common form of sleep-disordered breathing, affecting an estimated 9–38% of people — yet most cases are undiagnosed. It occurs when the upper airway repeatedly collapses during sleep, causing disturbed breathing and fragmented sleep.

**Why it matters:**
- Untreated OSA confers a 2–4 fold increase in risk of cardiovascular events (heart attack, stroke)
- Strongly associated with hypertension — OSA prevalence is 40–80% in people with resistant hypertension
- Increases risk of work and motor vehicle accidents, and cognitive decline

---

## Who is at risk?

**Main risk factors:**
- Overweight or obesity (accounts for up to 50% of OSA risk)
- Male sex (twice the incidence compared to females)
- Post-menopausal women (similar prevalence to men)
- Age over 50 years
- Neck circumference >42 cm (male) or >41 cm (female)
- Excessive alcohol consumption
- Sedative medications including opioids
- Enlarged tonsils
- Craniofacial abnormalities (e.g., retrognathia, small jaw)
- Family history of OSA

**Ethnic risk note:** Māori are 4.3 times more likely to have moderate-to-severe OSA than non-Māori (after controlling for age and sex). Asian patients may develop OSA at lower BMI than other groups — a lower threshold for investigation applies.

## Symptoms

### Night-time symptoms
- Loud, habitual snoring — often bothersome to others
- Witnessed pauses in breathing (apnoeas), often ending with a snort
- Waking to urinate (nocturia)
- Restless sleep with frequent arousals
- Nocturnal sweating

### Daytime symptoms
- Non-restorative sleep (waking as tired as when going to bed)
- Excessive daytime sleepiness
- Morning headache
- Morning confusion
- Difficulty concentrating, poor memory
- Irritability, mood changes, depression or anxiety
- Sexual dysfunction

### Presentation in women (important)
Women often do not present with classic symptoms:
- May not score highly on the Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS was validated for male subjects)
- 40% of women with OSA may lack classical symptoms (like those on STOP-BANG)
- Women may instead present with insomnia, restless legs, depression, nightmares, palpitations, or hallucinations

## Clinical assessment tools

### Epworth Sleepiness Scale (ESS)
A self-reported questionnaire measuring daytime sleepiness across 8 situations. Scored 0–24.
- Score **>10** is considered abnormal
- Score **>16** raises concern for high risk of motor vehicle accident (MVA)
- Score **18 or higher** is a high-risk feature requiring specialist assessment

> A doctor will use the ESS to measure how sleepy you are during the day. Scores above 10 are considered abnormal.

### STOP-BANG Questionnaire
Used to estimate pre-test likelihood of OSA. Asks 8 yes/no questions:
- **S**noring — Do you snore loudly?
- **T**ired — Do you often feel tired, fatigued, or sleepy during the daytime?
- **O**bserved — Has anyone observed you stop breathing during your sleep?
- **P**ressure — Do you have or are you being treated for high blood pressure?
- **B**MI — BMI greater than 35?
- **A**ge — Age over 50?
- **N**eck — Neck circumference >40 cm?
- **G**ender — Male?

Scoring: 0–2 = low risk; 3–4 = intermediate risk; 5–8 = high risk

Calculator: https://www.mdcalc.com/calc/3992/stop-bang-score-obstructive-sleep-apnea

> Your doctor will likely use the STOP-BANG questionnaire to assess how likely you are to have OSA before arranging any tests.

## Driving safety

OSA significantly increases the risk of motor vehicle accidents. **High risk of MVA** if any of:
- Patient reports falling asleep at the wheel
- ESS score above 16
- History of accidents or near-misses related to sleepiness

**If high MVA risk:** Do not drive until assessed by a specialist.

**If fit to drive, self-management advice:**
- Minimise unnecessary driving
- Take regular rest breaks; share driving if possible
- Pull over immediately at any warning sign of sleepiness (head nodding, blinking, yawning, lane drifting)
- Avoid driving when sleep-deprived, at times of normal sleep, or long distances without breaks
- Avoid alcohol or sedative medications before driving

> If you are concerned about falling asleep at the wheel, speak to your doctor before driving again. This is a patient safety issue that your doctor needs to know about.

## Investigation: Sleep testing

### Community sleep test (overnight oximetry)
A basic overnight breathing study, available through some pharmacies or private clinics.

**Appropriate for:** Snorers with moderate-to-severe daytime sleepiness, with no other explanation for tiredness.

**NOT appropriate if any of these high-risk features apply:**
- Sleepy-driver episode (collision or near-miss linked to sleepiness)
- High-risk occupation (heavy machinery operator, professional driver)
- Surgical patients with significant perioperative risk
- Significant cardiovascular comorbidities (severe heart failure, uncontrollable hypertension)
- Significant respiratory or neurological condition
- Extreme obesity with BMI >50, especially if daytime oxygen saturation ≤95%
- ESS score 18 or higher

**Also avoid** if the patient snores but is not sleepy, has insomnia, or is currently undergoing sleep restriction therapy.

> A community sleep test gives basic overnight breathing information. Your doctor will decide whether it's the right test for your situation, or whether you need a more specialised assessment.

## Treatment options

### 1. Lifestyle measures (all patients benefit)
These apply whether symptoms are mild or severe, alone or combined with other therapies:

**Weight management:**
- Weight loss can reduce OSA severity and may be curative with sufficient loss
- Weight gain is a strong factor for worsening snoring and OSA

**Smoking cessation:**
- Smoking causes irritation and swelling of the pharyngeal space, worsening snoring and OSA

**Alcohol and sedative medications:**
- Alcohol, benzodiazepines, barbiturates, anti-epileptics, antidepressants, antihistamines, and opioids may worsen OSA
- Avoid alcohol before bed; avoid medications that cause weight gain or sedation

**Exercise:**
- Regular exercise reduces snoring and OSA severity, even without weight loss

**Sleep hygiene:**
- Keep consistent sleep and wake times every day
- Get into bed only when feeling drowsy and ready to sleep
- Prioritise consistent morning wake time (helps with light exposure)
- Avoid napping during the day; if unavoidable, nap before 3:00 pm for no more than 30 minutes
- Avoid caffeine and alcohol before bed
- Engage in relaxing activities before sleep (music, bath — not too hot)

**Positional therapy:**
- Avoid sleeping on your back — try using pillows, raising the head of the bed, or alarms with positional sensors

### 2. CPAP therapy (Continuous Positive Airway Pressure)
The most effective treatment for moderate-to-severe OSA.

**Benefits:**
- Reduces hypertension risk
- May reduce cardiometabolic disease severity
- Reduces risk of motor vehicle accidents due to sleepiness

**Common side effects and solutions:**
- Mask discomfort — proper mask fit is key; try different mask styles
- Dry mouth — add a humidification circuit to the CPAP
- Nasal congestion — usually responds to nasal corticosteroid sprays

> CPAP is the most effective treatment for OSA. It keeps your airway open while you sleep. Many people find it takes time to adjust — speak to your doctor or sleep clinic if you're having trouble tolerating it.

### 3. Oral appliances (Mandibular Advancement Splint — MAS)
- An effective second-line treatment, particularly for milder OSA or when CPAP is not tolerated
- Works by advancing the lower jaw to keep the airway open
- Less effective than CPAP at reducing OSA severity, but some people tolerate it better
- Requires good teeth and no jaw joint (TMJ) problems
- Fitted by a dentist with experience in dental sleep medicine

### 4. Positional devices (for positional OSA)
For people whose OSA occurs mainly when sleeping on their back:
- Lumbar or abdominal binders
- Backpacks
- Full-length pillows
- Tennis balls attached to back of nightwear
- Alarms with positional sensors

### 5. Surgical options
Considered for patients with specific anatomical causes (nasal obstruction, deviated septum, enlarged tonsils, craniofacial abnormalities):
- **Tonsillectomy:** Benefits adults with large tonsils and few other airway abnormalities
- **Nasal surgery:** Less benefit for OSA unless severe nasal obstruction; can improve CPAP compliance
- **Bariatric surgery:** Consider if BMI >35

## Managing co-morbidities

OSA is closely linked to several other conditions — managing these is part of OSA management:
- **Cardiovascular:** Hypertension, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, arrhythmias
- **Endocrine:** Diabetes, impaired glucose tolerance, thyroid disease
- **Respiratory:** COPD, asthma, allergic rhinitis
- **Sinus disease and reflux** can worsen OSA — treat these

---

## When to see a specialist

Your doctor should refer you for specialist sleep assessment if:
- You have suspected OSA with any high-risk features (see above)
- A community sleep test shows at least moderate sleep apnoea with excessive daytime sleepiness
- Symptoms suggest another sleep disorder (narcolepsy, parasomnia, periodic limb movement disorder)
- The cause of your excessive sleepiness remains unknown after initial assessment
- Your symptoms persist or recur despite treatment

> If your symptoms are significant or you have any high-risk features, your doctor will refer you to a specialist sleep service rather than manage this in the community. Don't delay in speaking to your doctor if you're concerned.

## Frequently asked questions

**Q: Can OSA be cured?**
Lifestyle changes — particularly significant weight loss — can be curative for some people. CPAP treats the condition effectively while in use. Surgical options may provide more permanent relief in selected patients.

**Q: Is snoring the same as OSA?**
No. Snoring alone, without daytime sleepiness or breathing pauses, does not mean you have OSA. However, habitual loud snoring is the most common symptom of OSA and warrants assessment.

**Q: Do I have to use CPAP forever?**
If you achieve significant weight loss or have surgery to correct an anatomical cause, you may no longer need CPAP. This is assessed by a specialist. Do not stop CPAP without medical review.

**Q: Can women have OSA?**
Yes. OSA is underdiagnosed in women because their symptoms are often different from the classic pattern (snoring, witnessed apnoeas). Women may instead present with insomnia, depression, fatigue, or palpitations.

**Q: Will OSA affect my driving licence?**
Potentially. Patients with symptoms suggesting they are unfit to drive an unconditional vehicle need specialist assessment. You should not drive if you are at high risk of falling asleep at the wheel. Speak to your doctor.
