# Sweating in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

Profuse sweating (hyperhidrosis) occurs in approximately 16% of patients with advanced cancer and is often worse at night (nocturnal diaphoresis). Fluid loss can be significant. Sweating is considered severe when clothing and/or bed linen needs to be changed regularly. Your doctor will assess the likely cause and recommend appropriate management.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider possible causes of sweating, which may include:

- **Infection** — if on chemotherapy, neutropenic sepsis must be excluded
- **Pain**
- **Anxiety**
- **High ambient temperature**
- **Alcohol withdrawal**
- **Respiratory distress**
- **Disseminated cancer** (e.g. liver metastases, lymphoma)
- **Endocrine causes:**
  - Oestrogen or androgen deficiency (natural, surgical, or treatment-related)
  - Hypoglycaemia
  - Hyperthyroidism
- **Medication side-effects:**
  - Antidepressants (including antidepressant withdrawal)
  - Antipsychotic withdrawal
  - Hormone therapies (e.g. tamoxifen)
  - Opioids
  - Tramadol

---

## Management

### Treating reversible causes

Where a specific reversible cause is identified (e.g. infection), your doctor will treat it directly.

### Non-pharmacological measures

Measures to cool the skin:
- Fans
- Open windows
- Cotton bed linen or absorbent sheets
- Loose cotton clothing
- Oral fluids
- Tepid sponging

### Medications

Your doctor may consider medications if non-pharmacological measures are insufficient. In palliative care, some doses, indications, and routes of administration may be outside standard product approval.

**Paracetamol** — 1 g every six hours may be useful. Note that paracetamol itself can occasionally cause sweating.

**NSAID** (e.g. celecoxib 100–200 mg twice daily) — if sweating is due to paraneoplastic fever. Monitor effects and reduce to lowest effective dose or discontinue if no benefit after 2–3 days.

**Famotidine** (20–40 mg once daily) — if sweating is due to paraneoplastic fever. Use with caution in older adults; reduce dose in renal impairment.

**Corticosteroids** — if no known cause, a therapeutic trial may be considered:
- Prednisone 20–40 mg in the morning, or
- Dexamethasone 4–8 mg in the morning

Aim to reduce gradually to the lowest effective dose. If ineffective, stop after 5 days.

---

## When to Seek Specialist Input

If symptoms persist or advice is required, your doctor will seek adult palliative care support and advice.

---

*Always discuss your symptoms and any changes to medications with your doctor. Palliative care decisions are made together with the patient, family/whānau, and the care team.*
