# Palpable Thyroid Nodule

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and initial management of a palpable thyroid nodule in adults and children.

---

## Background

Thyroid nodules are common:

- Found on palpation in about 10% of women and 2% of men
- With sensitive imaging (ultrasound, CT, MRI) the prevalence is as high as 50% of adults
- Only about 5% of palpable thyroid nodules are malignant — but that 5% represents important cancers likely to cause ill health

About 10% of people will develop small papillary thyroid cancers in later life that never cause symptoms and carry no health benefit from detection. Balancing detection of important cancers against over-investigation is a key challenge — there is genuine uncertainty in guidelines worldwide.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

Your doctor should be seen promptly if you notice any of the following alongside a thyroid swelling:

- Unexplained voice change or stridor (noisy breathing)
- A thyroid nodule in a child or young person
- Swollen lymph nodes in the neck alongside the thyroid lump
- A painless thyroid mass that has grown noticeably over 2–3 months
- Family history of multiple endocrine neoplasm (MEN syndrome)
- A previous biopsy (FNA) result showing Bethesda 4, 5, or 6

Additionally, seek **emergency care** if there are severe compressive symptoms:

- Significant hoarseness
- Difficulty swallowing
- Facial swelling with distended neck or chest veins (possible superior vena cava obstruction)
- Breathing difficulty, stridor at rest, or a choking sensation

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you with a history and examination.

### History

- Thyroid hormone symptoms — weight changes, sweats, tremor, palpitations, pulse
- Eye symptoms suggestive of thyroid eye disease (gritty/irritable eyes, eyelid swelling, double vision, visual disturbance, proptosis)
- How long the lump has been present, whether it has grown, and any sudden increase in size or tenderness (which can indicate bleeding into a nodule)
- Compressive symptoms (see above)
- Thyroid cancer risk factors:
  - Neck irradiation in childhood
  - Personal or family history of thyroid adenoma
  - Hashimoto's thyroiditis (associated with a small risk of lymphoma)
  - Cowden's syndrome, familial adenomatous polyposis, or familial thyroid cancer
  - Family history of multiple endocrine neoplasm

### Examination

Your doctor will examine your neck, checking:

- Location of the lump (lower central neck suggests thyroid origin; a thyroid lump typically moves with swallowing)
- Size, shape, consistency, and whether it is tender or pulsatile
- Lymph nodes in the neck
- Head, neck, chest, and abdomen for other relevant signs

If the lump is thought to be a goitre rather than a single nodule, or a different type of neck lump, your doctor will follow a different assessment pathway.

---

## Initial Investigation

Your doctor will arrange a **TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) blood test**. This is usually the only initial test needed. Reflex testing of T3/T4 may follow if TSH is abnormal.

See also: Thyroid Function Tests for interpretation of results.

---

## Management

Management depends on your age, the level of suspicion for cancer, and the TSH result. Your doctor will determine the appropriate pathway.

### Children Under 15 Years

Thyroid nodules in children are uncommon but more often malignant than in adults. Urgent referral to a specialist is arranged, who will organise imaging.

### Adults with High Suspicion of Thyroid Cancer

If your doctor identifies red flag features (see above), they will arrange:

- A thyroid ultrasound
- An urgent referral to a thyroid specialist (endocrinologist or surgeon)

Referral will not be delayed while waiting for the ultrasound result.

### Adults Without High Suspicion of Cancer

Based on TSH result, your doctor will typically arrange a thyroid ultrasound and referral to a thyroid specialist. The specific sequence depends on local specialist guidance.

If ultrasound shows small nodules as part of a multinodular goitre with no suspicious features, no high-risk factors, and no compressive or cosmetic concerns, monitoring with yearly clinical review may be appropriate rather than immediate specialist referral.

### After Ultrasound

Thyroid specialist assessment is arranged if:

- The ultrasound shows any suspicious features (even without red flags for cancer) — urgent referral
- There are compressive symptoms
- There are cosmetic concerns about the neck swelling

### Incidental Findings on CT or MRI

If a thyroid nodule is found incidentally on CT or MRI, your doctor will arrange a follow-up thyroid ultrasound if:

- The nodule is larger than 15 mm and you are over 35 years
- The nodule is larger than 10 mm and you are under 35 years

### Bethesda 4 FNA Result

Although Bethesda 4 (indeterminate/suspicious FNA) does not necessarily mean cancer, the risk of malignancy is up to 30%, and review by an endocrinologist or surgeon is required.

### Hyper- or Hypothyroidism

If you are clinically hyperthyroid or hypothyroid, your doctor will manage this according to the relevant thyroid function pathway.

---

## Key Points

- Most palpable thyroid nodules are benign — only about 5% are important cancers
- TSH is the key initial test; ultrasound is arranged based on TSH result and clinical features
- Red flag features trigger urgent specialist referral; do not wait for imaging before referring
- Children with thyroid nodules are managed differently and warrant urgent specialist review
- Monitoring in the community (rather than immediate specialist referral) is appropriate for some low-risk presentations

*Always discuss your results and next steps with your doctor. This information is a guide to how doctors approach this condition — it is not a substitute for clinical assessment.*
