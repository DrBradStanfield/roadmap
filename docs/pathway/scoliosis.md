# Scoliosis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers scoliosis primarily in patients aged up to 25 years. For adults aged 26 and over, management follows the back and neck pain pathway.

---

## Background

Scoliosis is abnormal lateral (coronal) curvature of the spine, typically defined as a spinal curve greater than 10°.

It affects 2–3% of the population. It can develop in infancy, but the primary age of onset is 10 to 15 years.

Scoliosis can cause visible deformity, emotional distress, back pain, and rib deformity.

**Types in children and adolescents:**

- **Idiopathic scoliosis** — cause unknown; accounts for approximately 80% of cases; diagnosis of exclusion
- **Neuromuscular scoliosis** — secondary to neurological or muscular diseases
- **Congenital scoliosis** — embryological malformation of one or more vertebrae

**Adult degenerative scoliosis** is due to progressive, asymmetric degeneration of the disc, facet joints, and other spinal structures. Patients typically present with lumbar back pain worsening with activity, sometimes combined with radicular leg pain or neurogenic claudication.

**Non-structural scoliosis** occurs when the spine is structurally normal but has a lateral curvature — for example, due to leg-length discrepancy.

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you using the **Adam's Forward Bend Test**:

1. You stand and bend forward at the waist.
2. Your doctor looks at your back from behind and beside you for any asymmetry.
3. They check for back or rib cage abnormalities, such as a rib hump.
4. If a non-structural cause is suspected, the test may also be done sitting — a structural scoliosis persists in both positions, while non-structural scoliosis (e.g. from leg-length discrepancy) disappears when sitting.

> **Note:** Child or adolescent scoliosis is not usually painful. Pain in a young person with scoliosis suggests an alternative diagnosis.

Your doctor will also ask about:

- Family history of scoliosis
- Pain — including night pain or pain interfering with daily activities
- Bowel and/or bladder dysfunction
- Unexplained weight loss or constitutional symptoms
- Limb neurology
- Pubertal development (age of menarche, secondary sex characteristics) to assess skeletal maturity — a large spinal curve before the adolescent growth spurt is more likely to progress
- History of other congenital abnormalities

**Physical examination** may include:

- Inspecting for asymmetries, elevated shoulder, prominent scapula, rib hump, or uneven waist
- Checking spinal range of motion and whether the midline of the head is over the centre of the pelvis
- Examining the chest (e.g. for pectus excavatum or carinatum)
- Checking the skin for café-au-lait spots or subcutaneous nodules (signs of neurofibromatosis)
- Neurological examination if neurological symptoms are present
- In newborns, checking for cutaneous sinuses or a hairy midline patch

> **Note:** 90% of adolescent idiopathic scoliosis curves are right thoracic/left lumbar (convex to the right). A left thoracic curve is more likely to indicate underlying pathology.

## Red Flags — Features Suggesting Higher Risk or Urgent Review

Your doctor will be more concerned if any of the following are present:

- Significant curve before menarche or puberty
- Other congenital or constitutional abnormalities
- Left thoracic curve (curve bulging out towards the left)
- Neuromuscular cause for the scoliosis
- Night pain or pain interfering with daily activities
- Bowel or bladder dysfunction
- Rapidly progressing curve
- Neurological symptoms in the limbs

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following depending on the type and severity of scoliosis:

**For mild adolescent idiopathic scoliosis:**

- Monitoring in the community without X-rays initially
- Sequential photographs (by parents or at appointments) to track progression
- Sitting height measurements to monitor, removing leg-growth variability
- Review every 6 months until skeletally mature

**For more significant curves, rapid progression, or obvious rib hump:**

- Scoliosis X-rays (PA and lateral whole-spine view standing)
- If the Cobb angle is greater than 10° on X-ray, specialist scoliosis assessment may be requested

**Referral to orthopaedic or scoliosis specialist** may be arranged for patients aged 25 years or younger with:

- Congenital scoliosis
- Signs of a condition affecting the spine causing the scoliosis
- Neuromuscular scoliosis
- Scoliosis due to tumours or other abnormalities (e.g. syringomyelia)
- Clinically rapidly progressing curves
- Left thoracic curves
- Significant curves or significant rib humps
- Scoliosis with severe pain

**Lifestyle advice:**

- Regular walking and swimming are helpful
- Your doctor may consider a physiotherapy assessment, which can be beneficial across all types of scoliosis
