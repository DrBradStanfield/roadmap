# Poisoning and Drug Overdose

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial assessment and management of poisoning and drug overdose in adults and children.

---

## Red Flags — Call an Ambulance Immediately

Seek emergency care if the person:

- Has an unprotected airway, is not breathing, or has no circulation
- Is unconscious or cannot be roused
- Is having seizures
- Has collapsed or is severely unwell

---

## Background

- A poisoning is the occurrence of harmful effects produced by exposure to a substance introduced to the body. The dose makes the poison — all things can be poisonous in sufficient amounts.
- Poisoning is a common reason for emergency department presentation and is a leading cause of death in people under 40 years.
- In adults, many poisoning cases involve intentional self-harm or recreational drug use.
- In children under 5 years, accidental exposure to medications, household chemicals, or plants is common.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess:

1. **Airway, breathing, and circulation** — first priority in any overdose.
2. **What was taken:**
   - Correctly identifying the toxin, drug, chemical, or plant — trade names and homonyms can be misleading. The actual bottle or product label is helpful.
   - Whether it was a single ingestion or staggered over time.
   - Time of ingestion, and when the patient last had the opportunity to take something if the exact time is unknown.
   - Estimated dose per kilogram of body weight. If unknown, the maximum possible amount the patient could have taken is used.
   - Specific poisons of note:
     - **Iron overdose** — can be asymptomatic early; needs prompt assessment and blood levels.
     - **Paracetamol** — dosing and timing are critical, especially in children; repeated supra-therapeutic ingestion can be fatal.
     - **Mushrooms** — samples and photos should be obtained if possible; always discuss with the Poisons Centre.
   - Co-ingestants such as alcohol or other recreational drugs.
   - Whether the patient is already symptomatic.
3. **Intent:** Self-harm exposures are more likely to involve larger doses. Exploratory ingestions by young children often involve smaller doses and many turn out to be sub-toxic — however, some substances are dangerous in very small amounts (see below).
4. **Relevant co-morbidities** such as a pre-existing seizure disorder, prolonged QT interval, or impaired cardiac function.
5. **Toxidromes** — recognisable toxic syndromes that indicate a specific poison:

### Anticholinergic Toxidrome
*Examples: tricyclic antidepressants, antihistamines*
- Dry, warm skin
- Fast heart rate (tachycardia)
- High temperature (hyperthermia)
- Confusion and hallucinations
- Urinary retention

### Sympathomimetic Toxidrome
*Examples: amphetamines, cocaine, caffeine, theophylline*
- CNS excitation and convulsions
- High blood pressure (hypertension)
- Fast heart rate (tachycardia)
- Sweating
- Dilated pupils (mydriasis)

### Muscarinic (Cholinergic) Toxidrome
*Examples: organophosphates, some mushrooms*
- Defecation and urination
- Constricted pupils (miosis)
- Slow heart rate (bradycardia), airway secretions, and bronchospasm
- Vomiting, excessive saliva, and tearing

### Nicotinic Toxidrome
*Examples: insecticides, cigarettes, cigars*
- Fast heart rate and high blood pressure
- Muscle fasciculations (twitching)
- Weakness and paralysis

### Opioid Toxidrome
*Examples: morphine, codeine, methadone*
- CNS depression (drowsiness, unconsciousness)
- Slow breathing (hypoventilation)
- Constricted pupils (miosis)

### Sedative-Hypnotic Toxidrome
*Examples: ethanol, benzodiazepines, baclofen*
- CNS depression
- Low blood pressure (hypotension) and slow heart rate (bradycardia)
- Respiratory depression
- Low body temperature (hypothermia)

An **ECG** is usually arranged for most adults who have had an overdose.

## High-Risk Exposures for Children in Small Amounts

The following substances can cause serious harm to young children even in small doses:

- Amphetamines
- Baclofen
- Button batteries
- Calcium channel blockers (e.g. amlodipine, diltiazem)
- Carbamazepine
- Chloroquine
- Clozapine
- Corrosives — strong acids or alkalis
- Camphor
- Hydrocarbons (e.g. eucalyptus oil, solvents)
- Naphthalene (mothballs)
- Nicotine — especially liquid from e-cigarettes
- Organophosphates
- Opioids and methadone
- Paraquat
- Propranolol
- Sulphonylureas (e.g. glibenclamide, gliclazide)
- Theophylline
- Tricyclic antidepressants
- Venlafaxine

---

## Management

**For poisoning advice at any hour, call the National Poisons Centre: 0800 764 766 (0800 POISON).** Clinical toxicologists are available 24/7.

- If the person is unwell or unstable, call **111** for an ambulance. Your doctor or paramedics will provide supportive care.
- If the person is stable, your doctor may seek advice from the National Poisons Centre before deciding on next steps.
- If it was an accidental overdose, your doctor will provide education about prevention.
- If the overdose was intentional, your doctor will address the underlying mental health concern and any drug dependency.
