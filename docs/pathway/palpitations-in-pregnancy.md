# Palpitations in Pregnancy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of palpitations during pregnancy — including when they are a normal pregnancy change and when further investigation or specialist advice is needed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

Contact your doctor or midwife immediately, or go to the emergency department, if palpitations are associated with any of the following:

- Chest pain
- Shortness of breath on exertion
- Paroxysmal nocturnal dyspnoea (waking at night short of breath)
- Fainting or near-fainting (syncope or presyncope)

---

## Background

Palpitations are the conscious awareness of the beating heart. They may feel rapid, slow, irregular, or unusually forceful.

**Why palpitations are common in pregnancy:**

During pregnancy, the body undergoes significant cardiovascular changes. Blood volume increases by around 40% by 28–30 weeks, cardiac output rises by 30–50%, and the heart rate increases by up to 20 beats per minute. These normal changes can cause an increased awareness of the heartbeat, extra beats (extrasystoles), or a faster-than-usual heart rate.

Dehydration and electrolyte disturbances from severe nausea and vomiting (hyperemesis) can also cause palpitations.

**Common causes:**

- Increased awareness of the normal heartbeat
- Extrasystoles (ectopic beats) — extra or skipped beats that feel noticeable but are usually harmless
- Sinus tachycardia (fast but regular heart rate)
- Intermittent supraventricular tachycardia (SVT)

**Less common causes that require investigation:**

- Atrial flutter or atrial fibrillation — these are not normal in pregnancy and require assessment

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history to understand what you mean by palpitations and ask about:

- Associated symptoms — sweating, weight loss, shortness of breath, chest pain, fainting
- Previous cardiac problems (congenital or acquired), anxiety, or thyroid disease
- Family history of sudden unexplained death or familial arrhythmias (dysrhythmias)
- Caffeine or nicotine intake
- Other substances (e.g., cocaine, amphetamines)
- Medications that can affect the heart — such as beta-2 agonists (used for asthma) or cold and flu medicines containing pseudoephedrine
- Current life stressors that might be contributing to anxiety

Physical examination typically includes:

- Cardiac and respiratory examination
- Assessment for signs of dehydration, anaemia, or thyroid overactivity (thyrotoxicosis)

**Investigations your doctor may arrange:**

- **12-lead ECG** — note that some ECG changes are normal in pregnancy, including mild sinus tachycardia, minor ST and T-wave changes, and mild leftward axis deviation. Your doctor will interpret the ECG in the context of your pregnancy.
- **Blood tests** — full blood count (FBC), electrolytes and creatinine, thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), iron studies

**Features of concern that prompt further assessment:**

- Family history of sudden unexplained death or familial arrhythmias
- Abnormal examination findings or ECG changes
- Known structural heart disease

Further investigation is unlikely to be needed if you are otherwise well, have no features of concern, and have a normal ECG — for example, if symptoms are due to an increased awareness of the normal heartbeat or extrasystoles. Similarly, mild sinus tachycardia may not require further investigation once other causes (anaemia, medications, heart failure, thyroid disease, pulmonary embolism) have been excluded.

---

## Management

Management depends on what is found on assessment.

**If red flags or features of concern are present:**

Your doctor may seek specialist advice from an obstetric medicine physician or cardiologist if:

- Palpitations are associated with chest pain, exertional shortness of breath, waking at night short of breath, fainting, or near-fainting
- There are features of concern alongside an abnormal ECG

**If an underlying cause is identified:**

Your doctor may consider treating the underlying condition — for example, thyroid disease in pregnancy or anaemia in pregnancy — which may resolve the palpitations.

**If no serious cause is found:**

Most pregnant women with palpitations do not require referral or treatment. Your doctor may reassure you that the palpitations are related to normal pregnancy cardiovascular changes, and provide advice about when to seek review.
