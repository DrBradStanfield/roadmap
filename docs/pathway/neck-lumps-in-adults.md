# Neck Lumps in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and initial management of neck lumps in adults aged 15 years and over.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

The following are Ministry of Health criteria for high suspicion of head, neck, or salivary cancer. If any apply, your doctor will arrange urgent specialist assessment:

- Mass larger than 1 cm and present for more than 3 weeks
- Mass that is increasing in size
- Previous head and neck cancer, including skin cancer
- Facial palsy
- Any new unexplained upper respiratory tract symptoms — hoarseness, difficulty swallowing (dysphagia), throat or ear pain, blocked nose or ear, oral ulceration

---

## Background

Neck lumps in adults are relatively common and are very rarely the first sign of metastatic cancer. The most common primary for a metastatic neck lump is in the head and neck region (e.g., oropharyngeal or nasopharyngeal tumour). The patient may have symptoms from the primary site (throat pain, difficulty swallowing, hoarseness), but the primary site may also be asymptomatic. There may be a history of facial skin cancers.

Neck lumps are broadly divided into three categories:

**Thyroid-related lumps**
- Commonly found on examination or incidentally through imaging
- The main concern is determining whether they are benign or malignant

**Non-lymph node lumps**
- Lipomas and sebaceous cysts — the diagnosis is usually clinical; a fine needle aspiration (FNA) is often unnecessary
- Salivary gland tumours — these should be referred to an ENT surgeon regardless of FNA result

**Lymph nodes**
- Normal — often felt in children and young adults, particularly in the posterior cervical triangle and upper neck; symmetrical, elliptoid in shape, and long-standing
- Reactive — inflammation or infection, e.g., glandular fever, tonsillitis, dental abscess, tuberculosis
- Malignant — head and neck primary, metastatic, or lymphoma

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history and ask about:

- How long the lump has been present and whether it has changed over time (a lump present for more than 3 weeks needs investigation unless it is shrinking; lumps present for fewer than 3 weeks are usually due to infection)
- Any pain or tenderness
- Associated symptoms — mouth ulcers, difficulty swallowing, nasal obstruction or bleeding, hoarseness, ear pain
- Systemic symptoms — weight loss, night sweats
- Past history of any cancer, especially head and neck skin cancers
- Smoking and alcohol intake
- Recent travel, or arrival from a country with high rates of tuberculosis (TB)

Physical examination typically includes:

- Position of the lump — lateral, upper midline, or lower midline
- Characteristics of the lump: size, shape, whether it is single or multiple, mobile or fixed, smooth or lobulated, hard or soft, tender or non-tender, pulsatile
- Examination of the mouth (floor of mouth, tongue, tonsils, oropharynx, teeth, gums, salivary glands), nose, and skin
- Check for lymph nodes elsewhere in the body

**Common differential diagnoses by location:**

*Lateral neck lumps*
- Lymph node (inflammatory or malignant)
- Salivary gland disorders (parotid or submandibular)
- Branchial cyst — often presents in children and young adults as a cystic upper neck mass; can be difficult to distinguish from a cystic metastatic lymph node in adults; important to refer for removal before infection occurs
- Other: carotid body tumour, nerve sheath tumours, lipomas
- Normal variant: hyoid bone, carotid bulb
- Plunging ranula: soft, ill-defined swelling in the submental or submandibular region, may be associated with swelling in the floor of the mouth

*Lower midline lumps*
- Thyroid origin — the lump is located in the lower central neck and moves with swallowing
- May be a thyroid nodule or an enlarged thyroid gland (goitre)

*Upper midline lumps*
- More common in children but can present in young adults; usually developmental
- Thyroglossal duct cyst — commonly occurs over the hyoid bone, moves during swallowing, may become painful if infected; important to refer for removal before infection occurs
- Dermoid — occurs under the chin, usually in young children

Your doctor may consider investigations including:

- Fine needle aspiration (FNA) — not required for every neck lump; indicated when the clinical diagnosis is unclear or there is concern about malignancy
- Blood tests and chest X-ray — when a systemic cause is suspected (e.g., Epstein-Barr virus, cytomegalovirus, toxoplasmosis, cat scratch fever, HIV, lymphoma, TB), or if there is persistent hoarseness
- Ultrasound — recommended for specific indications

---

## Management

Your doctor will manage the lump according to the most likely diagnosis:

- **High suspicion of cancer with airway concern or significant difficulty swallowing:** Your doctor will arrange emergency ENT assessment
- **Any red flag suspicious for cancer:** Your doctor will arrange urgent (non-emergency) ENT assessment and flag as high suspicion of cancer; if there is persistent hoarseness, an urgent chest X-ray will also be arranged
- **Thyroid nodule or goitre:** Your doctor will manage according to the relevant thyroid pathway
- **Suspected TB lymphadenopathy:** Your doctor will manage according to TB guidelines
- **Suspected infective cause:** Your doctor may prescribe appropriate antibiotics if a bacterial cause is likely, with a follow-up review in 1 to 2 weeks; complete resolution may take a couple of months
- **Suspected dental cause:** Your doctor will manage or refer for the underlying dental problem
- **Thyroglossal or branchial cyst confirmed on ultrasound:** Your doctor will seek ENT advice regarding timing of referral for surgical removal

Always discuss with your doctor if you have concerns about a neck lump.
