# Knee Joint Aspiration and Injection

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway applies to adults aged 18 years and older. It covers the procedure for draining fluid from the knee joint (aspiration) and/or injecting corticosteroid into the knee joint.

This is a procedure requiring clinical skill and training. Your doctor will only perform it if they are competent to do so — otherwise they will refer you to an appropriate specialist or a radiology service for ultrasound-guided aspiration.

---

## When Is This Procedure Considered?

Your doctor may consider knee aspiration, with or without a corticosteroid injection, in the following situations:

- **Following trauma** — to relieve pain or to help diagnose whether a fracture is present
- **Inflammatory conditions or arthritis** — to help establish a diagnosis, provide pain relief, or exclude a joint infection when septic arthritis is considered less likely
- **Osteoarthritis** — corticosteroid injection may be used as an adjunct to other treatments for moderate to severe pain

**Important note on corticosteroid injections:** Evidence suggests they may provide short-term pain relief but typically make little difference to longer-term outcomes.

---

## Contraindications — When the Procedure Should Not Be Done

Your doctor will check for reasons not to proceed. The procedure **should not** be performed if:

- Septic arthritis (joint infection) is suspected
- You have a prosthetic (artificial) knee joint — this requires specialist orthopaedic assessment
- There is a skin problem overlying the injection site, such as cellulitis, active infection, or psoriasis
- You are unable to cooperate with the procedure

Hip aspirations require specialist advice and are not performed as part of this pathway.

---

## Red Flags — Signs That Need Urgent Attention

Seek urgent medical assessment if, at any time before or after the procedure, you develop:

- A hot, significantly swollen, or very painful joint
- Fever or feeling generally unwell
- Pain that does not settle within 48 hours after the procedure, or pain that begins 48 hours or more after the injection

These may indicate a joint infection (septic arthritis), which is a medical emergency.

---

## Adverse Effects

Your doctor will discuss the following risks before obtaining your consent.

**Joint aspiration risks** (rare):
- Infection
- Bleeding into the joint (more likely if you have a bleeding disorder or are on anticoagulant medication)
- Damage to surrounding structures

**Corticosteroid injection risks:**
- Post-injection pain flare — pain may temporarily worsen for up to 3 days
- Facial flushing and mood changes
- Less common risks: infection, bleeding or bruising, tendon weakening or rupture, nerve injury, local fat or skin thinning, skin colour change at the injection site, vasovagal (fainting) reaction
- **For people with diabetes:** a temporary rise in blood glucose levels can occur and may last up to 7 days — your doctor will advise on extra monitoring

---

## The Procedure

Your doctor will:

1. Confirm the indications and check for contraindications
2. Obtain your consent (verbal or written)
3. Prepare sterile equipment, including syringes, needles, specimen containers, and skin cleanser
4. Position you lying face-up with your knee fully extended, with access to the side of the knee
5. Clean the skin and may apply local anaesthetic (1% lidocaine) before inserting the needle
6. Insert the needle and aspirate (drain) as much fluid as possible
7. Examine the aspirate — if a layer of fat is visible on top of any blood, this indicates a fracture may be present
8. If appropriate, inject corticosteroid mixed with local anaesthetic before removing the needle

**If a corticosteroid injection is given, your doctor may use:**
- Methylprednisolone (Depo-Medrol, 40 mg/mL), 1 mL mixed with 3–5 mL of 1% lidocaine, or
- Triamcinolone acetonide (Kenacort, 40 mg/mL), 1 mL mixed with 3–5 mL of 1% lidocaine

**The corticosteroid injection will NOT be given if:**
- The aspirated fluid appears thick, creamy, or pus-like (suggesting infection)
- You have a joint fracture, active haemarthrosis (blood in the joint), or systemic infection
- You may need surgery on the joint within the next 12 months (relative contraindication)
- You are severely immunosuppressed (relative contraindication)
- You have had more than 3 injections in the past year, or a previous injection within the past 6 weeks (relative contraindication)

---

## After the Procedure

**Aspiration only:**
- A dressing will be applied and you will rest for at least 15 minutes
- Monitor for any signs of infection (see Red Flags above)

**Aspiration with corticosteroid injection:**
- Cold compresses and paracetamol or NSAIDs may help with pain once the local anaesthetic wears off
- A compression bandage (e.g. Tubigrip) may be applied
- Rest the joint and reduce weight-bearing activity for the first 1–2 days
- Resume exercise 1–2 days after the procedure, once the acute pain has settled
- **If you have diabetes:** monitor your blood glucose more closely — elevated levels can occur for up to 7 days

---

## Laboratory Results from the Aspirate

If fluid is sent to the laboratory, your doctor will interpret the results. Normal ranges and what they suggest:

| Finding | Normal / Non-Inflammatory | Inflammatory | Infectious |
|---|---|---|---|
| Colour | Colourless or pale yellow | Yellow | Yellow |
| Appearance | Clear, slightly cloudy | Turbid (cloudy) | Turbid, purulent |
| White cell count (per mm³) | Less than 2,000 | 2,000–50,000 | More than 50,000 |
| Predominant cell type | Mononuclear | Neutrophil | Neutrophil |
| Gram stain | Negative | Negative | Often positive |
| Culture | Negative | Negative | Positive |

**Important:** A negative gram stain or culture does not rule out septic arthritis.

**Crystal analysis** (examined under polarised light microscopy):
- **Gout** — strongly negative birefringent needle-shaped crystals (monosodium urate)
- **Pseudogout** — weakly positive birefringent rectangular or rhomboid-shaped crystals (calcium pyrophosphate dihydrate)

---

## Further Corticosteroid Injections

Your doctor may consider a repeat injection if needed:

- A single injection is often sufficient, but several may be needed for adequate symptom relief
- The interval between injections should generally be more than 3 months
- If there was no improvement from the first injection, the joint and diagnosis will be re-evaluated before proceeding

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Considered

Your doctor may consider referring you to orthopaedics or rheumatology if:

- You fail to respond to treatment
- Symptoms persist despite approximately 6 months of management
- Symptoms are severe
- The diagnosis needs reconsideration
