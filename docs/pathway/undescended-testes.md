# Undescended Testes

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment of undescended testes (UDT) in children — distinguishing undescended from retractile testes and determining when specialist referral is needed.

---

## Background

Undescended testes (UDT) affects about 5% of full-term male children and up to one third of preterm males. The condition is most commonly unilateral.

The cause may be:

- **Congenital** — the testes have not descended by birth; may be due to hormonal or genetic factors, though the cause is unknown in most cases
- **Acquired** — the testes are in the scrotum at birth, but the spermatic cord fails to grow adequately and pulls the testes up into the groin; this can occur between ages 1 and 10 years

UDT is associated with increased risk of infertility, testicular cancer, torsion, and traumatic injury.

Ambiguous genitalia may be present in up to 25% of patients with UDT and hypospadias.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. **Routinely check for UDT** at regular immunisation and developmental reviews
2. **Examine the testes** to determine whether each testis sits spontaneously at the bottom of the scrotum

Key examination steps your doctor will perform:

- Ensure the child is relaxed and warm (cold or fear can cause a descended testis to retract)
- Gently milk the testis toward the scrotum, grasp it, then release — if undescended, it will immediately spring back upward
- Check that the penis and foreskin are normal

Your doctor will then classify each testis as either **undescended** or **retractile**:

### Undescended Testes

- Cannot sit in the scrotum spontaneously
- Retracts out of the scrotum immediately when released
- Often smaller than the testis on the other side
- The scrotum may appear less developed on the affected side

### Retractile Testes

- Reside in the scrotum at least some of the time spontaneously
- Can be manipulated into the scrotum and will stay there for a while when released
- Are of normal size
- Note: retractile testes do not occur before age 1 year — in infants under 12 months, if a testis is not in the scrotum, it is treated as undescended

If a testis cannot be found in the scrotum or inguinal area, your doctor will consider other possible locations (parascrotal, femoral, perineal, pre-penile).

Note: ultrasound is **not** indicated for impalpable or undescended testes.

---

## Management

Your doctor will consider referral to paediatric surgery if:

- The testes have not descended by **3 months of age** (corrected age for premature infants)
- The testis springs back out of the scrotum immediately after release, suggesting it is undescended
- There is diagnostic uncertainty between undescended and retractile testes

**Corrected age** is calculated by subtracting the number of weeks born before 40 weeks gestation from the child's chronological age. For example, a baby born at 32 weeks (8 weeks early) who is 20 weeks old has a corrected age of 12 weeks.

If a markedly retractile testis is found fully descended, your doctor will monitor it every 1–2 years as some ascend over time and become undescended.

### What to Expect After Referral

If the paediatric surgeon diagnoses the testis as:

- **Impalpable** — a laparoscopy will be arranged to locate it
- **Undescended** — an **orchidopexy** (surgery to bring the testis into the scrotum) is typically performed around age 1 year as a day-case operation under general anaesthetic

After surgery, a check at 1 month post-surgery assesses wound healing. The paediatric surgeon will review the child at 1 year post-surgery and ongoing as needed to assess testis position and growth.
