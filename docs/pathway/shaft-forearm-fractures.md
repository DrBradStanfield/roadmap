# Shaft Forearm Fractures

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers isolated or combined fractures of the radius and ulna shaft, including fracture dislocations, in both children and adults.

---

## Background

Shaft forearm fractures include:

- Isolated fractures of either the radius or ulna
- Fractures in children
- Fracture dislocations of both the radius and ulna

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department immediately if there are signs of **compartment syndrome**:

- Increasing pain despite the fracture being immobilised
- Pain when the fingers are passively moved inside a cast
- Loss of active finger movement
- Swelling and muscle tenderness in the forearm
- Numbness or sensory changes in the hand

Compartment syndrome is a surgical emergency requiring urgent decompression.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess the injury by:

1. Taking a history of how the injury occurred
2. Examining the arm, including the elbow and wrist — looking for pain, swelling, or deformity
3. Checking upper limb neurovascular function:
   - **Sensation:** Testing the median nerve (thumb, index, middle, and radial half of ring finger on the palm side), the radial nerve (dorsolateral hand and dorsal thumb, index, and middle fingers), and the ulnar nerve (little finger and medial half of ring finger, both sides)
   - **Motor function:** Asking you to make the "OK" sign (median nerve), extend fingers or wrist against resistance (radial nerve), and spread fingers apart against resistance (ulnar nerve)
   - **Circulation:** Checking radial and ulnar pulses and capillary refill
4. Checking carefully for compartment syndrome — this is high risk with radius and ulna fractures
5. Arranging X-rays of the arm including AP and lateral views of both the wrist and elbow

---

## Fracture Types

Your doctor will determine which type of fracture is present:

- **Ulna and/or radius fracture** — both bones or one bone broken
- **Isolated midshaft ulna fracture (nightstick fracture)** — ulna only, often from a direct blow
- **Galleazzi fracture** — radius fracture with dislocation of the distal ulna
- **Monteggia fracture** — proximal ulna fracture with dislocation of the radial head, usually from a fall onto a pronated forearm

---

## Management

### Compartment Syndrome

If compartment syndrome is suspected, your doctor will arrange urgent orthopaedic assessment for decompression surgery.

### Children

Your doctor may consider:

- **If there is any displacement or angulation:** Urgent orthopaedic assessment. In children under 10, manipulation is often not needed as bones remodel well.
- **If undisplaced, non-angulated greenstick fracture:** Treatment in an above-elbow backslab.
- **If slow to regain movement more than 2 weeks after cast removal:** Physiotherapy assessment.

### Adults

Your doctor may consider:

- **Both ulna and radius fractures, or any fracture dislocation (Galleazzi or Monteggia):** Urgent orthopaedic assessment — these typically require surgery.
- **Isolated midshaft ulna fracture (nightstick fracture):**
  - If undisplaced, or minimally displaced (less than 5 mm) or minimally angulated (less than 10°): Above-elbow plaster of Paris (POP) cast for 6–8 weeks.
  - If displaced more than 5 mm or angulated more than 10°: Urgent orthopaedic assessment.
- **While in a cast:** Encourage movement of the neck, shoulder, and fingers to prevent stiffness.
- **After cast removal:** Active mobilisation is encouraged.
- **If slow to regain movement more than 2 weeks after cast removal:** Physiotherapy assessment.
