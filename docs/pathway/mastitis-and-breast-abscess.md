# Mastitis and Breast Abscess

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of mastitis and breast abscess in breastfeeding, lactating, and non-lactating patients.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Medical Attention

See your doctor urgently if you have:

- A persistent breast lump or redness (erythema) that is not improving with treatment
- Skin dimpling over the breast (peau d'orange appearance)

These may be signs of inflammatory breast cancer and require prompt assessment.

---

## Background

Mastitis and breast abscess usually affect breastfeeding or lactating people, but can also develop in non-lactating people (including men).

The progression typically follows this sequence:

1. **Inflammatory mastitis** — localised ductal inflammation causing a blocked or "plugged" duct
2. **Bacterial mastitis** — inflammatory mastitis that progresses to bacterial infection (requires antibiotics)
3. **Breast abscess** — bacterial mastitis that progresses to an infected fluid collection (requires drainage)

Other conditions that can present similarly:
- **Galactocele** — a smooth, round, non-tender cyst containing milk-like fluid; occurs during or shortly after lactation
- **Inflammatory breast cancer** — a rare but aggressive cancer that can look like mastitis; suspect if not responding to treatment

Key facts:
- Infective (bacterial) mastitis is most commonly caused by *Staphylococcus* or *Streptococcus* species
- No evidence exists that mastitis can be caused by *Candida*
- Mastitis and its treatment do not pose an infection risk to the breastfeeding child — breastfeeding should continue

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

### Take a history, including:

- Presenting symptoms: fever, flu-like symptoms, pain, redness, swelling, or palpable lumps; lymph node swelling
- Any previous episodes or treatment (including antibiotics)
- Predisposing factors:

**In breastfeeding or lactating patients:**
- Difficulty latching
- Nipple pain, distortion, or injury
- Prolonged feeding intervals
- Poor milk transfer (including tongue-tie in the baby)
- Hyperlactation (overproduction of milk)
- Incorrect use of breastfeeding devices (silicone collectors, breast pumps, nipple shields)
- Previous history of mastitis, breast abscess, or breast surgery

**In non-lactating patients (including men):**
- Smoking
- Diabetes
- Corticosteroid use
- Trauma
- Rheumatoid arthritis
- Note: periductal mastitis (infection of the subareolar ducts) particularly affects young women who smoke

### Perform a breast examination, looking for:

- Breast contours — dimpling, puckering, peau d'orange, visible lumps
- Nipple changes — inversion, erythema, eczema, ulcers
- Areas of erythema — localised or widespread, unilateral or bilateral
- Palpable lumps — size, shape, consistency, mobility, tenderness, exact position

Your doctor will also check for red flag features that may suggest breast cancer:

- A discrete breast or axillary lump, ulceration, or skin dimpling
- Persistent nipple eczema, ulceration, or recent nipple retraction
- Unilateral spontaneous nipple discharge that is blood-stained, clear, or serous (green or yellow discharge is not a red flag)
- Breast inflammation not responding to treatment — particularly without fever or abnormal blood tests
- Repeated presentations with the same breast symptoms

### Consider the most likely diagnosis:

**Inflammatory mastitis** (early, non-infective):
- Gradual onset of a pea-sized (or larger) area of unilateral breast inflammation and pain
- May be associated with localised swelling and overlying red streaks or pink skin
- Typically more painful before a breastfeed; pain improves with milk removal
- Deep massage or attempts to "pump to empty" can worsen symptoms
- May be associated with systemic symptoms (fever, chills, tachycardia) if more advanced

**Bacterial mastitis** (infective):
- Inflammatory mastitis that has progressed to bacterial infection
- Presents as cellulitis — a tender, hot, swollen area of the breast that can spread across quadrants
- Systemic symptoms persisting more than 24 hours: temperature ≥38.5°C, tachycardia, chills, headache, flu-like symptoms
- Always requires antibiotics

**Breast abscess**:
- Bacterial mastitis that has progressed to an infected fluid collection
- Presents as progressive pain, swelling, and redness with a well-defined lump (hard, painful, or fluctuant)
- Requires surgical aspiration or drainage — antibiotics alone are not sufficient

**Galactocele**:
- A smooth, round, non-tender cyst containing milk-like fluid
- Can range from 1 cm to >10 cm; may fluctuate in size with feeding
- Usually resolves with a single aspiration

**Inflammatory breast cancer**:
- Rare but aggressive — diffuse erythema and oedema involving a third or more of the breast skin
- Caused by tumour emboli in the dermal lymphatics
- Usually no fever; not necessarily related to breastfeeding
- Red flags: breast inflammation not responding to antibiotics, peau d'orange skin changes

### Investigations

Your doctor may consider:
- **Breast milk culture** in select cases (recurrent mastitis, severe or unusual presentations, failure to respond to antibiotics within 2 days, or risk factors for MRSA). Routine blood tests (FBC, CRP) are not recommended for diagnosis.
- **Breast ultrasound** only if bacterial mastitis is not improving after 48 hours of antibiotics and there is concern about a developing abscess — not needed before referral for a clinically obvious abscess or galactocele.

## Management

Your doctor will manage you based on the most likely diagnosis.

### Inflammatory Mastitis (breastfeeding/lactating patients)

Inflammatory mastitis will often resolve with conservative care:

- **Symptom management:**
  - Apply ice or cold compresses to the affected area (avoiding the nipple) for 20 minutes every hour
  - Avoid applying heat — this increases blood flow and worsens symptoms
  - Use gentle lymphatic drainage techniques to reduce swelling if desired
  - Avoid deep massage, vibrating devices, saline soaks, castor oil, and topical products
  - Wear an appropriately fitting, supportive bra
  - Your doctor may consider sunflower or soy lecithin (5–50 mg orally once daily) to reduce ductal inflammation, or therapeutic ultrasound if available

- **Breastfeeding advice:**
  - Continue breastfeeding normally — do not attempt to "pump to empty" as this worsens symptoms
  - If using a pump, express only the volume the baby needs
  - Hand-expressing small amounts for comfort is fine
  - A temporary drop in milk production may occur but will recover once inflammation settles

- **Other considerations:**
  - Your doctor may consider whether the baby has a tongue-tie
  - Antibiotics are reserved for bacterial mastitis only at this stage
  - Pain relief (paracetamol, ibuprofen) is safe while breastfeeding
  - Review in 48 hours, or sooner if symptoms worsen

### Bacterial Mastitis (breastfeeding/lactating patients)

Your doctor will prescribe oral antibiotics. It is safe to continue breastfeeding while on antibiotics.

**First-line antibiotic:** flucloxacillin 1 g orally three times a day for 5–7 days

**If penicillin allergic:**
- Mild allergy (no anaphylaxis): cefalexin 1 g orally three times a day for 5–7 days
- Severe allergy (anaphylaxis/immediate urticaria): clindamycin 450 mg orally three times a day for 5–7 days

Additional management:
- Pain relief: paracetamol and ibuprofen are safe while breastfeeding
- Symptom management as per inflammatory mastitis (ice, supportive bra)
- Breastfeeding should continue
- Consider tongue-tie in the baby; consider referral to a lactation consultant
- If no improvement after 48 hours or a lump is enlarging, your doctor may arrange an ultrasound to check for abscess
- Review in 48 hours or sooner if worsening

### Bacterial Mastitis (non-lactating patients, including men)

The antibiotic choice differs because anaerobic bacteria (not *Staphylococcus*) are the most likely pathogen.

**First-line antibiotic:** amoxicillin + clavulanic acid 625 mg orally three times a day for 7 days

**If penicillin allergic:**
- Mild allergy: cefalexin 1 g orally three times a day for 7 days
- Severe allergy: clindamycin 450 mg orally three times a day for 7 days

Additional management:
- Pain relief: paracetamol and NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen)
- Ice packs and supportive bra (where appropriate)
- Avoid deep massage
- Review in 48 hours or sooner if worsening
- If not improving after 48 hours of antibiotics, your doctor may arrange an ultrasound

### Breast Abscess

Antibiotics alone are not sufficient — surgical aspiration or drainage is required.

Your doctor will refer you for surgical assessment. Breastfeeding can usually continue after drainage (while on further antibiotics).

Post-surgery follow-up will include:
- Checking wound healing and symptom resolution
- Reviewing breastfeeding or milk drainage (in lactating patients)
- Pain relief as needed (paracetamol, ibuprofen — safe while breastfeeding)
- Referral to a lactation consultant if needed

### Galactocele

Your doctor will refer you for breast clinic assessment to confirm the diagnosis and for aspiration. The aspirate will consist of milk.

## When to Seek Urgent Care

Your doctor may need to arrange urgent hospital assessment if you:

- Are severely unwell with bacterial mastitis (high fever, tachycardia, systemic illness)
- Have a suspected breast abscess (always requires aspiration/drainage)
- Need IV antibiotics
- Have any red flag features suggesting breast cancer (including inflammatory breast cancer) — your doctor will note "high suspicion of cancer" on any referral
- Have a persisting breast abnormality (lump, altered contour, skin dimpling, persistent redness) after appropriate treatment
