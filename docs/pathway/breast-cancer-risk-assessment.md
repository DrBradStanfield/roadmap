# Breast Cancer Risk Assessment

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers how doctors assess breast cancer risk in people who have no current breast symptoms but may have an increased risk due to family history or personal history.

---

## Important Note

This pathway is for **asymptomatic** people (no current breast symptoms). If you have noticed a new breast lump, nipple discharge, skin changes, or other breast symptoms, see your doctor promptly — that is a different clinical pathway.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your breast cancer risk by taking a detailed history of:

### 1. Family History

Your doctor will ask about cancer diagnoses in your relatives, including:

- Any primary cancer in all first-degree and second-degree relatives on both sides of the family
- Age at diagnosis of any cancers
- Whether you have a first-degree relative who is a known gene mutation carrier (e.g., BRCA1 or BRCA2)

**Relative definitions:**

| Degree | Who counts |
|--------|------------|
| First-degree | Mother, father, daughter, son, sister, brother |
| Second-degree | Grandparent, grandchild, aunt, uncle, niece, nephew, half-sibling |
| Third-degree | Great-grandparent, first cousin, great-aunt/uncle |

Family history on each side of the family (maternal vs paternal) is considered separately.

### 2. Familial Risk Category

Your doctor will categorise your familial risk as:

**High risk (affects less than 1% of women):**
- Gene positive: BRCA1, BRCA2, TP53, or E-cadherin mutation
- Two or more first- or second-degree relatives on the same side of the family, plus one or more of:
  - Additional relative with breast or ovarian cancer
  - A relative diagnosed younger than 40 years
  - A male relative with breast cancer
  - A female relative with more than one primary breast cancer
  - A female relative with both breast and ovarian cancer
  - Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry

**Moderate risk (affects less than 4% of women):**
- One first-degree relative diagnosed younger than 50 years
- Two second-degree relatives on the same side of the family, with one diagnosed younger than 50 years
- One first-degree and one second-degree relative, with one diagnosed younger than 50 years
- Two first-degree relatives on the same side of the family, both diagnosed between ages 50–70
- Mother and maternal/paternal grandmother, both diagnosed between ages 50–70

**Average risk (affects 95% of women):**
- No family history of breast cancer
- One first-degree relative diagnosed older than 50 years
- One second-degree relative of any age
- Two second-degree relatives on different sides of the family
- Two second-degree relatives, both diagnosed older than 50 years
- Family history of ovarian cancer only, with no known gene mutation

### 3. Personal Risk Factors

Your doctor will also ask about personal history that increases breast cancer risk:

- Past chest irradiation or mantle radiotherapy
- Previous ovarian cancer
- Previous breast cancer or ductal carcinoma in situ (risk of a new breast cancer is approximately twice the normal population rate)
- Previous breast biopsy showing:
  - Atypical ductal hyperplasia (ADH) or atypical lobular hyperplasia (ALH)
  - Lobular carcinoma in situ (LCIS)
  - Radial scars with atypia

### 4. Other Risk Factors

**Non-modifiable factors that increase risk:**
- Age over 50 years
- Early menarche (first period before age 12) or late menopause (after age 55)

**Modifiable factors that increase risk:**
- Alcohol: drinking 2–5 standard drinks per day increases risk by approximately 1.5 times
- Obesity, particularly abdominal fat and postmenopausal obesity
- Sedentary lifestyle
- Never having children (having a child before age 30 is somewhat protective)
- Not breastfeeding
- Long-term menopause hormone therapy (MHT)
- Current use of combined oral contraceptive (COC) — a small increased risk that appears to return to normal within approximately 5 years of stopping

**Breast cancer is NOT caused by:** antiperspirants, bra underwires, soy/tofu intake, or induced abortion.

## Management

Your doctor will manage you based on your risk category:

### If you have potentially high familial risk:

Your doctor may:
- Reassure you that although the risk is elevated, many women in this group do not develop breast cancer
- Refer you to both a **breast clinic** and **genetic health service** for risk assessment, possible genetic testing, and a personalised management plan
- Advise that specialists may recommend surveillance including regular clinical breast examination and annual breast imaging with mammography (with or without MRI)

### If you have moderately increased familial risk:

Your doctor may:
- Reassure you that most women in this group do not develop breast cancer
- Arrange annual clinical breast examinations in general practice
- Arrange annual mammograms from age 40, then every 2 years after age 50
- Alternatively, start mammograms 5 years younger than the age your first-degree relative was diagnosed
- Refer you to breast clinic if a first-degree relative was diagnosed before age 40 (starting mammograms before 40 is discussed individually)

### If you have average familial risk:

Your doctor may:
- Reassure you that your risk is the same as or only slightly higher than the general population
- Advise you to attend BreastScreen Aotearoa for regular 2-yearly screening mammograms

### If you have personal risk factors:

Your doctor may:
- Seek breast clinic advice for an ongoing surveillance plan if one has not already been given
- Re-enrol you with BreastScreen Aotearoa if you have been discharged from breast team care

### Genetics referral:

Your doctor will assess whether you meet criteria for genetics referral using the eviQ guidelines. If you do, a referral will be sent to the genetic health service — you may be offered advice or an appointment.

### For everyone:

Your doctor will advise you to:
- Stay aware of the normal look and feel of your breasts and report any persistent or unusual changes promptly
- Maintain a healthy weight, exercise regularly, limit alcohol, and breastfeed if able
