# Depression in Children and Youth

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers depression in children and youth under 18 years (or under 19 years if still at school). A child is defined as prepubescent; a young person refers to adolescents.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Help

Contact your doctor urgently or seek emergency assessment if:

- Hopelessness and helplessness
- Suicidal ideation
- Major social isolation

If there is immediate risk of suicide, call emergency services or go to your nearest emergency department.

---

## Background

### About Depression in Children and Youth

- A pattern of persistent mood difficulties helps to differentiate depression from normal childhood or adolescent development.
- Youth with early depression (aged 12–16 years) are at increased risk of adverse psychological and social outcomes.
- The incidence of depression increases from puberty onwards. Before puberty, it is rare for children to present with mood difficulties alone.

### How Depression Presents in Young People

Young people often present differently to adults, and commonly within a context of difficulty or adversity. Adults may fail to appreciate how much a young person can be overwhelmed by their circumstances. Consider depression if the young person is:

- Isolating themselves from family
- Frequently presenting with minor physical, psychiatric, or behavioural complaints
- Risk-taking, truant from school, or misusing substances
- Showing increasing deliberate self-harm

### How Depression Presents in Children

Prepubescent children sometimes present with depression in the context of significant psychosocial stressors. They may present with sleep disturbances, or predominantly physical or vague complaints. Other undiagnosed factors may also be impacting on mood.

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. **Take the young person's complaints seriously** and, where possible, speak with them alone to allow confidential discussion.

2. **Consider the young person's developmental stage** and their ability to understand and participate in care decisions.

3. **Use depression screening questions.** Your doctor may ask whether, over the last two weeks, the young person has been bothered by either of:
   - Lack of interest or enjoyment in activities at school, home, or with friends
   - Feeling low in mood, hopeless, or worthless

   If yes to either, your doctor may use the **PHQ-A** (a validated questionnaire for adolescents) to assess further.

4. **Ask about specific features of depression**, including:
   - Persistent sad, depressed, or irritable mood lasting at least 2 weeks
   - Loss or markedly reduced interest in normal activities
   - Substantial changes in eating, sleeping, or activity levels
   - Significant negative self-referential thoughts and feelings
   - Difficulty concentrating
   - Considerable functional impairment
   - Recurrent thoughts of death or suicide

5. **Assess symptoms and impairment**, including:
   - Severity and duration of symptoms
   - Marked changes in usual mood or behaviour (e.g., apathy, withdrawal)
   - Symptoms suggesting a psychotic prodrome
   - Functional impairment: school performance, recreational activities, engagement at home, and risk-taking behaviours
   - Sleep, exercise, diet, screen time, and substance use

6. **Explore triggers and context**, such as:
   - Loss or stress (relationship or family breakup, bereavement, chronic illness)
   - Family situation and home environment
   - Conflict at home, school, or with friends (including bullying, sexual orientation issues, or cultural conflict)
   - Safety concerns (e.g., abuse or neglect)
   - Co-morbid mental illness or substance use

7. **Consider risk factors and background**, including:
   - Socio-economic deprivation
   - Social or ethnic diversity; gender identity issues
   - History of physical or sexual abuse, or other traumatic experience
   - Family history of mental health disorders and addiction

8. **Assess suicidality** at every visit, including previous suicide attempts and any active plans.

9. **Consider and exclude organic causes**, such as:
   - Thyroid disease
   - Diabetes
   - Anaemia
   - Altered calcium state
   - Renal disease
   - Autoimmune disease

10. **Consider whether alcohol or drug use** may be contributing to mood.

11. **Determine severity of depression:**
    - *Mild:* No or little functional impairment
    - *Moderate:* Some functional impairment (e.g., some impact on school, social life, or family)
    - *Severe:* Significant functional impairment across multiple areas

12. **Establish the young person's support network.** Speaking with parents or caregivers is best practice, and disclosure of imminent risk is necessary for safe management.

## Management

> There is no role in primary care for initiating antidepressant medication for children. In youth with severe or persistent depression, your doctor will discuss medication with a child and adolescent psychiatrist before starting any treatment.

### When Urgent Specialist Assessment is Needed

Your doctor will arrange urgent mental health assessment if:

- High risk of suicide
- Profound hopelessness
- Unsupportive or high-risk environment
- Severe self-neglect or significant functional impairment
- Manic or psychotic features

### General Principles for All Ages

Your doctor will:

- Build rapport and a trusting therapeutic relationship with the young person
- Reassure and explain the importance of monitoring, regular reviews, and self-management
- Treat any co-morbidities (e.g., substance abuse, eating disorders)
- Address underlying issues contributing to low mood, such as bullying, grief, or school difficulties

Lifestyle advice may include guidance on:

- Sleep hygiene (appropriate sleep for age)
- Physical activity
- Reduced screen time
- Spending quality time with family

---

### Management by Age Group and Severity

#### Children (Prepubescent)

Your doctor may consider:

- Phone support services such as **Kidsline** (0800-543-754, 24/7) or **0800 What's Up** (0800-942-8787, 1pm–10pm weekdays, 3pm–10pm weekends)
- Self-help web-based tools:
  - **Anxiety Canada — My Anxiety Plan (MAP):** CBT-based anxiety and mood management (anxietycanada.com)
  - **Smiling Mind:** Mindfulness meditation programmes (smilingmind.com.au)
  - **MoodTools:** Education, mindfulness, activity support, and basic CBT (moodtools.org)
  - **The Lowdown:** Information for NZ youth on anxiety and depression (thelowdown.co.nz)
- Involving support services already supporting the family
- Follow-up in 1–2 weeks, or earlier if there are particular risks; monthly if improving
- If low mood persists: play therapy, family therapy, or counselling
- **There is no role for antidepressant medications in children in primary care**
- If depression persists for more than 1 month, referral for specialist mental health assessment

#### Youth — Mild to Moderate Depression

Your doctor may consider:

- Phone support: **Youthline** (free text 234 or free phone 0800-376-633)
- Self-help treatments, including:
  - **SPARX e-therapy** — a CBT-based online programme developed for young people
  - **Anxiety Canada — My Anxiety Plan (MAP):** CBT-based programme (anxietycanada.com)
  - **Smiling Mind:** Mindfulness meditation (smilingmind.com.au)
  - **MoodTools:** Education, mindfulness, and CBT support (moodtools.org)
  - **The Lowdown:** NZ-based web support for adolescent depression (thelowdown.co.nz)
- Involving parents or caregivers (with consent, or without consent if there are safety concerns)
- School counsellors, youth community support, or family therapy where family issues play a significant role
- **Antidepressant medications** are reserved for severe cases, after consultation with a child and adolescent psychiatrist. Persistent moderate cases may also benefit.
- Follow-up in 2–4 weeks, or earlier if there are particular risks; 1–2 monthly if improving
- Suicide risk review at every assessment; a formal safety plan if indicated
- If not improving after 6–8 weeks, treat as severe depression

#### Youth — Severe Depression

Your doctor will:

- Determine urgency:
  - If immediate help is needed: arrange urgent mental health assessment; if suicide risk, advise caregivers about home safety
  - If not immediately urgent: arrange non-acute specialist mental health assessment
- Seek advice about initiating an **SSRI antidepressant** while awaiting assessment:
  - SSRIs are considered in young people if psychological therapy has been insufficient, or the condition is severe or life-threatening
  - **Fluoxetine** is the SSRI with the greatest evidence of benefit in young people. It has a long half-life, the lowest association with suicidality, and is relatively safe in overdose. The usual starting dose is 10 mg once daily, increasing to 20 mg once daily if necessary and tolerated. If anxiety is prominent, 5 mg once daily may be used initially.
  - **Sertraline** also has evidence of benefit for anxiety and depression in young people.
  - Paroxetine, venlafaxine, and tricyclic antidepressants are **not recommended** in this age group in primary care.
  - SSRI use in young people is off-label. Closely monitor for emergence of suicidal ideation. Use with particular caution if there are manic symptoms or a strong family history of bipolar illness.
- Involve family or whānau (consent is not required if there are safety concerns)
- Recommend psychological interventions (counselling or therapy)
- Arrange weekly follow-up until the young person is under specialist mental health care
- Re-evaluate suicide risk at each contact; create a safety plan if indicated
- Contact within one week of discharge if admitted to hospital
