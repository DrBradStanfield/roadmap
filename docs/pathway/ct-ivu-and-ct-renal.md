# CT IVU and CT Renal

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers when CT IVU (CT Urogram) and Multiphase Renal CT are used, the associated risks, and when ultrasound may be a better first choice.

---

## Background

CT IVU (CT Intravenous Urogram, also called CT Urogram or CTU) and Multiphase Renal CT are the best imaging options for investigating:

- Renal (kidney) masses
- Painless blood in the urine (haematuria)
- Causes of urinary tract obstruction

However, these scans involve multiple CT images of the entire abdomen taken at different phases, which means they are associated with **significantly higher radiation exposure** than a standard CT scan. CT IVU also uses a high dose of iodine contrast media, which can be toxic to the kidneys.

Because of this, a **renal ultrasound** is recommended as the first investigation in patient groups at higher risk from radiation or IV contrast.

---

## Radiation and Contrast Risks

Your doctor will weigh these risks before requesting a CT scan:

### Radiation Exposure

- CT uses ionising radiation, which carries a small risk of cancer later in life, and a risk of harm to an unborn child
- CT scanning is avoided in pregnancy unless the scan is critical
- For patients under 40, alternative imaging (such as ultrasound) is considered where appropriate, due to higher cumulative radiation exposure over a lifetime — particularly if follow-up scans may be needed

### IV Contrast

- IV contrast is used in CT IVU and Multiphase Renal CT to visualise soft tissue and blood vessels
- Iodinated contrast media is generally safe but can cause:
  - Mild, moderate, or severe (anaphylactic) allergic reaction
  - Contrast-induced kidney impairment in susceptible patients
  - Worsening or triggering of thyrotoxicosis (overactive thyroid)
  - Special precautions in patients taking metformin

---

## Contraindications to IV Contrast

Your doctor will check for the following before requesting a contrast CT:

- History of allergy to IV contrast
- eGFR less than 30 mL/min (reduced kidney function)
- Currently taking metformin
- Hyperthyroidism (overactive thyroid)

If any of these apply, your doctor may consider alternatives such as a renal ultrasound.

---

## When CT IVU is Used

Your doctor may consider CT IVU in the following situations:

- **Macroscopic haematuria** (visible blood in urine) — if a urinary tract infection has been excluded or haematuria persists after treating the UTI, and:
  - You are aged 40–85 years, or
  - You are younger than 40 and initial investigations (including renal ultrasound) were normal but haematuria persists
- **Microscopic haematuria** (blood in urine only visible on testing) — if this remains unexplained after initial investigations including a renal tract ultrasound, and a specialist recommends further investigation
- **Multiphase Renal CT** — if an ultrasound has found an incidental finding that needs further investigation, such as:
  - A solid lesion greater than 1 cm in diameter
  - A complex renal cyst (Bosniak category IIF, III, or IV)

---

## When Ultrasound is Recommended Instead

A **renal ultrasound** is the preferred investigation (rather than CT IVU) for:

- Macroscopic haematuria if you are:
  - Younger than 40 years
  - Aged 86 years or older
  - Aged 40–85 years **and** have a contraindication to IV contrast (see above)
- Microscopic haematuria as a first-line investigation
