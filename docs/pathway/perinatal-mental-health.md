# Perinatal Mental Health

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers mental health assessment and management for all parents (mothers, fathers, caregivers) during the pre-pregnancy, antenatal, and postpartum periods (up to 12 months after birth).

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Contact emergency services or go to the emergency department if:

- Suicidal thoughts or plans
- Risk of harm to the fetus or baby
- Acute psychosis (e.g., delusions, hallucinations, severely disorganised behaviour)

---

## Background

The perinatal period — from pregnancy through to 12 months postpartum — involves major physiological, social, and psychological changes. Key points:

- Women may have existing mental health conditions, or develop new symptoms during this period
- The risk of onset or recurrence of mental illness is higher around childbirth
- Women with a personal or family history of mental illness are more vulnerable to a new onset, worsening, or recurrence of problems
- Postnatal symptoms range from "baby blues" (common and short-lived) through to postnatal depression, bipolar disorder, and postpartum psychosis
- Untreated perinatal mental illness can affect the mother-baby relationship and the baby's development
- Early intervention improves outcomes for mothers, infants, and families
- Most women with mild to moderate mental distress can be managed in the community by their GP and support services
- Fathers and partners are also at risk of perinatal mental health difficulties and can be assessed in the same way

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you at each point of contact throughout the perinatal period. This includes:

### 1. History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Personal history of mental illness
- Current symptoms: their nature, when they started, what triggered them, and how they affect relationships, sleep, appetite, and parenting
- Medical and obstetric history
- Risk factors and protective factors

**Risk factors include:**
- Personal or family history of mental illness (especially in the perinatal period)
- History of abuse or trauma
- Recent loss (including miscarriage, stillbirth, termination, or loss of a baby)
- Unplanned pregnancy that has been difficult to accept
- History of fertility problems, pregnancy complications, traumatic birth, or premature birth
- Breastfeeding difficulties
- Relationship difficulties
- Poor social support or isolation
- Financial, employment, or housing difficulties
- Addiction issues
- High expectations of self, the birth experience, or parenting

**Protective factors include:**
- A supportive partner and family
- Good engagement with a GP or other healthcare provider

### 2. Screening

Your doctor may screen for:

**Depression** — using questions such as:
- "During the past month, have you been bothered by feeling down, depressed, or hopeless?"
- "During the past month, have you been bothered by little interest or pleasure in doing things?"

**Anxiety** — using questions such as:
- "During the past month, have you been worrying a lot about everyday problems?"

**Bipolar disorder / mania** — your doctor may use the ACE Model (Activity, Cognition, Emotionality) to look for signs of mania or hypomania. Signs can include feeling unusually energised or "wired," needing less sleep, overspending, making grandiose plans, being disinhibited, or having disorganised thinking.

**Eating disorders and substance use** — your doctor may ask about alcohol and drug use using brief screening questions.

### 3. Validated Screening Tools

Your doctor may use one or more of the following tools — these are indicators, not diagnoses, and do not replace clinical judgement:

**Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS):**
- A 10-item questionnaire used during pregnancy and postnatally
- Score 10–12: your doctor will repeat the screen in 2 weeks and monitor progress
- Score 13 or above: likelihood of depression is high — further assessment and management required

**PHQ-9 (Patient Health Questionnaire):**

| PHQ-9 Score | Provisional diagnosis |
|---|---|
| 10 to 14 | Mild depression |
| 15 to 19 | Moderate depression |
| 20 or above | Severe depression |

**GAD-7 (Generalised Anxiety Disorder scale):**

| GAD-7 Score | Provisional diagnosis |
|---|---|
| 5 to 9 | Mild anxiety |
| 10 to 14 | Moderate anxiety |
| 15 to 21 | Severe anxiety |

### 4. Medication Review

Your doctor will review all medications you are taking (including over-the-counter and herbal remedies) and carefully weigh the potential effects on the baby against the risk of relapse if treatment is stopped. You will be involved in all decisions.

### 5. Physical Examination and Blood Tests

Your doctor may check your general physical health and arrange blood tests including:
- Full blood count (FBC)
- Thyroid function (TSH)
- Vitamin B12 and folate
- Iron studies

Thyroid dysfunction and nutritional deficiencies can contribute to mood symptoms.

### 6. Postpartum — Additional Assessment

At postpartum appointments your doctor will also:
- Ask how things are going at home and with your baby
- Consider your level of sleep deprivation
- Observe the mother-infant relationship — including how you talk about and interact with your baby
- Assess risk of harm to yourself, others, and the baby
- Consider the wellbeing of any other children in the household

## Management

Management is collaborative and may involve your GP, lead maternity carer (LMC), antenatal clinic, and mental health services working together.

### Mild to Moderate Anxiety or Depression

- Medication is not the preferred first treatment option
- Your doctor may consider supportive counselling, brief psychological therapy, or peer support groups
- Talking therapies with good evidence in this setting include:
  - **Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)** — helps change unhelpful thought and behaviour patterns
  - **Interpersonal psychotherapy (IPT)** — focuses on improving communication skills and relationships

### Moderate Anxiety or Depression

- Talking therapies (CBT, IPT) are recommended as first-line
- If medication is indicated, your doctor will discuss the options appropriate for pregnancy or breastfeeding

### Severe Depression or Complex Mental Health Difficulties

- Your doctor will seek specialist mental health advice or arrange urgent mental health assessment

### Alcohol and Substance Misuse

- Your doctor may consider:
  - High-dose folic acid (5 mg daily) if there is sustained high alcohol intake
  - B-vitamin supplementation (particularly thiamine) if vitamin deficiency is suspected
  - Referral to specialist addiction services, including services that support pregnant women and parents of young children

### Ongoing Care

Your doctor will:
- Regularly review risk to you, others, and your baby
- Regularly review and update your management plan
- Ensure a crisis plan is in place
- Provide education and active support
- Involve your family/whānau where possible
- Liaise with your midwife

---

## When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will arrange specialist perinatal mental health assessment if:
- You have a moderate to severe mental health disorder and are pregnant, or are the primary caregiver of an infant under 12 months
- You have a known moderate to severe mental health disorder (e.g., bipolar disorder, schizophrenia) and are planning a pregnancy
