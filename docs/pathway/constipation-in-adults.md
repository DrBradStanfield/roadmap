# Constipation in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of constipation in adults — how doctors think about the causes, what to look for, and how it is treated.

> **Note:** This information is for educational purposes. Always discuss your symptoms and any treatment changes with your doctor or healthcare provider.

---

## Red Flags — See Your Doctor Promptly

See your doctor promptly if constipation is accompanied by:

- Unexplained weight loss
- Abdominal or rectal mass
- Iron deficiency anaemia
- Unexplained rectal bleeding

These symptoms require further investigation to rule out serious causes.

---

## What Is Constipation?

Constipation may be described as one or more of:

- Infrequent stools — generally less than once every 3 days, although this varies between people
- Hard or dry stools
- Difficulty passing stools, including straining, needing to alter posture, or needing to assist manually
- Sensation of incomplete evacuation, or anorectal blockage

Most cases of constipation are related to lifestyle factors.

Although constipation can be associated with bowel cancer, a change in bowel habit *to diarrhoea* is more strongly correlated with bowel cancer.

**Complications of constipation include:**
- Short-term: faecal impaction, anal fissure, haemorrhoids, urinary retention, or incontinence
- Long-term: faecal incontinence (rectal stretching causes loss of awareness of the need to pass a motion), rectal prolapse, diverticular disease, hernia, spurious diarrhoea

## How Your Doctor Assesses Constipation

### History Your Doctor Will Take

**About your bowel motions:**
- Frequency and consistency — the Bristol stool chart is a useful tool
- Whether you have alternating diarrhoea (often a sign of irritable bowel syndrome or overflow)
- Time spent on the toilet
- Whether this is a recent change or long-standing
- Whether onset was associated with a change in lifestyle, diet, or medication
- Any soiling of underwear

**Other symptoms:**
- Rectal bleeding and type (e.g., on toilet paper, coating stool, or mixed in with stool)
- Abdominal or rectal pain
- Sensation of incomplete evacuation
- Urinary retention or incontinence
- Unexpected weight loss
- Symptoms of iron deficiency (lethargy, breathlessness, palpitations)
- Symptoms of hypothyroidism (weight gain, lethargy, cold intolerance)

> In older adults, constipation may present as confusion, overflow diarrhoea, abdominal pain, urinary retention, nausea, and loss of appetite.

**Lifestyle and medical history:**
- Current medications (many drugs cause constipation — see below)
- Thyroid or calcium problems
- Diet and fluid intake
- Exercise routine
- Caffeine intake
- Family history of bowel, ovarian, or breast cancer, IBS, or coeliac disease
- Recent hospitalisation, bed rest, or immobility

### Medications That Can Cause Constipation

The following medications commonly contribute to constipation (not an exhaustive list):

- Antacids containing aluminium or calcium
- Antimuscarinic drugs (e.g. hyoscine butylbromide, oxybutynin, procyclidine)
- Antipsychotics (e.g. clozapine)
- Antispasmodics (e.g. mebeverine)
- Antidepressants (e.g. tricyclics)
- Anti-epileptics (e.g. carbamazepine, gabapentin, pregabalin, phenytoin)
- Calcium channel blockers, particularly verapamil
- Diuretics
- Dopaminergic drugs used in Parkinson's disease
- 5-HT antagonists (e.g. ondansetron)
- Iron or calcium supplements
- Opioids (e.g. codeine, morphine)
- Sedating antihistamines (e.g. promethazine)

### Causes Your Doctor Will Consider

**Primary causes:**
- Functional constipation (e.g. slow transit — very infrequent stools)
- Constipation-predominant irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)
- Dyssynergic defecation (also called anismus) — failure of the pelvic floor muscles to relax during defecation

**Secondary causes:**

Gastrointestinal:
- Anal fissures, haemorrhoids
- Coeliac disease, diverticular disease
- Intestinal obstruction
- Colorectal cancer

Metabolic:
- Hypothyroidism, hypercalcaemia, diabetes, hypopituitarism, hypokalaemia

Neurological:
- Parkinson's disease, stroke, spinal lesion, multiple sclerosis

Other:
- Connective tissue disorders, depression
- Low fibre / low fluid diet
- Eating disorders (anorexia nervosa, bulimia)
- Pregnancy, rectocele, immobility, change of environment
- Medications (see above)

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will typically:

- Measure height, weight, and BMI
- Palpate the abdomen for masses and tenderness; check bowel sounds
- Examine the perineum and rectum, including a digital rectal examination — straining in the left lateral position can reveal dyssynergic defecation
- Consider a bimanual pelvic examination if clinically indicated

### Investigations

Blood tests may be arranged if clinically indicated, to exclude secondary causes:
- Full blood count (FBC)
- Ferritin
- TSH (thyroid)
- Calcium
- Coeliac serology
- Electrolytes and creatinine
- HbA1c

Abdominal X-rays are not routinely useful for chronic constipation and should not be arranged routinely.

## Management

### If Red Flags Are Present

Your doctor will follow the Colorectal Symptoms pathway for further investigation. Note: constipation as an isolated symptom does not by itself meet referral criteria for direct access colonoscopy or CT colonography.

### Treating Faecal Impaction

If impaction is suspected (history of severe constipation, bloating, feeling of needing to evacuate, sometimes with overflow diarrhoea):

- High-dose oral macrogol: 4 sachets on day 1, increasing by 2 sachets each day to a maximum of 8 sachets per day (reduced to 2–3 sachets/day maximum in frail elderly patients)
- If inadequate response, suppositories or enemas may be added

### Managing Contributing Causes

Your doctor will address underlying conditions that contribute to constipation, including: anal fissure, haemorrhoids, hypothyroidism, hypercalcaemia, diabetes, IBS, diverticular disease, coeliac disease, Parkinson's disease, and eating disorders.

Medications causing constipation should be reviewed and replaced with non-constipating alternatives where possible.

### Lifestyle Measures (First-Line for Everyone)

The following measures are recommended for all patients and should be tried first:

- **Dietary fibre and fluid:** Both are required together — increasing fibre without adequate fluid can worsen constipation
- **Exercise regularly**
- **Limit alcohol and fatty foods**, which can worsen constipation
- **Respond promptly to the urge to defecate** — don't ignore it
- **Regular toileting routine:** Morning or shortly after meals is ideal, as the bowel is most active then
- **Correct toileting position:**
  - Knees higher than hips, feet flat on floor, lean forwards with elbows on knees, bulge abdomen out, straighten spine
  - A small footstool to raise the feet can help
- **Avoid straining or lingering** (e.g. reading) on the toilet
- Keep a bowel diary and review with your doctor in 2–4 weeks if not improving

## Laxative Treatment

Your doctor will choose laxatives based on the type of constipation, severity, and your circumstances. Choice of laxative matters — different types work in different ways.

### General Principles

- Aim for 1–2 soft-formed stools per day
- If one laxative is insufficient, combining two from *different* classes works better than switching (e.g. stool softener + stimulant, or stool softener + bulking agent)
- Avoid lactulose in IBS patients — it often worsens symptoms
- Take care with osmotic enemas in older adults due to risk of electrolyte disturbance
- Long-term laxatives are appropriate when the underlying cause (e.g. opioid use, neurological disease) cannot be resolved

### Weaning Off Laxatives

- Reduce gradually once regular, comfortable bowel movements are established (typically 2–4 weeks of regular soft-formed stools)
- If using multiple laxatives, stop one at a time — reduce stimulant laxatives first where possible
- Weaning can take several months
- Relapses are common — treat early with increased doses

### Types of Laxatives

**Bulk-forming laxatives** (e.g. psyllium — Konsyl-D, Mucilax, Metamucil):
- Increase faecal bulk to stimulate peristalsis
- Full effect may take several days
- Useful for small, hard stools when fibre intake cannot be increased enough
- Adequate fluid intake is essential
- Side-effects include flatulence and bloating
- Avoid in patients taking opioids or clozapine, or with intestinal obstruction

**Osmotic laxatives** (avoid in intestinal obstruction):
- Draw water into the bowel or retain fluid; effectiveness depends on adequate hydration
- Onset of action: 24–48 hours
- Options:
  - Lactulose (avoid in IBS)
  - Macrogols (e.g. Molaxole, Lax-Sachets, Movicol) — safe for long-term use
  - Magnesium sulfate (Epsom salts) — avoid long-term use, especially with renal impairment

**Stimulant laxatives** (avoid in intestinal obstruction):
- Increase intestinal motility; may cause abdominal cramps
- Safe for long-term use in spinal injury, chronic neuromuscular disease, opioid-induced constipation, and severe constipation — no convincing evidence of long-term colon harm
- Onset of action: 6–12 hours
- Options:
  - Bisacodyl (e.g. Lax-Tabs, Dulcolax, Fleet)
  - Sennoside (e.g. Laxsol, Coloxyl with Senna, Senokot)

**Stool-softening agents** (avoid in intestinal obstruction):
- Docusate sodium (e.g. Coloxyl) — acts as both a softener and mild stimulant
- Combination products with additional stimulants may cause abdominal cramps

**Suppositories and enemas** (for impaction or left-sided constipation only):
- Glycerol suppositories — lubricant only
- Sodium citrate enemas (e.g. Micolette, Microlax)
- Sodium phosphate enemas (e.g. Fleet) — contraindicated in chronic renal failure or heart failure
- Bisacodyl suppositories (e.g. Lax) — stimulant

### Constipation in Pregnancy

If dietary and lifestyle changes are insufficient, laxatives can be used safely in pregnancy. Your doctor may recommend:
- A bulk-forming laxative (first choice)
- An osmotic laxative such as lactulose
- A stimulant laxative such as bisacodyl or senna if needed

## Dyssynergic Defecation

If your constipation is caused by failure of the pelvic floor muscles to relax properly during defecation (dyssynergic defecation or anismus), your doctor may refer you for pelvic floor rehabilitation with a physiotherapist.
