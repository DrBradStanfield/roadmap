# Unexpected Deterioration in an Older Adult

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to a sudden or accelerating decline in function in an older person — assessing urgency, identifying the cause, and deciding how to manage it.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance if:

- Life-threatening conditions are suspected
- Severe pain

---

## Background

- Normal ageing involves a gradual, steady decline in function and reduced ability to bounce back from illness.
- Managing chronic disease well can help preserve function, compressing the burden of ageing into fewer years near end of life.
- A sudden decline in function in an older person is almost always due to a change in health — it is **never** just normal ageing. The change should be investigated and treated urgently.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a careful history. Key areas include:

**New functional problems**

If presenting with falls, delirium, drug interactions, or incontinence, your doctor will look for a new or worsening medical problem such as infection.

**Speed of decline**

Your doctor will assess whether deterioration occurred over hours or days.

**Markers of deterioration**

Your doctor will look for signs that function is declining, including:

- Sudden or accelerating decline in physical, cognitive, or social function
- Recurrent or unplanned hospital presentations or ambulance calls
- Concern expressed by a service provider or carer
- Carer strain or loss of a carer

**Collaborative history**

If cognitive impairment is actual or possible, your doctor will seek information from other sources — family, carers, neighbours, old notes or letters, or service providers. Some people with cognitive impairment can give apparently appropriate responses that are not fully reliable.

**Common triggers to consider**

Your doctor will look for potential causes of the deterioration, including:

- COPD exacerbation
- Pneumonia
- Heart failure
- Urinary tract infection
- Other infections
- Sepsis
- Drug withdrawal
- Delirium
- Dehydration
- Recent falls
- Changes in bladder or bowel function
- Alcohol use
- Decline in food intake or unintentional weight loss
- Medications — recent changes (prescription or over-the-counter), side-effects, or drug interactions. Adverse reactions are more common with increasing numbers of medications. Your doctor will pay particular attention to anticholinergic, sedative, hypotensive, or delirium-causing effects.

**Comparison to recent baseline**

Your doctor will compare the current situation to the most recent assessment and consider:

- What the patient was like before
- What the patient is like now
- The degree of change in function
- Whether the change is potentially reversible
- Whether an advance care plan applies

**Investigations**

Investigations will be arranged only if they are likely to help make a diagnosis or guide management.

---

## Management

### If life-threatening or severe pain

Your doctor will arrange an ambulance and emergency department assessment, unless an advance care plan specifies otherwise.

### Treating the cause and the functional decline

Your doctor will consider whether this is a new acute problem or an acute-on-chronic problem, and:

- If the condition is reversible, treat it.
- If the condition is not reversible, look for ways to improve or maintain function.
- If neither the condition nor function is reversible, a palliative approach may be appropriate.

### Where to manage

Your doctor will decide whether management at home or hospital is most appropriate. Key factors include:

| Factor | Home-based management | Hospital |
|---|---|---|
| Carer availability | Competent, willing carer available | No carer available |
| After-hours GP support | Available if needed | Likely to be needed but not available |
| Speed and severity of decline | Rate of decline not steep | Rate of decline a concern |
| Treatment options | Oral or IV therapy available in the community | More intensive treatments needed |
| Community support services | Acute community management services available | Support insufficient or inappropriate |

### For acute hospital assessment

Your doctor may consider requesting acute general medicine assessment if:

- The patient is acutely unwell
- Treatment or support needed is not available in the community
- There is concern about the rate of decline

### New acute problem

Your doctor may consider managing the acute cause, which could include:

- COPD exacerbation
- Cognitive impairment
- Community-acquired pneumonia
- Heart failure
- Pulmonary embolus
- Urinary tract infection
- Sepsis
- Dehydration
- Falls

If there are multiple problems or the diagnosis is unclear, your doctor may seek specialist input from an older person's health service. If a single system is involved, the relevant specialty may be consulted (e.g., cardiology, respiratory, or general medicine).

Your doctor will discuss the risks and benefits of treatment options and patient preferences. If cognitive impairment is an issue, carers will be included in the discussion. Your doctor will also check whether an advance care plan applies.

Clear advice will be given about prognosis and what to do if things worsen, including contact numbers and documentation in case an acute admission becomes necessary.

**Follow-up:** Your doctor will ensure you and your carer understand how, when, and where follow-up will take place.

### Acute-on-chronic problem

If this is an acute flare of an ongoing condition, your doctor will treat the acute problem and review the existing management plan:

- If the plan has been followed, consider escalating to specialist advice or referral.
- If the plan has not been followed, review whether more education and support is needed.
- If there is no current plan, consider creating an acute action plan.
