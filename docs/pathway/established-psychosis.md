# Established Psychosis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway is for patients with a confirmed diagnosis of psychosis or a psychotic disorder.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Help

Contact your doctor or emergency services immediately if:

- There is imminent risk of harm to self or others
- There is unexplained generalised decline in function or social withdrawal in a young person (may signal early relapse)
- A physical (organic) cause is suspected, such as a brain tumour or delirium

---

## Background

### What is psychosis?

Psychosis refers to symptoms of severe mental disorders characterised by:

- **Delusions** — abnormal thinking; false, fixed beliefs not shared by others of a similar cultural background
- **Hallucinations** — abnormal perceptions without external stimulus, most commonly auditory
- **Thought disorder** — disorganised thinking with disordered speech patterns

Other features can include:

- **Catatonia** — abnormality of movement and behaviour (repetitive activity, resistance to movement, or rigidity)
- **Negative symptoms** — poverty of speech (alogia), flat affect, loss of motivation, inability to experience pleasure (anhedonia), social withdrawal
- **Cognitive deficits** — problems with attention, memory, and executive function
- **Secondary features** — sleep disturbance, depression, anxiety

Around 3 in 100 people experience psychosis. Two thirds of psychotic episodes occur before age 25, and in more than half of cases onset is gradual over 6 or more months.

Early detection and treatment can alter the course of the illness and improve long-term outcomes.

### Types of psychotic disorder

Your doctor may consider these possible diagnoses:

- **Schizophrenia** — combination of delusions, hallucinations, thought disorder, and negative symptoms, usually starting in young adulthood, lasting at least 1 month, without a clear mood cause
- **Affective psychosis** — psychotic symptoms in the context of a clear mood disorder (mania or depression), where mood symptoms precede and persist beyond the psychotic symptoms
- **Substance-induced psychosis** — closely linked in time to substance use; often features hallucinations (may be visual) with fewer negative symptoms
- **Delusional disorder** — non-bizarre delusions (possible but false beliefs) without other psychotic symptoms, usually starting in mid-to-late adulthood
- **Organic (medical) causes** — psychosis as a direct result of an underlying medical condition, including brain tumour, stroke, dementia, epilepsy, thyroid disorders, HIV, delirium, substance intoxication or withdrawal

## Assessment

Your doctor will review you regularly during stable periods. Assessment typically includes:

### Psychotic symptoms

Your doctor will observe and ask about:

- **Positive symptoms** (presence of abnormal processes):
  - Delusions — false fixed beliefs
  - Hallucinations — sensory experiences without external stimulus
  - Disorganised thoughts and behaviours
- **Negative symptoms** (absence of normal function):
  - Avolition or apathy — inability to pursue goals
  - Alogia — diminished speech
  - Anhedonia — loss of enjoyment
  - Asociality — withdrawal from social activity
  - Flattened affect — reduced emotional expression

### Early warning signs of relapse

Your doctor will also watch for early warning signs, which can include:

- Changes in mood — anxiety, depression, irritability, anger, apathy
- Changes in thinking — poor concentration or memory, unusual preoccupations, abstract or dissociated thinking
- Behavioural changes — odd, aggressive, or disruptive behaviour
- Attenuated psychotic symptoms — suspiciousness, paranoid ideas, perceptual abnormalities
- Brief auditory experiences — for example, hearing one's name called
- Physical changes — sleep disturbance, fatigue, somatic complaints
- Social withdrawal or decline in role functioning (school, work)
- Diminished self-care

### Treatment review

Your doctor will ask about:

- Current antipsychotic medication — whether you are taking it as prescribed, and any side effects (including extrapyramidal effects such as stiffness or tremor, and metabolic effects such as weight gain)
- Alcohol, recreational drugs, over-the-counter medications, and supplements — these can interact with antipsychotics and affect symptoms

### Physical health

People with psychosis are at higher risk of physical health problems. Your doctor will monitor:

- Weight and BMI (monthly)
- Blood pressure and heart rate
- Lipids, HbA1c, and blood glucose
- Movement disorders

### Annual blood tests

Your doctor will typically arrange annual investigations, which may include:

- Full blood count (FBC)
- Liver function tests (LFT)
- Thyroid function tests (TFT)
- Lipids
- HbA1c
- eGFR (kidney function)
- ECG (heart tracing) at initial assessment and then as needed

Cardiovascular risk screening is offered from age 25.

If you use substances, your doctor may arrange a urine drug screen to check for amphetamines, cannabis, opiates, and benzodiazepines.

## Management

Your doctor will review you every 3 months once medication and symptoms are stable.

### Medication monitoring

Your doctor may consider the following medications and monitoring schedules. All medication decisions are made by your doctor or specialist.

**Antipsychotics** (e.g., risperidone, quetiapine, olanzapine, chlorpromazine, paliperidone):

- Weight — weekly for 6–8 weeks, then at 3, 6, and 12 months
- Blood pressure, heart rate, and waist circumference — at 3 months, 6 months, then annually
- Fasting lipids, fasting glucose, HbA1c, liver function tests, and full blood count — at 3 months, 6 months, then annually
- ECG — at baseline, 6 months, then annually
- Prolactin — only if specific clinical indications are present (e.g., galactorrhoea, menstrual changes, or low testosterone in men)

**Clozapine:** Requires specialist oversight and regular mandatory blood monitoring.

**Mood stabilisers** your doctor may consider:

- *Carbamazepine* — drug levels and liver function every 6 months; full blood count, sodium, and potassium annually
- *Lithium* — lithium level, kidney function, and electrolytes every 3–6 months; thyroid function tests, calcium, and weight every 6 months; ECG, lipids, and HbA1c annually if over 40 or obese
- *Sodium valproate* — drug levels and liver function every 6 months; full blood count annually

### Lifestyle and physical health

Your doctor will recommend:

- Healthy eating and regular exercise
- Reducing or stopping alcohol and substance use
- Smoking cessation (note: smoking significantly affects clozapine levels — discuss with your doctor before making changes)

### Relapse prevention

Your doctor will help you develop a plan that includes:

- Identifying your personal early warning signs
- Strategies to maintain wellbeing (adequate sleep, avoiding known triggers, planning for stressful periods)
- Who to contact if your mental health deteriorates
- A crisis plan for acute deterioration

### Recovery goals

Your doctor can discuss referral for:

- Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)
- Occupational therapy, counselling, social work, or family therapy
- Dietitian assessment
- Community mental health support for peer support and social rehabilitation

### Carers and support people

If family or friends support you, your doctor may also check how they are coping and whether any respite support is needed.

## When Your Doctor May Refer or Seek Specialist Advice

Your doctor may request a specialist mental health assessment if:

- There are active psychotic symptoms with early warning signs
- Symptoms are not settling with treatment
- Your diagnosis needs formal review
- There are concerns about your medication, side effects, or tolerability
- You have never been assessed by mental health services before
- You are pregnant or considering pregnancy

Your doctor may seek mental health advice if:

- You have complex medication needs or physical health problems that complicate management
- Repeated relapses are being considered for respite admission
- There are concerns about clozapine treatment (e.g., side effects, poor compliance, or if you plan to stop smoking)
