# Nausea and Vomiting in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of nausea and vomiting in adults with advanced illness. The cause is often multifactorial, and management depends on the likely cause and the patient's goals of care. Always discuss treatment with your doctor.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history, which may include:

- Whether the problem is nausea, vomiting, or both
- Timing, daily patterns, and things that make it worse
- Frequency, volume, and nature of vomitus
- Whether constipation is present
- Associated symptoms that may point to the cause:
  - Nausea worsened by eating or relieved by vomiting — suggests a gastrointestinal cause
  - Early satiety — suggests delayed gastric emptying
  - Abdominal distension and reduced bowel motions — may suggest bowel obstruction
  - Dry retching worse with smell or sight of food — suggests a central (brain) cause
  - Headache or neurological symptoms — may suggest raised intracranial pressure
  - Fever, sweating, or urinary symptoms — may suggest infection
  - Anxiety
- Any new medications or dose changes
- Response to antiemetics already tried

Your doctor will also examine you (including abdominal examination, and rectal examination if constipated) and assess your hydration. They will review your stage of illness and goals of care when deciding on investigations and management.

---

## Common Causes

| Cause | Typical features |
|-------|-----------------|
| Chemical (drugs, metabolic, toxins) | Persistent nausea not relieved by vomiting |
| Gastrointestinal (gastric stasis, bowel obstruction, constipation, reflux) | Intermittent nausea relieved by vomiting; large-volume vomiting; altered bowel habit |
| Central nervous system (raised intracranial pressure) | Headache; early morning symptoms; vomiting without nausea; neurological signs |
| Vestibular/cerebellar (motion sickness, inner ear) | Nausea and vomiting worse with movement |
| Psychological (anxiety, pain, anticipatory) | Low-volume vomiting; anticipatory nausea before known triggers |

## Management

Management will vary depending on the stage of illness and the patient's goals of care.

### Treat underlying causes (where possible)

Your doctor will first try to address any treatable cause, such as:

- Constipation
- Bowel obstruction
- Raised intracranial pressure
- Urinary tract infection
- Hypercalcaemia
- Medications that may be causing nausea (ceased or changed)
- Reflux or peptic ulcer disease
- Anxiety, distress, or pain

### Non-pharmacological measures

Your doctor may suggest:

- Avoiding known triggers (e.g., certain smells or sights)
- Trying bland foods served at room temperature
- Avoiding sweet, salty, fatty, and spicy foods
- Trying cool, carbonated drinks
- Smaller, more frequent servings
- Acupuncture or acupressure (evidence supports effectiveness for chemotherapy-induced nausea)
- Aromatherapy with essential oils

### Antiemetic medications

Your doctor will choose an antiemetic based on the most likely cause. General principles:

- Start one antiemetic and increase to the maximum tolerated dose before adding a second.
- Use regular dosing and ensure breakthrough doses are available.
- Subcutaneous administration may be needed if vomiting prevents oral intake.

**Chemical causes** (e.g., opioids, metabolic disturbances):
- **Haloperidol** — Potent centrally acting antiemetic. Effective for opioid-induced nausea, hypercalcaemia, and renal failure. Contraindicated in Parkinson's disease. Starting dose typically 0.5–1.5 mg at night.
- **Levomepromazine (methotrimeprazine)** — Acts at multiple receptor sites; useful when other antiemetics have failed. Common side-effects include postural hypotension and drowsiness.

**Gastrointestinal causes** (gastric stasis, partial bowel obstruction, constipation):
- **Metoclopramide** — Upper GI prokinetic. Do not use in complete bowel obstruction or Parkinson disease. Watch for extrapyramidal side-effects (especially at higher doses or in elderly patients).
- **Domperidone** — Similar prokinetic effect without central side-effects; safe in Parkinson disease. No injectable form.
- **Levomepromazine** — See above.

**Central nervous system causes** (raised intracranial pressure):
- **Dexamethasone** — Reduces cerebral oedema. Dose and duration tailored by your doctor.

**Vestibular/cerebellar causes** (motion sickness, inner ear pathology):
- **Cyclizine** — Antihistaminic and antimuscarinic. Note: reduces the prokinetic effect of metoclopramide or domperidone.
- **Prochlorperazine** — Oral or buccal tablet formulations.

**Psychological/anticipatory causes**:
- **Lorazepam** — Benzodiazepine useful for anticipatory nausea related to fear or anxiety.

*Note: Some patients enquire about medicinal cannabis. Local specialist experience is that it can be effective for some patients but may increase nausea in others.*

### Monitoring and escalation

Your doctor will review effectiveness and watch for side-effects, particularly extrapyramidal effects with metoclopramide and haloperidol. Combination therapy (up to two antiemetics from different categories) may be considered if one medication is inadequate.

Subcutaneous or intravenous fluids may be considered depending on goals of care.

---

## When to Contact Your Doctor or Care Team

Contact your doctor or palliative care team if:

- Nausea or vomiting is severe or persistent
- You are unable to keep fluids down
- Initial treatment has not helped
- Nausea or vomiting is significantly affecting your quality of life

Your doctor will seek specialist palliative care advice if nausea and/or vomiting persists despite a combination of two antiemetic medications.
