# Dyspnoea in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers management of breathlessness (dyspnoea) in adults receiving palliative care for a life-limiting illness.

---

## Background

Breathlessness is a subjective awareness of difficulty breathing. It is a common and often distressing symptom in palliative care.

**Causes include:**
- Respiratory disease
- Heart failure
- Metabolic causes
- Neuromuscular weakness
- Anxiety
- Malignancy — including direct invasion of lung tissue, airway obstruction, cachexia, lymphangitis carcinomatosis, or superior vena cava obstruction

---

## Management Approach

Your doctor will explore your fears and anxieties about breathlessness, and explain and reassure where possible.

**Reversible causes** are treated alongside the breathlessness itself — these include heart failure, COPD, asthma, pleural or pericardial effusion, mechanical airway obstruction, anaemia, anxiety, superior vena cava obstruction, and pulmonary embolus.

If anxiety is prominent, your doctor may address this separately with medications or specialist input.

If cough is also present, see the [Cough in Palliative Care](cough-in-palliative-care.md) pathway.

If cancer is the underlying cause, your doctor may consider oncology treatments (chemotherapy, radiotherapy, blood transfusion, or draining an effusion) to help with symptoms.

---

## Non-Pharmacological Techniques

These techniques work best when taught while the patient is not actively breathless. Your doctor or a nurse may walk you through them.

### Understanding Breathing

At rest, breathing should be mostly diaphragmatic, through the nose, at 10–14 breaths per minute. When this pattern changes — due to anxiety, pain, or lung disease — breathing may become more effortful and tiring.

**Hyperventilation** (breathing faster than the body needs) can cause dizziness, chest tightness, pins and needles, and air hunger. This is often related to anxiety.

### Practical Techniques

- **Activity pacing** — plan activities in advance, arrange them around your best time of day, break tasks into smaller parts with rest in between

- **Pursed lips breathing** — breathe in gently through the nose; breathe out slowly through pursed lips. The out-breath should be about twice as long as the in-breath (e.g. 2 seconds in, 4 seconds out). Works at rest and during activity.

- **"Blow as you go"** (exhalation with effort) — breathe out during the effort part of activity (e.g. standing up). Avoid holding your breath during exertion.

- **Forward-leaning position** — lean forward (on forearms on a table, or bracing arms on a walking frame). This helps the diaphragm work more efficiently and lets neck muscles assist breathing.

- **Passive relaxation** — consciously relax muscles from toes upward, focusing on slow, regular breathing.

- **Progressive muscle relaxation** — tense and then release muscle groups progressively from toes upward, with focused breathing.

- **Meditation** — focusing attention on an object or image to clear the mind and reduce anxiety.

- **Visualisation** — imagining a calm place in detail, bringing breathing into the picture. Placing hands on the stomach helps awareness of deep breathing.

- **Walking aids** — using a walker can significantly reduce breathlessness by allowing a forward-leaning posture. Your doctor may refer you to an occupational therapist for equipment assessment.

## Pharmacological Management

In palliative care, some medications are used at doses or by routes outside their standard licensed indications — your doctor will explain the rationale.

### Morphine

Morphine reduces the sensation of breathlessness by reducing respiratory effort. It is the main medication for breathlessness in palliative care.

- Lower doses are needed than for pain — commonly starting at 2.5 mg subcutaneously or 2.5–5 mg oral liquid every four hours
- Dose is gradually increased until comfortable (not necessarily until breathlessness resolves)
- 30 mg per day of oral morphine is usually sufficient for breathlessness alone
- If using more than 2 doses of liquid morphine daily, your doctor may convert to slow-release morphine capsules or a continuous subcutaneous infusion
- If kidney function is impaired, your doctor may consider an alternative

### Benzodiazepines

Used when anxiety is contributing to breathlessness. Not used for breathlessness alone.

- **Lorazepam** — 0.5 mg orally every 4–6 hours as a starting dose
- **Midazolam** — short-acting; can be given intranasally (absorbed quickly) or subcutaneously (1.25–2.5 mg per dose). If two subcutaneous doses give limited relief, specialist advice is sought.

### Corticosteroids

Used when breathlessness is caused by bronchial obstruction, superior vena cava obstruction, or radiation pneumonitis.

- Prednisone 20–40 mg in the morning, or dexamethasone 4–8 mg in the morning (sometimes up to 16 mg)
- If the cause is unclear, a 3–4 day trial may be used; stopped if no effect
- Dose is gradually reduced to the lowest effective dose

### Nebulisers

- Saline nebulisers help loosen thick sputum
- Ipratropium and salbutamol may help in COPD or asthma (not more than every 4 hours)

### Oxygen

The benefit of oxygen in non-cyanotic patients with low oxygen saturation is uncertain, but a trial is reasonable if other measures have failed. Patients who wish to die at home may be considered for home oxygen.

## Breathlessness Management Plans

Your doctor may create a written plan that combines the above techniques — starting with non-pharmacological approaches, with step-by-step medication instructions if those don't work. The plan includes who to call if it's not effective.

---

## Nearing End of Life

If nearing the end of life, your doctor may use a **syringe driver** to deliver morphine and midazolam continuously under the skin to maintain comfort. Higher doses of midazolam may be needed and are prescribed in consultation with a palliative care specialist.

---

## When to Involve a Specialist

Your doctor will seek adult palliative care support and advice for:
- Anticipatory planning and acute management
- Arranging a joint specialist visit
- Considering hospice admission

Occupational therapy referral may be made for equipment and activity strategy assessment.
