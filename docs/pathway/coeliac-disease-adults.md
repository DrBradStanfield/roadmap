# Coeliac Disease (Adults)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers how doctors assess and manage coeliac disease in adults — from initial testing through to long-term monitoring.

---

## What is Coeliac Disease?

- An immune-mediated condition triggered by gluten, found in wheat, rye, and barley.
- Affects about 1% of people in New Zealand; more common in people of European descent. Rare in Māori, Pacific Island, and Chinese populations.
- If untreated, can lead to anaemia, nutritional deficiencies, bone thinning, and rarely malignancy.
- Requires a **gluten-free diet for life**.
- Confirmation requires a tissue biopsy via gastroscopy — serology (blood tests) is first line to guide who needs referral.

---

## Who Should Be Tested?

Your doctor will consider testing if you have **high-risk conditions** including:

- Dermatitis herpetiformis (a blistering skin rash)
- Thyroid disease
- Type 1 diabetes (about 6% have coeliac disease)
- IgA deficiency
- Down syndrome or Turner syndrome
- Low-impact fracture or osteoporosis
- Infertility or recurrent miscarriage
- Unexplained abnormal liver function tests
- Unexplained neurological disorder or epilepsy
- Family history of coeliac disease (10% prevalence in first-degree relatives; higher with multiple affected relatives)

Coeliac disease may also be considered in the **differential diagnosis** for:

- Iron deficiency anaemia or nutritional deficiencies
- Altered bowel habits — diarrhoea, constipation, abdominal pain, bloating, or flatulence
- Unexplained weight loss or gain
- Mouth ulcers
- Fatigue
- Osteoporosis
- Infertility

## Testing

### You Must Be Eating Gluten Before Testing

For blood tests and biopsy to be reliable, you must be consuming gluten for **at least 4 weeks before testing** — approximately 4 slices of wheat bread per day for adults. Do not start a gluten-free diet before testing is complete.

### Blood Tests (Serology — First Line)

Your doctor will order:

- **tTG IgA** (tissue transglutaminase IgA antibody) — the standard first-line test.
- Some laboratories also add **IgG deamidated gliadin peptide (dGP)**.

If tTG IgA is positive, your doctor will also arrange:

- FBC, ferritin, folate, and vitamin B12 (to check for nutritional deficiencies).
- Referral to a gastroenterologist for biopsy (see below).

### IgA Deficiency

- If you have IgA deficiency, tTG IgA can give a false-negative result.
- Laboratories routinely check for this and will use alternative IgG-based tests (IgG tTG and IgG dGP) if IgA deficiency is found.
- 10% of people with IgA deficiency have coeliac disease, so this is still important to investigate.

### Other Tests (Rarely Needed)

- **Endomysial antibodies (EMA):** Sometimes used if tTG result is equivocal.
- **HLA DQ2/DQ8 gene testing:** Rarely used. A positive result does not confirm coeliac disease (over 30% of the general population carry these genes). Mainly used to exclude coeliac disease in someone already on a gluten-free diet who cannot do a gluten challenge. Costs approximately $47.
- **Point-of-care testing kits** available commercially are not recommended.

---

## Diagnosis

Definitive diagnosis requires a **gastroscopy with small bowel biopsy**, arranged by a gastroenterologist. You must continue eating gluten until the biopsy is done.

Your doctor will refer you for biopsy if:

- tTG IgA is positive.
- Serology is negative or equivocal but there is high clinical suspicion and risk factors.

---

## Management After Diagnosis

### Gluten-Free Diet (Lifelong)

The cornerstone of treatment is a strict **gluten-free diet for life**. Your doctor will refer you to a dietitian who specialises in coeliac disease.

A gluten-free diet is more expensive than a standard diet. A **government subsidy is available** — your doctor applies via Special Authority (requires confirmation of diagnosis by small bowel biopsy).

### Additional Investigations at Diagnosis

Your doctor will arrange:

- **DXA bone density scan** — to check for bone thinning (osteopenia/osteoporosis).
- Blood tests: FBC, iron, vitamin B12, folic acid, thyroid function, calcium, phosphate, vitamin D, liver function tests.

### Testing First-Degree Relatives

Your doctor will recommend that all first-degree relatives (parents, siblings, children) be tested for coeliac disease.

### If Symptoms Persist on a Gluten-Free Diet

If bowel symptoms continue despite following a gluten-free diet, your doctor will consider:

- Alternative diagnoses: inflammatory bowel disease, irritable bowel syndrome, colorectal cancer.
- Additional diagnosis: lymphocytic colitis.
- A trial of a **low-FODMAP diet** for co-existing functional gut symptoms (implemented with dietitian supervision).

  FODMAPs are short-chain carbohydrates that can cause gut symptoms:
  - **F**ermentable
  - **O**ligosaccharides (fructans, GOS)
  - **D**isaccharides (lactose)
  - **M**onosaccharides (fructose)
  - **A**nd
  - **P**olyols (sorbitol, mannitol, xylitol, etc.)

---

## Annual Review

Your GP will review you regularly. At each review they will typically:

1. Consider repeat tTG IgA testing only if there are ongoing symptoms, compliance concerns, or unexplained findings (e.g., continuing iron deficiency). Note: tTG IgA can take up to 2 years to normalise after diagnosis.
2. Refer back to a dietitian if there are persistent symptoms.
3. Check for side effects of the gluten-free diet (e.g., constipation).
4. Measure and monitor BMI.
5. Check comorbidities and arrange bloods: vitamin D, B12, folate, iron, and any indicated investigations (e.g., repeat DXA).
