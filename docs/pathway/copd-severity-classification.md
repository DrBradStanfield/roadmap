# COPD Severity Classification

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This page explains how doctors classify the severity of chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), using breathlessness scores and lung function tests, and how classification guides treatment decisions.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess how much COPD is affecting your day-to-day life using one or more of the following tools.

### Modified Medical Research Council (MMRC) Dyspnoea Scale

This scale rates breathlessness from 0 to 4 based on what activities trigger it:

| Grade | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| 0 | "I only get breathless with strenuous exercise." |
| 1 | "I get short of breath when hurrying on level ground or walking up a slight hill." |
| 2 | "On level ground, I walk slower than people of the same age because of breathlessness, or I have to stop for breath when walking at my own pace on the level." |
| 3 | "I stop for breath after walking about 100 metres or after a few minutes on level ground." |
| 4 | "I am too breathless to leave the house" or "I am breathless when dressing." |

Your doctor may also use your MMRC grade as part of the **BODE index**, which combines Body mass index, airflow Obstruction, Dyspnoea, and Exercise capacity to help assess prognosis.

### COPD Assessment Test (CAT)

The CAT is a short questionnaire your doctor may use to measure how COPD affects your symptoms and quality of life. Results are tracked over time to monitor disease progression.

### Spirometry (Lung Function Test)

Spirometry measures how much air you can breathe out in one second (FEV1) compared to what is expected for someone your age, sex, and height. Your doctor will use the result after a bronchodilator (reliever) inhaler to classify severity.

---

## Severity Classification

Your doctor classifies COPD into three categories based on your symptoms and lung function. Note that exacerbations (flare-ups) can occur at any stage of severity, including mild.

| | Mild | Moderate | Severe |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Typical Symptoms** | Few symptoms. Breathlessness on moderate exertion only. Little or no effect on daily activities. Cough and sputum production. | Breathlessness walking on level ground. Recurrent chest infections. Exacerbations needing oral corticosteroids and/or antibiotics. | Breathless on minimal exertion. Daily activities severely limited. Exacerbations of increasing frequency and severity. |
| **Lung Function (FEV1)** | 60–80% of predicted | 40–59% of predicted | Less than 40% of predicted |

*Adapted from: Asthma + Respiratory Foundation NZ — NZ COPD Guidelines 2021.*

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following approaches based on your severity classification. Treatment decisions are always made in the context of your full history and individual circumstances.

**Mild COPD:** Your doctor may consider a short-acting bronchodilator (e.g., salbutamol) for use when needed, alongside smoking cessation support and advice on exercise and vaccination (influenza, pneumococcal).

**Moderate COPD:** Your doctor may consider regular long-acting bronchodilators — such as a long-acting muscarinic antagonist (e.g., tiotropium) or a long-acting beta-agonist (e.g., salmeterol, formoterol). Pulmonary rehabilitation and referral to a respiratory specialist may be appropriate.

**Severe COPD:** Your doctor may consider combination inhaler therapy — long-acting bronchodilators with or without an inhaled corticosteroid. Your doctor will also assess whether you need home oxygen, and may refer you to a respiratory specialist. Frequent exacerbations may require an individualised action plan and closer monitoring.

At all stages, your doctor will discuss smoking cessation, vaccination, and how to manage exacerbations early.
