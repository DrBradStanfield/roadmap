# Vertigo

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of vertigo in adults — distinguishing between peripheral and central causes, and guiding appropriate treatment.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

Your healthcare provider will treat the following as urgent:

- Vertigo with unilateral sensorineural hearing loss and acute otitis media — possible acute bacterial labyrinthitis requiring urgent assessment

If central vertigo is suspected (see below), urgent medical or neurology assessment is needed.

---

## Background

Vertigo is a false sensation that you or your surroundings are moving or spinning, usually accompanied by nausea and loss of balance.

The term "dizziness" means different things to different people. Your doctor will ask you to describe exactly what you experience:

- **Vertigo** — the room or you seem to spin (illusion of movement, often horizontal and rotatory)
- **Dysequilibrium** — feeling unsteady, without spinning; can result from problems with the peripheral nervous system, eyes, or vestibular system
- **Presyncope** — feeling like you might faint; caused by reduced blood flow to the brain
- **Lightheadedness** — non-specific; may relate to anxiety, panic attacks, or hyperventilation

Causes of vertigo are broadly divided into:

- **Central** — involving the brain (cerebral cortex, cerebellum, brainstem), e.g., cerebrovascular disease, migraine, multiple sclerosis, vestibular schwannoma
- **Peripheral** — involving the inner ear or vestibular nerve, e.g., viral labyrinthitis, vestibular neuronitis, BPPV, Menière disease

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history and perform a neuro-otological examination. This typically includes:

- Description of symptoms — spinning, unsteadiness, or faintness; associated nausea, vomiting, earache, hearing loss, tinnitus
- Cerebellar tests, gait assessment, cranial nerve examination, and otoscopy
- **Head impulse test** — tests the vestibulo-ocular reflex (VOR) by rapidly turning the head to one side; a positive test (corrective eye saccades) suggests peripheral vestibular disease
- **Tuning fork tests** — to assess hearing
- **Dix-Hallpike positional test** — to diagnose BPPV; the patient's head is turned 45 degrees and they are laid back; a positive test produces acute vertigo and torsional nystagmus

### Distinguishing Central from Peripheral Vertigo

| Feature | Central | Peripheral |
|---|---|---|
| Intensity | Often less intense (though brainstem/posterior fossa strokes can be severe) | Intense |
| Position-related | Usually not | Yes — aggravated by position change |
| Associated signs | Ataxia, facial numbness or weakness, diplopia, dysphagia, hemiparesis, headache | Nausea, vomiting; with or without tinnitus or hearing loss |
| Examples | CVA, TIA, haemorrhage, MS, migraine, neoplasm | BPPV, vestibular neuronitis, Menière disease, labyrinthitis |

## Common Causes of Peripheral Vertigo

| Condition | Description | Associated Features | Duration | Tinnitus | Hearing Loss |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| BPPV | Triggered by head turning or movement | Nausea, vomiting; possible history of head injury | Attacks less than 1 minute; settles if head kept still | No | No |
| Menière Disease | Triad of vertigo, tinnitus, and hearing loss; persistent dysequilibrium | "Pressure" sensation in affected ear | Lasts 1 to 24 hours | Yes (worsens over time) | Comes and goes; eventually permanent |
| Vestibular Neuronitis | Sudden severe episode followed by dysequilibrium | Nausea, vomiting; may follow viral illness | Hours to days; resolves within one week | No | No |

## Central Causes of Vertigo

Your healthcare provider will consider the following if central vertigo is suspected:

- **Migraine** — vertigo can occur as a migraine aura, with or without headache
- **Vertebrobasilar ischaemia** — dizziness and vertigo are common preceding symptoms; vascular ischaemic vertigo is typically brief (minutes)
- **Cerebellar infarction** — presents with vertigo and ataxia; patients are usually unable to stand; if the head impulse test is negative, early MRI is needed to exclude cerebellar infarct
- **Multiple sclerosis** — early presentation may be balance and gait disturbance; a demyelinating lesion at the 8th nerve root entry zone can mimic vestibular neuritis
- **Vestibular schwannoma** — presents with tinnitus, unilateral hearing loss, and subtle balance deterioration; occasionally with acute vertigo

---

## Medications That May Cause Vertigo

Your doctor will review your medications, as the following can cause or contribute to vertigo:

- Quinine
- Anticonvulsants (e.g., phenytoin, carbamazepine)
- Diuretics (e.g., furosemide)
- Antibiotics (e.g., erythromycin, minocycline, aminoglycosides)
- NSAIDs (e.g., aspirin, ibuprofen, naproxen, indometacin)
- Cytotoxic medications

Alcohol is also a recognised cause.

## Management

### General Measures

- Explanation and reassurance — anxiety can worsen vertigo
- Discuss driving implications, particularly with Menière disease (sudden severe attacks may affect fitness to drive)
- Intravenous fluids are only needed if vomiting causes dehydration that does not respond to antiemetics and oral rehydration

### Benign Paroxysmal Positional Vertigo (BPPV)

- Your doctor may perform the **Epley Canalith Repositioning Procedure (CRP)** — a series of head and body position changes designed to move dislodged crystals (otoconia) out of the affected semicircular canal. This is successful in approximately 80% of cases.
- Home exercises: your doctor may recommend **Brandt-Daroff exercises**, performed 2–3 times daily for 6 weeks
- Most cases are short-lived. If symptoms persist, your healthcare provider may refer you for specialist ENT/Otolaryngology assessment.

### Vestibular Neuronitis

- Caused by sudden unilateral vestibular failure, likely viral
- Acute vertigo can last up to one week; balance recovery can take a month or longer in older individuals
- Your healthcare provider may recommend antiemetics such as prochlorperazine or scopolamine patches to manage vertigo and vomiting
- Some patients experience recurrent attacks and may need self-administered antiemetics (e.g., buccal prochlorperazine)
- Early mobilisation is encouraged to aid recovery
- Follow-up is recommended if symptoms persist beyond 2 weeks

### Menière Disease

- An inner ear disorder with attacks of vertigo (typically lasting less than 12 hours), usually accompanied by hearing loss, tinnitus, and a blocked feeling in the affected ear
- Diagnosis is established by electrocochleography

**For acute attacks**, your healthcare provider may recommend:
- Antiemetics for 1–2 days (e.g., prochlorperazine, cyclizine, promethazine)
- A short course of prednisone (e.g., 40 mg daily for 5 days, then tapered over 10 days)
- Urea crystals (30 g once, dissolved in juice)

**Maintenance to prevent further attacks:**
- A thiazide diuretic (e.g., bendroflumethiazide 2.5 mg, potentially increasing to 5 mg)
- Betahistine may be considered on specialist recommendation — current evidence on its effectiveness is limited

**Lifestyle changes:**
- Low-salt diet
- Reduced caffeine and alcohol intake

**Other management:**
- Discuss hearing aids and tinnitus support with your healthcare provider
- Advise about driving restrictions during severe attacks
- If severe ongoing problems persist, referral to an ENT/otolaryngologist may be considered (e.g., for grommets or intratympanic medication)

---

## When Specialist Referral May Be Needed

Your healthcare provider may arrange specialist review if:

- Red flags are present — urgent ENT/otolaryngology assessment
- Central vertigo is suspected — urgent general medicine or neurology assessment
- Peripheral vertigo with new unilateral hearing loss — further assessment via a hearing loss pathway
- BPPV or Menière disease does not respond to primary care management — non-acute ENT/otolaryngology assessment
- Ongoing chronic dizziness or balance problems — assessment at a specialist dizziness and balance centre may be appropriate
- Vestibular rehabilitation physiotherapy may be beneficial for some patients
