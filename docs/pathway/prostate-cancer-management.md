# Prostate Cancer — Management

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers treatment options and management for men diagnosed with prostate cancer. Most care is provided by specialist urology and radiation oncology services — your GP may be involved in follow-up.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Assessment

Go to the emergency department or call for urgent medical help if you develop:

- Significant bleeding
- Urinary retention (unable to pass urine)
- Suspected kidney failure

If you have symptoms suggesting your prostate cancer is progressing — such as increasing PSA, bone pain, significant urinary symptoms, or unexplained weight loss — your doctor will arrange urgent specialist review.

---

## Treatment Options

Your doctor and specialist team will discuss all treatment options with you. In around 40% of cases — particularly low-volume, low-grade disease — active treatment is not required straight away.

The best treatment option is decided jointly with you and your specialists, taking into account:

**Patient factors:**
- Life expectancy and age
- Other medical conditions (comorbidities)
- Urinary symptoms
- Bowel symptoms or prior bowel disease (e.g., inflammatory bowel disease)
- Previous radiotherapy or pelvic surgery

**Disease factors:**
- Tumour grade, using either:
  - ISUP grading system: Grade 1 (low), Grade 2 (intermediate), Grades 3–5 (high)
  - Gleason score (older system, being phased out): 6 (low), 7 (intermediate), 8–10 (high)
- Tumour, nodes, and metastasis (TNM) staging
- PSA level
- Prostate volume

**Your personal preferences.**

### Active Surveillance

Your doctor may consider active surveillance if your cancer meets all of the following criteria:

- Low Gleason score (≤6 combined / ISUP Grade 1)
- Low stage (less than T2)
- Low PSA (≤10)
- Life expectancy of at least 10 years

Active surveillance means no treatment is given at this stage. The aim is to monitor closely so that curative treatment can begin if the disease progresses. Around 40% of men with prostate cancer are managed this way.

What active surveillance involves (carried out by your urologist):
- PSA and/or digital rectal examination every 3 months initially, then every 6 months, then annually
- Repeat biopsy within 12 months of diagnosis, then every 2–5 years
- Prostate MRI (usually before or after biopsy) to ensure no cancer is missed at the start

About 30% of men on active surveillance exit the programme — usually within 2 years — if:
- Life expectancy falls below 10 years (transition to watchful waiting)
- Repeat biopsy shows disease progression (higher Gleason grade or increased tumour volume)
- The patient chooses to stop

A rising PSA alone is not usually enough reason to start treatment without biopsy confirmation of progression.

### Surgery

Your doctor may consider one of the following surgical options, both of which have curative intent:

**Laparoscopic or robotic radical prostatectomy (minimally invasive):**
- The prostate and seminal vesicles are removed (with or without pelvic lymph nodes)
- 1–2 nights in hospital; urethral catheter in place for 1–2 weeks
- Return to work and normal activities in approximately 2–3 weeks
- Benefits over open surgery: less blood loss, less pain, quicker recovery

**Open radical prostatectomy:**
- Similar disease control outcomes to minimally invasive surgery and radiation therapies
- Can also be used as a palliative procedure
- 2 nights in hospital; urethral catheter in place for 1–2 weeks
- Return to work and normal activities in approximately 6 weeks

**Complications of surgery:**
- Bleeding and infection (slightly lower risk with minimally invasive approaches)
- Erectile dysfunction — around 50% (note: 50% of men aged 60 or over already have some erectile dysfunction before surgery)
- Urinary incontinence — around 10%
- Bladder neck stricture (scarring)
- Rectal injury
- Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE)

### Radiation Treatments

Your doctor may consider one of the following radiation approaches:

**External beam radiation treatment:**
- Curative intent; similar outcomes to surgery
- Delivered as an outpatient, Monday to Friday, over 7–8 weeks
- Often preceded by 6 months of hormone therapy with an LHRH agonist
- Usually able to continue working and normal activities during treatment

Short-term complications (usually settle within 4–6 weeks):
- Bowel: loose motions, urgency, rectal bleeding, mucus discharge (severe in fewer than 5% of cases)
- Bladder: urinary frequency, discomfort passing urine
- Tiredness or fatigue

Long-term complications:
- Bowel changes in fewer than 5% of cases (altered bowel habit, blood, mucus)
- Impotence in around 50% of cases
- Very rarely, a second cancer (this risk increases over time, so external beam radiation is usually recommended for older patients or those with lower life expectancy)

**Seed brachytherapy (low dose rate):**
- Available in private practice
- Radioactive seeds are temporarily placed in the prostate via the perineum under general anaesthetic
- Curative intent; similar outcomes to surgery and external beam radiation
- Requires minimal lower urinary tract symptoms and a small prostate
- Recovery: a few days off work; back to normal activities within one week

Complications:
- Bruising in the perineum
- Urinary frequency and urgency
- Acute urinary retention requiring catheter (rare)
- Infection
- Long-term: impotence, lower urinary tract symptoms

**High dose rate brachytherapy:**
- Temporary placement of a radioactive source within the prostate
- Used alongside external beam radiotherapy for intermediate and high-risk prostate cancer
- Patient selection based on general health, prostate size, bladder symptoms, and urine flow studies
- Reportedly high cancer control rates, but with increased bladder side effects
- Currently only available privately

### Advanced Disease — Watchful Waiting and Palliative Management

For advanced or metastatic disease, the aim is not to cure but to delay progression and manage symptoms. Your doctor will discuss the following options:

**Hormone therapy:**
- Hormone deprivation therapy (orchidectomy — surgical removal of the testicles) or an LHRH agonist
- LHRH agonists — such as **goserelin** or **leuprorelin** — are given as injections and reduce testosterone to slow cancer growth
- Initial treatment for metastatic disease often involves an LHRH agonist (or occasionally an LHRH antagonist)
- Medications and surgery can control disease for many years

**Other palliative options:**
- Early chemotherapy may lengthen survival and improve quality of life
- Palliative radiation therapy for local symptoms (e.g., bone pain, bleeding)
- Palliative chemotherapy for general symptoms (e.g., fatigue)

Follow-up for advanced disease typically involves PSA measurement and clinical review in primary care every 6–12 months.

Men with significant other health conditions or limited life expectancy are generally not suitable for curative treatment.

---

### After Surgery

If you have had prostate surgery, your doctor may refer you for pelvic floor physiotherapy to help with urinary control.

If you experience problems with a urinary catheter after surgery, do not attempt to remove it yourself — contact your care team urgently.
