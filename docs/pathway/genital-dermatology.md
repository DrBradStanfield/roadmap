# Genital Dermatology

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of dermatological conditions affecting the genital skin — including itch, rash, pain, and lumps — in both males and females.

> **Note:** This information is for general understanding only. Genital skin conditions often require a physical examination to diagnose accurately. Always discuss symptoms and management with your doctor.

---

## Possible Causes

Genital skin symptoms have varied causes:

- **Autoimmune:** lichen sclerosus, lichen planus, inflammatory bowel disease-associated ulceration
- **Bacterial:** syphilis, donovanosis, *Staphylococcus*
- **Fungal:** tinea cruris, candidiasis
- **Inflammatory:** eczema, psoriasis, lichen simplex, hidradenitis suppurativa
- **Malignancy:** pre-cancerous or cancerous lesions
- **Normal anatomic variants**
- **Parasites:** scabies, lice
- **Trauma:** washing, shaving, waxing, friction, chafing
- **Viral:** HPV, molluscum contagiosum, human herpesvirus

Always consider genital skin cancer, especially if a lesion is not responding to treatment, or if you are immunocompromised or have HIV — genital skin cancers can look similar to other skin conditions.

---

## Assessment

### What Your Doctor Will Ask

Your doctor will ask about:

- Your concerns and how the condition affects you
- Symptoms: rash, itch, pain, blisters, ulcers, lumps, splits, fissures
- Urinary symptoms or pain with intercourse
- When it started, where it is, what makes it better or worse
- Contact factors: wet wipes, hygiene products, lubricants, soaps, sanitary pads, incontinence pads
- Current or recent medications — including antibiotics, over-the-counter treatments, genital deodorants, douches, and hair removal
- Relationship to sexual activity
- Whether other areas of skin are affected
- History of eczema, psoriasis, or other skin conditions

### Examination

Your doctor will perform a thorough examination of the affected area, including surrounding skin, groin lymph nodes, and possibly other body areas (skin, nails, mouth) since some genital conditions also affect other sites.

Clinical photographs may be taken with your permission for documentation and follow-up.

### Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- **Biopsy** — if the diagnosis is uncertain, treatment is not working, or malignancy is suspected. A biopsy involves removing a small piece of tissue for analysis.
- **Swabs** — if infection is suspected (bacteria or herpes virus)
- **Skin scraping** — if a fungal infection (dermatophyte) is suspected
- **Blood tests** — usually not needed for primary skin conditions, but may be arranged to check for thyroid function (relevant to lichen sclerosus), syphilis, or HIV if clinically indicated

## Common Conditions and Features

### In Females

**Genital itch with or without rash:**

- **Eczema (dermatitis)** — most common cause; includes contact, atopic, and seborrhoeic types. Irritants include urine, detergents, and scrubbing.
- **Candidiasis** — fungal infection
- **Genitourinary syndrome of menopause** — vaginal dryness, irritation, itching after menopause
- **Lichen simplex** — thickened, lichenified (leathery) skin from chronic scratching
- **Pruritus ani** — perianal itch
- **Pubic lice, scabies**
- **Intertrigo** — skin fold irritation
- **Psoriasis**
- **Lichen sclerosus** — see below
- **Lichen planus** — see below
- **Extramammary Paget's disease (EMPD)** — rare; consider if chronic anogenital skin change does not respond within 4–6 weeks to standard treatment

**Genital pain in females:**

In addition to the above, consider:
- Non-STI ulceration, boils, folliculitis
- Abscess — Bartholin's or Skene's gland
- Genital herpes or other STI
- **Vulvodynia** — chronic vulval pain with no identifiable cause; can be provoked or spontaneous

Less common: Crohn's disease, herpes zoster, Behçet's disease, mpox

**Genital lumps in females:**

More common:
- Normal anatomical variants (e.g., Fordyce spots, vestibular papillae)
- Genital warts, molluscum contagiosum
- Bartholin's or Skene's cyst

Less common: pre-malignant or malignant lesions (VIN, AIN, squamous cell carcinoma, mpox)

### In Males

**Genital itch with or without rash:**

- Eczema (dermatitis)
- Lichen simplex
- Pruritus ani
- Fungal infection
- Balanitis (inflammation of the glans penis)
- Pubic lice, scabies
- Intertrigo, psoriasis
- Extramammary Paget's disease

**Genital pain in males:**

In addition to the above, consider:
- Non-STI ulceration, boils, folliculitis
- Genital herpes or other STI
- **Lichen sclerosus (male)** — see below
- Dysaesthesia — penile or scrotal pain without a primary skin condition

Less common: Behçet's disease, fixed drug eruption, herpes zoster, Crohn's disease, mpox

**Genital lumps or skin changes in males:**

More common:
- Boil/folliculitis
- Genital warts, molluscum contagiosum, pearly penile papules
- Normal anatomical variants (Fordyce spots, Tyson's glands)

Less common: Peyronie's disease, penile or anal cancer, lichen planus, mpox

## Key Conditions Explained

### Lichen Sclerosus (LS)

An autoimmune skin condition. In females (vulval LS), it can occur at any age but peaks in prepubertal girls and after age 50. In males (penile LS, previously called balanitis xerotica obliterans), it is more common in childhood and middle age.

**Symptoms (may be asymptomatic):**
- Itch or irritation
- Soreness of affected area
- Pain with intercourse (females) or painful erections (males)
- Urinary difficulties — dysuria, poor stream, or retention (males)
- Non-retractile foreskin (males)

**Appearance:** Pale, white, hypertrophic or atrophic areas of skin. In females, may form a "figure of eight" pattern around vulva and perianal area. In males, ivory-white plaques on glans, prepuce, and coronal sulcus.

**Important:** Lichen sclerosus is associated with increased risk of genital cancer (<5% lifetime risk in females; higher with untreated male LS). Lifelong treatment and follow-up are usually required.

### Lichen Planus

An inflammatory autoimmune condition, less common than lichen sclerosus. More common after age 40. Affects skin and mucosal areas — coexisting genital, oral, and skin lichen planus is common.

**Appearance:**
- In females: lacy white streaks (Wickham's striae) or erosive red plaques at vaginal introitus
- In males: annular plaques or shiny papules on penile shaft or glans; red, purple, or white patches

Lichen planus is often difficult to treat and usually requires specialist assessment.

### Lichen Simplex

Thickened, lichenified (leathery) skin that develops from chronic rubbing or scratching in response to itch. Often unilateral or asymmetrical.

### Genital Psoriasis

Genital area can be the first site for psoriasis. Presents as well-demarcated red plaques — usually symmetrical, rarely with scale. Does not affect mucous membranes.

### Extramammary Paget's Disease (EMPD)

A rare skin cancer (intraepithelial adenocarcinoma). Appears as itchy, well-delineated, asymmetrical, red or pink scaly plaque in the anogenital area. Consider if chronic anogenital skin change does not respond within 4–6 weeks to standard topical treatment.

### Normal Anatomical Variants (Not Requiring Treatment)

- **Pearly penile papules** — soft, dome-shaped papules around the corona
- **Vulvar/vestibular papillae** — uniform tubular projections
- **Fordyce spots / Tyson glands** — yellow or white smooth papules (ectopic sebaceous glands)
- Sebaceous cysts, skin tags, seborrhoeic keratoses

## Management

### General Skin Care (All Patients with Genital Itch)

Your doctor will advise on genital skin care. Key principles:

**Avoid:**
- Soaps, detergents, and perfumed products on the affected area
- Exposure to urine, faeces, and menstrual blood on the skin
- Scratching or rubbing (even a single scratch prolongs itch for a week)
- Wet swimwear or clothing left on the skin
- Scented sanitary pads or panty liners — consider tampons, menstrual cups, or period underwear

**Encourage:**
- Washing once or twice daily with warm water and a pH-balanced cleanser (e.g., Cetaphil, Dermaveen, Aveeno)
- Keeping cool, especially at night
- Loose-fitting, natural fibre underwear and clothing
- Cold compresses to reduce scratching

**Protective agents your doctor may recommend:**
- Bland, fragrance-free moisturiser (e.g., fatty cream or cetomacrogol with 10% glycerine) — often more soothing if refrigerated
- Barrier creams (e.g., petroleum jelly, zinc and castor oil) if skin is sensitive or if incontinent

### Eczema/Dermatitis

Your doctor will advise on avoiding triggers and may prescribe:
- Mild topical steroid (1% hydrocortisone) twice daily for 2–4 weeks for mild cases
- Moderate to potent topical steroid for more severe cases
- Very potent steroids are not used for genital eczema
- Continue moisturisers after steroid course; repeat short courses for flares

### Psoriasis

Psoriasis is not curable — treatment controls symptoms. Your doctor may prescribe:
- Mild to moderate topical steroids on a rotating or sequential basis
- Calcineurin inhibitors (e.g., pimecrolimus cream) as an alternative — may cause initial stinging
- Coal tar and calcipotriol are not suitable for the genital area
- If psoriasis persists and is distressing, your doctor may refer for systemic therapy (e.g., methotrexate)

### Lichen Sclerosus — Females (Vulval LS)

Treatment is lifelong. Goals are symptom control, preventing cancer, preventing scarring, and maintaining urinary and sexual function. Asymptomatic patients with visible signs still need treatment.

Your doctor will prescribe a **potent or very potent topical steroid** (e.g., clobetasol propionate ointment):
- Apply a pea-sized amount to affected (white) areas nightly for 1–3 months, then taper to twice weekly for long-term maintenance
- Pruritus usually settles within 1 week; signs improve over 1–3 months
- A 30 g tube should last at least 12 weeks; ongoing maintenance typically requires 30–60 g per year

**Maintenance options** (chosen individually):
- Very potent steroid (clobetasol propionate) once to twice per week ongoing
- Potent steroid (mometasone furoate or betamethasone valerate) once per week for less severe disease

**Annual review:** Your doctor will review symptoms, examine the skin, and discuss adherence. Well-controlled lichen sclerosus can be managed in primary care.

Contact your doctor promptly if you develop new sores, ulcers, lumps, or skin thickening that persist for more than 4 weeks.

### Lichen Sclerosus — Males (MGLSc)

Your doctor will coordinate care with a urologist or dermatologist. Treatment goals are the same as for females.

Your doctor may prescribe:
- Very potent topical steroid (clobetasol propionate 0.05% ointment) once daily for 3 months, or twice daily for 1 month
- Repeat course for relapse; long-term maintenance steroids are not usually required
- Circumcision may be needed if phimosis is present

If no response after 3 months, specialist review, biopsy, or circumcision may be needed.

### Lichen Simplex

Your doctor may prescribe:
- Moderate topical steroid (e.g., triamcinolone acetonide) once daily for 2–4 weeks, tapering over subsequent months
- If no response, a potent steroid for a short course; if still no response, specialist assessment
- Note: if itch is neuropathic (nerve-related), it may persist despite skin improvement — topical steroids are not effective for neuropathic itch

### Lichen Planus

If suspected, your doctor will begin treatment similar to lichen sclerosus and refer for specialist assessment, as lichen planus is difficult to treat and may require systemic therapy.

### Genitourinary Syndrome of Menopause (GSM)

If other causes have been excluded, management includes:

**Vaginal estrogen cream** (e.g., estriol cream — Ovestin): applied nightly for 2 weeks, then twice weekly ongoing. This is considered safe in most women, including those with a history of most gynaecological cancers. Ask your doctor if this is appropriate for you.

**Non-hormonal options for mild symptoms:**
- Vaginal moisturisers (e.g., Replens, YES VM) — use 3 times per week
- Water-based or silicone-based lubricant at time of intercourse
- Pelvic floor exercises — your doctor may refer you to a physiotherapist

### Genital Ulcers

If genital herpes is confirmed or likely, your doctor will prescribe antiviral medication (valaciclovir 500 mg twice daily for 7 days) plus pain relief as needed.

## When to See a Doctor Urgently

Seek prompt assessment if:

- A lesion is not responding to treatment as expected
- You develop a persistent sore, ulcer, firm lump, or thickened skin that lasts more than 4 weeks
- You are immunocompromised or have HIV and develop any unexplained genital skin change
- You need very frequent or high-dose topical steroids to control symptoms
- Your symptoms are significantly affecting urination, sexual activity, or quality of life

Your doctor will refer you to a specialist if any of these apply. Urgency depends on the suspected diagnosis.
