# Blepharitis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of blepharitis — a common, chronic inflammation of the eyelids.

---

## Red Flags — See Your Doctor Promptly

See your doctor urgently if you have:

- Unilateral (one-sided only) blepharitis — this may suggest a malignant process such as basal cell carcinoma
- Abnormalities of eyelash growth — this also warrants prompt medical review

---

## Background

Blepharitis is a low-grade inflammation of the eyelids. It can affect the anterior eyelid, the posterior eyelid, or commonly both.

Dandruff-like flakes of skin or debris collect around the lashes, often attracting bacteria (staphylococci) which infect the flakes and live deep in the eyelash glands.

The meibomian glands normally secrete a lubricant to keep the eyes moist and comfortable. Blepharitis impairs this function and reduces the lubricant available in the eye.

Blepharitis is one of the most common causes of dry eye, and is frequently associated with rosacea.

It is a chronic condition with no cure. Flare-ups are likely and regular treatment is often needed — but it rarely causes any sight-threatening problems.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Bilateral (usually) sharp, gritty irritation — a sensation of something in the eye
- Epiphora (excessive tearing)
- Red eyelids and mildly red eyes
- Crusting and discharge on lashes and lids
- Vision and light sensitivity
- Development of chalazia (meibomian cysts)
- Itchy eyelids

Examination will check for:

- Normal visual acuity
- Thickened and irregular eyelid margins with signs of inflammation
- Crusting at the base of eyelashes
- Mild eyelid or conjunctival redness
- Blocked meibomian glands
- Chalazia
- Epiphora due to reflex increased tear secretion

Your doctor will also consider other possible causes such as eyelid tumours (basal cell carcinoma, squamous cell carcinoma, sebaceous gland carcinoma), contact or atopic dermatitis, infection (impetigo or cellulitis), conjunctivitis, contact lens problems, and dry eye syndrome.

---

## Management

Lid hygiene and lubricant drops are the mainstay of treatment. Antibiotics are only considered if these first-line measures fail, or if you present with moderate to severe disease.

### Step 1 — Lid Hygiene (All Patients)

Your doctor will advise regular lid hygiene, which includes warm compresses, eyelid massage, and eyelid scrubs:

- Apply a warm compress (e.g. a wheat bag, available from optometrists) to closed eyes for 5 minutes daily to loosen oily secretions
- Clean the lids 1–2 times daily using a commercial preparation (e.g. Sterilid) or diluted baby shampoo — gently massaging around the closed eyelids in the shower
- Continue daily lid hygiene for 3–4 weeks until symptoms improve, then twice weekly to maintain hygiene
- Recommence daily hygiene if symptoms return
- Lid hygiene is ineffective if not done regularly

### Step 2 — Lubricant Eye Drops

Your doctor may recommend lubricant (artificial tear) drops 4–6 times daily. These are available over the counter or on prescription. For long-term use, a preservative-free option is preferred.

Options include:
- Hypromellose with dextran eye drops (e.g. PolyTears)
- Polyvinyl alcohol eye drops (e.g. Vistil, Vistil Forte)
- Hypromellose eye drops 0.5% (e.g. Methopt)

### Step 3 — Topical Antibiotics (if lid hygiene insufficient)

Your doctor may consider topical antibiotics if regular lid hygiene does not adequately control symptoms. Note that the evidence for antibiotic efficacy is not firmly established.

- **Fusidic acid gel** applied along the eyelid margins once or twice daily for 2–4 weeks may be helpful

### Step 4 — Topical Steroids (if fusidic acid insufficient or more severe symptoms)

Your doctor may consider:

- **Dexamethasone + neomycin + polymyxin B sulfate** (e.g. Maxitrol) or **dexamethasone** ointment (e.g. Maxidex) applied at night along the eyelids
- Topical steroids should not be used for more than one 2-week course due to side effects
- This is typically followed by fusidic acid at night for 1 month, then lubricant drops used indefinitely

### Step 5 — Oral Antibiotics (anti-inflammatory use)

Your doctor may consider oral antibiotics mainly for their anti-inflammatory effects. The evidence for this use is not well established.

- **Doxycycline** 100 mg daily for a minimum of 8 weeks, reducing to alternate days after clinical improvement. Can be repeated when necessary
- In pregnancy or breastfeeding: **erythromycin ethyl succinate** 400 mg once daily is used instead

### Additional Advice

- Including omega-3 fatty acids in your diet (oily fish, walnuts, chia seeds) may be beneficial; supplements are an option
- You can be reassured that even though blepharitis is a chronic problem that will intermittently flare, it will not affect your vision

### Specialist Referral

If simple measures do not work, your doctor may consider referral to private optometry or private ophthalmology. Intense pulsed light (IPL) treatment may also be available through private ophthalmology. Note that this condition is not managed through public ophthalmology outpatient services.
