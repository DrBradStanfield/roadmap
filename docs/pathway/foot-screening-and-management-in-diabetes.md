# Foot Screening and Management in Diabetes

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers annual foot screening for people with diabetes — how to assess risk, what the findings mean, and how feet are managed based on risk category.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

Go to the emergency department or contact your doctor urgently if you have diabetes and any of the following affecting your foot or leg:

- **Severe or spreading infection** — rapidly worsening ulcer, deep abscess, spreading redness (cellulitis), or feeling generally unwell
- **Critical limb ischaemia** — absent pulses with rest or night pain lasting more than 2 weeks, ulceration, or gangrene
- **Suspected Charcot foot** — hot, swollen foot (possibly painless), especially with neuropathy
- **Any foot ulcer**

These are high-risk situations associated with a significant risk of amputation. Your doctor will arrange urgent specialist assessment.

---

## Background

People with diabetes are at risk of serious foot complications due to two main mechanisms:

1. **Neuropathy** — nerve damage reduces sensation, so injuries go unnoticed and worsen
2. **Peripheral arterial disease (PAD)** — reduced blood flow impairs healing

The lifetime risk of a diabetes foot ulcer (DFU) is around 25%. Most foot ulcers and amputations are preventable through regular screening, early identification of risk factors, and prompt referral to specialist services. Māori people have a significantly higher rate of diabetes-related lower limb amputations.

---

## Screening Schedule

Your doctor will offer foot screening according to this schedule:

- **Type 1 diabetes:** start 5 years after diagnosis, or at age 10 years — whichever comes first
- **Type 2 diabetes:** start at diagnosis
- **All patients:** at least annually; more often if at high risk (e.g., poorly controlled diabetes)

## What Your Doctor Examines

### History

Your doctor will ask about:
- Current foot problems — corns, ulcers, blisters, cracks, or problem nails
- Symptoms — numbness, burning, pain, tingling (paraesthesia)
- Claudication (leg pain on walking) and rest pain
- Occupation, activities, visual impairment, physical disability (e.g., stroke, obesity)
- Footwear and hosiery

### Visual Inspection

Your doctor will look for:
- Foot ulcers or infection
- Nail condition and impingement on adjacent toes
- Problems between the toes (associated with leg cellulitis)
- Tinea pedis (fungal infection, which can be between toes or on the foot surface)
- Skin changes — dry or fissured skin, skin thinning, calluses, corns, or blisters (ulcers can be hidden under deep callus)
- Foot deformity with callus — high arch, clawed toes, bunions
- Foot swelling
- Ill-fitting or inappropriate footwear or hosiery
- Previous amputation

Skin changes and foot deformity that cause calluses or difficulty fitting shoes are risk factors for ulceration, especially in the presence of neuropathy.

### Neuropathy Assessment

Signs of a **neuropathic foot:**
- Warm foot with bounding pulses (may have distended dorsal veins)
- Reduced sweating → dry skin and heel fissures
- Numbness, tingling, burning

People with neuropathy may be unaware of injuries (cuts, abrasions, burns, blisters, infections, foreign bodies, or fractures) and continue to walk on them.

Signs of an **ischaemic foot:**
- Cool, hairless skin with diminished or absent pulses
- Pallor or dependent rubor (redness when leg is down) with thinned skin
- May be painful
- Ulcers typically on the edges of the feet with little callus
- Intermittent claudication, rest pain, or gangrene

A foot can have a mix of both neuropathic and ischaemic features (neuroischaemic foot).

### Tests Your Doctor Performs

- **10 g monofilament or Ipswich Touch the Toes Test** — checks sensation in the feet
- **Tuning fork** — checks vibration sense
- **Tendon reflexes** — ankle; if absent, knee reflexes are checked
- **Pedal pulse palpation** — checks blood flow

## Risk Categories

Based on assessment, your doctor assigns a diabetic foot risk category:

### Low Risk
- Normal sensation
- Pedal pulses present; no signs of peripheral arterial disease

**Management:** Annual foot check; foot care education; supportive footwear; private podiatry if needed (no publicly funded podiatry for low-risk feet).

### Moderate Risk
Any one of:
- Loss of sensation
- Peripheral arterial disease (more than one pulse absent, claudication, pallor, dependent rubor, previous lower limb arterial intervention)
- Significant callus
- Significant foot deformity
- Pre-ulcerative lesion
- eGFR < 15 (CKD stage 5)

**Management:** Foot assessment every visit if neuropathy is present; foot care education; supportive footwear; referral to funded podiatry services.

### High Risk
One of (previous complications):
- Previous amputation
- Previous foot ulcer
- Consolidated Charcot foot

*Or* two or more moderate-risk factors (including loss of sensation, peripheral arterial disease, significant callus, significant deformity, pre-ulcerative lesion, eGFR < 15, Māori ethnicity).

**Management:** Feet checked at every healthcare visit (83-fold increased risk of ulceration); foot care education; supportive or custom footwear; referral to funded podiatry services (may need care every 4–6 weeks). High-risk feet very rarely revert to moderate risk.

### Active Diabetic Foot Disease
- Foot ulcer
- Severe or spreading infection
- Post-operative open wound
- Critical limb ischaemia (rest pain, pale or mottled feet, dependent rubor, ischaemic ulceration, gangrene)
- Acute Charcot foot

**Management:** See urgent assessment section above.

## Charcot Foot — What to Know

Charcot foot is uncommon (1–2% of people with diabetes) and is often misdiagnosed. Key features:

- Usually presents as a **hot, swollen foot** — often painless initially, but may become intensely painful
- Foot pulses are usually present
- Commonly mistaken for infection, gout, sprain, or DVT
- If ulceration is present, osteomyelitis should be considered
- Delayed diagnosis can cause permanent disability
- Late stage: foot becomes deformed with a "rocker bottom" appearance from stress fractures and unstable joints

If your doctor suspects Charcot foot, urgent specialist referral is arranged.

## Treatment of Foot Infections

Your doctor manages diabetic foot infections according to severity:

### Mild Infection
- Less than 4 weeks duration
- Not systemically unwell; infection involves only skin and subcutaneous tissue; redness extends less than 2 cm from wound

Treatment (5 days):
- Flucloxacillin 1000 mg four times a day
- Low-risk penicillin allergy: cefalexin 1000 mg three times a day
- Significant penicillin allergy or MRSA: co-trimoxazole 960 mg twice a day

### Moderate or Chronic Infection
- More than 4 weeks duration, or deeper tissue involvement, or redness extending more than 2 cm from wound

Treatment (7 days):
- No allergies: "boosted Augmentin" (amoxicillin-clavulanate 625 mg plus amoxicillin 500 mg) three times a day
- Low-risk penicillin allergy: cefalexin 1000 mg three times a day plus metronidazole 400 mg twice a day
- Significant penicillin allergy or MRSA: co-trimoxazole 960 mg twice a day plus metronidazole 400 mg twice a day

Moderate or chronic infections are also referred urgently to podiatry via the diabetes service.

## Painful Diabetic Foot Neuropathy

If you have painful neuropathy, your doctor may recommend:

- **Mild pain:** paracetamol or simple analgesia
- **Moderate to severe pain:** neuromodulator medications (your doctor will discuss options)
- **Topical capsaicin 0.075%:** can help localised neuropathic pain; funded if endorsed for diabetic neuropathy. It works by depleting substance P and takes 1–4 weeks to show full effect. An initial burning sensation usually settles with continued use. Apply 3–4 times daily; do not massage in; wash hands after use.
- **Improved diabetes control** often improves neuropathic pain — your doctor will review your HbA1c and glycaemic management

If pain does not respond to these measures, your doctor may refer you to a diabetes specialist or chronic pain service.

## Good Foot Care — What You Can Do

- **Inspect your feet daily** — look for cuts, blisters, redness, swelling, or changes
- **Keep feet clean and dry** — clean between the toes
- **Wear well-cushioned, well-fitting closed shoes** — check inside shoes for rough edges or foreign objects before putting them on
- **Do not walk barefoot**
- If you are elderly, have poor vision, or have physical disabilities, ask for help with regular foot care
- **Contact your doctor promptly** if you notice any new wound, blister, redness, or swelling

## Overall Diabetes Management

Foot health is closely linked to overall diabetes management. Your doctor will also:

- Optimise glycaemic control (HbA1c)
- Manage cardiovascular risk factors
- Encourage smoking cessation (smoking worsens peripheral arterial disease significantly)

If cost is a barrier to appropriate footwear, orthotics, or podiatry, your doctor can help explore funding options (e.g., Work and Income Disability Allowance; Veterans' Affairs if eligible).

---

*Always discuss your foot symptoms, footwear concerns, or any new changes with your doctor or nurse. Do not attempt to treat foot ulcers or infections at home.*
