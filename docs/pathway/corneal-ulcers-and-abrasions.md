# Corneal Ulcers and Abrasions

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of corneal ulcers and abrasions in adults.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Assessment

Your doctor will arrange urgent ophthalmology assessment if:

- Corneal infiltrate, haze, or opacity
- Unilateral red eye (especially if painful)
- Painful red eye in a contact lens wearer
- Significantly reduced vision

---

## Background

**Corneal ulcers:**

- Are deeper than abrasions and involve the corneal stroma, accompanied by stromal inflammation and infection
- Are potentially sight threatening
- Give the affected area an opaque appearance

**Corneal abrasions:**

- Are an area where the cornea is devoid of its surface epithelium
- Are a consequence of mild to moderate ocular trauma
- Are very common
- Usually heal in a couple of days with no ongoing signs or symptoms

Keratitis refers to infection or inflammation of the cornea. A corneal ulcer specifically refers to loss of corneal tissue, which may or may not accompany keratitis.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider corneal ulcers and abrasions in a patient presenting with:

- Painful, red, photophobic eyes
- A foreign body sensation
- Decreased vision

**History your doctor will take:**

- Whether there is any history of penetrating eye injury (high-impact mechanism such as hammering metal on metal, metal grinding, or severe impact — if suspected, a different pathway applies)
- Whether a superficial foreign body is present or has already been removed
- Previous history of corneal abrasions or ulcers
- Initiating events such as a fingernail, branch, foreign body, or difficult contact lens removal
- History of trauma from vegetation (may increase risk of fungal keratitis)
- Ophthalmological history (surgery, contact lens use, amblyopia, eye drops)
- Risk factors for ulcers — in patients with contact lenses and a painful red eye, infectious keratitis is assumed until proven otherwise

**Examination your doctor will perform:**

- Visual acuity assessment with pinhole: patients with an abrasion usually have near-normal vision, while an ulcer may reduce vision depending on its location
- Topical anaesthetic drops (e.g. tetracaine, oxybuprocaine) may be used temporarily to alleviate pain during examination — these are never prescribed for ongoing home use, as they mask symptoms and delay healing
- Corneal examination using a direct ophthalmoscope: looking for opacity, haze, or thickening
- Fluorescein staining using blue or fluorescent light to identify epithelial defects — key to diagnosis
- Eversion of the eyelid to check for retained foreign bodies
- Checking the pupil (should be round and reactive), conjunctival injection pattern, and overall eye appearance
- Looking for hypopyon (pus in the anterior chamber) — a sinister sign

## Types of Corneal Abrasion or Ulcer

Your doctor will determine which type is present:

### Uncomplicated Corneal Abrasion

- History suggests superficial foreign body; sinister pathologies excluded
- A small foreign body may be visible on examination
- Visual acuity is not usually affected (depends on location and size)
- Typically heals within 24 to 48 hours

### Recurrent Corneal Erosion Syndrome (RCES)

- Patient wakes during the night or early morning with intense, sharp eye pain, watering, and blurred vision
- Vision and corneal examination may be normal if examined later that morning
- Pain recurs daily, always in the early morning
- Symptoms usually settle over 1 to 2 hours
- Usually has an initial organic cause (e.g. fingernail, branch) but can be idiopathic in 30–40% of cases
- Heals within 12 to 24 hours but frequently recurs — it takes many months for the epithelial surface to firmly adhere after a corneal abrasion

### Marginal Keratitis

- Common and can be recurrent
- History of marginal keratitis, ocular rosacea, or staphylococcal blepharitis
- Ulcer develops over days with irritation, milder pain, and normal visual acuity
- Located at the 2, 5, 7, and 10 o'clock positions near the limbus (border of cornea and sclera), separated from it by a thin area of clear cornea
- May not stain with fluorescein initially

### Herpes Simplex Keratitis (Dendritic Ulcer)

- Classic dendritic (branching) ulcer on fluorescein staining
- Photophobia, watering, and reduced visual acuity
- Fluorescein uptake is usually thin and branching but may involve a larger area if presentation is delayed

### Infectious Keratitis

This is a sight-threatening emergency.

- Corneal opacification or opacity with an epithelial defect
- Hypopyon (pus in the anterior chamber)
- Contact lens wearer with a red, painful eye
- May also be associated with corneal trauma

## Management

### Uncomplicated Corneal Abrasion

Most uncomplicated corneal abrasions will heal in 24 to 48 hours, but minor symptoms may continue for up to 2 weeks.

Your doctor may consider:

- **Topical chloramphenicol** 1% eye ointment or 0.5% drops, 4 times a day until symptom-free for 24 hours (this may take 3 to 5 days)
- **Oral analgesia** — paracetamol and NSAIDs are very effective
- **Eye padding** — if sinister pathologies have been excluded but the patient is uncomfortable, your doctor may advise temporarily padding the eye shut. This does not speed healing but may provide relief. You cannot drive with an eye pad in place.
- Advising you not to wear contact lenses until the abrasion has healed and you have been symptom-free for 24 hours
- Follow-up in 24 to 48 hours in general practice, or with an optometrist (who has a slit lamp for examination)

If a larger defect is present or it involves the visual axis, your doctor will discuss with an ophthalmology registrar.

At follow-up, your doctor will check:

- That pain and photophobia are subsiding
- Visual acuity
- That the corneal defect is healing using fluorescein staining
- For signs of corneal infiltrate (which would require same-day specialist assessment)

If symptoms are still present at day 4, further review is needed.

### Recurrent Corneal Erosion Syndrome (RCES)

Your doctor may consider:

- **Lubricating eye ointment containing paraffin** (e.g. Refresh Night Time or Poly-Visc) nightly for 6 weeks. These can cause temporary visual disturbance and are best suited to night-time application; do not use while wearing contact lenses.
- Episodes of corneal erosion usually heal within 12 to 24 hours
- If not improving after 6 weeks of treatment, referral for non-acute ophthalmology assessment
- Optometry assessment for bandage contact lenses may also be considered

### Marginal Keratitis

Your doctor will seek ophthalmology advice before initiating treatment — it can be difficult to distinguish marginal keratitis from infectious keratitis, and specialist input is required.

Your doctor may consider:

- **Chloramphenicol** eye drops or ointment 4 times a day
- Treatment of blepharitis if present

### Herpes Simplex Keratitis

Your doctor will arrange same-day acute ophthalmology assessment if:

- Significantly reduced vision or visible corneal opacity, or
- The patient is a child

For a dendritic ulcer or epithelial keratitis, ophthalmology assessment within a few days will be arranged.

### Infectious Keratitis

This is a sight-threatening emergency. Your doctor will arrange same-day acute ophthalmology assessment immediately.

---

## When to See Your Doctor Urgently

Return for same-day assessment if:

- Pain worsens
- Vision decreases
- Symptoms persist for more than 72 hours
- You develop a new painful red eye while wearing contact lenses
