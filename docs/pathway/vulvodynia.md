# Vulvodynia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of vulvodynia — a chronic pain condition affecting the vulva (external female genitalia).

> This information is for general education. Vulvodynia is a complex diagnosis that requires assessment by a doctor to exclude other causes of vulval pain. Always discuss symptoms and treatment options with your doctor.

---

## Red Flag

**Change in vulval skin** — if you notice new or changing skin abnormalities on the vulva, see your doctor promptly. This needs to be assessed to exclude vulval intraepithelial neoplasia (VIN), a precancerous condition.

---

## What Is Vulvodynia?

Vulvodynia is chronic vulvar discomfort or pain — characterised by burning, stinging, irritation, or rawness of the vulva in the **absence of infection or skin disease**. It is a diagnosis of exclusion (other causes must be ruled out first).

**Types:**
- **Generalised or localised** (pain across the vulva, or confined to a specific area such as the vestibule — called vestibulodynia)
- **Provoked** (pain triggered by touch, sexual intercourse, tampon insertion, or tight clothing), **unprovoked**, or **mixed**

Vulvodynia likely represents a form of neuropathic pain — abnormal nerve activity persisting after an original injury or trigger (which may have been minor or unrecognised). Pelvic floor muscle dysfunction may also contribute.

---

## Common Comorbidities

Vulvodynia may occur alongside:
- Fibromyalgia
- Irritable bowel syndrome
- Bladder pain syndrome (interstitial cystitis): chronic pelvic pain, dysuria, urinary frequency and urgency, in the absence of proven infection

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:
- Take a detailed history of your pain, treatments tried so far, and medical and sexual health history
- Examine the vulva using a cotton swab to map where the pain is — gentle touch may reproduce the pain in localised provoked vulvodynia
- Look for skin abnormalities (e.g., whitening or changes that may indicate lichen sclerosus)
- Take swabs for candida and herpes if there are fissures
- Perform a speculum examination (sometimes a small speculum is used; occasionally vaginismus makes a full examination difficult)
- Take vaginal, vulval, and STI swabs

---

## Management

Vulvodynia usually requires a **multidisciplinary approach** — treatment involves multiple strategies used together. Your doctor will coordinate care and may involve other specialists.

### Skin Abnormalities

If there are skin abnormalities that do not respond to treatment for common conditions (thrush, dermatitis), your doctor may refer you to gynaecology for review or biopsy.

### Support and Education

Understanding the condition is an important part of treatment:
- Vulvodynia is not uncommon (may affect up to 15% of women)
- Causes are often unclear, but symptoms can be managed
- The condition can affect relationships — discussing this openly with your doctor and partner can help

**Patient information resources:** Vulvodynia Support NZ; DermNet NZ – Vulvodynia; International Society for the Study of Vulvovaginal Disease.

### Avoiding Vulval Irritants

Your doctor will recommend avoiding:
- Scented products, dyes, and chemical products (including those in toilet paper and washing powder)
- Tight, synthetic, or uncomfortable clothing
- Abrasive activities (cycling, horse riding)
- Daily use of panty liners / mini pads
- Soap on the vulva — use a fatty cream or cetomacrogol (soap substitute) instead

### Pain Medications

Your doctor may prescribe:
- **Low-dose tricyclic antidepressants** (e.g., amitriptyline or nortriptyline) as first-line for neuropathic vulval pain
- **Gabapentinoids** (gabapentin or pregabalin) if tricyclic antidepressants are not effective

> Gabapentinoids require careful consideration if you take opioids or have a history of substance use — they can enhance opioid effects and carry misuse potential.

Your doctor can also provide guidance on the full range of medications used for chronic neuropathic pain.

### Local Anaesthetic Creams

Topical lidocaine gel (2%) may provide temporary relief. Your doctor will advise you to apply it 15 minutes before intercourse, then wipe it away to reduce numbness for your partner.

### Women's Health Physiotherapy

Pelvic floor physiotherapy from a physiotherapist specialising in women's health can help address muscle dysfunction that may contribute to vulval pain.

### Psychosexual Support

Vulvodynia can significantly affect sexual wellbeing and relationships. Your doctor may refer you to a psychosexual counsellor or sex therapist who can provide:
- Guidance on rebuilding a comfortable sexual relationship
- Strategies for you and your partner
- Advice on sex without penetration if that is appropriate

**Practical tips discussed with your doctor may include:**
- Avoiding all contact with the painful area for several weeks
- Ensuring you are physically and emotionally ready before intercourse
- Prioritising foreplay and adequate lubrication before intercourse
- Using sexual lubricants without alcohol or preservatives
- Water-based lubricants (e.g., Sylk, widely available in supermarkets) are generally well tolerated

### Chronic Pain Management

If targeted treatments are not effective, your doctor may manage vulvodynia as a chronic pain condition following a chronic pain management pathway.

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you to:
- **Gynaecology:** if skin abnormalities persist or biopsy is needed; if diagnosis is uncertain or initial treatments have failed
- **Women's health physiotherapy:** for pelvic floor assessment and treatment
- **Psychosexual counselling:** for support with sexual wellbeing and relationship impacts
