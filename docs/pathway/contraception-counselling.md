# Contraception Counselling

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers contraception counselling — helping patients choose the most appropriate contraceptive method based on their individual circumstances, preferences, and medical history.

---

## Key Principle

Long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs — intrauterine contraception and the contraceptive implant) are recommended as a first-line contraceptive choice for all eligible patients due to their excellent efficacy and safety. No method should feel pressured — the goal is informed, shared decision-making.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a thorough history covering:

**Personal preferences and life stage**
- Plans for future pregnancy (important — e.g., a single Depo-Provera injection may delay return to fertility for up to 12 months and cannot be reversed once given)
- Relationship status, job, and study plans
- Convenience preference: daily versus long-acting "fit and forget"
- Willingness to cope with non-scheduled bleeding or absence of periods
- Desire to manipulate cycles
- Previous contraceptive experience, including what has worked or caused side effects

**Gynaecological and obstetric history**
- Previous pregnancies and outcomes, including miscarriage
- Menstrual history and bleeding patterns
- Previous gynaecological procedures or known conditions (e.g., bicornuate uterus)
- Symptoms of sexually transmitted infections (STIs)

**Related conditions that may influence choice**
- Acne, polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), heavy periods, painful periods (dysmenorrhoea)
- Menstrual migraine, premenstrual syndrome (PMS), endometriosis

**Medical conditions that may affect safety**
Some conditions mean certain methods pose an unacceptable health risk. These include:
- BMI 35 or greater
- Personal or family history of venous thromboembolism (VTE), or known thrombogenic mutations
- Cardiovascular disease or multiple cardiovascular risk factors (hypertension, dyslipidaemia, diabetes, smoking)
- Current or previous breast or endometrial cancer, or gene mutations associated with breast cancer (BRCA1, BRCA2)
- Migraine with aura
- Stroke or transient ischaemic attack (TIA)
- Hepatobiliary disease, HIV, pelvic tuberculosis
- Diabetes with nephropathy, retinopathy, or neuropathy
- Positive antiphospholipid antibodies (including with SLE)
- Unexplained vaginal bleeding requiring investigation
- Undiagnosed breast symptoms or mass

**Medications that may affect contraceptive efficacy**
Several drugs reduce the effectiveness of hormonal contraception by inducing liver enzymes (CYP 3A4 and CYP 3A5). These include:
- Anti-seizure medication: carbamazepine, phenytoin, oxcarbazepine, phenobarbital, primidone, rufinamide, eslicarbazepine, topiramate
- Antibiotics: rifampicin, rifabutin
- Antiretrovirals: nevirapine, nelfinavir, tipranavir/ritonavir, efavirenz, etravirine
- Other: St John's wort, bosentan, modafinil, aprepitant, sugammadex

Note: it takes 28 days for the effect of enzyme-inducing drugs to dissipate, even after a single dose. These drugs affect the combined oral contraceptive pill (COC), progestogen-only pill (POP), and contraceptive implant.

Lamotrigine interactions:
- COCs increase the metabolism of lamotrigine — this raises seizure risk during active pills and lamotrigine toxicity in the pill-free interval
- POPs may increase lamotrigine levels and adverse effects

Griseofulvin (antifungal): may affect efficacy of COC, POP, and contraceptive implant during use and for 28 days after stopping.

**STI risk factors**
- Multiple sexual contacts, recent change in partner, a partner with an STI
- Sexually active patients younger than 30 years
- Men who have sex with men (MSM)

**Physical examination**
- Blood pressure (COC is contraindicated if BP ≥160/100 mmHg on office readings; cardiovascular risks generally outweigh benefits if BP is 140–159/90–99 mmHg)
- Weight and BMI
- Abdominal or pelvic examination if indicated by symptoms

**Investigations**
- Cervical screening — your doctor will check whether this is up to date
- STI testing if indicated (genital symptoms or elevated risk)
- Pregnancy should be reasonably excluded before starting a new method

## Contraceptive Methods

Your doctor will discuss the features of each method, including efficacy, how it works, duration of action, common side effects, and reversibility.

### Long-Acting Reversible Contraceptives (LARCs) — First-Line

**Intrauterine Contraception**
- Copper intrauterine device (Cu-IUD): hormone-free, immediately effective after insertion, also effective as emergency contraception
- Levonorgestrel intrauterine system (LNG-IUS): releases a small amount of progestogen locally; reduces or stops periods in many patients

**Contraceptive Implant**
- A small rod inserted under the skin of the upper arm (commonly known as "the rod" or "the bar")
- Lasts up to 3–5 years; quickly reversible on removal
- Note: there is a small reduction in efficacy with increasing body weight for Jadelle implants — your doctor may discuss early replacement if this is a concern

### Hormonal Methods — Short-Acting

**Combined Oral Contraceptive Pill (COC)**
- Contains oestrogen and progestogen; taken daily
- Not suitable if BMI ≥35, known hypertension, migraine with aura, or cardiovascular risk factors
- Interacts with lamotrigine (see above)

**Progestogen-Only Pill (POP)**
- Progestogen only; taken daily
- Suitable for patients who cannot use oestrogen
- May be used with caution in patients on lamotrigine (monitoring for side effects recommended)

**Contraceptive Injection (Depot Medroxyprogesterone Acetate — DMPA / Depo-Provera)**
- Given every 12–14 weeks
- May delay return to fertility by up to 12 months after stopping — important for patients planning pregnancy soon

### Barrier Methods

**Male (external) condoms**
- 82% effective with typical use; 98% effective when used consistently and correctly
- Protect against STIs — recommended in addition to other methods for patients at elevated STI risk
- Latex-free options available for patients with latex allergy

**Female (internal) condoms**
- 79% effective with typical use; 95% effective when used consistently and correctly

**Diaphragms**
- Require professional fitting; generally not recommended due to limited availability and high typical-use failure rate

### Fertility Awareness Methods

- Pregnancy rates with typical use range from 2% to 23% depending on the method
- Not recommended as sole contraception if a pregnancy would pose significant health risk, or if using drugs with teratogenic effects
- Relying on fertility awareness alone is not recommended until at least 3 regular cycles have been established after stopping hormonal contraception
- The sympto-thermal method, taught by a trained practitioner, is the most effective fertility awareness approach
- Condoms should be used concurrently to prevent STIs

### Permanent Contraception

- Surgical options (vasectomy, tubal ligation) for patients who are certain they do not want future pregnancies

## Special Circumstances

### Adolescents (under 16)
- All contraceptive methods including IUDs are suitable
- Condom use is especially important to prevent STIs and unintended pregnancy
- Patients younger than 16 can consent to their own contraceptive treatment

### Transgender Patients
- All forms of contraception are available to transgender men and non-binary people who have a uterus
- Testosterone therapy does not provide reliable contraception and is contraindicated in pregnancy
- Progestogen-based methods are suitable for transmasculine individuals
- A short course of vaginal oestrogen may be offered to facilitate more comfortable IUD insertion

### Epilepsy
- Patients on enzyme-inducing anti-seizure medications (ASMs) — carbamazepine, oxcarbazepine, phenobarbital, phenytoin, primidone, rufinamide, topiramate — should use an IUD (copper or LNG-IUS) as the preferred method, as this is not affected by enzyme induction
- Two effective forms of contraception including condoms are recommended for all patients on ASMs
- Some ASMs (sodium valproate, topiramate, carbamazepine, phenytoin, and others) may cause fetal anticonvulsant syndrome (FACS) if taken in pregnancy
- Folic acid 5 mg daily is recommended for patients of childbearing age on ASMs, even if not planning pregnancy
- Pre-conception planning discussions should occur at least 6–12 months before trying for pregnancy

For emergency contraception in patients on enzyme-inducing ASMs: the copper IUD is the only method not affected. If declined or unavailable, levonorgestrel 3 mg (double dose, unapproved) may be considered.

### Overweight Patients (BMI ≥35)
- COC is not recommended due to elevated VTE risk
- Oral levonorgestrel emergency contraception may be less effective if BMI >26 or weight >70 kg — copper IUD is preferred for emergency contraception

### Pre-Conception Planning
- LARCs have no long-term effect on fertility after removal
- DMPA injection may delay return to fertility by up to 12 months
- An interval of less than 12 months between birth and conceiving again is associated with increased risk of preterm birth and low birth weight

### After Pregnancy
- All methods except the COC can be started immediately after birth
- If contraception is started 21 or more days after birth (or 5 or more days after abortion, ectopic pregnancy, or miscarriage), additional precautions are needed
- An interval of less than 12 months between birth and conceiving again is associated with increased risk of preterm birth and low birth weight

### Perimenopause
- Contraception should be continued for 1 year after the last menstrual period (if aged 50 or older), or 2 years (if younger than 50)

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following:

- **LARC (IUD or implant):** First-line for most eligible patients — most effective, no daily action required
- **COC:** Suitable for patients without cardiovascular risk factors, BMI under 35, no migraine with aura — discuss drug interactions (lamotrigine, enzyme-inducing drugs)
- **POP:** Preferred when oestrogen is contraindicated; use with caution in patients on lamotrigine
- **DMPA injection:** Discuss fertility delay; suitable when other methods are not preferred
- **Barrier methods (condoms):** Recommended in addition to other methods for STI protection
- **Fertility awareness:** Discuss limitations and suitability; offer emergency contraceptive supply
- **HPV vaccination:** Offered if relevant

For patients on medications that interact with contraceptives, your doctor may seek advice from the prescribing specialist or another relevant specialist.
