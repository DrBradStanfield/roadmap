# Iritis (Anterior Uveitis)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and initial management of iritis (anterior uveitis) — inflammation of the iris that requires prompt specialist review.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

See your doctor urgently if you have:

- A unilateral (one-sided), painful, red eye with decreased vision and significant photophobia (light sensitivity)

---

## Background

Iritis, also known as anterior uveitis, is inflammation of the iris. It can occur in isolation or may be associated with:

- Underlying systemic autoimmune, inflammatory, and connective tissue disorders — e.g., ankylosing spondylitis, rheumatoid arthritis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), and inflammatory bowel disease
- Granulomatous conditions — e.g., sarcoidosis, tuberculosis
- Infections — e.g., HIV, Lyme disease, herpes

Complications can include glaucoma, cataract, and macular oedema.

**Recurrence:** About 20% of people who have a first episode of uncomplicated iritis will have a recurrence in their lifetime — sometimes up to 20 years later. If there have been 2 recurrences, the chance of another episode rises to about 35%. Recurrence risk is much higher in people with ankylosing spondylitis or who are HLA-B27 positive.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Onset and duration of a unilateral red eye with deep aching pain
- Sensitivity to light (photophobia)
- Whether you have had iritis before — recurrence is common, and can occur even without a red eye
- Presence of floaters — these are more prominent in bilateral uveitis, which tends to have a more insidious onset with little pain

Physical examination typically includes:

- Looking for an acutely inflamed red eye with redness around the edge of the cornea (limbus)
- Checking the pupil — in iritis it is often very small, poorly reactive to light, and sometimes distorted (posterior synechiae)
- Looking for a hypopyon (collection of inflammatory cells at the bottom of the anterior chamber) or fibrin webs in severe cases
- Assessing for discharge (typically absent) and checking whether the cornea is clear or cloudy
- Measuring visual acuity in each eye

Your doctor will also exclude more serious conditions, including:

- **Endophthalmitis** — marked decrease in acuity, recent eye surgery or injections, history of IV drug use or sepsis, significant floaters, absent red reflex
- **Posterior uveitis** — worsening floaters, marked decrease in acuity, poor red reflex, and poor view to the fundus

---

## Management

Your doctor will manage iritis as follows:

- **First episode of acute iritis:** Urgent ophthalmology assessment is arranged. This is not something that is treated at home without specialist involvement.
- **Recurrent iritis:** Your doctor may seek ophthalmology advice before arranging referral.
- **If already under specialist care:** Your doctor may also contact that specialist for guidance.

Your doctor may consider the following treatments, typically under specialist supervision:

- **Topical steroids** — usually prednisolone eye drops (Pred Forte), tapered over several weeks
- **Cycloplegic (dilating) eye drops** — usually cyclopentolate hydrochloride 1%, to prevent the iris from sticking to the lens (posterior synechiae)

If visual acuity is normal or some features of iritis are absent, your doctor may arrange a non-urgent ophthalmology assessment rather than an emergency review.

In rare circumstances, if a patient has had iritis before and urgent travel to a specialist is not feasible, your doctor may arrange a short-term treatment plan by phone until specialist review can occur.
