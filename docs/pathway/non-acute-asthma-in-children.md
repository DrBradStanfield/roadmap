# Non-acute Asthma in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway applies to children aged 5 to 11 years with recurrent wheeze or confirmed asthma outside of an acute episode.

---

## Red Flags — See Your Doctor Promptly

The following symptoms may suggest a diagnosis other than asthma and need prompt medical review:

- Frequent wet or productive cough (may suggest bronchiectasis)
- Faltering growth or failure to thrive (may indicate another chronic condition)

---

## Background

Asthma is a chronic respiratory condition characterised by reversible narrowing of the small airways. The main symptom is wheeze. Between acute episodes, the chest examination is usually normal — if it is not, another diagnosis should be considered.

Asthma severity is determined by the treatment step needed to keep symptoms controlled. It is a dynamic condition requiring regular review, even when well controlled.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your child by:

### Symptoms to look for

Asthma is more likely if your child has **more than one** of:
- Expiratory wheeze (the most specific symptom)
- Breathlessness
- Chest tightness
- Cough (note: isolated cough alone is unlikely to be asthma)

Symptoms are more likely to be asthma if they:
- Are worse at night or early morning
- Are triggered by exercise, cold air, allergens, viral infections, irritants, or stress
- Are recurrent or seasonal
- Improve with a reliever inhaler (bronchodilator)

### Other history

Your doctor will also ask about:
- History of eczema, hay fever, or allergies (atopy)
- Family history of asthma
- Exposure to cigarette smoke or vaping in the home
- Persistent ear, nose, or sinus infections (which may suggest an alternative diagnosis)

### Risk factors for severe asthma

Your doctor will ask about factors that increase the risk of serious asthma, including:
- Poor asthma control or poor inhaler technique
- Under-use of preventer inhaler (inhaled corticosteroid)
- Hospital or emergency department visit for asthma in the past year
- Using more than one reliever inhaler per month
- History of sudden severe attacks, or intensive care admission
- Need for frequent oral steroids (more than 14 days in a 12-month period)
- Major psychosocial difficulties or severe food allergy
- Exposure to tobacco smoke, cold or damp housing, or financial hardship

### Examination

Your doctor will:
- Check growth and plot it on a growth chart
- Look for signs of allergic rhinitis, nasal polyps, or eczema
- Examine the hands for finger clubbing
- Examine the chest (during wheeze-free periods, the chest is usually normal)

### Investigations

- **Spirometry** may be arranged for children aged 6 years and over. A 12% improvement in lung function after a bronchodilator strongly suggests asthma. A normal result does not rule it out.
- **Chest X-ray** is only needed if an alternative diagnosis is suspected.

### Alternative diagnoses to consider

Your doctor will consider other explanations for symptoms, including:
- Constant wheeze or wheeze that never varies — suggests fixed airway obstruction
- Inspiratory stridor — suggests upper airway obstruction
- Frequent wet or productive cough — may indicate bronchiectasis
- Wheeze linked to feeding, vomiting, or lying down — may suggest aspiration
- Symptoms that have never improved with a reliever inhaler
- Cough with choking during exercise in older children — consider laryngeal disorders
- Faltering growth or finger clubbing — may indicate cystic fibrosis, bronchiectasis, heart disease, immunodeficiency, or other serious conditions

## Trial of Therapy (Confirming the Diagnosis)

If asthma is likely, your doctor may arrange an 8-week trial of treatment to confirm the diagnosis and assess response.

### Inhaler devices

Your doctor will select an age-appropriate device:

| Inhaler device | 5–7 years | 8–11 years |
|---|---|---|
| pMDI + spacer (no mask) | Preferred | Preferred |
| Dry powder device (e.g., Turbuhaler, Accuhaler) | Possible | Yes |
| Breath-activated device | Possible | Yes |

A spacer is always preferred with a pressurised metered dose inhaler (pMDI). Replace spacers annually (or every 6 months with regular use).

### Trial medications

Your doctor may prescribe:

**Reliever (as needed):** Salbutamol 1–2 puffs via pMDI and spacer for mild symptoms. For severe or worsening symptoms, seek urgent medical review.

**Preventer (low-dose inhaled corticosteroid, twice daily):** Your doctor may consider one of:
- Beclometasone dipropionate (Beclazone pMDI) 100 micrograms twice daily
- Beclometasone dipropionate ultrafine (Qvar pMDI) 50 micrograms twice daily
- Budesonide (Pulmicort dry powder device) 100 micrograms twice daily
- Fluticasone propionate (Flixotide pMDI or dry powder device) 50 micrograms twice daily

A review is usually scheduled at 8 weeks. Seek earlier assessment if symptoms worsen.

## Management

### Goals of asthma control

Asthma is well controlled when your child has:
- No acute flare-ups
- No night waking due to wheeze, cough, or breathlessness
- Reliever inhaler needed no more than twice per week
- No limitation of normal activity

### Stepwise medication management

Your doctor will use a stepwise approach — adding or removing treatment based on symptom control:

| Step | Maintenance treatment | As-needed reliever |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | None | Salbutamol (SABA) |
| 2 | Low-dose inhaled corticosteroid (ICS) | Salbutamol |
| 3 | Low-dose ICS + long-acting beta agonist (LABA) | Salbutamol |
| 4 | Standard-dose ICS/LABA; consider adding montelukast | Salbutamol |
| 5 | Standard-dose ICS/LABA + montelukast; specialist referral | Salbutamol |

- Step **up** one step if symptoms are not controlled after 1–2 months
- Step **down** one step when symptoms have been well controlled for 3 months
- If control is poor at any step, your doctor will check inhaler technique and adherence first

**Step 1 — Reliever only (salbutamol as needed)**

Your doctor will confirm a trial of inhaled corticosteroid (preventer) has been offered and considered. If your child is using a reliever frequently, a preventer is likely needed.

**Step 2 — Low-dose ICS (preventer) + salbutamol as needed**

Your doctor may prescribe a preventer inhaler (see options above) if your child has frequent or severe symptoms, or has needed more than three reliever prescriptions per year.

Your doctor may also consider **montelukast** as an alternative (especially if your child also has hay fever, exercise-induced asthma, obstructive sleep apnoea, or has difficulty using inhalers regularly):
- Montelukast is a once-daily chewable tablet
- Dose: 5 mg once daily (children over 5 years)
- The most common side-effect is headache
- **Important:** Montelukast may cause behaviour changes, sleep disturbance, and in older children, suicidal thoughts or self-harm. Stop the medication and seek medical review immediately if these occur. Stop if there is no improvement after 6 weeks.

**Step 3 — Low-dose ICS/LABA combination + salbutamol as needed**

Your doctor may consider one of the following combination inhalers (preventer + long-acting bronchodilator):
- Fluticasone 50 mcg + salmeterol 25 mcg (Seretide 50/25 pMDI) — 1 inhalation twice daily
- Budesonide 100 mcg + formoterol 6 mcg (Symbicort 100/6 dry powder) — 1 inhalation twice daily
- Budesonide 100 mcg + formoterol 6 mcg (Vannair 100/6 pMDI) — 1 inhalation twice daily (if aged over 6)

Note: LABAs must never be prescribed without an ICS. Symbicort SMART therapy in children under 12 requires a paediatric specialist recommendation.

**Step 4 — Standard-dose ICS/LABA + consider montelukast**

Your doctor may increase the ICS/LABA to standard doses and consider adding montelukast (see above for precautions). Specialist advice may be sought.

Standard-dose options your doctor may consider:
- Seretide 50/25 pMDI: 2 inhalations twice daily, reducing to 1 when controlled
- Symbicort 100/6 dry powder: 2 inhalations twice daily, reducing when controlled
- Symbicort 200/6 dry powder: 1 inhalation twice daily, reducing when controlled
- Vannair 100/6 pMDI: 2 inhalations twice daily, reducing when controlled

**Step 5 — Standard-dose ICS/LABA + montelukast + specialist referral**

At this step, your doctor will refer your child to a paediatric specialist. While awaiting review, your doctor may consider temporarily increasing to high-dose ICS/LABA.

### Supporting good asthma control

Your doctor will also:

**Address environmental factors:** Asthma can be worsened by cold, damp, or mouldy housing; unflued gas heaters; and house dust mite exposure. If housing is a concern, referral to support services may be offered.

**Address household smoke and vaping:** Exposure to cigarette smoke or vaping (inside, outside, or in the car) worsens asthma. Complete cessation by household members is recommended. Smoking cessation support can be arranged.

**Encourage physical activity:** Exercise-induced asthma can be managed. Regular physical activity is encouraged.

**Diet:** Dietary changes are unlikely to improve asthma unless a specific food allergy has been confirmed.

**Inhaler technique:** Your doctor or nurse will check and teach inhaler technique at every visit. Make sure your child has their own spacer (replace at least annually).

**Annual vaccinations:** Influenza vaccine is recommended for children with asthma on inhaled steroids.

### Asthma Action Plan

Your doctor should create a personalised **Child Asthma Action Plan** for your child. This tells caregivers what to do at each level of symptoms — including when to seek emergency help. All caregivers should have a copy.

### Follow-up schedule

Regular reviews are important, even when asthma is well controlled. Your doctor will typically plan:
- A review every 3 months
- A review 6 weeks after any change in treatment
- A review after any acute episode requiring medical help
- An annual growth check (height and weight)

---

## When to Seek Specialist Review

Your doctor will consider referring your child to a paediatric specialist if:
- Poor asthma control despite 1 month of good compliance with Step 4 treatment
- Your child requires Step 5 treatment
- There are significant risk factors for severe asthma or death from asthma at Step 4
