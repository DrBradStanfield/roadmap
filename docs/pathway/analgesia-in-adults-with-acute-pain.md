# Analgesia in Adults with Acute Pain

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to managing acute pain in adults — from pain assessment through to step-up analgesia, including opioid safety and neuropathic adjuvants.

> **Note:** Medication prescribing and pain management decisions should always be made by your doctor. This information explains the clinical approach so you can understand what your doctor is considering.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a structured pain history covering:

**Description of the pain:**
- Location and any radiation
- Onset and circumstances associated with it
- Characteristics (e.g., sharp, throbbing, burning) — burning, shooting, stabbing, or allodynia (pain from gentle touch) suggest neuropathic pain
- Duration (continuous vs intermittent)
- Factors that exacerbate or relieve it (movement, eating, medications)
- Associated symptoms (e.g., nausea)

**Pain score:**

| Rating | Pain level |
|--------|-----------|
| 0 | No pain |
| 1–3 | Mild (nagging, annoying, interferes little with daily activities) |
| 4–6 | Moderate (interferes slightly with daily activities) |
| 7–10 | Severe (disabling, unable to perform daily activities) |

Your doctor will also:
- Assess functional impairment (effect on activity, work, sleep)
- Consider factors that affect treatment response — beliefs about pain, anxiety or mood disorders, expectations, coping style
- Examine you, focusing on the site and likely cause
- Consider drug-seeking behaviour in relevant presentations (checking dispensing history)

## Management

### Step-Up Analgesia (WHO Pain Ladder Approach)

Your doctor uses a step-up approach — each step adds an analgesic to the previous one.

---

#### Step 1 — Mild to Moderate Pain (score 1–3): Paracetamol + NSAIDs

**Paracetamol:**
- 1000 mg every 4–6 hours, maximum 4 g per day
- Reduced dose (maximum 2–3 g/day) if elderly, frail, low body weight, or liver disease

**NSAIDs — relative contraindications include:**
- Advanced age
- Bleeding disorders
- Renal dysfunction
- Upper gastrointestinal dysfunction
- Asthma (may cause bronchospasm or angioedema)
- Fractures (may impair bone healing)
- Bowel surgery (increases risk of anastomotic leak)

**NSAID options:**
- **Celecoxib** (selective COX-2 inhibitor) — best first-line NSAID. Lower GI side-effect risk, does not cause bronchospasm or increase bleeding risk. 100–200 mg every 12 hours orally.
- **Ibuprofen** — 400–600 mg every 6–8 hours orally, maximum 2400 mg/day
- **Diclofenac** — up to 150 mg/day in divided doses
- **Naproxen** — up to 1000 mg/day in divided doses
- Consider adding a proton pump inhibitor to reduce NSAID-associated ulcer risk

---

#### Step 2 — Moderate Pain (score 4–6): Weak Opioids

**Tramadol:**
- Immediate release 50 mg every 4 hours; can increase to 100 mg if tolerated
- Side-effects: nausea, vomiting, confusion (elderly), serotonin toxicity (rare at normal doses)
- Do not use with a history of seizures or MAOI use within 15 days
- Note: morphine is often a better choice for moderate to severe pain

**Codeine:**
- 15–60 mg orally every 4–6 hours; maximum 240 mg/day
- Slow to act, low analgesic potency — less useful for severe pain
- Around 10% of Caucasian patients and 1% of Asian and Polynesian patients are poor metabolisers (no benefit); others are rapid metabolisers (greater effect)
- May be less effective if the patient takes SSRIs
- Constipation is very common — consider regular laxatives

---

#### Step 3 — Severe Pain (score 7–10): Strong Opioids

**Oral opioid equivalences:** morphine 10 mg = codeine 100 mg = tramadol 100 mg = oxycodone 5–7.5 mg

**Morphine** (first-line strong opioid):
- Preferred unless contraindicated (e.g. renal impairment, previous intolerance)
- IV is the preferred route: 1–2 mg every 5 minutes titrated to pain; increase to 5 mg every 5 minutes if ineffective; or 0.02 mg/kg over 1 minute IV. Elderly: start 0.5–1 mg every 5 minutes
- Subcutaneous or intramuscular: 0.15 mg/kg every 3–6 hours (beware depot formation if hypotensive)

**Oxycodone** (alternative if morphine contraindicated):
- 5–10 mg orally every 4 hours
- Not safer or less dependence-forming than morphine — only use if morphine not tolerated

**Pethidine** is no longer first-line due to seizure risk and addiction potential.

---

### Opioid Safety — Naloxone

Patients given parenteral opioids must be closely observed. Naloxone (opioid antagonist) should be available.

**Naloxone use:**
- Dilute one ampoule (400 micrograms) with 9 mL saline → each mL = 40 micrograms
- Excessively sedated but breathing: 1 microgram/kg IV/IM/subcutaneous; repeat every 1–2 minutes, up to 5 doses
- Apnoeic and unrousable: 400 micrograms (1 ampoule); if no response after 1 minute give 800 micrograms; if still no response repeat 800 micrograms; then 2 mg (up to 4 mg in serious poisoning)
- Observe for 4–6 hours after naloxone if toxicity was from a short-acting opioid

**Hospital assessment is needed if:**
- Repeat doses of naloxone are required
- Opioid toxicity was from a long-acting opioid or methadone
- High doses of naloxone needed (> 2 mg)

Note: naloxone reverses all opioid effects including analgesia — pain control may be difficult to re-establish.

---

### Antiemetics

- Not routinely given with opioids unless nausea or vomiting is present
- Ondansetron is preferred over metoclopramide for abdominal pain (metoclopramide is prokinetic)

---

### Opioid-Induced Constipation

Manage proactively — regular laxatives are often needed.

---

### Entonox (Nitrous Oxide 50% / Oxygen 50%)

For moderate to severe pain while awaiting definitive analgesia. Alters pain perception rather than providing direct analgesia.

**Particularly useful for:** fractures, dislocations, traumatic wounds, short-term severe pain (e.g., wound dressings)

**Avoid if:** first trimester of pregnancy, head injury, pneumothorax

---

### Non-Pharmacological Treatments

Consider alongside medications: exercise, physiotherapy, massage, heat application.

---

### Neuropathic Pain Adjuvants

For neuropathic or mixed pain. Note: these take 2–3 weeks to have effect — not useful in the very acute setting.

**Tricyclics:**
- **Nortriptyline** — 10–25 mg (maximum 75 mg) at night, 1 hour before bed
- **Amitriptyline** — 5–10 mg (maximum 75 mg) at night, 1 hour before bed (more sedating — useful when sleep is also impaired)
- Start low, increase weekly
- Stop after 1 month if no improvement; consider gabapentinoid

**Gabapentinoids** — caution in patients on opioids or with substance use history (enhanced opioid effect, overdose risk, misuse potential; pregabalin carries higher misuse risk):
- Start low, increase if partial response
- Stop if no improvement after 1 month
- Consider switching back to tricyclic if gabapentinoid fails

- **Gabapentin:** start 300 mg at night, titrate to 600 mg three times daily (start 100 mg if elderly)
- **Pregabalin:** start 75 mg at night (25 mg if elderly/frail), titrate to maximum 300 mg twice daily

---

### Exit Strategy — Weaning (Reverse Pain Ladder)

An exit plan should be in place from the start. Analgesic requirements reduce over time; wean as soon as appropriate:

1. Wean strong opioids (morphine, oxycodone)
2. Wean weak opioids (tramadol, codeine)
3. Wean paracetamol and NSAIDs

Your doctor will arrange follow-up to review efficacy, side-effects, and weaning progress.

---

## When to Seek Further Help

Your doctor will consider escalating care or seeking specialist advice if:

- Severe pain requiring hospital assessment and admission
- Pain is not adequately controlled or improving as expected
- High or repeated doses of naloxone have been needed for opioid toxicity
- Opioid toxicity occurred with a long-acting opioid or methadone
