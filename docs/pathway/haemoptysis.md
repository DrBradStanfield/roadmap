# Haemoptysis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of haemoptysis (coughing up blood) in adults.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Your doctor will arrange urgent assessment if any of the following are present:

- Significant haemoptysis: > 20 mL (1 tablespoon) in a single episode, or 5 mL (1 teaspoon) repeatedly
- Life-threatening haemoptysis: ≥ 150 mL in 24 hours, or ≥ 100 mL/hour
- Signs of sepsis: fever, low oxygen levels, altered consciousness, low blood pressure, rapid heart rate
- Acute breathlessness and/or chest pain
- Haematuria (blood in urine) associated with haemoptysis

**If any of these are present, your doctor will not wait for community investigations — urgent hospital assessment is needed.**

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess whether the haemoptysis is minor, significant, or life-threatening, then look for the underlying cause.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- The haemoptysis — when it started, how often, how much blood, what it looks like (frank blood, pink frothy sputum, or blood-streaked sputum)
- Cough and sputum (colour, volume, frequency)
- Chest pain
- Constitutional symptoms: fever, malaise, weight loss, poor appetite
- Blood in urine (haematuria) — if present alongside haemoptysis, raises concern for rare but serious conditions (Goodpasture syndrome, ANCA-associated vasculitis)
- Bleeding from other sites: nosebleeds, gum bleeding, blood in stools, easy bruising, heavy periods, excessive bleeding after procedures
- Past medical history: respiratory conditions, previous blood clots (DVT or PE), any cancer history
- Medications: anticoagulants (blood thinners), antiplatelets (e.g., aspirin, clopidogrel)
- Social history: smoking, travel or migration history, occupational exposures (asbestos, diesel exhaust)

### Examination

Your doctor will check:

- Vital signs: respiratory rate, oxygen saturation, blood pressure, heart rate, temperature, level of consciousness
- General appearance: weight loss, skin changes (telangiectasia), finger clubbing, lymph node swelling
- Signs of bleeding or bruising suggesting a clotting problem
- Nose and throat for upper airway bleeding sources
- Heart and lung examination

### Investigations

For most patients, your doctor will arrange:

- **Chest X-ray** — key initial investigation
- **Blood tests**: full blood count, electrolytes, CRP, coagulation studies
- **D-dimer** if pulmonary embolism is suspected
- **ECG** if chest pain is present
- Further tests based on clinical suspicion: sputum culture, sputum for acid-fast bacilli (TB testing), sputum cytology
- **Urine dipstick** for blood; if positive, urine sample for red cell casts

## Possible Causes

### Minor haemoptysis (streaks or < 1 teaspoon)

More commonly caused by:

- **Lower respiratory tract infection** (bronchitis, pneumonia, COPD exacerbation) — usually with cough and coloured sputum
- **Pulmonary embolism (blood clot in the lung)** — haemoptysis is rarely the only symptom; breathlessness and/or pleuritic chest pain are usually also present
- **Anticoagulant or antiplatelet medication**
- **Cardiac causes** (heart failure, mitral valve disease) — typically produces pink frothy sputum rather than clots

### Significant or life-threatening haemoptysis

More commonly associated with:

- **Lung cancer** — especially with risk factors (smoking, occupational exposures, older age, family history) or persistent symptoms > 3 weeks
- **Tuberculosis** — especially in people from high-prevalence countries or known TB contacts
- **Bronchiectasis** — in those with a history of chronic productive cough
- **Lung abscess**

### Features raising suspicion of lung cancer

Your doctor will be particularly alert to lung cancer if you have:

- Unexplained haemoptysis
- Unexplained, persistent symptoms lasting > 3 weeks (or less if risk factors present): new or changed cough, chest/shoulder pain, breathlessness, hoarseness, unexplained weight loss, unresolved chest infection, abnormal chest signs, finger clubbing, swollen lymph nodes in the neck, signs of spread
- Imaging showing: new pleural effusion, pleural mass, or slowly resolving consolidation
- Persistent or unexplained haemoptysis in patients aged > 40 years

## Management

### Urgent referral

Your doctor will arrange **urgent hospital respiratory assessment** if:

- Any red flag is present (significant/life-threatening haemoptysis, sepsis, acute breathlessness/chest pain, haematuria)
- Haemoptysis is associated with new kidney impairment and haematuria (concern for pulmonary-renal syndrome)
- Chest X-ray shows diffuse changes suggesting pulmonary haemorrhage

### Treating an identified cause

If there is clear evidence of a treatable cause (e.g., chest infection, pneumonia, over-anticoagulation), your doctor will:

- Treat the underlying condition
- Reassess within 4 weeks

Related conditions managed via their own pathways: lung cancer, pulmonary embolism, bronchiectasis, tuberculosis, COPD, heart failure.

### Non-urgent respiratory specialist referral

Your doctor will refer for **non-urgent respiratory assessment** if:

- No clear treatable cause is found
- Haemoptysis persists for more than 2–3 weeks
- Haemoptysis continues despite treatment
- There is high clinical suspicion of lung cancer (even if the chest X-ray is normal)

If lung cancer is strongly suspected, your doctor will mark the referral as **urgent** and note "high suspicion of cancer."

---

## What to Expect

- If your haemoptysis is minor and due to a known cause (e.g., a chest infection), it is likely to resolve with treatment. Follow up with your doctor within 4 weeks.
- If no clear cause is found, or symptoms persist, you will be referred to a respiratory specialist.
- If there are any urgent features, you may be referred directly to hospital for same-day assessment.
