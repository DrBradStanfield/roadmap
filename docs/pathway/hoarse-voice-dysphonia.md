# Hoarse Voice (Dysphonia)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: August 2016.*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of a hoarse voice (dysphonia). Reflux, postnasal drip, frequent throat clearing, smoking and stress can all play a part.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department if you have:**
- Stridor (noisy, high-pitched breathing).

**See your doctor the same day if you have any of these. Your doctor will arrange an urgent ear, nose and throat (ENT) assessment:**
- Coughing up blood (haemoptysis).
- Constant hoarseness for more than 4 weeks.
- Trouble swallowing solid food (dysphagia to solids).
- Fluids going down the wrong way (aspiration of fluids).
- Coughing when drinking.
- Ear pain that comes from the throat (referred otalgia).
- A lump in the side of the neck.

The ENT assessment may include a laryngoscopy (a camera look at the voice box), with or without videostroboscopy.

---

## Background

Hoarseness is described by how much of the voice is lost and whether it comes and goes:
- **Constant hoarseness:** the voice never fully recovers. It may still vary in severity.
- **Intermittent hoarseness:** the voice fully recovers at times.
- **Total loss of voice:** you can only whisper, with no recognisable voice.

**Stress** can cause hoarseness through muscle tension. Stressors that cause or add to hoarseness include:
- Voice overuse at work (for example teachers, sports coaches and salespeople).
- Psychological tension.

---

## Assessment

### History

Your doctor may ask about:
- How long you have been hoarse, and whether the hoarseness is total or partial, intermittent or constant.
- Symptoms of reflux or postnasal drip, and frequent throat clearing.
- Smoking.
- Stressors, including voice overuse and psychological tension.

### Examination

- Your doctor may examine your chest and neck, checking for lumps.

### Investigations

If you need a specialist referral, your doctor may consider:
- **If there is a lump you can feel:**
  - In the middle of the neck: an ultrasound.
  - In the side of the neck: a fine needle aspiration (FNA), or a referral.
- **Thyroid function tests (TFT).**
- **Chest X-ray,** looking for aspiration pneumonia, lung cancer or masses in the middle of the chest (mediastinal masses).
- Further tests as for any neck lump.

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider:
1. **Stopping smoking,** if relevant. If you smoke, your doctor may consider a chest X-ray.
2. **A trial of a proton pump inhibitor (PPI)** such as omeprazole, with or without Gaviscon, for 6 weeks. This applies if the hoarseness is intermittent and you have:
   - postnasal drip or frequent throat clearing, or
   - symptoms of reflux.
3. **Managing any stressors** that may be causing or adding to the hoarseness.
4. **Further assessment** if symptoms show no improvement after 4 weeks, or have not resolved after 8 weeks (see below).

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you:
- **For an urgent ENT assessment** if there are any red flags (see above).
- **For a non-urgent ENT assessment** if:
  - there is no improvement after more than 4 weeks on a PPI, Gaviscon or stopping smoking, or
  - symptoms have not resolved after more than 8 weeks despite a PPI, Gaviscon or stopping smoking.
- **To a speech and language therapist** if stress is adding to the hoarseness, or if you have had total loss of voice for more than 2 weeks. The therapist may discuss your case with the ENT team and arrange an ENT assessment if needed.
