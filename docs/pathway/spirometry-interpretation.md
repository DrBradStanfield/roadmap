# Spirometry Interpretation

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This page explains what the measurements from a spirometry (breathing) test mean and how your doctor interprets them.

---

## What Spirometry Measures

A spirometry test measures how much air you can breathe out and how fast. The key measurements are:

- **FEV1** (forced expiratory volume in one second) — the volume of air you can breathe out in the first second of a hard, forced breath out. This is the most important number for detecting and grading airflow obstruction.
- **FVC** (forced vital capacity) — the total volume of air you can breathe out forcefully after a full breath in.
- **FEV1/FVC ratio** — the proportion of your total breath-out capacity exhaled in the first second, expressed as a percentage. This is used to detect airway obstruction.
- **% predicted** — how your results compare to the expected normal values for someone of your age, sex, ethnicity, and height.

---

## How Your Doctor Interprets the Results

### Step 1: Is the test technically valid?

Your doctor checks that at least 3 acceptable attempts were performed, with consistent results (less than 150 mL difference between the top two efforts). A poor effort — from fatigue, coughing, or early stopping — can make results look worse than they are.

### Step 2: Is there airflow obstruction?

Your doctor compares your FEV1/FVC ratio to the **lower limit of normal (LLN)** for someone with your characteristics.

- **If FEV1/FVC is below the LLN** → an obstructive pattern is present (airways are narrowed or blocked, as seen in asthma and COPD).
- **If FEV1/FVC is at or above the LLN** → move to step 3.

**If obstruction is present**, your doctor grades its severity using FEV1 % predicted:

| Severity | FEV1 % predicted |
|---|---|
| Mild | > 70% |
| Moderate | 60–69% |
| Moderately severe | 50–59% |
| Severe | 35–49% |
| Very severe | < 35% |

Your doctor may also test your response to a bronchodilator (reliever inhaler): a > 200 mL and > 12% improvement in FEV1 or FVC is considered significant, but this alone does not definitively distinguish asthma from COPD.

### Step 3: Is there a restrictive pattern?

If the FEV1/FVC ratio is normal but FVC is **below the LLN**, this suggests a restrictive pattern — meaning the lungs are not filling fully. Causes include:

- Pulmonary fibrosis or interstitial lung disease
- Neuromuscular disease
- Morbid obesity
- Pleural disorders
- Heart failure or pneumonia

Further lung function testing (measuring total lung capacity and diffusing capacity) may be needed to confirm this.

### Step 4: What does a normal result mean?

- **Normal spirometry excludes clinically significant COPD.**
- Normal spirometry does not exclude other important respiratory conditions (e.g., asthma that is not active at the time of testing). If symptoms persist, your doctor may arrange further tests.

---

## Important Notes

- Your doctor interprets spirometry results alongside your symptoms, history, and other tests — not in isolation.
- If your doctor has any doubt about interpretation, they may refer you for specialist respiratory assessment.
- Always discuss your spirometry results and what they mean with your doctor.
