# Flat Feet in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of flat feet (pes planus) in children aged under 15 years.

---

## Red Flags — See Your Doctor Promptly

Contact your doctor if your child has:

- A flat foot on one side only (unilateral flat foot)
- A painful flat foot, especially if your child is older than 8 years
- A spinal abnormality alongside flat feet

---

## Background

- Flat feet (pes planus) means the arch of the foot is reduced or absent when standing.
- There are two types: **flexible** (arch returns when not weight-bearing) and **rigid** (arch does not return).
- Flexible flat feet in children are most often completely normal and physiological.
- It is very common for infants and children under 6 years to have a flattened arch — this typically improves during the first 6 years of life and requires no treatment.
- Up to 15% of children continue to have flat feet as adults. Adults with flat feet are no more likely to have foot pain than those with a normal arch.
- Flat feet can occasionally be associated with conditions involving generalised ligamentous laxity (e.g., Marfan syndrome, Down syndrome, osteogenesis imperfecta).
- Surgery is rarely needed and is generally only considered for rigid flat feet.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- General developmental and growth history
- Any pain in the foot
- Previous treatment for the feet
- Any problems with shoes or footwear
- Any functional limitations or disability
- Recurrent ankle sprains (which can be a sign of tarsal coalition)

Physical examination typically includes:

- Observing your child's gait (how they walk)
- Determining whether the flat foot is one-sided or both sides
- Checking the spine for signs of abnormality (e.g., spina bifida)
- Inspecting shoes for wear patterns along the inner border
- Checking whether the arch reconstitutes (reappears) when the child stands on tiptoes — if it does, this is reassuring
- Assessing calf tightness (ankle flexibility)
- Looking for any painful bony prominence, skin breakdown, or callus formation on the inner border of the foot
- Checking for swelling or pain in other joints
- A brief neurological examination to rule out muscle tone or reflex abnormalities

**In adolescents**, your doctor will also consider tarsal coalition — a congenital condition where two or more foot bones are fused or fail to separate. This typically causes a painful, stiff foot in early adolescence, often with recurrent ankle sprains and a flat foot where the arch does not reconstitute on tiptoes.

**Weight-bearing X-rays** may be arranged if the flat foot:

- Is rigid at any age
- Becomes persistently or progressively more painful
- Shows significant bony deformity

---

## Management

**If flexible and pain-free with no other abnormalities:** No treatment is needed. Your doctor will reassure you that the arch will improve with age in most children, and that flat feet alone do not cause problems in the long term.

**Your doctor may consider referral to a paediatric orthopaedic specialist if:**

- The foot is rigid (arch does not return on tiptoes) or painful
- The flat foot is on one side only
- There is a marked bony abnormality in an infant
- Your child has a known neurological condition (e.g., cerebral palsy) and the flat feet are causing problems
- Your child is older than 8 years and cannot take part in age-appropriate activities (e.g., sport at school) because of flat feet

**Your doctor may consider referral to a paediatric specialist** if other joints are also affected, or if a neurological condition is suspected.

**Your doctor may consider physiotherapy** for children with flexible flat feet and calf tightness (contracture), though this is not always available in the public system.
