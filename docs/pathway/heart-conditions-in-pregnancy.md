# Heart Conditions in Pregnancy

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of known or suspected heart conditions during pregnancy.

---

## Background

Pregnancy causes major cardiocirculatory changes:

- Cardiac output rises by 30–50%, peaking around 30 weeks' gestation
- Stroke volume increases in the first half of pregnancy; heart rate increases after that
- Systemic blood pressure decreases in the first trimester, reaching its lowest around 20 weeks, then rising toward pre-pregnancy levels before term
- Underlying cardiac disease may be significantly affected — mainly in the second half of pregnancy and at delivery, but can occur earlier
- Cardiac disease may have been previously asymptomatic

The incidence of maternal cardiovascular disease appears to be growing, likely due to increasing maternal age and cardiovascular risk factors including obesity, diabetes, and hypertension.

### Cardiac problems that can arise during pregnancy include:

- Heart failure due to valvular disease or pre-existing undetected cardiomyopathy
- Arrhythmia
- Thromboembolic complications
- Peripartum cardiomyopathy
- Myocardial infarction
- Aortic dissection
- Coronary artery dissection

### Heart murmurs in pregnancy

- Innocent systolic murmurs are common in pregnancy due to increased cardiac output — they are soft ejection systolic murmurs in the pulmonary or aortic area, with no radiation outside the precordium, and become substantially quieter when the patient sits forward
- **Diastolic murmurs are always abnormal in pregnancy** and require further assessment

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess for cardiac symptoms and relevant history, including:

**History:**
- Any chronic heart disease (known valvular disease, cardiomyopathy, arrhythmia, long QT syndrome)
- Heart murmur
- Congenital heart disease
- Marfan syndrome
- Rheumatic fever — rheumatic heart disease without a known history of rheumatic fever is common; if in a high-risk population, a lower threshold for further assessment applies
- Current or previously uninvestigated symptoms: palpitations, syncope, oedema, new onset shortness of breath or asthma
- Family history of cardiac disease or sudden death
- Unexplained breathlessness or exercise intolerance

**Examination typically includes:**
- Pulse, blood pressure, jugular venous pressure (JVP), and check for peripheral oedema
- Heart auscultation and evaluation of any murmurs
- Assessment for signs of anaemia or thyrotoxicosis

**Symptoms and signs — what is and is not normal in pregnancy:**

Normal for pregnancy:
- Mild breathlessness late in pregnancy (though underlying heart disease should still be considered)
- Presyncope when lying on the back (supine presyncope)
- Mild peripheral oedema (though underlying heart disease should still be considered)

Abnormal for pregnancy:
- Significant shortness of breath on exertion
- Orthopnoea (breathlessness when lying flat)
- Pitting or progressive peripheral oedema
- Chest pain

**Investigations your doctor may consider:**
- 12-lead ECG if any symptoms or signs are present — note that some ECG changes are non-specific in pregnancy:
  - Mild sinus tachycardia (heart rate increases by 15–20 beats per minute above baseline, so this may be normal)
  - Non-specific ST segment and T wave changes
  - Small or inverted T waves in lead III
  - Mild leftward axis deviation
- Blood tests: full blood count (FBC), electrolytes, renal function, thyroid function, NT-pro brain natriuretic peptide (BNP)
- Echocardiogram — if being considered, your doctor will seek specialist cardiology input first

---

## Management

Your doctor will tailor management to your specific cardiac history and the stage of pregnancy. The approach differs based on whether the assessment is before or during pregnancy.

**If you have a pre-existing heart condition before becoming pregnant,** your doctor may advise:
- Specialist assessment to evaluate risks and suitability of pregnancy before conceiving
- Review and optimisation of any cardiac medications
- Avoiding pregnancy until risk has been assessed if you have known cardiac disease or significant symptoms
- Contraception advice if appropriate

**During pregnancy,** your doctor may:
- Refer for specialist obstetric or cardiology assessment if you have known congenital heart disease, a cardiac condition that could deteriorate in pregnancy, or diastolic or uncertain murmurs
- If you have rheumatic valve disease, arrange specialist cardiology or obstetric medicine review
- Manage any associated hypertension according to pregnancy-specific guidelines

All decisions about investigation and referral will be made by your doctor based on your individual situation.
