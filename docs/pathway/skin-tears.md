# Skin Tears

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of skin tears — wounds where the skin separates from underlying tissue. Skin tears most commonly affect older adults but can also occur in critically ill patients, infants, and those with certain skin or chronic diseases.

---

## When to Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department immediately if:

- Bleeding that cannot be controlled with direct pressure
- Deep flap wounds
- Extensive or full-thickness skin flaps that appear necrotic (black or dead tissue)
- Muscle, bone, or tendon is visible in the wound

---

## Background

Skin tears occur when the epidermis separates from the dermis (partial thickness), or when both the epidermis and dermis separate from underlying structures (full thickness — a flap tear).

Risk factors include:
- Very young or advanced age
- Fragile or thin skin
- Chronic illness, polypharmacy, poor nutrition
- Reduced mobility or history of falls
- Previous skin tears

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess the wound by:

1. Examining the wound bed for dirt, debris, or foreign bodies
2. Checking whether skin edges can be realigned easily
3. Assessing the viability of any skin flap — a pale, dusky, or darkened flap may not survive

**ISTAP Skin Tear Classification:**

- **Type 1 — No skin loss:** The skin flap can be repositioned to fully cover the wound bed
- **Type 2 — Partial flap loss:** Part of the flap is missing and cannot fully cover the wound bed
- **Type 3 — Total flap loss:** The entire flap is absent and the wound bed is exposed

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following:

### 1. Stop Bleeding and Clean the Wound
- Apply direct pressure and elevate the limb if appropriate
- Irrigate with clean tap water or sterile saline; remove any debris, clots, or foreign material

### 2. Pain Relief
- Appropriate analgesia will be given based on your age and the severity of pain

### 3. Approximate the Skin Flap (if viable)
- The flap is gently rolled back over the wound using a moist cotton bud or dampened gloved finger
- The skin is not forced or stretched — a small gap of up to 10 mm is acceptable, as tension can impair healing
- If the skin has completely detached, it is generally not replaced, as it is unlikely to heal and may become a source of infection
- **Skin glue** (e.g., Dermabond) may be used for Type 1 tears where there is no skin loss, ideally within 24 hours of injury

### 4. Dress the Wound
Dressing choice depends on the tear type and how much fluid (exudate) the wound is producing. Your doctor will select from:

| Dressing Type | Examples | Used For |
|---|---|---|
| Silicone interface or low-adherent mesh | Mepitel, Cuticerin | All types; atraumatic removal |
| Hydrogel | IntraSite Conformable | Dry wounds (Types 2–3) |
| Skin glue | Dermabond | Type 1 only, within 24 hours |
| Foam | Biotain, Mepilex | Moderate exudate; longer wear time |
| Alginate sheet | Algisite | Minor bleeding; moderate–heavy exudate |
| Hydrofibre/Gelling fibre | Aquacel, Exufibre | Moderate–heavy exudate (Types 2–3) |
| Silver hydrofibre | Aquacel Ag Extra | Infected wounds; 2-week course maximum |

**Dressings to avoid:** sutures and staples, iodine solutions, film or hydrocolloid dressings, and gauze (these can cause further trauma or impair healing).

An arrow is drawn on the dressing indicating the direction the flap lies — this guides safe removal at future dressing changes.

A firm supportive bandage (orthopaedic wool, crepe, and tubular retention bandage) is applied over the dressing to protect the wound. For a lower limb wound, bandaging runs from the base of the toes to below the knee.

### 5. Tetanus
If indicated based on your vaccination history, tetanus management will be provided.

### 6. Infection
If there are signs of local, spreading, or systemic infection (redness spreading beyond the wound, fever, increased pain, pus, or feeling generally unwell), treatment for wound infection will be arranged — which may include antibiotics.

Signs that may indicate serious infection or complications:
- Wound breakdown or increasing size
- Severe or worsening pain (necrotising fasciitis must be excluded)
- Redness extending more than 2 cm from the wound edge
- Fever, chills, rapid heart rate, or feeling very unwell

### 7. Other Wound Healing Factors
Your doctor may also address:

- Underlying conditions that can impair healing (e.g., heart failure, diabetes, peripheral arterial disease, chronic venous insufficiency)
- Medications that may slow healing
- Nutrition — adequate protein, vitamins, and trace elements are important for all stages of wound healing
- Smoking cessation advice (smoking significantly impairs wound healing)

### 8. Advice to Take Home
- Limit mobility for the first 24–48 hours
- Elevate the affected limb when resting
- Consider wearing protective garments (e.g., long sleeves or stockings) to reduce the risk of future skin tears
- Moisturise the skin of the limbs twice daily

---

## Follow-Up

Your doctor will review the wound within 24 hours:

- The outer dressings are removed while leaving the primary dressing in place where possible
- The dressing is removed in the direction of the arrow marked on it
- The flap and wound bed are reassessed
- Wound dimensions are measured and documented

If the wound has not adequately reduced in size within **3 weeks**, further specialist wound care will be arranged.

---

## When Specialist Review Is Needed

Your doctor will refer to specialist wound care services if:

- The wound is not healing satisfactorily within 3 weeks
- The wound is on a lower limb and you have a history of venous leg ulcers or arterial disease
