# Pterygium and Pinguecula

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of pterygium and pinguecula — two common benign eye lesions — and when to refer to an ophthalmologist.

---

## Background

**Pterygium** is a triangular wedge of fibrovascular conjunctival tissue that typically starts medially on the nasal conjunctiva and extends laterally onto the cornea. It is associated with chronic sun exposure.

**Pinguecula** is a degenerative eye condition often confused with pterygium. It appears as a yellowish, slightly raised conjunctival lesion that remains confined to the conjunctiva without corneal involvement. There is usually a gap between the pinguecula and the edge of the cornea.

It is important to differentiate these benign lesions from **conjunctival neoplastic lesions** (possibly malignant), which often present as fast-growing, gelatinous, raised lesions that are not triangular, and not always at the 3 o'clock or 9 o'clock position that characterises pterygium.

---

## Warning Signs — See Your Doctor Urgently

See your doctor promptly if the lesion:

- Is growing rapidly
- Is not triangular in shape
- Is gelatinous or unusually raised
- Has corkscrew-shaped blood vessels
- Is not located at the 3 o'clock or 9 o'clock position on the eye
- Is causing decreased vision

These features may suggest a more serious conjunctival lesion requiring urgent ophthalmology review.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Whether the lesion is bilateral (affects both eyes)
- Whether it extends onto the corneal surface (usually nasally)
- Symptoms of redness and irritation
- Any change in size over time — a rapidly progressing lesion may indicate alternative pathology
- Risk factors: prolonged sunlight exposure, dusty or sandy environments, and age

Physical examination typically includes:

- **Visual acuity** testing in each eye separately (with glasses if worn), using a pinhole if acuity is reduced
- Slit lamp examination if available
- Assessing features of pterygium: a band of redness and thickening extending from the cornea as a wedge
- Assessing features of pinguecula: a yellowish, slightly raised lesion confined to the conjunctiva, with a gap between it and the corneal edge
- Looking for features of possible conjunctival neoplasia (see warning signs above)

---

## Management

Your doctor will advise simple measures that help most patients' symptoms:

- **Lubricant eye drops** as needed to relieve irritation and redness. Funded options include hypromellose with dextran (PolyTears) and polyvinyl alcohol eye drops (Vistil, Vistil Forte).
- **NSAID eye drops** — your doctor may consider diclofenac sodium eye drops four times a day for one to two weeks to help chronic inflammation. It is normal for these to sting. They are generally safe to use for repeated courses as needed.
- **Sun protection** — wearing wrap-around UV400 sunglasses and a hat to reduce UV exposure. This may also slow progression and reduce the risk of recurrence after surgery.

Redness or irritation alone are not indications for surgical excision unless significant and unresponsive to the above treatment.

Your doctor may consider referral to an ophthalmologist if:

- You have vision symptoms or the lesion is noted to be growing
- The pterygium extends 3 mm or more onto the cornea
- The edge of the lesion is at or beyond the pupil margin in normal room lighting
- Irritation persists despite simple measures and optimal medical management
- The lesion has an atypical appearance, is fast growing, or ocular surface neoplasia is suspected — in which case the referral is marked urgent ("high suspicion of cancer") and should be seen within 28 days
