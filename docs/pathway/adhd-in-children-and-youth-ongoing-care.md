# ADHD in Children and Youth — Ongoing Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway supports the ongoing management of children and youth who already have a formal diagnosis of ADHD. It covers medication reviews, side-effect management, behavioural supports, and monitoring.

---

## Background

- ADHD is best managed with a holistic approach — combining non-pharmacological behavioural strategies at school and home with medication when needed.
- First-line treatment is usually behavioural and educational intervention, particularly for mild ADHD or younger children.
- Medication is generally recommended for moderate-to-severe ADHD, or when non-pharmacological strategies alone are not sufficient.
- ADHD symptoms vary over time. Life transitions (puberty, school changes, relationship changes) may require medication adjustments.
- 70–80% of children treated with stimulant medication show noticeable improvement in some symptoms.
- Stimulant medications generally cause few significant side-effects; most side-effects can be managed by adjusting formulation, dose, or timing.
- Specialists initiate stimulant medications. Dose adjustments can be made by paediatricians, psychiatrists, or primary care clinicians.

---

## Assessment — What Your Doctor Reviews

At each review (typically every 6 months), your doctor will:

1. **Review the current medication regimen** — including whether it is still the right type and dose.

2. **Take a history**, asking about:
   - Improvement in symptoms and general wellbeing at school, home, and with peers (including the young person's own perspective)
   - Missed doses or medication holidays, and the impact of these
   - Smoking, drug, and alcohol use (in adolescents)
   - Safety considerations (e.g. driving, operating dangerous equipment in adolescents)
   - Medication side-effects (see below)
   - Status of known co-morbid conditions, and whether any new ones have emerged
   - Life transitions that may require planning

3. **Review prescribing history** — check for increasing prescription frequency or multiple prescribers, which may occasionally suggest diversion (medication being used by others).

4. **Examination** — measure height and weight (plotted on a growth chart) and blood pressure and pulse rate. These are checked every 6 months, or more frequently if there are concerns.

## Medication Options

Your doctor may consider the following medications, depending on what has been prescribed and how well it is working.

### Stimulant Medications

Approximately 70% of people respond well to the first class of stimulant tried. Of those who do not respond, around two thirds will respond to the alternate class.

#### Methylphenidate (MPH)

Typical effective range: 0.3–1.0 mg/kg/day, usually to a maximum of 60 mg/day. Titrated from a low starting dose to achieve optimal symptom control with tolerable side-effects.

- **Immediate release** (Rubifen, Ritalin): Acts within 1 hour; lasts 2–4 hours; given 2–3 times daily (usually breakfast and lunchtime).
- **Sustained release** (Ritalin SR, Rubifen SR): Onset 1–2 hours; lasts up to 8 hours; once or twice daily; can eliminate need to take medication at school.
- **Extended release** (Concerta, Methylphenidate Sandoz XR, Teva): Once daily morning dosing; lasts up to 12 hours. Concerta and Methylphenidate Sandoz XR are bioequivalent. Teva uses a different delivery system and may require a medication review if switching to or from it.
- **Modified release** (Ritalin LA): Once daily; lasts 8–10 hours. The capsule can be opened and sprinkled on food as long as it is not chewed.

#### Dexamfetamine-Based Stimulants

- **Dexamfetamine sulfate** (immediate release): Generally second-line. Acts within 1 hour; lasts 4–6 hours; given 2–3 times daily. Has more abuse potential than methylphenidate.
- **Lisdexamfetamine** (long-acting): Once daily; lasts up to 13 hours. Available in 30 mg, 50 mg, and 70 mg capsules. Has little to no abuse potential due to its pro-drug formulation. Cannot be used in combination with a funded methylphenidate preparation.

### Non-Stimulant Medications

#### Atomoxetine

Your doctor may consider atomoxetine in specific situations, including:
- Co-morbid anxiety worsened by stimulant use
- History of psychosis or first-degree relative with schizophrenia
- Stimulant medications are contraindicated
- Inadequate response, serious adverse reaction, or worsening substance abuse with stimulants
- Significant risk of diversion where low-abuse-potential stimulants have not been effective

Dosing: Once daily, starting at 0.5 mg/kg/day and increasing to a target of 1.2 mg/kg/day. Effect builds over 7–10 days and can be delayed up to 4 weeks. Common side-effects include nausea and gastrointestinal upset. **Liver abnormalities are rare but serious** — seek medical attention if abdominal pain, unexplained nausea, malaise, dark urine, or jaundice develop.

*Atomoxetine combined with a stimulant is not standard treatment — specialist advice is needed before considering this.*

#### Alpha Agonists

- **Clonidine**: Occasionally started off-label by specialists; primary care may continue prescribing once dose is titrated. Dose titration requires specialist input.
- **Guanfacine**: Unfunded in New Zealand. Long-acting, once daily. Cannot substitute directly for clonidine. Titration must be led by a paediatrician or psychiatrist.

## Managing Side-Effects

### Sleep Difficulties

- Recommend regular physical activity and good sleep hygiene: limit night-time screen time, consistent bedtime routine, consistent sleep and wake times.
- Your doctor may consider melatonin if there is evidence of sleep onset delay despite good sleep hygiene. Melatonin is subsidised via Special Authority when there is a specialist diagnosis of persistent distressing insomnia secondary to a neurodevelopmental disorder.
- Ensure a healthy diet. There is no conclusive evidence for dietary supplements or elimination diets in ADHD.

### Appetite Suppression and Weight Loss

- Your doctor may consider dose reduction, a change in formulation or timing, or a different stimulant type.
- Eating a good breakfast before the morning dose helps; offer larger meals and snacks when medication has worn off.
- "Medication holidays" on weekends or school holidays can allow catch-up growth.
- If significant weight loss occurs, your doctor may seek paediatric advice.

### Irritability

- May occur at peak medication effect or while medication is wearing off.
- Your doctor may consider adjusting the dose or switching to a longer-acting formulation.
- Physical activity or low-demand special interest activities can help during these periods.

### Cardiovascular Side-Effects

- Cardiac side-effects are uncommon, especially when medication is started at a low dose and increased slowly.
- If symptoms began after a dose increase, the dose may be reduced to the previously tolerated level.
- If symptoms are concerning, medication should be withheld and a cardiovascular assessment (including ECG) arranged.

### Tics

- Tics are common in young people with ADHD. They naturally wax and wane and may be falsely attributed to medication changes. There is no evidence that stimulant medications worsen tics.

## Behavioural and Non-Pharmacological Supports

### Education and Psychoeducation

Your doctor may provide education suited to the young person's stage:

- **Childhood**: Understanding ADHD symptoms, treatment benefits, and adherence — usually involving parents and teachers.
- **Adolescence**: Self-management, medication adherence, impact on school and social functioning; developing autonomy and self-regulation, especially during life transitions.
- **Young adulthood**: Managing co-morbidities and informed self-care decisions.

### Behavioural Strategies

Your doctor may recommend strategies tailored to the child's difficulties, including:
- Maintaining consistent routines and regular mealtimes
- Giving instructions one at a time
- Providing positive reinforcement for desired behaviour
- Developing agreements for managing challenging situations (e.g. device usage)
- A neuro-affirming approach: viewing ADHD in a positive light, choosing activities aligned with the child's interests, supporting emotional regulation skills
- Managing screen time thoughtfully

### Parenting Programmes

For **primary to intermediate aged children**, your doctor may recommend:
- [Triple P](https://www.triplep-parenting.net.nz/nz/parenting-courses/triple-p-online/) — evidence-based parenting programme (funded in some regions)
- [Incredible Years](https://www.incredibleyears.com/early-intervention-programs/parents) — 14-week programme (generally funded)
- [Tuning in to Kids](https://tuningintokids.org.au/parents/) — 6-session programme on emotional connection
- [Everyday with ADHD](https://www.adhd.org.nz/events) — ADHD-specific, 6-week online course
- [Rolling with ADHD](https://healthymindslearning.ca/courses/rolling-with-adhd/) — free, self-paced online course

For **high school aged children**, your doctor may focus on:
- Executive functioning skills (planning, focus, emotional regulation)
- Self-management and independence
- Healthy lifestyle: physical activity, sleep, and nutrition
- [Rolling with ADHD for Teens](https://healthymindslearning.ca/rolling-with-adhd-for-teens/) resource booklet

### School Support

Your doctor may encourage families to discuss concerns with the school. Older children may be eligible for [special exam conditions](https://www2.nzqa.govt.nz/ncea/ncea-for-teachers-and-schools/special-assessment-conditions/) (e.g. quieter room, extra time). Your doctor may be asked to provide supporting information.

## Ongoing Monitoring

- Once on an effective dose with normal growth, review every 6 months (or more frequently if concerns arise).
- Prescriptions can be written for up to 3 months' supply, dispensed monthly.
- If a dose change is not working, or side-effects develop, your doctor should be contacted before the next scheduled review.

---

## Trials Without Medication

During periods of stability, your doctor may discuss a trial without medication:

- Carefully timed — ideally during school terms (not at the start of the year, during exams, or at major life transitions).
- In some adolescents, neurological development post-puberty can result in significant symptom reduction.
- If ADHD symptoms significantly worsen, medication can be resumed.
- Be cautious about stopping medication at times when impulse control matters (e.g. driving, new employment).
- Special Authority approval is lifelong — resuming medication is straightforward if needed.

---

## Life Transitions

Life transitions (leaving school, starting work, learning to drive, leaving home, relationship changes) can pose additional challenges for young people with ADHD. Your doctor may:

- Review and adjust medication timing and formulation to support the transition
- Encourage cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) or social skills training to build self-regulation
- Screen for substance use
- Provide advice on appropriate medication use and risks of misuse during this period

---

## When Your Doctor May Seek Specialist Advice

Your doctor may seek paediatric or child and youth mental health specialist advice if:

- Ongoing side-effects are not resolving with adjustments
- Concerns about medication diversion
- Support needed with a trial without medication
- Managing pre-existing or new co-morbidities
- Considering atomoxetine alongside a stimulant medication
- Titrating the dose of clonidine
- Significant weight loss on stimulant medications
- Cardiovascular symptoms requiring assessment
- The patient is under 12 years and paediatric input is needed
- The patient has a history of trauma, mental health diagnosis, or involvement with Oranga Tamariki (child protection)
