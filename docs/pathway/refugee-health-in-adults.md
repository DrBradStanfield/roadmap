# Refugee Health in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and ongoing management of adults of refugee background following initial refugee health screening.

---

## Background

People of refugee background are often affected by complex health inequalities arising from pre- and post-migration experiences. They may:

- Have physical and psychological conditions resulting from pre-migration torture, trauma, and prolonged detention
- Face significant post-migration challenges:
  - Navigating an unfamiliar health system
  - Language barriers
  - Establishing new social connections
  - Separation from family
  - Housing, employment, and financial difficulties
  - Loss of mana, purpose, and role
  - Feeling unsafe or experiencing marginalisation

Former refugees and asylum seekers have often missed preventive care opportunities. A sensitive, supportive approach helps ensure physical and psychological conditions do not become chronic issues or barriers to settlement.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. **Prepare for the appointment** — extended appointments are often needed; an interpreter may be required. Family members and friends should not be used as interpreters. Informed consent may be legally invalid if an interpreter is not used when needed.

2. **Review prior health assessments** — depending on how the person entered New Zealand (Quota Programme, Family Support Category, Community Organisation Sponsorship, or asylum seeker), different prior medical assessments may have been completed, including chest X-rays and blood tests (FBC, hepatitis B, hepatitis C, HIV, syphilis, HbA1c, lipids, vitamin D, ferritin, renal and liver function).

3. **Use a syndrome-based approach** — former refugees may present with typical conditions, but also conditions specific to their background:

| Presentation | Additional considerations in people of refugee background |
|---|---|
| Fever | Malaria, measles, typhoid fever, dengue, hepatitis A or B |
| Respiratory symptoms | Tuberculosis, pertussis (vaccine may not have been available), parasite infections (rarely cause wheeze), sickle cell acute chest syndrome |
| Abdominal pain | Schistosomiasis, Strongyloides, H. pylori gastritis, hepatitis, typhoid, female genital mutilation |
| Diarrhoea | Parasite infection, bacillary or amoebic dysentery, lactose intolerance (more common in some ethnic groups) |
| Skin conditions | Strongyloides (urticarial rash, larva currens), measles, leprosy, cutaneous leishmaniasis |
| Eye conditions | Trachoma, onchocerciasis (river blindness) |
| Musculoskeletal pain | Low vitamin D, somatising presentations |
| Low weight / nutrition | Food insecurity, iron, vitamin B12, or thiamine deficiency, H. pylori, dental disease |
| Fatigue | Iron or B12 deficiency, blood disorders (including sickle anaemia) |
| Mental health concerns | PTSD, experience of violence or conflict, family separation, detention history |

4. **Consider commonly seen concerns:**
   - Poor oral health — dental decay, gum disease, oral cancer signs
   - Hearing and vision issues
   - Intestinal parasites (e.g., schistosomiasis, Strongyloides)
   - Somatising presentations (e.g., headaches, back pain, pelvic pain)
   - Immunisation gaps

5. **Consider specific population groups:**
   - **Women** — obstetric and gynaecological history; consider female genital mutilation
   - **Pregnant women** — initial antenatal assessment; screen for vitamin D deficiency, thiamine deficiency, and iron deficiency
   - **Older adults** — health and frailty assessment, cognition (use RUDAS scale for multicultural assessment), medication review, carer stress
   - **LGBTQIA+ people** — awareness of discrimination, hate crimes, family rejection, and increased mental health distress
   - **People with disabilities** — activities of daily living, cultural concerns about home help and residential care
   - **Asylum seekers** — consider PTSD, suicide risk, and separation from people left behind

6. **Assess mental health and family situation:**
   - Ask about coping, support networks, and any safety concerns
   - Ask about sleep (including nightmares), appetite, energy, concentration, mood, and past mental health history
   - Screen for depression, anxiety, and PTSD
   - Ask about trauma only if appropriate; use a trauma-informed care approach when disclosed
   - Ask about family violence (routine inquiry for all patients over 16)

7. **Consider chronic disease and cancer screening** — cardiovascular risk assessment, cervical screening, breast cancer screening, bowel screening.

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following depending on findings:

### Infectious Diseases

**Intestinal parasites (not Strongyloides or schistosomiasis):**
- Threadworm/pinworm (*Enterobius vermicularis*): mebendazole 100 mg immediately, repeated in 2 weeks
- Roundworm, whipworm, hookworm: mebendazole 100 mg immediately then twice daily for 3 days
- Tapeworm (*Taenia saginata*): praziquantel 10 mg/kg as a single dose (with food)
- Dwarf tapeworm (*Hymenolepis nana*): praziquantel 25 mg/kg as a single dose (with food)
- *Entamoeba histolytica*: metronidazole 400–800 mg every 8 hours for 5–10 days, followed by paromomycin 25–35 mg/kg in three divided doses for 7 days
- *Giardia*: ornidazole 1.5 g as a single dose (adult), or metronidazole 400 mg three times a day for 5 days
- Non-pathogenic parasites (*Entamoeba coli*, *Blastocystis hominis*, *Dientamoeba fragilis*, and others) do not require treatment

**Schistosomiasis** (serology positive or equivocal):
- Check urine dipstick for haematuria; arrange stool microscopy
- Your doctor may consider praziquantel 20 mg/kg every 4 hours for three doses (with food) if not pregnant and no acute illness

**Strongyloides** (serology positive or equivocal):
- Your doctor may consider ivermectin 0.2 mg/kg once a day for 2 days (on an empty stomach) if not pregnant and weight ≥15 kg
- If from a *Loa loa*-endemic country, blood film for *Loa loa* is done first

**Helicobacter pylori:** if stool antigen positive, manage as per dyspepsia/GORD pathway

**Malaria:** your doctor will seek infectious disease advice if malaria is suspected

**Tuberculosis:**
- If active TB is suspected, your doctor will follow the TB pathway and notify the Medical Officer of Health (this is a legal requirement)
- Latent TB (positive IGRA or abnormal screening chest X-ray) is managed with specialist referral
- A previous negative TB screening chest X-ray does not preclude TB developing later — latent TB may become active at any time

**Blood-borne viruses and STIs:** treated according to relevant pathways (Hepatitis B, Hepatitis C, HIV, chlamydia, gonorrhoea, syphilis)

**Skin infections:** tinea, scabies, leprosy, onchocerciasis, and other infections are managed according to diagnosis

**Eye infections:**
- Trachoma (caused by *Chlamydia trachomatis*): your doctor will seek ophthalmology review; azithromycin may be considered pending assessment
- Onchocerciasis (river blindness): ophthalmology or dermatology referral as appropriate

### Nutritional Deficiencies

**Iron deficiency:** your doctor will assess for causes and may treat with oral iron or IV iron infusion

**B12 deficiency:** your doctor will check for H. pylori and manage according to the B12 deficiency pathway

**Vitamin D deficiency:**
- Higher risk in those with darker skin, who cover their skin for religious or personal reasons, who avoid sun exposure, or who have intestinal malabsorption or chronic liver/renal disease
- Your doctor may prescribe colecalciferol supplementation (dose based on risk factors and vitamin D level)

**Thiamine deficiency:**
- More common with rice-based diets (e.g., parts of Myanmar, Thailand, Nepal, Bhutan)
- Higher risk in pregnant and lactating women
- Features: peripheral neuritis, cardiac insufficiency, oedema
- Your doctor may prescribe vitamin B complex (for at-risk pregnant/lactating women) or oral thiamine (for symptomatic patients)

---

### Blood Disorders

**Anaemia:** your doctor will investigate and treat causes; may arrange haemoglobin electrophoresis to exclude thalassaemia or haemoglobinopathy

**Thalassaemia:**
- If positive thalassaemia screen (alpha or beta thalassaemia trait, haemoglobin E, sickle cell trait), your doctor will discuss partner testing and arrange family member screening where indicated
- If negative screen with iron deficiency, repeat testing once iron-replete (iron deficiency may mask beta thalassaemia trait)

**Sickle cell disease and trait:**
- Sickle cell crisis: urgent general medicine assessment
- Sickle cell disease: haematology referral
- Sickle cell trait: partner screening; genetic advice if partner also has sickle cell or thalassaemia trait

**G6PD deficiency:**
- X-linked recessive condition more common in people of Middle Eastern, African, and Mediterranean descent
- Triggers for haemolytic crisis: fava beans, mothballs (naphthalene), henna dye, nitrofurantoin, sulfonylureas, fluoroquinolones, some antimalarials
- Acute crisis features: severe anaemia (pallor, extreme fatigue, palpitations, shortness of breath), jaundice, dark urine — seek haematology advice urgently

**Eosinophilia:** your doctor will investigate for allergies and parasites (Strongyloides, schistosomiasis, filariasis in endemic regions)

**Benign essential (ethnic) neutropenia:** neutrophil counts below standard reference ranges are a normal variant in 25–50% of people of African descent and some from the Middle East — not a pathological condition

---

### Dental Issues

Your doctor may arrange a dental referral. Dental funding is available for people over 18 years who need immediate and essential dental treatment.

---

### Pre-existing Injuries

**Traumatic brain injury:** your doctor may request neurology assessment; note that injuries occurring before arrival in New Zealand are not eligible for ACC

**Limb amputations:** your doctor may refer to Peke Waihanga (artificial limb service) for prosthetic assessment; ACC does not cover pre-arrival injuries

**Burn and other scars:**
- Gentle stretching, massage, and moisturisers are recommended
- Hypertrophic scars or contractures may be referred to occupational therapy
- Scars causing significant psychosocial distress or functional impact may be referred to plastic surgery

---

### Mental Health and Trauma

**Somatising presentations:**
- Your doctor will exclude physical causes with appropriate examination and investigations
- Treatment for anxiety and depression may be considered
- Refugee mental health and well-being support may be recommended using a destigmatising approach

**Mental health support:** if mental health or well-being issues are identified, your doctor will arrange support through migrant and refugee services

---

### Other Management

- **Chronic pain:** managed according to the chronic non-cancer pain pathway
- **Disabilities:** your doctor may refer to appropriate specialists, request needs assessment, and arrange community support services
- **Immunisation:** if not up to date, vaccinations will be given according to the catch-up immunisation schedule
- **Medication instructions:** your doctor will ensure you understand how to take any prescribed medications, using diagrams or translated materials if needed
- **Antenatal care:** pregnant patients are supported into antenatal care as early as possible
