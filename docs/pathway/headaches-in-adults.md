# Headaches in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to headaches in adults — assessing the cause, identifying red flags, and managing the most common headache types.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to an emergency department or call an ambulance if you have a headache with any of the following:

- Sudden "thunderclap" headache — worst headache of your life, or feels like a blow to the head, reaching peak intensity within minutes
- Vomiting with no other features of migraine
- Fever, neck stiffness, drowsiness, or rash (possible meningitis)
- New weakness, speech difficulty, or facial drooping (possible stroke)
- Eye pain, red eye, or sudden visual disturbance
- Headache following a head injury in the last month
- New or different headache if you are over 50 years old
- Headache that is steadily worsening over days to weeks with no relief

---

## Background

Most headaches in adults are benign (not dangerous). The most common types are:
- **Migraine**
- **Tension-type headache**
- **Medication overuse headache**
- **Cluster headache**

Serious underlying pathology is found in less than 1% of outpatient headache consultations, but up to 15% of emergency department presentations. Your doctor will assess which type you have.

A **chronic headache** is defined as headache present on 15 or more days per month for at least 3 months.

---

## What Your Doctor Will Assess

Your doctor will take a detailed history looking for features that help distinguish a dangerous secondary cause from a benign primary headache.

### Features Suggesting a Serious Secondary Cause

| Feature | Possible Cause |
|---|---|
| Sudden thunderclap onset, peak within minutes | Subarachnoid haemorrhage, cerebral artery dissection, acute hypertensive crisis |
| Worse with coughing, sneezing, or bending | Raised intracranial pressure (tumour or abscess) |
| Head injury in the last month | Subdural haemorrhage |
| Over 50 years old, new or different headache | Giant-cell arteritis |
| Fever, neck stiffness, rash | Meningitis or encephalitis |
| Limb or facial weakness, speech difficulty | Stroke or tumour |
| Eye pain, red eye, visual disturbance | Acute angle-closure glaucoma |
| Neck pain and occipital headache after neck trauma or manipulation | Vertebral artery dissection |

**Reassuring features (less likely to be serious):**
- Recurrent episodic headache with a long history
- No neurological symptoms or signs

### Examination

Your doctor will typically check:
- Blood pressure and temperature
- Vision and eye movements
- Balance (heel-to-toe walking)
- Reflexes and nerve function
- Eye fundi (looking inside the eye) if indicated
- Neck movement and muscle tenderness

### Investigations

Investigations are not always needed. Your doctor will recommend blood tests (such as CRP/ESR if giant-cell arteritis is possible) or an urgent CT head scan if specific criteria are met — particularly if headaches have changed in character with concerning associated features, especially in those over 50.

Consider keeping a **headache diary** to track frequency, severity, possible triggers, and medication use.

## Headache Types

### Migraine

**Diagnosis requires at least 5 attacks with:**
- At least 2 of: unilateral location; throbbing or pulsating quality; moderate to severe pain; aggravated by routine activity
- And at least one of: light and sound sensitivity; nausea and/or vomiting
- Duration: 4 to 72 hours untreated

**General features:**
- Onset often in teens but can start at any age
- May wake you at night
- Often worse in perimenopause/menopause and around menstruation
- Family history is common
- Exacerbated by movement — patients often prefer to lie still in a dark, quiet room
- **Aura** (10–20% of patients): visual symptoms (shimmering zigzag patterns, visual loss), sensory symptoms (tingling in arm or face), or word-finding difficulty — usually preceding the headache by 5–60 minutes

**Common triggers** (most patients have none):
- Skipping meals, dehydration, alcohol, caffeine or caffeine withdrawal
- Stress or post-stress relaxation
- Bright or flickering light
- Menstruation
- Strenuous physical activity

---

### Tension-Type Headache

- Most common recurring headache type
- Mild to moderate — never severe
- Pressure or band-like sensation, typically bilateral or frontal
- May radiate to neck
- May be associated with sound sensitivity *or* light sensitivity (but not both)
- Not associated with nausea or vomiting
- Not worsened by activity — patient remains active
- Worsens as day progresses; often linked to stress
- Risk of progressing to medication overuse headache if pain medications are used too frequently

---

### Medication Overuse Headache

Medication overuse headache (MOH) is common and under-diagnosed. It should be considered in anyone with chronic headaches.

**Definition (ICHD-3):**
- Headache on 15 or more days per month
- In a patient with a pre-existing headache disorder
- Due to regular use of acute headache medication for more than 3 months

**Risk thresholds:**
- Triptans and opioids: risk if used more than 10 days per month on average
- Paracetamol, aspirin, NSAIDs: risk if used more than 15 days per month on average

**Features:**
- Headache worsens with frequent pain medication use
- Often worst on waking (medication wore off overnight)
- May cause rebound headache if medications are stopped suddenly
- Associated with anxiety, irritability, and poor concentration

---

### Cluster Headache

- Mostly affects men aged 20–40 (6:1 male to female ratio)
- Often misdiagnosed or delayed — uncommon in general practice

**Diagnosis requires 5 or more attacks with:**
- Severe or very severe unilateral pain around or behind the eye
- Duration: 15 minutes to 3 hours if untreated
- Frequency: 1 to 8 attacks per day
- Accompanying autonomic features: eye redness/tearing, nasal congestion/runny nose, forehead/facial sweating, droopy eyelid, or restlessness/agitation

**General features:**
- Attacks occur in bouts of 6–12 weeks, every 1–2 years (episodic type)
- Often occur at the same time of day and year
- Alcohol triggers attacks during a bout, but not during remission
- Patients develop longer remission intervals with age

## Management

Your doctor will provide management tailored to your headache type. The following is an overview of how each type is treated.

> **Important for all headache types:** Opioid pain medications (including codeine and tramadol) should be avoided. They are no more effective than other medications and significantly increase the risk of medication overuse headache.

---

### Migraine — Acute Treatment

Treatment is most effective when taken at the very onset of the headache.

**Step 1 — Simple analgesia:**
- A short-acting NSAID (ibuprofen, diclofenac, naproxen, or aspirin) combined with paracetamol, with or without an anti-nausea medication (metoclopramide or ondansetron)

**Step 2 — Triptans** (if step 1 is inadequate):
- Triptans are effective only for the headache phase, not the aura
- Options include sumatriptan (oral or injection) and rizatriptan (oral)
- Can be repeated after 2 hours if headache returns, but a second dose is unlikely to work if the first had no effect
- Limit to 10 or fewer treatment days per month to avoid medication overuse headache

Limit acute treatment to 2–3 days per week. Avoid taking triptans more than 3 days in a row.

If severe vomiting and dehydration occur despite the above, your doctor may arrange intravenous fluids and injected anti-nausea medications.

---

### Migraine — Prevention

Preventive treatment is worth considering if you have 2 or more disabling migraines per month. Success is defined as at least 50% reduction in frequency or severity.

**Lifestyle measures that may help:**
- Regular meals and good hydration
- Regular, sufficient sleep
- Regular aerobic exercise (avoid sudden strenuous exertion)
- Stress management (meditation, yoga, breathing techniques)
- Limit caffeine; if reducing, taper slowly to avoid withdrawal headaches

**Preventive medications** — your doctor will discuss options including:
- Beta blockers (e.g. propranolol, nadolol, metoprolol)
- Low-dose tricyclic antidepressants (e.g. amitriptyline, nortriptyline) — also useful if there is co-existing anxiety, depression, or insomnia
- Topiramate — note: must not be used in pregnancy; requires experienced prescriber supervision
- Candesartan — note: not safe in pregnancy
- Sodium valproate — used only with specialist recommendation; significant risks in pregnancy
- Newer options (not publicly funded): atogepant (Aqupita), galcanezumab (Emgality), fremanezumab (Ajovy) — monthly injections or tablets; approximately $300–$400/month

Trial a medication for 2–3 months before changing. Consider withdrawal after 6–12 months of effective prevention.

**Menstrually triggered migraine:** Your doctor may consider short-course NSAIDs around menstruation, or specific hormonal approaches.

**Migraine in pregnancy:** Your doctor will discuss safe options — paracetamol is the safest acute treatment; propranolol and low-dose amitriptyline have the best evidence for prevention during pregnancy.

---

### Medication Overuse Headache — Management

The key to recovery is stopping all pain medications.

- **Abrupt withdrawal** is the preferred method for most medications — headache will initially worsen, but most people improve within 14 days
- **Gradual withdrawal** is needed for opioids and benzodiazepines
- It may take up to 6 weeks to see improvement
- Your doctor may prescribe a short course of low-dose tricyclic antidepressants or other preventive medications to support withdrawal
- Reassurance: most people improve significantly over time

Discuss with your doctor before stopping any regular medications, as withdrawal needs to be managed safely.

---

### Tension-Type Headache — Management

- Reassurance and explanation of the diagnosis
- Address stress, anxiety, or depression if present
- Exercise, posture improvement, and relaxation techniques
- Physiotherapy (manual therapy, posture, breathing/relaxation)
- Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) if appropriate
- **Episodic headaches:** paracetamol, NSAIDs, or aspirin as needed — but limit to 2–3 days per week to avoid medication overuse headache
- **Chronic headaches:** your doctor may consider a low-dose tricyclic antidepressant (amitriptyline or nortriptyline) taken nightly

---

### Cluster Headache — Management

Standard pain medications are not effective for cluster headaches.

**Acute treatment options (prescribed by your doctor):**
- Sumatriptan subcutaneous injection — most effective; works within 10–15 minutes
- High-flow oxygen (100%, non-rebreather mask at 7–15 L/min for 10–20 minutes)
- Oral triptans (slower onset; second-line)

**Preventive treatment** (started during an active cluster bout):
- Verapamil (calcium channel blocker) — requires specialist neurology supervision and ECG monitoring; started at low dose and titrated up
- Prednisone (short course) — used alongside verapamil while the dose is being titrated; tapered over 2–3 weeks

Avoid alcohol completely during cluster episodes. Verapamil is withdrawn slowly once the cluster resolves — it is not a long-term medication.

If cluster headaches are not controlled with the above, your doctor will refer to neurology.

## When Referral or Specialist Input Is Needed

Your doctor will refer or seek specialist advice in the following situations. These decisions are always made in consultation with you:

- **Emergency department:** thunderclap headache, fever with neck stiffness, new neurological symptoms, or other red flags
- **Urgent neurosurgery:** suspected serious intracranial pathology
- **Neurology (non-urgent):** failed 2 or more preventive medications for migraine; cluster headache not controlled; consideration of Botox for migraine prevention; medication overuse headache persisting after 6 weeks without medication; specialist supervision of verapamil for cluster headache
- **Obstetrics/neurology:** if taking topiramate and pregnancy is confirmed (stop topiramate immediately and seek advice)
- **Endocrinology:** if menstrually triggered migraine prevention is ineffective
- **Talking therapy / physiotherapy:** for tension-type headache management
