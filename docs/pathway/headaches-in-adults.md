# Headaches in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: September 2025.*

This pathway covers headaches in adults: assessing the cause, identifying red flags, and managing the most common types.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call 111 or go to the emergency department if you have a headache with any of the following:

- Sudden "thunderclap" headache that feels like a blow to the head and reaches peak intensity within minutes
- Fever, drowsiness, neck stiffness, or rash (possible meningitis or encephalitis)
- New limb or facial weakness, or speech difficulty (possible stroke)
- Other unusual neurological signs
- A change in personality or thinking
- Pain in one eye, a red eye, or visual disturbance
- Headache following a head injury in the last month
- Headache made markedly worse by coughing, sneezing, bending, or exertion
- Neck pain and a headache at the back of the head after blunt trauma or neck manipulation, including for treatment
- New or different headache if you are over 50 years old

See a doctor the same day if you have:

- Vomiting with no other features of migraine
- A headache that started recently, over weeks, or keeps getting worse without any break

---

## Background

Most headaches in adults are benign (not dangerous) primary headaches. The most common types are:
- **Migraine**
- **Medication overuse headache**
- **Tension-type headache**
- **Cluster headache**

Benign secondary causes include fever from a general illness, and problems with the sinuses, ears, teeth, jaw, neck, or eyes.

Serious underlying pathology is found in less than 1% of outpatient referrals for headache, but up to 15% of emergency department presentations. Your doctor will assess which type you have.

A **chronic headache** is defined as headache present on 15 or more days per month for at least 3 months.

---

## What Your Doctor Will Assess

Your doctor will take a detailed history looking for features that help distinguish a dangerous secondary cause from a benign primary headache. They will also look for contributing factors such as sinusitis, depression, sleep apnoea, alcohol, concussion, teeth grinding, vision problems, dehydration, hormonal changes, and medications.

### Features Suggesting a Serious Secondary Cause

| Feature | Possible Cause |
|---|---|
| Sudden thunderclap onset, peak within minutes | Subarachnoid haemorrhage, cerebral artery dissection, cerebral venous sinus thrombosis, acute hypertensive crisis, reversible cerebral vasoconstriction syndrome, pituitary apoplexy |
| Made markedly worse by coughing, sneezing, bending, or exertion | Raised intracranial pressure (tumour or abscess) |
| Head injury in the last month | Subdural haemorrhage |
| Over 50 years old, especially with no past headaches | Giant-cell arteritis |
| Fever, drowsiness, neck stiffness, rash, recent infection | Meningitis or encephalitis |
| Limb or facial weakness, speech difficulty | Stroke or tumour |
| Pain in one eye, red eye, visual disturbance, nausea | Acute angle-closure glaucoma, idiopathic intracranial hypertension |
| Neck pain and occipital headache after blunt trauma or neck manipulation | Vertebral artery dissection |

A significant new headache, or a change in pattern, is more concerning if you are older than 50 years, immunosuppressed, pregnant or postpartum, drink heavily, take anticoagulants or two antiplatelet medicines, have had cancer (especially lung, breast, or melanoma), or have a family history of subarachnoid haemorrhage.

**Reassuring features (less likely to be serious):**
- Recurrent episodic headache with a long history
- No neurological deficit (brief neurological symptoms are common in migraine)

### Examination

Your doctor will typically check:
- Blood pressure and temperature
- Vision, visual fields, and eye movements
- Balance (heel-to-toe walking)
- Reflexes
- Eye fundi (looking inside the eye) and/or the temple arteries, if indicated
- Neck movement and muscle tension or tenderness

### Investigations

Investigations are not needed if the headache is typical of a primary headache and there are no concerning features. Your doctor may check CRP and ESR if you are older than 50 years with new headaches and giant-cell arteritis is possible. Normal results make it less likely but cannot rule it out. An urgent CT head scan (within 2 weeks) is arranged when headaches become steadily more frequent or severe, especially if aged 50 years and older, together with specific features such as cancer, persistent vomiting not typical of migraine, or new weakness or speech problems.

Consider keeping a **headache diary** or using an app to track frequency, severity, possible triggers, and medication use.

## Headache Types

### Migraine

**Diagnosis requires at least 5 attacks with:**
- At least 2 of: unilateral location; throbbing or pulsating quality; moderate to severe pain; aggravated by routine activity
- And one or both of: light and sound sensitivity; nausea and/or vomiting
- Duration: 4 to 72 hours untreated

**General features:**
- Onset often in teens but can start at any age
- May wake you at night
- May be worse in perimenopause and menopause, and with premenstrual syndrome (PMS)
- Family history is common
- Exacerbated by movement; patients often prefer to lie still in a dark, quiet room
- **Aura** (10 to 20% of patients): usually visual (shimmering zigzag patterns, visual loss); tingling in the arm or face and word-finding difficulty are also common. It usually precedes the headache and lasts 5 to 60 minutes, but may occur with it or without a headache

**Common triggers** (most patients have none):
- Skipping meals; foods such as chocolate, aged cheese, processed meats, artificial sweeteners, or MSG
- Too little fluid, alcohol, caffeine, or caffeine withdrawal
- Stress or relaxation
- Bright or flickering light
- Menstruation
- Strenuous physical activity (including sex)

---

### Tension-Type Headache

- Most common chronic recurring headache type
- Mild to moderate, never severe
- Pressure or band-like sensation, typically symmetrical; may be on both sides, frontal, or front-to-back
- May radiate to neck
- May be associated with sound sensitivity *or* light sensitivity (but never both)
- Not associated with nausea or vomiting
- Not worsened by activity; patient remains active
- May worsen as the day goes on, and may be made worse by stress
- Can progress to medication overuse headache if pain medications are used inappropriately

---

### Medication Overuse Headache

Medication overuse headache (MOH) is common and under-diagnosed. It should be considered in anyone with chronic headaches.

**Definition (ICHD-3):**
- Headache on 15 or more days per month
- In a patient with a pre-existing headache disorder
- Due to regular overuse of acute headache medication for more than 3 months
- Usually, but not always, resolves once the overuse stops

**Risk thresholds** (unless needed for a condition other than headache):
- Triptans and opioids: should not be used on more than 10 days per month on average
- Paracetamol, aspirin, NSAIDs: should not be used on more than 15 days per month on average

**Features:**
- Headache worsens with frequent pain medication use
- Often worse on waking (no medication taken overnight)
- Sometimes rebound headache if medications are stopped suddenly
- May be associated with anxiety, irritability, and poor concentration

---

### Cluster Headache

- Mostly affects men aged 20 to 40 (6 males to every female)
- May be missed, as it is uncommon in general practice

**Diagnosis requires 5 or more attacks with:**
- Severe or very severe unilateral pain around or behind the eye
- Duration: 15 minutes to 3 hours if untreated
- Frequency: 1 to 8 attacks per day
- Autonomic features on the same side (eye redness or tearing, blocked or runny nose, forehead or facial sweating, small pupil or droopy eyelid), or restlessness or agitation

**General features:**
- Attacks occur in bouts of 6–12 weeks, every 1–2 years (episodic type)
- Often occur at the same time of day and year
- Alcohol can trigger attacks during a bout, but not during remission
- Most patients develop longer remission intervals with age

## Management

Your doctor will provide management tailored to your headache type. This includes reassurance and education. The following is an overview of how each type is treated.

> **Important for all headache types:** Opioid pain medications (including codeine and tramadol) should be avoided. They are no more effective than other medications, carry a risk of dependency, and carry a high risk of medication overuse headache.

---

### Migraine — Acute Treatment

Treatment is most effective when taken at the onset of the headache. Effective combinations vary between people.

**Step 1. Simple analgesia:**
- A short-acting NSAID (ibuprofen, diclofenac, naproxen, or aspirin) combined with paracetamol, with or without an anti-nausea medication (metoclopramide or ondansetron)

**Step 2. Triptans** (if step 1 is inadequate):
- Triptans are effective only for the headache phase, not the aura
- Options include sumatriptan (oral or injection) and rizatriptan (oral; a lower dose is used if you take propranolol)
- Can be repeated after 2 hours if headache returns, but a second dose is unlikely to work if the first had no effect
- Using triptans more than twice a week risks medication overuse headache; limit to 10 or fewer days per month on average

Ideally, limit acute treatment to 2 to 3 days per week. Avoid taking triptans more than 3 days in a row.

If you have significant vomiting and dehydration despite these medicines, seek same-day care: see your doctor, or call 111 or go to the emergency department. You may need intravenous fluids and injected anti-nausea medication.

---

### Migraine — Prevention

Preventive treatment is worth considering if you have 2 or more disabling migraines per month. Success is defined as at least 50% reduction in frequency or severity.

**Lifestyle measures that may help:**
- Regular meals and good hydration
- Regular, sufficient sleep
- Regular aerobic exercise (avoid sudden strenuous exercise)
- Stress management (meditation, yoga, breathing techniques)
- Limit caffeine (for example, no more than 2 cups of coffee a day); if reducing, taper over several weeks to avoid withdrawal headaches

**Preventive medications:** your doctor will discuss options including:
- Beta blockers (e.g. propranolol, nadolol, metoprolol): may suit people with anxiety; avoided in asthma
- Low-dose tricyclic antidepressants (e.g. amitriptyline, nortriptyline): also useful if there is co-existing anxiety, depression, tension-type headache, or insomnia
- Topiramate: must not be used in pregnancy, as it has been linked to birth defects and neurodevelopmental disorders. A pregnancy test and effective contraception are needed first for anyone who could become pregnant. If pregnancy occurs, stop it immediately and seek advice
- Candesartan: must not be used in pregnancy; kidney function and electrolytes are checked after each dose increase
- Sodium valproate: used only with specialist recommendation and where there is no suitable alternative, because of risks in pregnancy
- Not funded: atogepant (daily), galcanezumab and fremanezumab (injections every 4 weeks). These are not more likely to work than funded medicines but are well tolerated and need no dose build-up. Avoid before or during pregnancy and when breastfeeding. Approximate cost (as of June 2025) is $300 to $400 a month
- Second-line options: Botox every 12 weeks (given in a hospital clinic after neurology referral), venlafaxine, lamotrigine, gabapentin, or pregabalin

Doses are increased slowly. Trial a medication for 2–3 months before changing. Consider gradual withdrawal after 6 to 12 months of effective prevention.

**Menstrually triggered migraine:** If usual treatments are inadequate, your doctor may consider naproxen started before your period and continued for 5 to 7 days, or hormonal approaches (such as continuous combined oral contraceptive, progesterone-only contraception, or short-course estrogen around the period).

**Migraine in pregnancy and breastfeeding:** Your doctor will discuss safe options. Paracetamol can be used throughout pregnancy and breastfeeding. NSAIDs are never used in the third trimester, and are otherwise avoided unless the benefits outweigh the risks. Triptans are used in pregnancy only if there are no other options. For prevention, propranolol has the best evidence of safety in pregnancy, and amitriptyline may be used at the lowest effective dose.

### Medication Overuse Headache — Management

The key to recovery is stopping all pain medications.

- **Abrupt withdrawal** is the preferred method for most medications. The headache will initially worsen, but most people improve within 14 days
- **Gradual withdrawal** is needed for opioids and benzodiazepines, or if you feel you cannot stop abruptly (reducing by 2 days per month, on average)
- It may take up to 6 weeks to see improvement
- If withdrawal fails, your doctor may prescribe a low-dose tricyclic antidepressant for 4 to 6 weeks, then try withdrawal again; topiramate is an alternative
- Reassurance: most people improve over time

Talk to your doctor before stopping regular medications, especially opioids or benzodiazepines, which need gradual withdrawal.

---

### Tension-Type Headache — Management

- Reassurance and explanation of the diagnosis
- Address stress, anxiety, or depression if present
- Exercise and posture advice
- Physiotherapy (manual therapy, posture, breathing/relaxation)
- Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) if appropriate
- **Episodic headaches:** paracetamol, NSAIDs, or aspirin as needed, but limit to 2 to 3 days or fewer per week to avoid medication overuse headache
- **Chronic headaches:** your doctor may consider a low-dose tricyclic antidepressant (amitriptyline first, or nortriptyline) taken nightly

---

### Cluster Headache — Management

Standard pain medications are not effective for cluster headaches.

**Acute treatment options (prescribed by your doctor):**
- Sumatriptan subcutaneous injection: recommended first-line; works within 10 to 15 minutes
- High-flow oxygen (100%, non-rebreather mask at 7 to 15 L/minute for ten to twenty minutes)
- Oral triptans (slow onset; second-line)

**Preventive treatment** (both started together during an active cluster bout):
- Verapamil: requires specialist neurology supervision and an ECG before starting and at each dose increase; the dose is increased in steps
- Prednisone (short course): used alongside verapamil while its dose is increased, then tapered over two to three weeks

Avoid alcohol completely during cluster episodes. Verapamil is withdrawn slowly once the cluster resolves. It is not a long-term medication.

If cluster headaches are not well controlled with the above, your doctor will refer to neurology.

## When Referral or Specialist Input Is Needed

Your doctor will refer or seek specialist advice in the following situations. These decisions are always made in consultation with you:

- **Emergency department or hospital neurosurgery assessment:** thunderclap headache, fever with neck stiffness, new neurological signs, or other red flags. Call 111 or go to the emergency department.
- **Neurology (non-urgent):** failed 2 or more preventive medications for migraine; cluster headache not well controlled; consideration of Botox for migraine prevention; medication overuse headache persisting after 6 weeks without medication; specialist supervision of verapamil for cluster headache
- **Obstetrics/neurology:** if taking topiramate and pregnancy is confirmed (stop topiramate immediately and seek advice)
- **Endocrinology:** if menstrually triggered migraine prevention is ineffective
- **Talking therapy / physiotherapy:** for tension-type headache management
