# Thrombotic Disorders

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: December 2021.*

Thrombotic disorders (also called thrombophilia) significantly increase the risk of venous thromboembolism (VTE), such as deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism (PE). This pathway covers identifying and managing these disorders to help prevent VTE. See also the Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) and Pulmonary Embolism (PE) pathways.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Background

Thrombotic disorders can be inherited or acquired.

**Inherited (primary) causes:**
- Lower levels of proteins that prevent clotting:
  - Antithrombin deficiency
  - Protein C deficiency
  - Protein S deficiency
- Higher levels of proteins that promote clotting:
  - Factor V Leiden (activated protein C resistance)
  - Prothrombin gene mutation G20210A

**Acquired causes:**
- Pregnancy and the period after birth (postpartum).
- Hormone therapy: menopause hormone therapy (MHT) and the combined oral contraceptive pill (COCP).
- Cancer and myeloproliferative disorders.
- Antiphospholipid syndrome.
- Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia.
- Recent surgery or trauma, which can slow blood flow in the veins and start clot formation.
- Inflammatory disorders, such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) and infections.
- Nephrotic syndrome.

## Assessment

> **Practice point:** Your doctor will check whether testing is recommended before arranging it. Thrombophilia testing is expensive and may not help with clinical management.

### History

Your doctor may ask about:
- Any previous VTE, and whether it had a provoking cause or risk factors. If a provoking cause or risk factors were present, thrombophilia testing is generally not needed.
- Where the clot was, noting an unusual site such as the gut (mesenteric), brain (cerebral), or portal vein.
- Any family history of VTE in a first-degree relative (brother or sister, parent, or child). The abnormality found, the age at diagnosis, and whether the clot was provoked all affect how significant it is.
- Any recurrent pregnancy loss (miscarriage or stillbirth).

**Provoking causes of VTE:**
- **Strong:** major surgery; major trauma (including hip or leg fracture); spinal injury; hip or knee replacement; active cancer.
- **Moderate:** knee arthroscopy; laparoscopic surgery; central venous lines; chemotherapy; congestive heart failure or respiratory failure; hormone replacement therapy; oral contraceptive therapy; pregnancy and the period after birth; previous VTE; thrombophilia; varicose veins (if associated with thrombophlebitis).

If a previous VTE was properly investigated, and either no significant provoking cause was found or a previous thrombophilia screen was negative, no further investigation is needed. If a previous VTE was **not** investigated, a thrombophilia screen may be recommended.

**Weak factors, not counted as a provoking cause:**
- Bed rest for more than 3 days.
- Immobility from sitting, such as travel.
- Short flights (for example, 4 hours).
- Increasing age.
- Obesity.

### Who may be offered inherited thrombophilia testing

Testing should only be done for a person with:
- Unprovoked VTE when younger than 45 years.
- Warfarin-induced skin necrosis, who no longer takes anticoagulants.
- Purpura fulminans (children).
- VTE in an unusual site without an obvious cause (such as cerebral, mesenteric, or portal), but not retinal vein thrombosis, upper limb clots, or catheter-related clots.
- Unprovoked VTE and a strong first-degree family history (one or more family members) of unprovoked VTE.
- A brother or sister with homozygous factor V Leiden, prothrombin mutations, or a compound heterozygote.
- Potential solid organ donors.
- IVF with recurrent implantation failure (activated protein C resistance test only).
- Other reasons, case by case. Your doctor may seek advice from a haematologist (blood specialist) before arranging tests.

### When testing is not recommended

- While taking anticoagulants, although molecular (genetic) tests are not affected.
- At the time of an acute VTE, or within 3 months of a clot.
- If a decision has already been made to treat with lifelong anticoagulation.
- For relatives without symptoms who have a low-risk thrombophilia, such as factor V Leiden or prothrombin gene mutations.
- For people with:
  - arterial thrombosis
  - retinal vein thrombosis
  - a clot related to a central venous catheter
  - a clot in a vein of the arm (upper limb venous thrombosis).
- For children without symptoms who cannot give informed consent, even with a positive family history of inherited thrombophilia.
- For people without symptoms who have a strong family history of VTE, when the combined oral contraceptive pill or hormone replacement therapy is being considered. Current British guidelines recommend avoiding hormone therapy in these settings. A negative test result may be falsely reassuring.

**A strong family history of VTE means:**
- A first-degree relative has had an unprovoked clot aged under 45 years, or
- Two or more first-degree relatives have had unprovoked clots, or
- A first-degree relative has had VTE and a confirmed inherited thrombophilia.

### When testing is avoided or postponed

- During pregnancy or within 8 weeks after birth.
- While taking oestrogen hormone replacement therapy.
- While taking anticoagulants.

### Tests

If testing is appropriate and likely to change treatment, your doctor may arrange a thrombophilia screen. Tests include:
- Antithrombin III.
- Protein C.
- Protein S.
- Activated protein C resistance (APCR) and, if APCR is positive, factor V Leiden.
- Prothrombin gene mutation (G20210A).

## Management

### Preventing VTE

Your doctor may talk with you about VTE symptoms and risk factors. Clot risk builds up as risk factors add together, for example:
- Recent surgery or trauma.
- Cancer, whether known or not yet found.
- Previous clot or a family history of clots.
- Pregnancy, the period after birth, or current oestrogen therapy (such as the combined oral contraceptive or hormone replacement therapy).
- Immobilisation.
- Travel: an airline flight more than 6 hours.
- A high platelet count (thrombocytosis).
- Obesity.
- Varicose veins.

**Your individual risk if you have been tested:** your doctor may explain your risk if your results were positive, or if they were negative but you have a strong family history and no previous VTE.
- With antithrombin, protein C, or protein S deficiency, the yearly risk of a first VTE is 1.5% (15 to 19 times the risk of people in the general community).
- With factor V Leiden or the prothrombin 20210A mutation, the yearly risk of a first VTE is 0.34% (3 to 5 times the risk of people in the general community).

### Protection during higher-risk periods

Your doctor may consider preventive treatment (thromboprophylaxis) at times of higher risk:

- **Major surgery:** prevention with low-molecular-weight heparin (LMWH) or a direct oral anticoagulant.
- **Menopause hormone therapy (MHT):** MHT increases the risk of DVT and PE (a relative risk of about 2), and thrombophilia adds to that risk. MHT through the skin (transdermal) appears not to carry a significant clot risk.
- **Oral contraceptive pill (OCP):** when thrombophilia or a significant clot risk is present, your doctor may suggest one of:
  - progesterone-only contraception, which appears to have no DVT risk
  - an intrauterine device (IUD)
  - non-hormonal contraception.

  If you have been taking the OCP without problems and are then found to have the factor V Leiden mutation, it is reasonable to continue it.
- **Pregnancy:** your doctor will follow the Venous Thromboembolism (VTE) Risk in Pregnancy pathway.
- **Air travel:** your doctor will first decide whether you are at higher risk of VTE, based on a previous VTE, active cancer, or recent surgery on the legs. If any of these apply, your doctor may advise you to:
  - stand up and move every 3 hours during the flight
  - avoid excess alcohol and sedatives when travelling, and keep well hydrated
  - wear compression stockings (travel socks) to help blood flow back from the legs
  - take preventive rivaroxaban 10 mg for four days, starting on the day of flying, or self-funded enoxaparin 40 mg just before the flight.

  Aspirin has not been shown to prevent VTE during air travel.

### After a VTE

Your doctor will discuss how long anticoagulant treatment should last, based on the reason for it. See the Deep Vein Thrombosis (DVT) and Pulmonary Embolism (PE) pathways.

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you to or seek advice from a haematologist:
- **Non-urgent haematology assessment:** if management is needed after a VTE.
- **Haematology advice:** if you are pregnant, or before arranging thrombophilia testing for a reason outside the usual criteria.

## Key Concepts for Patients

- Thrombophilia testing is only useful in specific situations. It is expensive and may not change treatment.
- A clot with a clear trigger, such as major surgery, generally does not need thrombophilia testing.
- Testing is not recommended during an acute clot, within 3 months of one, or while taking anticoagulants, and is avoided or postponed during pregnancy.
- With a strong family history of clots, a negative test result may be falsely reassuring.
- Always discuss any new symptoms or medication questions with your doctor before making changes.
