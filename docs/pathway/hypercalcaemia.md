# Hypercalcaemia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of high blood calcium (hypercalcaemia) — including how to identify the cause and when urgent or specialist care is needed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

Your doctor will arrange emergency hospital assessment if:

- Calcium > 3.5 mmol/L (marked hypercalcaemia)
- Calcium > 3 mmol/L combined with nausea, vomiting, or signs of dehydration

---

## What Causes High Calcium?

The most common causes of hypercalcaemia are:

- **Primary hyperparathyroidism** — overactive parathyroid glands (most common cause)
- **Malignancy** — myeloma, or cancers of the breast, lung, or kidney
- **Medications** — lithium, thiazide diuretics, excess vitamin D
- **Sarcoidosis**
- **Thyrotoxicosis** (overactive thyroid)
- **Cortisol deficiency**
- **Other causes** — prolonged immobility, milk-alkali syndrome

---

## Assessment

High calcium is often found incidentally on a routine blood test. Symptoms, if present, can be non-specific.

**Non-specific symptoms** (which may prompt a blood test):
- Muscle aches, fatigue, weakness
- Loss of appetite
- Mild depression or poor concentration

**More specific symptoms of hypercalcaemia:**
- Nausea, vomiting, constipation, abdominal pain
- Thirst, passing more urine than usual (polyuria)
- Confusion

Your doctor will check the **corrected calcium** level (automatically adjusted by the lab if albumin is low, using: Corrected calcium = observed calcium + [(40 − albumin g/L) × 0.02 mmol/L]).

---

## Investigations

Your doctor will arrange blood tests including sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium, phosphate, alkaline phosphatase, albumin, creatinine, and **PTH (parathyroid hormone)**.

### If PTH is raised or inappropriately normal

A high calcium should suppress PTH. If PTH is still normal or high, **primary hyperparathyroidism** is the most likely diagnosis.

Your doctor will then:

- Arrange a **urine calcium-to-creatinine ratio** (preferably fasting) — this distinguishes primary hyperparathyroidism (where surgery may be considered) from familial hypocalciuric hypercalcaemia (where surgery is not appropriate)
- Consider complications of primary hyperparathyroidism:
  - Osteoporosis
  - Renal impairment
  - Renal calculi (kidney stones)
- Arrange a **DEXA scan** (bone density) if you are at risk of low bone density — with hyperparathyroidism, bone density may be normal at the hip and spine but reduced at the radius
- Only investigate for renal calculi if symptoms are present

### If PTH is suppressed

Your doctor will consider other causes and may arrange further tests (after seeking endocrinology advice if needed):

- 25(OH) Vitamin D
- Chest X-ray (for lung cancer or sarcoidosis)
- Full blood count, ESR
- Thyroid function tests
- Serum protein electrophoresis, immunoglobulin levels, and serum free light chain analysis (to check for myeloma)
- X-ray of painful bones; consider radionuclide bone scan
- Urine calcium-to-creatinine ratio
- PTH-related peptide (PTHrP) assay if malignancy is suspected

---

## Management

### Emergency

If red flags are present (see above), your doctor will arrange immediate hospital assessment.

### Primary Hyperparathyroidism

Your doctor will consider referral to endocrinology, particularly if you meet criteria for parathyroid surgery:

- Age under 50 with primary hyperparathyroidism
- Kidney stones
- Significant osteoporosis
- Renal impairment (eGFR < 60)
- Corrected calcium ≥ 2.85 mmol/L

**If asymptomatic with mildly elevated calcium (< 2.85 mmol/L)**, your doctor may monitor you in general practice with:

- Blood tests (calcium and renal function) every 6 months
- Bone density monitoring and treatment if osteoporosis is found

**Advice your doctor may give you:**
- Avoid medications that raise calcium (e.g., lithium, thiazide diuretics)
- Avoid dehydration, prolonged bed rest or inactivity, and a high-calcium diet
- Drink adequate fluids each day to reduce kidney stone risk
- Maintain moderate dietary calcium intake — around 1000 mg per day
- Ensure adequate vitamin D (400–600 units per day)
- Know the warning signs of a parathyroid crisis (sudden rise in calcium, often triggered by illness such as vomiting or diarrhoea): abdominal pain, nausea, confusion — seek prompt medical attention

### Other Causes

Your doctor will treat the underlying cause. Depending on the cause (e.g., malignancy, sarcoidosis, medication effect), referral to the relevant specialist will be arranged.

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor will consider specialist referral in the following situations:

- **Red flags present** — acute general medicine assessment
- **Primary hyperparathyroidism meeting surgery criteria** — non-acute endocrinology assessment
- **Suppressed PTH with no obvious cause** — non-acute endocrinology assessment
- **Suppressed PTH with a likely cause** — referral to the relevant specialty (e.g., haematology for myeloma, oncology for malignancy)
- **Uncertain diagnosis** — endocrinology advice
