# Melanoma

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: September 2021.*

This pathway covers pigmented skin lesions, particularly melanoma. Melanoma can be found at an early, survivable stage, so early diagnosis is essential.

> This information is for background understanding. Your doctor will assess and manage any skin lesion of concern.

---

## Background

New Zealand has one of the highest reported rates of invasive melanoma in the world: 35 to 45 new diagnoses per 100,000 people per year. Melanoma accounts for 80% of NZ's skin cancer-related deaths.

Survival is inversely related to how thick the lesion is, which is why early diagnosis matters. About half of melanomas are first noticed by the patient, because of a new or changing pigmented spot.

UV radiation exposure is the only risk factor that can be changed through behaviour.

---

## Risk Factors

Your doctor will consider whether you have any of the following (in order of significance):

- Age over 50, male sex
- Personal history of melanoma or other skin cancer
- More than 100 moles
- Atypical naevi, or large or multiple congenital naevi
- First-degree family member with melanoma
- Fair skin, blue or green eyes, red or blond hair
- Skin that burns easily and never tans
- Any history of sunburn
- Actinic (sun) skin damage
- Immunosuppression (e.g., HIV, leukaemia, immunosuppressant medications)

---

## Warning Signs

See your doctor promptly if a skin lesion is:

- **Changing:** growing in size, changing shape or colour
- **Itchy:** intermittently
- **Not healing:** ulcerated or bleeding
- **Painful or tender** to touch

---

## How Doctors Assess a Suspicious Lesion

### Full-body skin check

Your doctor will examine your whole body, not just the spot you're worried about. This includes nails, soles, between the toes, and areas not usually exposed to the sun. Lesions of concern may also be looked at with a dermatoscope (a magnifying skin light).

### The ABCDEFG checklist

| Letter | What it means |
|--------|--------------|
| A | **Asymmetry** |
| B | **Border** irregularity: notched, blurred, ragged or variable edges |
| C | **Colour** variation: different colours (brown, black, white, red, blue) within one lesion. Can be non-pigmented |
| D | **Different:** looks unlike your other spots, freckles or moles ("ugly duckling") |
| E | **Evolution** or elevation: any change over time in colour, shape, elevation, structure or symptoms |
| F | **Firm** to the touch |
| G | **Growing:** most melanoma are over 6 mm and keep growing, but up to 25% of new lesions can be smaller |

A score of 2 or more may indicate melanoma. A score under 2 does not rule it out, and clinical judgement is needed. Most melanoma start with a flat phase. Melanoma can appear anywhere on the body, including areas covered by clothes, the scalp, palms, feet, under nails, the eyes, and mucosal surfaces.

Any lesion that is **elevated, firm, and growing** for more than one month should raise suspicion of melanoma. Bleeding, crusting, inflammation, itch and pain are also suspicious features.

### Types of melanoma your doctor may consider

**Melanoma in situ (including lentigo maligna):** A slowly growing or changing patch of discoloured skin. Lentigo maligna is the name used when it occurs on the sun-damaged face of an older person. About 5% invade the deeper skin layer (dermis) and are then reclassified as invasive melanoma.

**Superficial spreading melanoma:** The most common type. Grows slowly as a flat, discoloured patch over months to years. Can occur in teenagers.

**Nodular melanoma:** The second most common type. Usually a firm, raised, uniformly coloured nodule that is often not pigmented. It can grow rapidly, over weeks to months, and may bleed or ulcerate. About 15% of melanomas are nodular, but they make up more than 60% of melanomas that are more than 3 mm thick.

**Amelanotic melanoma:** About 1 to 8% of melanomas. Usually red or pink, sometimes with a faint tan edge, so it is often not recognised as melanoma. Consider it for any persistent, enlarging, ulcerating, or rapidly growing nodule.

**Acral melanoma:** Arises on the palms or soles. Common in darker-skinned people, including Māori and Pacific peoples. Not related to sun exposure.

**Subungual melanoma:** Arises in the nail growth area (nail matrix). May appear as discolouration or a dark band along the nail, or pigment spreading to the surrounding skin (Hutchinson's sign).

## Management

### What happens depends on the level of suspicion

**Low-suspicion flat pigmented lesion:**
Your doctor may monitor the lesion with photos over 3 months. You'll be asked to self-monitor and return in 3 months, or urgently if the lesion changes.

**Suspected melanoma:**
Your doctor will arrange an **excision biopsy**, removing the lesion with a 2 mm margin of normal skin around it. This is done promptly (at the time or within 1 to 2 weeks at the latest). Punch or shave biopsies are not suitable for suspected melanoma. If melanoma is confirmed, further treatment, including re-excision with wider margins, is likely.

**After the biopsy, if melanoma is confirmed:**

The pathology report guides next steps. It includes Breslow thickness (depth in mm), Clark level, margins, mitotic rate, and whether there is ulceration.

- **Melanoma in situ:** Wide re-excision so the total margin is at least 5 mm. If this can be done with a simple closure, your GP may do it. If not, you'll be referred urgently.
- **Thin invasive melanoma (T1a, less than 0.75 mm Breslow thickness):** Wide excision with 10 mm margins may be done by an experienced GP if primary closure is possible and there are no signs of spread.
- **Deeper or more complex melanoma:** Urgent specialist assessment. This covers the need for sentinel node biopsy, the right margin, and any further tests, adjuvant therapies or clinical trials.
- **Recurrent melanoma or suspected spread:** Urgent specialist assessment or lymph node biopsy for staging.

### Long-term follow-up after diagnosis

Follow-up is structured by stage and is based on limited evidence. It mostly involves regular skin checks and examination of lymph nodes. Your doctor will coordinate this.

| Stage | Follow-up schedule |
|-------|-------------------|
| Stage I | Annually for 10 years in primary care |
| Stage Ib or IIa | Every 6 months for 2 years, then annually to 10 years; plus 6-monthly lymph node ultrasound for 2 years |
| Stage IIb to IIc, IIIa to IIId | Every 4 months for 2 years, then 6-monthly in year 3, then annually to 10 years; plus 6-monthly lymph node ultrasound for 2 years |
| Stage IV | Specialist-led, with additional visits as needed |

About 80% of recurrences happen within the first 3 years. At every check, your doctor will examine the original site, all lymph nodes, and your whole skin for a new melanoma. No tests are needed unless something of concern comes up in your history or examination.

Your doctor may consider vitamin D replacement.

## Psychosocial Support

Most people diagnosed with melanoma need significant psychosocial support. Common concerns include body image, fear of dying, the risk of recurrence or a second melanoma, and fear of returning to normal life, particularly outdoors. Your doctor can provide information and support at every stage.

---

## Sun Safety (Prevention and Ongoing Care)

UV exposure is the only melanoma risk factor you can change:

- Avoid direct sun during peak UV times
- **Slip** on clothing, **Slop** on sunscreen, **Slap** on a hat, **Wrap** on sunglasses
- Apply sunscreen at least 20 minutes before going outside, again 10 to 20 minutes after going outside, and every 2 hours after that
- Avoid sunbeds and other artificial UV sources

**Medications that may increase the risk of sunburn:** tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, sulfonamides, NSAIDs, diuretics (frusemide, bumetanide, hydrochlorothiazide), retinoids (isotretinoin, acitretin), sulfonylureas, antipsychotics (phenothiazines, thioxanthenes), immunosuppressants (e.g., imatinib, hydroxychloroquine), some heart and blood pressure medicines (amiodarone, diltiazem, enalapril), and quinine-containing medications. Your doctor may talk with you about any of these you take.

Regular skin checks matter because of the risk of a new melanoma. First-degree relatives of someone with melanoma also have an increased risk.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Care

Go back to your doctor promptly if:
- A known lesion changes in size, shape, colour, or starts bleeding
- You notice a new or changing skin lesion
- Any lump appears under the skin, particularly near where a melanoma was removed or in the nearby lymph nodes
