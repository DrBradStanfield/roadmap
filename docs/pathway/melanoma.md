# Melanoma

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers pigmented skin lesions — particularly the assessment and management of melanoma. Melanoma is serious but highly treatable when caught early. This information is for background understanding; your doctor will assess and manage any skin lesion of concern.

---

## Background

New Zealand has one of the highest rates of invasive melanoma in the world — 35 to 45 new diagnoses per 100,000 people per year. Melanoma accounts for 80% of NZ's skin cancer-related deaths.

Survival is directly related to how early it is found: early-stage melanoma is highly curable; thick or spread melanoma is much harder to treat. About half of melanomas are first noticed by the patient themselves.

UV radiation exposure is the main modifiable risk factor.

---

## Risk Factors

Your doctor will consider whether you have any of the following (in order of significance):

- Age over 50, male sex
- Personal history of melanoma or other skin cancer
- More than 100 moles
- Atypical or large congenital naevi
- First-degree family member with melanoma
- Fair skin, blue or green eyes, red or blond hair
- Skin that burns easily and never tans
- History of sunburn
- Actinic (sun) skin damage
- Immunosuppression (e.g., HIV, leukaemia, immunosuppressant medications)

---

## Warning Signs

See your doctor promptly if a skin lesion is:

- **Changing** — growing in size, changing shape or colour
- **Itchy** — intermittently
- **Not healing** — ulcerated or bleeding
- **Painful or tender** to touch

---

## How Doctors Assess a Suspicious Lesion

### Full-body skin check

Your doctor will examine your whole body — not just the spot you're worried about — including nails, soles, and areas not usually exposed to the sun.

### The ABCDEFG checklist

| Letter | What it means |
|--------|--------------|
| A | **Asymmetry** |
| B | **Border** irregularity — notched, blurred, or ragged edges |
| C | **Colour** variation — different colours (brown, black, white, red, blue) within one lesion |
| D | **Different** — looks unlike all your other moles ("ugly duckling") |
| E | **Evolution** — any change over time in colour, shape, elevation, or symptoms |
| F | **Firm** — hard to the touch |
| G | **Growing** — most melanoma are over 6 mm and keep growing |

A score of 2 or more suggests possible melanoma, but even a score under 2 does not rule it out — clinical judgement is essential. Melanoma can appear anywhere on the body, including under nails, on the scalp, and on mucosal surfaces.

Any lesion that is **elevated, firm, and growing** for more than a month is suspicious, even if it looks pink or skin-coloured (amelanotic melanoma).

### Types of melanoma your doctor may consider

**Melanoma in situ (including lentigo maligna):** A slowly changing flat patch, often on sun-damaged skin of older people. Has not yet invaded deeper layers.

**Superficial spreading melanoma:** The most common type. Grows slowly as a flat, discoloured patch over months to years. Can occur at any age.

**Nodular melanoma:** The second most common. Firm, raised, often uniformly coloured nodule growing rapidly over weeks to months. Accounts for over 60% of thick melanomas — particularly dangerous.

**Amelanotic melanoma:** About 1–8% of cases. Pink, red, or skin-coloured — often not recognised as melanoma. Consider for any persistent, enlarging, or ulcerating nodule.

**Acral melanoma:** Found on palms or soles. More common in Māori and Pacific peoples. Not related to sun exposure.

**Subungual melanoma:** Arises under the nail. May appear as a dark band on the nail or pigment spreading to the surrounding skin (Hutchinson's sign).

## Management

### What happens depends on the level of suspicion

**Low-suspicion flat pigmented lesion:**
Your doctor may monitor the lesion with photos over 3 months. You'll be asked to self-monitor and return if anything changes.

**Suspected melanoma:**
Your doctor will arrange an **excision biopsy** — removing the lesion with a 2 mm margin of normal skin around it. This is done promptly (at the time or within 1–2 weeks). Punch or shave biopsies are not appropriate for suspected melanoma.

**After the biopsy — if melanoma is confirmed:**

The pathology report guides next steps. It includes Breslow thickness (depth in mm), Clark level, margins, mitotic rate, and whether there is ulceration.

- **Melanoma in situ:** Wide re-excision with at least 5 mm margins. If this is straightforward, your GP may do it. If not, you'll be referred urgently.
- **Thin invasive melanoma (T1a, less than 0.75 mm Breslow thickness):** Wide excision with 10 mm margins may be done by an experienced GP if primary closure is possible, with no signs of spread.
- **Deeper or more complex melanoma:** Urgent referral to a specialist for wider excision, possible sentinel node biopsy, and further planning.
- **Recurrent melanoma or suspected spread:** Urgent specialist referral for staging.

### Long-term follow-up after diagnosis

Follow-up is structured by stage and mostly involves regular skin checks and examination of lymph nodes. Your doctor will coordinate this.

| Stage | Follow-up schedule |
|-------|-------------------|
| Stage I | Annually for 10 years |
| Stage Ib or IIa | Every 6 months for 2 years, then annually to 10 years; plus 6-monthly lymph node ultrasound for 2 years |
| Stage IIb–IIId | Every 4 months for 2 years, then 6-monthly in year 3, then annually to 10 years; plus 6-monthly lymph node ultrasound for 2 years |
| Stage IV | Specialist-led, with additional visits as needed |

About 80% of recurrences happen within the first 3 years. At every check, your doctor will examine the original site, all lymph node areas, and your whole skin for new lesions. No routine blood tests or scans are needed unless something is found on examination.

Vitamin D supplementation may be considered.

## Psychosocial Support

A melanoma diagnosis can bring significant anxiety. Common concerns include body image, fear of recurrence, and worry about outdoor activities. Your doctor can help connect you with support services if needed.

---

## Sun Safety (Prevention and Ongoing Care)

Reducing UV exposure lowers the risk of new melanomas:

- Avoid direct sun during peak UV hours
- **Slip** on clothing, **Slop** on sunscreen (SPF 50+, applied 20 minutes before going outside and reapplied every 2 hours), **Slap** on a hat, **Wrap** on sunglasses
- Avoid sunbeds and artificial UV sources

**Medications that can increase sun sensitivity:** tetracyclines, fluoroquinolones, sulfonamides, NSAIDs, diuretics (frusemide, hydrochlorothiazide), retinoids, sulfonylureas, some antihypertensives (amiodarone, diltiazem), antipsychotics, immunosuppressants, quinine-containing medications. Tell your doctor if you take any of these.

First-degree relatives of someone with melanoma have an elevated risk — encourage them to have regular skin checks too.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Care

Go back to your doctor promptly if:
- A known lesion changes in size, shape, colour, or starts bleeding
- You notice a new skin lesion that worries you
- Any lump appears under the skin, particularly near where a melanoma was previously removed
