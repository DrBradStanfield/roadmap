# X-ray Musculoskeletal (MSK) – Adult

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers when X-ray imaging may be appropriate for musculoskeletal conditions in adults. Before requesting an X-ray, your doctor will consider the clinical indication, potential radiation exposure, and whether imaging will change management.

---

## Important Note on Radiation

X-ray involves ionising radiation. Your healthcare provider will only refer for imaging when the expected benefit outweighs the radiation risk.

---

## Spine

### Emergencies — Do Not Delay for Imaging

Call an ambulance or go to the emergency department immediately if you have back pain with any of the following — these may indicate **cauda equina syndrome** or **spinal infection**, which require urgent hospital assessment:

**Cauda equina syndrome:**
- Sphincter disturbance (problems controlling bladder or bowel)
- Gait disturbance
- Saddle anaesthesia (numbness in the inner thighs and around the genitals)
- Loss of bowel or bladder control
- Abnormal reflexes, or loss of muscle power or feeling in the legs

**Spinal infection** (osteomyelitis, discitis, or epidural abscess):
- Fever, malaise, or looking generally unwell alongside back pain
- Risk factors include penetrating injury, intravenous drug use, recent spinal surgery, or underlying immunosuppression

---

### When Your Doctor May Refer for an Urgent Spine X-ray (within 2 weeks)

Your healthcare provider may recommend an urgent spine X-ray if you have:

- Acute localised back pain where an **osteoporotic fracture** is suspected, and you have either known osteoporosis or risk factors (including prolonged or recent corticosteroid use)
- Back pain with any of the following **red flags for malignant bone disease:**
  - History of cancer — particularly cancers that commonly spread to bone (breast, prostate, lung)
  - Unexplained weight loss (more than 5% of body weight over 3–6 months)
  - Pain that wakes you from sleep
  - Pain made worse, not better, by lying down
  - Persistent pain unrelated to movement

---

### When Your Doctor May Refer for a Routine Spine X-ray (within 6 weeks)

- Suspected **ankylosing spondylitis**, when you meet criteria for specialist assessment — your doctor may request a lumbar spine and sacroiliac joint X-ray as a prerequisite for referral
- When recommended by a specialist or radiologist for follow-up

---

### When Imaging Is Not Typically the First Step

Your doctor may advise against or defer an X-ray if you have:

- **Progressive neurological deficit** — specialist orthopaedic or neurology input is needed instead of plain X-ray
- **Recent spinal trauma** — imaging may be arranged through your ACC claim
- **Scoliosis** — if referred to orthopaedics, the specialist will arrange any required imaging
- **Acute uncomplicated spine pain** (within the first 6 weeks) with no red flags — early X-ray rarely changes management; discuss with your doctor whether imaging adds value
- **Suspected spinal osteoarthritis or chronic back pain** without red flags — X-ray typically does not change conservative management

---

## Upper Limb (Humerus, Elbow, Forearm, Wrist, Hand)

### Emergencies — Seek Same-Day Assessment

Go to the emergency department or seek urgent medical assessment if:

- **Septic arthritis** is suspected — a hot, swollen, acutely painful joint that may indicate joint infection
- **Osteomyelitis** is suspected — deep bone pain with fever or systemic illness

These conditions require urgent assessment; imaging should not delay treatment.

---

### When Your Doctor May Refer for an Urgent Upper Limb X-ray (within 2 weeks)

- New, unexplained bony swelling or joint deformity
- Pain with any of the following **red flags:**
  - Persistent deep pain unrelated to activity
  - Unexplained pain at night
  - History of cancer known to spread to bone
- Pain in a joint where you have had a joint replacement (arthroplasty)
- Undiagnosed joint pain lasting more than 4 weeks, where the presentation is **not typical for osteoarthritis**, including:
  - Age under 45 years
  - Recent trauma
  - Signs of joint inflammation (swelling, redness, warmth, morning stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes)
  - Significant nocturnal pain or pain at rest
  - Weight loss or systemic symptoms
  - Inability to bear weight
- Swelling in the hand and/or wrist involving multiple joints for more than 6 weeks, where **inflammatory arthritis** is suspected

---

### When X-ray May Be Done if Referred for Surgery

Your doctor may request an X-ray of an arthritic joint **only** if you are being referred for an orthopaedic surgical opinion and no X-ray has been done in the last 6 months.

---

### When X-ray Is Not Typically Indicated

Your doctor may advise against immediate X-ray for:

- **Suspected osteoarthritis** without the features above — managed clinically
- Recent pain onset without red flags, with mild symptoms and normal examination
- **Epicondylitis (tennis or golfer's elbow)** — managed conservatively; diagnosis is clinical
- **Acute gout** — managed medically; X-ray is generally only considered if inflammation persists beyond 12 weeks

---

## Pelvis, Hip, and Lower Limb (Femur, Knee, Tibia/Fibula, Ankle, Foot)

### Emergencies — Seek Same-Day Assessment

Go to the emergency department or seek urgent medical assessment if:

- **Septic arthritis** is suspected — acutely hot, swollen, severely painful joint
- **Osteomyelitis** is suspected — deep bone pain with fever or systemic illness

---

### When Your Doctor May Refer for an Urgent Lower Limb X-ray (within 2 weeks)

- New, unexplained bony swelling or joint deformity
- Pain with any of the following **red flags:**
  - Persistent deep pain unrelated to activity
  - Unexplained night pain
  - History of cancer known to spread to bone
- Pain in a joint where you have had a joint replacement (arthroplasty)
- Undiagnosed joint pain lasting more than 4 weeks, where the presentation is **not typical for osteoarthritis**, including:
  - Age under 45 years
  - Recent trauma
  - Signs of inflammation (swelling, redness, warmth, morning stiffness lasting more than 30 minutes)
  - Significant nocturnal pain or pain at rest
  - Weight loss or systemic symptoms
  - Inability to bear weight
- Swelling in the ankle or foot involving multiple joints for more than 6 weeks, where **inflammatory arthritis** is suspected

---

### When X-ray May Be Done if Referred for Hip Surgery

If you have hip osteoarthritis with significant functional impairment and are being referred to orthopaedics for consideration of surgery, your doctor may request an AP pelvis and lateral X-ray of the affected hip — only if no X-ray has been done in the last 6 months. A lateral X-ray of the opposite hip is not routinely needed.

---

### When X-ray Is Not Typically Indicated

Your doctor may advise against immediate X-ray for:

- **Suspected osteoarthritis** without the features listed above
- **Acute gout** — managed medically; X-ray considered only if inflammation persists beyond 12 weeks
- **Trochanteric bursitis (gluteus medius tendinopathy)** — managed clinically
- **Osgood-Schlatter disease** — history and examination are usually sufficient to diagnose
- **Bursitis** — history and examination are usually sufficient
- **Sinding-Larsen-Johansson disease (patellar tendinitis / Jumper's knee)** — history and examination are usually sufficient
- **Patellofemoral pain syndrome** (including chondromalacia patella)
- **Bunions (hallux valgus)** — managed conservatively
- **Flat feet**
- **Plantar fasciitis** — history and examination are usually sufficient; managed conservatively
- **Sever's disease** — history and examination are usually sufficient

---

## Trauma-Related Injuries

If your musculoskeletal condition resulted from an accident, X-ray imaging may be arranged through an accident compensation pathway (such as ACC in New Zealand). There is generally no time limit on imaging related to a covered injury. Your healthcare provider will advise on the appropriate referral route.
