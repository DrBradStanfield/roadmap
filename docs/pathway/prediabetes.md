# Prediabetes

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of prediabetes in adults who are not pregnant.

---

## Important Note on HbA1c Thresholds (From July 2026)

New Zealand's national diagnostic thresholds for HbA1c are changing effective 1 July 2026:

- **Normal** — HbA1c less than 42 mmol/mol
- **Prediabetes** — HbA1c 42 to 47 mmol/mol (previously 41 to 49 mmol/mol)
- **Diabetes** — HbA1c 48 mmol/mol or greater (previously 50 mmol/mol or greater)

There is no change to glucose diagnostic criteria for diabetes.

---

## What Is Prediabetes?

Prediabetes is diagnosed when blood sugar is elevated but not yet in the diabetic range. It is defined as any one of:

- HbA1c 41 to 49 mmol/mol
- Fasting glucose 6.1 to 6.9 mmol/L (impaired fasting glucose)
- 2-hour glucose on a 75 g glucose tolerance test 7.8 to 11 mmol/L (impaired glucose tolerance)

Prediabetes affects around 20% of adults. It is commonly associated with obesity (particularly abdominal), high blood pressure, and abnormal blood fats. Despite the name, prediabetes is not a benign condition — it carries an increased risk of cardiovascular disease and early death, even before progressing to type 2 diabetes.

---

## Risk Factors for Diabetes

Higher-risk individuals include those with:

- Non-European ethnicity
- Previous prediabetes or type 2 diabetes in remission
- Family history of type 2 diabetes onset before age 40
- Signs of insulin resistance (polycystic ovarian syndrome, acanthosis nigricans, high blood pressure, abnormal lipids)
- History of cardiovascular disease
- Long-term corticosteroid or antipsychotic use
- Post-transplant status
- Obesity:
  - BMI above 30 kg/m² (or above 27 kg/m² in Indo-Asian ethnicity)
  - Waist circumference above 94 cm in men or above 80 cm in women

Higher-risk features that your doctor will watch for include HbA1c above 45 mmol/mol, a rising HbA1c trend, and age under 40.

---

## Monitoring

Your doctor will:

- Measure HbA1c (or fasting glucose in some situations) at least once per year to check whether prediabetes has progressed to type 2 diabetes
- Repeat HbA1c after 3 months of lifestyle changes, then every 6 to 12 months depending on risk level
- Check weight, diet, physical activity, and cardiovascular risk factors at each review

**Note:** HbA1c can be unreliable in people with haemoglobinopathies (e.g. sickle cell anaemia, thalassaemia), altered red cell turnover (bleeding, haemolysis, iron deficiency), or following a blood transfusion. Your doctor may use fasting glucose instead in these situations.

---

## Management

Management of prediabetes is primarily through lifestyle change. Medication may be added in some cases.

### Lifestyle Changes

Lifestyle modification is the most important treatment for prediabetes. Your doctor will help you set goals in these areas:

**Weight loss**
- This is the cornerstone of management
- A 5 to 10% loss of body weight may reverse prediabetes

**Healthy eating**
- A dietitian referral can help with individualised advice, especially if BMI is above 30
- Keeping a food diary can help identify patterns

**Physical activity**
- Aim for 150 minutes of moderate to high-intensity aerobic exercise per week, spread over at least 3 days, with no more than 2 consecutive days without exercise
- Add resistance (strength) exercise at least twice per week
- Avoid sitting for longer than 30 minutes at a time
- Even small increases in activity help — a brisk 5-to-6-minute daily walk is associated with 4 additional years of life; adding just 500 steps per day is associated with up to 10% reduction in mortality
- Exercise intensity may need adjustment if you have other health conditions (e.g. heart disease)

**Smoking cessation**
- Stopping smoking improves overall cardiovascular and metabolic health

**Reducing alcohol intake**
- Excess alcohol impairs glucose metabolism and contributes to weight gain

### Medication — Metformin

Metformin is the only medication recommended for routine management of prediabetes. It is not a substitute for lifestyle change — it works best alongside it.

Doses of 250 to 850 mg twice a day have been shown to halve the progression to type 2 diabetes.

Your doctor may consider metformin if:

- After 6 to 12 months, your HbA1c continues to rise despite lifestyle changes
- Your HbA1c is close to the diabetes threshold and not falling (i.e. 46 to 49 mmol/mol)

Typical starting dose: 500 mg once or twice daily with food, increased gradually as tolerated to a maximum of 850 mg twice daily. A lower starting dose of 250 mg daily may be used if there are tolerability concerns.

*Always discuss medication decisions with your doctor. Metformin is a prescription medicine and requires medical supervision.*

---

## Complications and Co-existing Conditions to Manage

Prediabetes is an independent risk factor for cardiovascular disease. Your doctor will also assess and manage:

- **High blood pressure (hypertension)**
- **Cardiovascular risk** — reducing HbA1c to 45 mmol/mol or below appears to reduce cardiovascular disease risk
- **Obesity-related conditions** — fatty liver disease, obstructive sleep apnoea

---

## When to See Your Doctor

Discuss prediabetes management with your doctor, who will tailor a plan to your situation. Seek earlier review if:

- Your symptoms change or worsen
- You develop symptoms suggestive of diabetes (increased thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, blurred vision)
- You have concerns about your blood sugar levels or medication

*This information is for general guidance only. Always consult your doctor before making changes to your treatment or lifestyle plan.*
