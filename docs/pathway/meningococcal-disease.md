# Meningococcal Disease

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the recognition and emergency management of acute meningococcal disease — including meningococcal meningitis and sepsis — in adults and children.

> **This is a medical emergency.** If meningococcal disease is suspected, call an ambulance immediately. Do not wait for a rash to appear.

---

## Red Flags — Call an Ambulance Immediately

If meningococcal disease is suspected, your doctor will call the emergency services at once. Do not drive yourself to hospital.

Features that raise urgent concern include:

- Sudden-onset severe headache with fever, neck stiffness, or photophobia
- Petechial or purpuric rash (small red/purple spots or blotches that do not fade when pressed)
- Signs of rapid deterioration: drowsiness, confusion, delirium, or unconsciousness
- Prostration (unwillingness or inability to stand) or signs of shock
- In infants: high-pitched cry, bulging fontanelle, poor feeding, fever with cold hands/feet, irritability

The rash may not be present early in the illness and is absent in about one third of cases — absence of rash does not rule out meningococcal disease.

---

## Who Is Affected

Meningococcal disease can affect anyone, but is most common in:

- Infants under 1 year old (50% of cases are in people under 15)
- Māori and Pacific peoples, who have significantly higher rates and mortality
- People from areas of high deprivation
- People in communal living situations (hostels, boarding houses)

Common serotypes are B and C. Serotype W can affect older adults and tends to cause more severe, atypical illness (including abdominal pain, pneumonia, or septic arthritis).

---

## Recognising the Illness

### In Infants

Symptoms are often non-specific:

- Fever, pallor, or cold peripheries
- Irritability, high-pitched crying
- Poor feeding or not waking for feeds
- Lethargy
- Rash (petechial or purpuric, but may be absent)
- Vomiting or diarrhoea
- Bulging fontanelle, neck retraction, back arching

### In Older Children and Adults

**Meningitis symptoms:**
- Severe headache
- Photophobia (sensitivity to bright light)
- Neck stiffness
- Vomiting
- Progression to confusion or coma

**Septicaemia symptoms:**
- Fever
- Leg pain, myalgia, or arthralgia
- Rash (petechial or purpuric — red/purple spots or blotches)
- Prostration or extreme lethargy
- Progression to shock

Consider meningococcal group W if atypical features are present (abdominal pain, pneumonia, septic arthritis, endocarditis).

---

## What Your Doctor Will Do

Your doctor will assess you urgently. This is not a condition where watchful waiting is appropriate — speed of treatment is critical.

Your doctor will:

- Take a rapid history including any contact with a confirmed or suspected case, prior antibiotic use, and the timeline of symptoms
- Examine for signs of sepsis, rash, neck stiffness, altered conscious state, and fontanelle in infants
- Call an ambulance immediately if meningococcal disease is suspected

Signs of sepsis your doctor checks in adults include:

- Blood pressure below 90 mmHg systolic (or more than 40 mmHg below baseline)
- Heart rate 130 beats per minute or more
- Fever above 38°C or temperature below 36°C
- Breathing rate 25 or more breaths per minute
- Oxygen saturation below 92%
- Confusion or drowsiness

---

## Emergency Treatment Before Hospital

Your doctor may begin treatment before the ambulance arrives. The key steps are:

1. **Call emergency services** — ring 111 or the St John healthcare professional line
2. **IV or IO fluids** — given before antibiotics if the patient is in shock (shock typically worsens after antibiotics without fluids)
3. **Antibiotics** — given urgently if the patient is seriously ill or transfer will take more than 15–20 minutes:
   - **Ceftriaxone** (first-line): 2 g IV or IM for adults; 50 mg/kg (up to 2 g) for children under 30 kg
   - **Benzylpenicillin** (if ceftriaxone unavailable): 2.4 g IV or IM for adults; 50 mg/kg (up to 2 g) for children
4. **Do not delay transport** to repeat blood draws or gain IV access

---

## Public Health Obligations

Meningococcal disease is a notifiable disease. Your doctor is legally required to notify the Medical Officer of Health if the disease is suspected — though notification must never delay clinical care or transfer to hospital.

Public Health will then:

- Identify and follow up close contacts
- Determine who qualifies as a close contact
- Prescribe and administer prophylactic antibiotics (rifampicin, ciprofloxacin, or ceftriaxone) and vaccination where indicated

**If you think you may have been a close contact of a meningococcal case:** contact your doctor, who will contact Public Health for guidance. Do not expect prophylaxis to be prescribed until Public Health has confirmed you meet the definition of a close contact.

---

## Close Contact Management

If you have been in close contact with someone with confirmed or suspected meningococcal disease:

- See your doctor promptly
- Your doctor will contact Public Health for advice before prescribing anything
- If prophylaxis is indicated, antibiotics (and possibly vaccination) will be arranged — usually through Public Health or at the request of Public Health
- Follow up as directed

---

## After Hospital

Recovery from meningococcal disease can be prolonged. Some people experience ongoing complications including hearing loss, limb damage from tissue death, or neurological sequelae. Your treating team will arrange follow-up and rehabilitation as needed.

---

## Vaccination

Vaccination is available for serogroups A, B, C, Y, and W. Ask your doctor about:

- **Bexsero** — meningococcal B vaccine
- **MenQuadFi / MedQuaFi** — quadrivalent vaccine (A, C, Y, W)

Vaccination is particularly important for infants, adolescents, and people with certain medical conditions.
