# Testosterone Deficiency in Men

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: May 2023.*

Confirming significant testosterone deficiency in men can be difficult, particularly in older men. Diagnosis rests on symptoms and signs together with blood tests that show clearly and consistently low testosterone.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## Red Flags

- **Pituitary disease** is the red flag in this pathway.
- Symptoms and signs of a mass near the pituitary gland include:
  - Vision changes, such as subtle changes in sharpness or an unexplained pattern of vision loss.
  - Double vision.
  - Headaches, which may be mild.
- If there is a visual field defect or signs of reduced pituitary function (hypopituitarism), your doctor may mark the specialist referral as urgent.

---

## Background

- Testosterone levels decline with age. Total testosterone is below the normal laboratory range in about 25% of men aged more than 70 years, and 50% aged more than 80 years.
- Low testosterone can be **caused by**:
  - Problems in the testes: trauma, radiation, infection.
  - Pituitary diseases, including tumours.
  - Pituitary suppression by medications, especially opioids.
- Low testosterone can be **associated with**:
  - Obesity.
  - Obstructive sleep apnoea.
  - Type 2 diabetes.
  - Liver or kidney disease.
  - Medications, e.g. glucocorticoids, anabolic steroids, opiates.
- There are no agreed evidence-based clinical markers or blood tests to define androgen deficiency in men.
- Diagnosis is based on symptoms and signs in combination with testing that shows unequivocally and consistently low serum total testosterone (less than 5 nmol/L).
- The potential risks of long-term testosterone treatment are largely unknown, particularly relating to prostate cancer and vascular disease.

---

## Assessment

### Symptoms and signs of testosterone deficiency

- Low libido.
- Erectile dysfunction, reduced morning erections.
- Breast tissue enlargement (gynaecomastia).
- Hot flushes, night sweats.
- Reduced energy, muscle bulk and strength, body hair, bone density (or osteoporosis), and testicular size. Testes may be normal size with pituitary disease.

### Other questions your doctor may ask

- **Fertility:** whether fertility is your main concern, and whether the frequency of intercourse is adequate to achieve pregnancy.
- **How long the problem has been present:** any delay in puberty, ambiguous genitalia or undescended testes, whether you have ever fathered a child, past mumps orchitis or testicular trauma (e.g. torsion, sports injuries), when you last felt you had a normal libido, and how long erectile dysfunction has been a problem.
- **Osteoporosis risk**, because this will influence whether or not to treat.

### Risk factors and contributors

- Obesity and diabetes.
- Pituitary disease, e.g. prolactinoma, hypophysitis, pituitary tumour, iron overload syndromes or haemochromatosis.
- Previous chemotherapy or radiation.
- Mumps orchitis (infection as an adult).
- Testicular trauma.
- Long-term use of medicines such as anabolic steroids, opioids, glucocorticoids, ketoconazole, or statins.
- Other chronic disease, e.g. cirrhosis, kidney failure, HIV, sleep disorders.
- Alcohol and marijuana abuse.
- Idiopathic hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism.
- Other illness associated with ageing.

**Congenital conditions** that can contribute to low testosterone:
- Klinefelter's syndrome: small testes, wide hips, tall stature, long limbs, gynaecomastia.
- Kallmann's syndrome: your doctor may look for other midline defects and check your sense of smell.
- Myotonic dystrophy: progressive muscle weakness, typical facial appearance, stiffness, cataracts at a young age, heart defects.

### Examination

Your doctor may check:
- Height, weight and BMI.
- Visual fields and double vision.
- Breast tissue (gynaecomastia).
- Body hair changes.
- Testicular size. Testes are abnormal when less than 4 cm in length or less than 20 mL in volume. Testes may be normal size with pituitary disease.

### Blood tests

Testosterone is **not measured routinely**. Your doctor may order it if the clinical assessment suggests testosterone deficiency.
- Morning serum testosterone: a fasting sample taken before 9.00 am.
- If the level is low, the test is repeated with follicle-stimulating hormone (FSH) and luteinising hormone (LH).

### Interpreting results

Results are interpreted in the context of the clinical picture. Your doctor may consider further tests:
- **Low testosterone with low or normal LH:** consider pituitary disease. Tests may include morning plasma cortisol, free thyroxine, and prolactin. Prolactin testing is indicated for male secondary hypogonadism (testosterone less than 5 nmol/L with normal LH).
- **Low testosterone with high LH and FSH:** consider Klinefelter's syndrome and a chromosome test (karyotype).
- **Equivocal levels:** repeat in 3 months' time.

## Management

- If you are overweight, weight loss improves gonadal function and general health.
- Treating or optimising chronic disease (e.g. sleep apnoea, type 2 diabetes, obesity) can help to improve testosterone level.
- Your doctor may consider reducing corticosteroids or opiate pain relief.

### Before testosterone replacement therapy

Treatment is started only on the recommendation of an endocrinologist or other suitably qualified specialist.

**Contraindications:**
- Advanced, metastatic, or incurable prostate cancer.
- Breast cancer.

**Precautions:**
- Undiagnosed prostate abnormalities found on examination, with or without a raised PSA.
- Severe lower urinary tract symptoms (International Prostate Symptom Score more than 19).
- Untreated polycythaemia (too many red blood cells).
- Untreated severe obstructive sleep apnoea.
- Unstable or inadequately treated heart disease, e.g. poorly controlled heart failure or ischaemia, recent cardiovascular events.
- When fertility is desired.
- When subject to occupational drug testing.

**Treatment considerations:**
- Testosterone treatment in men aged more than 65 years with serum testosterone between 6 and 9 nmol/L has little benefit and may cause harm.
- Testosterone should not be started based on a blood level without compelling clinical symptoms.
- Replacing testosterone too early can complicate investigation of secondary hypogonadism, or may be inappropriate (e.g. if a prolactinoma is the cause). Long-term replacement may cause testicular shrinkage and reduced sperm production.
- Before starting testosterone in men aged more than 50 years, your doctor may arrange an FBC to exclude polycythaemia and assess for prostate cancer.
- If fertility is the main concern, your doctor may consider a semen analysis, repeated in 3 to 6 months or as clinically indicated.

### Testosterone replacement options

Your doctor or specialist may consider:
- **Oral testosterone undecanoate 40 mg capsules:** best taken with food. Can be useful to induce puberty, or for older men, when lower target levels of testosterone are often adequate. The usual adult replacement dose is 80 mg twice daily.
- **Injectable testosterone esters:** Sustanon 250 mg or testosterone cipionate 200 mg, given as an intramuscular injection every 3 to 4 weeks.
  - The aim is a trough testosterone level (before the next injection) in the lower half of the normal range.
  - The dose should not be increased on the basis of nonspecific symptoms, e.g. fatigue, lethargy.
- **Testosterone undecanoate injections** are another injectable form.
- **Testosterone implants** require a minor surgical procedure and can be used under specialist supervision.
- **Testosterone gels.**

Testosterone patches are being discontinued.

### Monitoring on testosterone therapy

- An FBC every 6 to 12 months for all patients, as testosterone therapy may cause polycythaemia.
- For men aged more than 50 years on testosterone therapy, your doctor will discuss prostate cancer screening. Studies of low statistical power have not shown increased risk compared with men of the same age with normal testosterone.

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you to an endocrinologist (non-urgent) if:
- Pituitary disease is suspected. The referral may be marked urgent if there is a visual field defect or hypopituitarism.
- Early morning testosterone is low in 2 or more test results.
- Testosterone replacement therapy is being considered. Your doctor may instead seek endocrinology advice.

## Key Concepts for Patients

- Testosterone naturally falls with age, and a low result alone does not mean treatment is needed.
- Diagnosis needs both symptoms and consistently low morning testosterone on repeat testing.
- Testosterone treatment is started only on a specialist's recommendation, and long-term risks are largely unknown.
- Always discuss any new symptoms or medication questions with your doctor before making changes.
