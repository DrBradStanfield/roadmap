# Ring Pessaries

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the use of ring pessaries for pelvic organ prolapse — including fitting, routine checks, and managing complications.

---

## Background

A ring pessary is the first-line pessary option for pelvic organ prolapse. It provides support for the prolapse and is an effective long-term management option. It should not cause pain or discomfort, and can be used in women who are sexually active.

Ring pessaries are usually checked every 6 months.

A ring pessary is particularly useful if you:

- Are not fit for, or do not want, surgery
- Are planning to have children, or have not completed your family
- Experience symptoms only intermittently — for example, when exercising

---

## Fitting and Inserting

Your doctor will assess you before fitting a ring pessary. More than 85% of people who choose pessary treatment are successfully fitted. It may take more than one attempt to find the right size.

**Before fitting, your doctor may:**

- Check for contraindications to estrogen cream (history of endometrial cancer or breast cancer)
- Advise you to use estrogen cream (Ovestin) on alternate nights for 2 weeks beforehand, if appropriate
- Perform a bimanual vaginal examination to check for pelvic masses
- Perform a speculum examination to check for any concerning changes to the cervix or vaginal tissue
- Take a cervical smear if you are under 70 and one is due

**Risk factors for fitting difficulties include:**

- Short vaginal length (less than 7 cm)
- Wide vaginal opening
- Previous hysterectomy or pelvic floor reconstructive surgery

**After fitting, your doctor will ask you to:**

- Walk around, bend, cough, and pass urine in the clinic to check the pessary sits correctly
- If the pessary falls out, a larger size is needed; if it causes discomfort or you cannot void, a smaller size is tried

**After a successful fitting, your doctor will:**

- Provide written information on how to remove, clean, and replace the pessary if you wish to remove it during intercourse or use it intermittently
- Advise that some increase in vaginal discharge is normal — report smelly or blood-stained discharge to your doctor
- Recommend ongoing use of estrogen cream (Ovestin) twice a week to reduce the risk of ulceration and bleeding
- Review you in 4 weeks, then every 6 months

---

## Ongoing Checks (Every 6 Months)

At each 6-monthly review, your doctor will check for:

- Difficulty passing urine or recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs) — may need a smaller ring
- Pain, discharge, or bleeding — may indicate infection or ulceration

Your doctor will remove and inspect the ring, then perform a speculum examination.

**Vaginal discharge** is common with a pessary and is usually not due to infection — but a swab may be taken if discharge is profuse or offensive.

**If there is early vaginal irritation or redness:**
- Your doctor may consider a smaller ring
- Estrogen cream (Ovestin) is prescribed on alternate nights for 4 to 6 weeks
- Review in 3 months

**If there is ulceration, bleeding, or ongoing redness:**
- The ring is removed
- Estrogen cream (Ovestin) is prescribed, with review after 4 to 6 weeks
- If healed, the pessary is refit (possibly a smaller size)
- If not healed, estrogen cream is continued with further review in 4 to 6 weeks
- If ulceration does not heal after 2 to 3 months of Ovestin, your doctor will refer you to gynaecology for biopsy

**Return to your doctor sooner** if you have difficulty passing urine or having a bowel motion, pain, discharge, or vaginal bleeding.

If the pessary is expelled between appointments, this is not an emergency — bring the pessary back to your doctor for resizing.

---

## Managing a Forgotten Ring Pessary

It is not uncommon to present with a ring that has been in place for longer than intended, and which may be embedded in the vaginal lining.

- Your doctor may consider estrogen cream (Ovestin) daily for 1 week or more to reduce inflammation and make removal easier
- If the ring is stuck and causing acute symptoms (pain or bleeding), urgent gynaecology assessment is needed
- If the ring cannot be easily removed but you are otherwise well, your doctor will refer you to gynaecology

---

## Estrogen Cream (Ovestin)

Ovestin (estriol cream) is used to maintain vaginal tissue health during pessary use. Your doctor may recommend:

- **Twice a week** for ongoing maintenance use (to reduce risk of ulceration and discharge)
- **Alternate nights for 2 weeks** before a new pessary is inserted (if no contraindications)
- **Daily for 1 week or more** to reduce inflammation before removing a forgotten or embedded ring
- **Alternate nights for 4 to 6 weeks** if early signs of vaginal irritation are present

Contraindications to estrogen cream include a history of endometrial cancer, or breast cancer (seek specialist advice before prescribing in this case).

---

## When Gynaecology Referral May Be Needed

Your doctor may refer you to gynaecology if:

- A stuck ring is causing acute symptoms (pain or bleeding) — urgent referral
- The ring cannot be easily removed — non-acute referral
- You need a pessary fitted and your doctor is not trained to do this
- Ulceration does not heal after 2 to 3 months of Ovestin — referral for biopsy
- A well-fitted ring pessary has not adequately managed the prolapse
