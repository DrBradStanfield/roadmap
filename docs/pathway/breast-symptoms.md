# Breast Symptoms

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to breast symptoms — including lumps, nipple changes, nipple discharge, skin changes, and breast pain — and how doctors assess whether further investigation or referral is needed.

---

## Red Flags — See a Doctor Promptly

The following features raise concern for breast cancer and require prompt medical assessment:

- A discrete breast or axillary (armpit) lump, ulceration, skin dimpling, or breast distortion
- Persistent nipple eczema, ulceration, or nipple retraction/distortion that has developed within the last 3 months
- Unilateral (one-sided) nipple discharge that is bloody, clear, or serous (watery-yellow) — green or yellow discharge is **not** a red flag
- Breast inflammation or redness that is **not responding to treatment**, especially without fever
- Repeated consultations about the same breast symptoms (other than pain)

**Note:** Systemic symptoms (e.g., fatigue, weight loss) are unusual in breast cancer and alone do not increase concern beyond a lump being present.

**Breast cancer also occurs in men.** About 1% of breast cancers are in men. Men can have mammograms.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a detailed history and examine both breasts. Here is what to expect:

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- **Lumps:** location and side, when first noticed, any changes over time, relationship to your menstrual cycle or hormones, associated pain or discharge, nearby swollen lymph nodes
- **Nipple changes:** location, duration, whether the nipple has become retracted or inverted, any skin/areolar changes, ulceration, or bleeding
- **Nipple discharge:** which side, volume, colour (bloodstained, clear, serous — these matter; green/yellow less so), whether it occurs spontaneously
- **Skin changes:** location, duration, description, any itch or pain
- **Breast pain:** cyclical or non-cyclical, local or diffuse, one-sided or both sides, severity, any relationship to arm activity or trauma
- **Past breast history:** previous imaging (mammogram, ultrasound), surgeries, implants
- **Family history:** breast and ovarian cancer in first- and second-degree relatives, and at what age

**Family history risk categories:**
- *High risk* (rare, < 1% of women): gene-positive (BRCA1, BRCA2, TP53, or E-cadherin); or two or more relatives on same side plus an additional risk factor (e.g., relative diagnosed under 40, male relative with breast cancer, same relative had both breast and ovarian cancer, Ashkenazi Jewish ancestry)
- *Moderate risk* (< 4%): one first-degree relative under 50; two second-degree relatives on same side, one under 50; two first-degree relatives aged 50–70
- *Average risk* (95% of women): no significant family history, or family history in one older first-degree relative, or second-degree relatives only

Your doctor will also ask about:
- Hormonal status and menstrual history
- Reproductive history (pregnancies, breastfeeding)
- Medications, particularly hormonal contraceptives, hormone therapy, antidepressants, or fertility treatments
- For men with breast enlargement (gynaecomastia): medication use, alcohol, any testicular changes

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will perform a bilateral breast examination, with you in both sitting and lying positions. They will:

- Visually inspect both breasts and check for contour changes, skin dimpling, or tethering when you raise your arms or place your hands on your hips
- Palpate both breasts and into the armpit and neck for swollen lymph nodes
- Document any lump's size, shape, consistency, mobility, tenderness, and exact location (using "o'clock" position and distance from nipple)
- Attempt to reproduce any nipple discharge

Concerning features on examination include: a lump that is firm to hard, fixed rather than mobile, unilateral, off-centre from the nipple, or associated with skin dimpling, nipple discharge, or swollen lymph nodes.

### Investigations

Your doctor will only arrange laboratory tests if clinically appropriate. For example, if there is bilateral multi-duct galactorrhoea (milky discharge unrelated to breastfeeding), they may check:
- Pregnancy test (if applicable)
- TSH (thyroid function)
- Prolactin (if specific indications are met)

Prolactin testing in primary care is appropriate for: galactorrhoea more than 12 months after stopping breastfeeding; prolonged irregular periods (> 6 months, unexplained by other causes); or male secondary hypogonadism. It is **not** routinely indicated for gynaecomastia, mastalgia, or subfertility without menstrual changes.

## Management

Management depends on the findings.

### If Red Flags Are Present

Your doctor will refer you urgently to a breast clinic for **triple assessment** (clinical examination + imaging + biopsy), marking the referral "high suspicion of cancer." This applies if:
- Any red flag features are present (see above)
- Suspected inflammatory breast cancer — a rare, aggressive cancer characterised by diffuse redness and swelling involving at least a third of the breast, skin dimpling (peau d'orange), and erythema not responding to antibiotics. Often no fever. Can occur during pregnancy or breastfeeding.
- Suspected Paget's disease of the breast — a scaly, raw, vesicular or ulcerated lesion beginning on the nipple spreading to the areola. May not have an associated lump.
- You have had prior breast surgery and develop new skin, breast, or armpit symptoms (other than pain)

### If Pain Is the Only Symptom

- If you are due for routine breast screening, your doctor may arrange a mammogram through BreastScreen.
- If you are anxious or outside the screening age range but there are no concerning features, your doctor may discuss whether private breast imaging would be reassuring. This can identify benign causes (cysts, fibrocystic changes, fat necrosis after injury).

### Likely Benign and Fibrocystic Changes

Your doctor may consider:
- Reassurance, lifestyle advice, and guidance on routine screening
- If you are under 30, breast cancer is very rare in this age group and lumps often have a hormonal cause. Your doctor may suggest waiting until after your next period before deciding on further investigation
- If there is hormonal thickening or nodularity: a routine mammogram (if due), treatment of pain, and review in 2–3 months. If it persists, onward referral for breast clinic advice

### Fibroadenoma

Fibroadenomas are benign, hormone-dependent lumps — more common in younger women. Most do not require treatment.

- If **less than 20 mm**: no action needed
- If **20–25 mm**: your doctor may refer you to a breast clinic if you are 20 years or older and the lump is 2–3 cm and not yet confirmed by imaging. They may also suggest changing from combined hormonal contraception to progesterone-only or intrauterine contraception, which can reduce fibroadenoma size
- If **greater than 30 mm**: non-urgent referral to a breast clinic for consideration of surgical removal

Your doctor will refer you for specialist breast clinic advice if there are any concerns about your symptoms, or if you remain worried about a lump.
