# Contraceptive Injection (Depo-Provera)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the use of the depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA) injection as a contraceptive — who it suits, how it works, how to start, and how to manage common issues.

---

## Background

The contraceptive injection is 150 mg of depot medroxyprogesterone acetate (DMPA), given as Depo-Provera. It is administered by deep intramuscular injection into the gluteal or deltoid muscle every 13 weeks.

DMPA works primarily by inhibiting ovulation. It also thickens cervical mucus to reduce sperm penetration and changes the endometrium to make it less suitable for implantation.

**Effectiveness:**
- 94% effective with typical use
- 99.8% effective with perfect use (failure rate 0.2%)

**Non-contraceptive benefits:** DMPA may also reduce the pain of dysmenorrhoea and endometriosis.

**Long-acting reversible contraceptives (LARCs) — such as intrauterine devices and the contraceptive implant — are recommended as a first-line contraceptive choice** due to their excellent efficacy and safety. The contraceptive injection is a reasonable alternative, particularly for those who cannot or do not wish to use a LARC.

---

## Contraindications

Your doctor will check whether DMPA is appropriate for you.

**Absolute contraindication (do not use):**
- Current breast cancer

**Relative contraindications (use with caution — your doctor will weigh risks and benefits):**
- Less than 6 weeks postpartum with other risk factors for venous thromboembolism (VTE)
- Multiple cardiovascular risk factors (e.g. smoking, diabetes, hypertension, obesity, abnormal cholesterol)
- Hypertension with vascular disease
- Current or previous ischaemic heart disease
- Stroke or transient ischaemic attack (TIA)
- History of VTE or being on anticoagulants
- Known thrombogenic mutations (e.g. Factor V Leiden, protein S deficiency)
- Positive antiphospholipid antibodies
- Major surgery planned
- Unexplained vaginal bleeding
- Completed treatment for breast cancer
- Chronic kidney disease (nephrotic syndrome, haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis)
- Decompensated cirrhosis
- Benign or malignant liver tumour

---

## Bone Density Considerations

DMPA may reduce bone mineral density; this is usually reversible when the injection is stopped. Your doctor will check for risk factors that could add to this effect.

**Major risk factors for osteoporosis:**
- BMI less than 20
- Current or past history of eating disorders
- Rheumatoid arthritis
- Family history of osteoporosis
- Current smoking
- More than 2 alcoholic drinks daily
- Medical conditions such as coeliac disease, hyperthyroidism, COPD, hyperparathyroidism
- Medications including: glucocorticoids (5 mg or more oral prednisone daily for more than 3 months), anticonvulsants, chemotherapy drugs, suppressive doses of thyroxine, lithium, methotrexate, pioglitazone, GnRH agonists, aromatase inhibitors

If major risk factors for reduced bone density develop during DMPA use, your doctor will advise switching to a different contraceptive method.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

1. Confirm full contraception counselling has taken place
2. Take a targeted history to check for contraindications and osteoporosis risk factors
3. Ask about any abnormal uterine bleeding (this should be investigated before starting)
4. Exclude current pregnancy (see criteria below)
5. Ask about previous DMPA injections and when the last one was given
6. Discuss the advantages, disadvantages, and alternatives
7. Calculate BMI
8. Check cervical screening is up to date
9. Offer STI screening if indicated

**Excluding current pregnancy — your doctor will consider pregnancy reasonably excluded if:**
- Within 5 days of the start of a normal menstrual period
- Within 5 days of abortion, miscarriage, ectopic pregnancy, or uterine evacuation
- No intercourse since the last normal menstrual period, childbirth, or pregnancy loss
- Consistently using a reliable contraceptive method
- Less than 21 days postpartum
- Fully breastfeeding, amenorrhoeic, and less than 6 months postpartum
- No intercourse for more than 21 days and a negative urine pregnancy test (sensitive to hCG 20 mIU/mL)

## Advantages and Disadvantages

### Advantages
- No daily compliance or invasive insertion required
- Can be given at most general practices
- Useful for those who cannot use oestrogen-containing contraception
- Useful for those with malabsorption conditions
- Effective even with liver-enzyme-inducing drugs (unlike some other methods)
- May help with heavy menstrual bleeding, dysmenorrhoea, and endometriosis
- May benefit those with sickle cell disease
- Safe during breastfeeding
- Not detectable by others
- Fewer contraindications and drug interactions than the combined oral contraceptive pill (COC)

### Disadvantages
- Requires an injection every 13 weeks
- Cannot be reversed once given
- No protection against sexually transmitted infections (STIs)
- Return to fertility may be delayed by up to 12 months after stopping

---

## Side Effects

- **Altered bleeding patterns** are common — 50–70% of people become amenorrhoeic (no periods) over time; up to 30% have irregular bleeding
- **Weight gain** — possible, particularly in those younger than 18 with a BMI of 30 or more. If weight gain exceeds 5% in the first 6 months, your doctor will consider alternative contraception
- **Reduced bone density** — usually reversible on stopping
- **Possible small increased risk of meningioma** — based on limited data; the average age of women in the relevant study was 57.6 years
- **Other reported side effects** include headaches, nausea, acne, decreased libido, mood swings, hot flushes, and vaginitis. These typically reduce over time

**Cancer risk notes:**
- No increased risk of ovarian or endometrial cancer; DMPA may offer some protection
- Weak association between current use and breast cancer — any increased risk is likely small and reduces after stopping
- Weak association between long-term use (5+ years) and cervical cancer — risk appears to reduce after stopping

## Management

### Starting the Injection

Your doctor may consider the following starting approaches:

**If not currently using contraception:**
- Traditional start: first injection during the first 5 days of the menstrual cycle
- Quick start method: injection at any time in the cycle, after excluding pregnancy. Additional contraceptive precautions (condoms) are required for 7 days. A follow-up pregnancy test at least 21 days after last unprotected intercourse is recommended

**After emergency contraception:**
- DMPA can be given immediately after the levonorgestrel emergency contraceptive pill. Additional precautions are required for 7 days

**After abortion or miscarriage:**
- If given within 5 days of ectopic pregnancy, miscarriage, abortion, or uterine evacuation — effective immediately, no additional precautions needed
- If given 5 or more days after — traditional or quick start method applies

**After childbirth (postpartum):**
- Effective immediately if given less than 21 days postpartum
- If 21 days or more after delivery — traditional or quick start method applies

### Subsequent Injections

- Injections are given every 13 weeks
- Early re-administration can occur from 10 weeks if needed
- If more than 14 weeks since the last injection, your doctor will exclude pregnancy before giving the next dose and advise additional contraceptive precautions for 7 days

### Managing Irregular Bleeding

Altered bleeding is common. Your doctor may consider:
- Investigating for underlying causes (pregnancy, STI, endometrial or cervical pathology)
- If bleeding persists beyond 3 months or a new pattern develops — further investigation including possible endometrial biopsy (particularly for those 45+ years, or younger with risk factors)
- If no underlying cause found, treatment options may include:
  - A combined oral contraceptive pill (COC) taken cyclically or continuously for up to 3 months (if medically eligible)
  - A COX-2 inhibitor or NSAID (e.g. celecoxib 100–200 mg twice daily or ibuprofen 400 mg two to three times daily for 5–10 days) or a short course of tranexamic acid
  - An early repeat DMPA injection from 10 weeks

If bleeding continues, your doctor may discuss switching to a different contraceptive method.

### When to Consider Switching

Your doctor may discuss switching if you:
- Experience side effects (troublesome bleeding, headaches, nausea, acne, decreased libido, mood swings, hot flushes) that do not settle over time
- Gain more than 5% of baseline body weight in the first 6 months
- Are planning a pregnancy (note potential delay in return to fertility)
- Reach age 50 — continuing after 50 is possible if you understand the benefits and risks
- Develop major risk factors for osteoporosis

### Stopping

- If you want to conceive, return to fertility may be delayed by up to 12 months after the last injection
- If you do not want to conceive, start an alternative contraceptive method before or at the time of the next scheduled injection — even if you have no periods

---

## Follow-Up

At review appointments, your doctor will:
- Confirm cervical screening is up to date
- Discuss HPV vaccination if relevant
- Advise condoms to prevent STIs
- Review the benefits, risks, and suitability of continuing DMPA every 2 years

---

## Common Concerns

Your doctor can advise that:
- There is no increased risk of pregnancy in those with higher body weight (though data is limited for BMI 40+)
- Efficacy is not reduced with liver-enzyme-inducing drugs
- DMPA can be used in those under 18 after considering benefits, risks, and alternatives
- Those over 50 may continue DMPA if they have discussed risks and benefits
- No causal association has been found between DMPA and venous thrombosis
- DMPA does not protect against STIs — condoms are still recommended
