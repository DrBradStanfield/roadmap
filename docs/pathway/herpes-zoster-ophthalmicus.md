# Herpes Zoster Ophthalmicus

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers herpes zoster ophthalmicus (shingles affecting the eye region) — a potentially sight-threatening condition that requires urgent treatment.

> **Important:** Herpes zoster ophthalmicus may be sight threatening and requires urgent treatment. Always seek medical assessment promptly.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Care

Go to the emergency department or seek same-day ophthalmology assessment if any of the following are present:

- Acutely inflamed red eye
- Corneal haziness
- Deteriorating or double vision

---

## Background

- Herpes zoster ophthalmicus can affect nearly every part of the eye and orbit.
- Most patients suffer only from a mucopurulent conjunctivitis and can be managed in primary care.
- The most common serious ocular complications are iritis and keratitis — these are sight-threatening and need urgent specialist review and treatment.

**Important note on antiviral dosing:** Aciclovir and valaciclovir doses must be reduced in patients with renal impairment. Aciclovir (the active metabolite of valaciclovir) is 80% eliminated by the kidneys. Accumulation can cause neurotoxicity. Ensure adequate hydration and watch for lethargy, confusion, agitation, or hallucination.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will check for the following:

**Features of herpes zoster ophthalmicus:**
- Pain around the eye
- Rash and skin eruption in the V1 dermatome, usually spreading up into the hairline
- Vesicles on the tip of the nose — this indicates involvement of the nasociliary branch and a higher risk of severe ocular disease
- Upper lid swelling and mild eye redness
- Early conjunctivitis and mild corneal changes (mucous plaques on the surface) are common in the first 10 days

**Visual acuity:** Your doctor will test vision in each eye separately. Central corneal changes usually reduce vision; peripheral corneal changes may leave vision intact.

**Serious complications — iritis and keratitis** (typically appear 10–14 days after rash onset):
- More likely if there are vesicles on the tip of the nose
- Associated with a higher risk of postherpetic neuralgia
- Signs include:
  - Reduced vision
  - Photophobia (light sensitivity)
  - White opacity on the cornea or hazy iris from corneal swelling
  - Marked redness with a small pupil

**Cranial nerve complications** (less common, but your doctor will also check for):
- Double vision
- Reduced eye movements
- Reduced vision
- Relative afferent pupillary defect (RAPD)

---

## Management

Your doctor will manage this condition as follows:

**1. Antiviral treatment — started immediately**

Every adult with V1 herpes zoster should receive oral antiviral medication straight away.

- **Valaciclovir** (preferred) — better absorption and three-times-daily dosing:
  - Valaciclovir 1 g orally, three times a day
  - Dose must be reduced if creatinine clearance is less than 50 mL/min (your doctor will calculate this)

- **Aciclovir** (alternative):
  - Aciclovir 800 mg orally, five times a day
  - Dose reduced with renal impairment (eGFR 10–25: three times daily; eGFR less than 10: twice daily)
  - Topical aciclovir is of no benefit

- The usual course is **7 days**, but local ophthalmologists recommend continuing for **10 days**.

**2. Antibiotic eye drops or ointment**

Your doctor may consider an antibiotic drop or ointment to soothe the eye and possibly prevent secondary bacterial infection, though this is often unnecessary.

**3. Pain relief**

Your doctor will provide appropriate analgesia.

**4. Follow-up**

- If **no red flags:** Your doctor will arrange a follow-up appointment at 10 days to check for serious complications (iritis, keratitis).
- If **red flags are present** (red eye, corneal haziness, vision deterioration, double vision): Your doctor will refer you for urgent acute ophthalmology assessment.

**5. Special situations**

If you are immunosuppressed or have been in contact with a pregnant woman who is not immune to varicella, your doctor will follow additional guidance for herpes zoster.
