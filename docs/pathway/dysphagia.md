# Dysphagia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: January 2024.*

Dysphagia is difficulty in the act of swallowing. Swallowing is usually divided into 3 stages: oral, pharyngeal, and oesophageal. The causes of dysphagia may be structural, neuromuscular, or both.

> **Always discuss your symptoms and any medication changes with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department if:**
- You cannot swallow your own saliva. This is a total obstruction and needs hospital assessment.
- Food or an object is stuck and will not go down, such as a soft food bolus or a chicken or fish bone. This needs hospital assessment.

**Red flags your doctor will look for:**
- Swallowing difficulty that keeps getting worse (progressive dysphagia).
- Silent aspiration. Some people do not cough when food or drink goes into the airway.
- A neck lump with a sore throat on one side, ear pain, a change in voice, or noisy breathing (stridor).

**Tell your doctor promptly** if you cough or choke when swallowing, or have signs of aspiration pneumonia: fever, cough, chest pain, or shortness of breath. Concerns about protecting the airway may need same-day specialist advice.

---

## Background

- Dysphagia is different from two other throat symptoms:
  - **Globus pharyngeus:** a painless feeling of a lump or fullness in the throat, while food and drink pass normally. It is often eased by eating or drinking, may be noticeable when swallowing saliva, and is often linked to reflux or muscle tension in the throat.
  - **Odynophagia:** pain when swallowing, usually from a problem in the throat (pharynx) or oesophagus.
- Difficulty with solid food usually points to a structural narrowing (mechanical obstruction). A narrowing that keeps getting worse eventually makes liquids hard to swallow too.
- Difficulty with liquids, or with liquids and solids together, usually points to a neuromuscular cause. People with a neurological cause, such as motor neuron disease or myasthenia gravis, may find liquids harder than solids.
- On its own, dysphagia is rarely associated with cancer.

## Assessment

### History

Your doctor may ask about:
- When symptoms started, how long they have lasted, and how fast they are getting worse.
- Whether you have trouble with solids, liquids, or both, and any foods you now avoid.
- Whether you can swallow your saliva.
- Weight loss or loss of appetite.
- Voice changes, or throat or ear pain on one side.
- Regurgitation or vomiting, early or delayed.
- Spitting, coughing, or vomiting blood.
- Reflux symptoms.
- Symptoms of a pharyngeal pouch (Zenker's diverticulum): bringing up undigested food, gurgling in the neck, bad breath, or a chronic cough.
- Other neurological symptoms, such as double vision, drooping eyelids, difficulty chewing, or neck or limb weakness.
- Smoking and alcohol.
- Medical history: previous head and neck surgery; radiotherapy to the head, neck, chest, or cervical spine; connective tissue, neurological, or thyroid disease; recent cancer treatment; unexplained or recurrent chest infections; chronic lung disease; reflux (GORD); atopy such as asthma, hay fever, or eczema; recent severe illness, such as an ICU admission.

### Medications

There are approximately 160 known medications that list dysphagia as a possible side effect. Examples include:
- Antipsychotics: movement side effects may impair swallowing.
- Anticonvulsants: may cause drowsiness and poor coordination, both of which may affect swallowing.
- Dry mouth can occur with ACE inhibitors, anti-arrhythmics, anticholinergics, antiemetics, antidepressants, antihistamines, and diuretics.
- Irritation of the oesophagus lining may come from steroids (causing oesophageal thrush), and from bisphosphonates taken by mouth, doxycycline, iron supplements, NSAIDs, and potassium chloride.
- Irritation is more likely when tablets are swallowed lying down, taken just before bed, or taken without enough fluid.

### Where the problem is

Some people can point to roughly where food gets stuck. Symptoms may help:
- **Oral stage:** difficulty controlling food, liquid, or saliva in the mouth; drooling; food getting stuck in the mouth; prolonged or difficult chewing; avoiding certain food textures.
- **Pharyngeal stage:** delay or difficulty starting a swallow; coughing, choking, or food coming up through the nose; unexplained or recurrent chest infections from silent aspiration; needing to swallow repeatedly to get food down; food sticking in the throat; a wet or "gurgly" voice.
- **Oesophageal stage:** discomfort or a blocked feeling in the lower neck or behind the breastbone. This is often poorly localised and may be felt in the lower neck.

### Possible causes

- **Oral or pharyngeal stage:**
  - Neuromuscular causes are more common. Sudden onset: stroke, brain injury. Onset over days or weeks: Guillain-Barré syndrome, myasthenia gravis (which can come and go), cranial nerve palsies. Onset over months: Parkinson's disease, motor neuron disease, multiple sclerosis.
  - Structural causes: tumours of the mouth, pharynx, or larynx; after surgery or radiotherapy; cricopharyngeal dysfunction, with or without a pharyngeal pouch (people with a pouch may regurgitate food even hours after eating); pressure from outside, such as a goitre; a foreign body.
- **Oesophageal stage:**
  - Structural causes: progressive dysphagia from a tumour or a stricture linked to reflux; intermittent dysphagia from rings and webs; inflammation such as eosinophilic oesophagitis (linked with asthma or atopy in younger people); a foreign body; trauma; medications; pressure from outside, such as enlarged lymph nodes in the chest.
  - Neuromuscular causes: progressive dysphagia from scleroderma or achalasia; intermittent dysphagia from reflux or dysmotility.
- **Pain on swallowing (odynophagia):**
  - Throat causes: infections (pharyngitis, tonsillitis, quinsy, epiglottitis, retropharyngeal abscess), mucositis, pharyngeal or laryngeal cancer, trauma or a foreign body.
  - Oesophageal causes: infections (Candida, herpes simplex virus, cytomegalovirus), drug-induced or radiation injury, Crohn's disease, skin conditions, severe reflux, dry mouth (from medicines or Sjögren's syndrome), oesophageal cancer.

### Cancer risk

Your doctor may consider symptoms and risk factors for head and neck, oesophageal, and stomach cancer. Having several risk factors or symptoms increases the risk.

- **Head and neck cancer symptoms:** a neck lump; a hoarse voice for more than 3 weeks; pain on swallowing; throat or ear pain, especially on one side; blocked or noisy breathing (stridor, stertor); weight loss.
- **Oesophageal and stomach cancer symptoms:** persistent upper abdominal pain or indigestion (heartburn most days for 3 weeks or longer); pain on swallowing; food sticking when swallowing; unexplained weight loss; vomiting blood or black stools; feeling full early; unexplained nausea and bloating; unexplained anaemia.
- **Risk factors:**
  - Head and neck cancer: smoking and alcohol; male sex; increasing age, especially older than 40 years; HPV infection; Epstein-Barr virus infection; radiation exposure; previous head and neck cancer.
  - Oesophageal cancer: smoking and alcohol; male sex; increasing age, especially older than 50 years; Barrett's oesophagus; obesity; reflux; caustic injury; achalasia.
  - Stomach cancer: smoking; increasing age, especially older than 50 years; *Helicobacter pylori*; previous partial gastrectomy; pernicious anaemia; family history; Asian descent.

### Examination

- Height and weight to work out BMI, and possibly the percentage of weight lost.
- Head and neck examination, including the mouth and throat, lymph nodes, and thyroid.
- A neurological examination if needed, including the cranial nerves. Your doctor may look for drooping eyelids, double vision, jaw or facial weakness, or tongue wasting or twitching; generalised weakness and twitching (motor neuron disease); weakness that tires with use (myasthenia gravis); or a hoarse voice, uneven palate movement, a "bovine" cough, or stridor (vagal or recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy).
- Occasionally, motor neuron disease and myasthenia gravis can present with dysphagia as the main or only symptom, with little else abnormal on examination.

### Investigations

**Your doctor may consider:**
- Blood tests: full blood count (FBC), ferritin, and kidney function.
- Other tests, such as a barium swallow or a videofluoroscopic swallowing study (VFSS), are not routinely arranged before referral. The specialist team is best placed to organise them.
- If food or an object is stuck, an X-ray is not advised, as it may delay specialist assessment and treatment.
- **Gastroscopy** is the first test for most people with oesophageal dysphagia, including older people. A normal gastroscopy does not rule out an oesophageal cause, because the cause may be neuromuscular.

## Management

### Acute obstruction

- If food or an object is stuck, your doctor will arrange urgent hospital assessment. The team depends on the likely cause: the emergency department if it is unclear whether it is food or a foreign body; ear, nose and throat (ORL) for a bone; gastroenterology for a soft food bolus, or if you cannot swallow saliva.
- If a soft food bolus cleared on its own, your doctor may refer you to gastroenterology (non-urgent) to look for an underlying cause. Take care when swallowing in the meantime.
- If ORL found nothing but symptoms continue, your doctor may seek urgent ORL advice about a CT scan of the neck.

### Ongoing (non-acute) dysphagia

**Your doctor may discuss:**
- Diet changes, such as soft foods, and nutritional support.
- The risk of aspiration and the signs of aspiration pneumonia: fever, cough, chest pain, and shortness of breath.

If a diagnosis has been made but more help is needed, your doctor may refer you to a speech-language therapist.

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor may refer you according to where the problem seems to be:

- **Oral or pharyngeal stage, no previous diagnosis:** ear, nose and throat (ORL) assessment. Neurology if a neurological disorder is suspected.
- **Oral or pharyngeal stage with known neurological disease:** speech-language therapy assessment.
- **Neuromuscular signs:** neurology and speech-language therapy if a neurological disorder is likely. ORL and speech-language therapy if there is an isolated vagal or recurrent laryngeal nerve palsy after surgery or radiotherapy to the head, neck, chest, or cervical spine.
- **Concerns about protecting the airway** (coughing or choking when swallowing, or aspiration pneumonia): same-day advice from neurology, ORL, or general medicine.
- **Oesophageal stage:** gastroenterology, to consider a gastroscopy. The referral is marked urgent if the dysphagia is progressive, particularly if you are struggling to swallow fluids or maintain nutrition, or have significant regurgitation.
