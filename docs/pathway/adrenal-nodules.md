# Adrenal Nodules

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the approach to adrenal nodules found incidentally on CT or MRI scanning — sometimes called adrenal incidentalomas.

---

## What Is an Adrenal Incidentaloma?

An adrenal incidentaloma is an adrenal lesion 1 cm or larger found unexpectedly on abdominal imaging done for an unrelated reason. They occur in about 3–4% of abdominal scans.

Most are benign and non-functioning, but workup is needed to assess:
- Whether the lesion is overproducing hormones (cortisol, aldosterone, catecholamines, or adrenal androgens)
- Whether it could be malignant

Malignant tumours account for less than 5%, and metastases for less than 3%, of adrenal incidentalomas. Cancers that commonly spread to the adrenal gland include lung, colon, breast, renal, stomach, melanoma, and lymphoma.

---

## Red Flags — Tell Your Doctor Promptly

Contact your doctor if you are told your adrenal nodule has any of these features:

- Size 4 cm or greater
- Rapid growth on repeat imaging
- Clinical signs of hormone overproduction (see below)

These features require urgent specialist assessment.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess the scan report and then evaluate two things: malignancy risk and hormone activity.

### Malignancy Risk

Your doctor will look at:

**Lower risk features:**
- Maximum size less than 4 cm
- Slow or no growth (benign adenomas may grow less than 3 mm per year)

**Higher risk features (any of the following raises concern):**
- Maximum size 4 cm or greater
- Irregular shape or margins, calcification, or heterogeneous appearance
- Stranding or invasion into surrounding tissue
- Rapid growth — malignant nodules generally grow more than 5 mm per year
- History of a cancer that commonly spreads to the adrenal gland (lung, melanoma, renal cell, colorectal, or breast)

Your doctor will also compare with any previous imaging to track growth over time.

### Hormone Overproduction Screening

If malignancy risk appears low, your doctor will check for signs of excess hormone production.

#### Cushing's Syndrome (Cortisol Excess)

Symptoms your doctor will ask about:
- Unexplained weight gain
- Central (abdominal) fat accumulation, including fat pads above the collarbones or at the back of the neck
- Facial rounding
- Acne
- Proximal muscle weakness (difficulty standing from a chair, climbing stairs)
- Thin skin, wide purple stretch marks
- Easy bruising, poor wound healing
- Emotional and cognitive changes
- High blood pressure
- High blood sugar
- High cholesterol
- Osteoporosis

**Screening test:** 24-hour urine collection for cortisol and creatinine, or a 1 mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test (DST). Your doctor will advise which is appropriate — certain medications (steroids, the oral contraceptive pill, carbamazepine) can interfere with the DST.

#### Phaeochromocytoma (Catecholamine Excess)

Many people have no symptoms. Your doctor will ask about:
- Severe or progressive high blood pressure
- Episodic "spells" or "funny turns"
- Palpitations
- Flushing
- Sweating
- Headache
- Tremor
- Anxiety
- Unexplained weight loss

**Screening test:** Plasma metanephrines blood test. Certain medications (levodopa, antidepressants — especially tricyclics), recreational stimulants, untreated sleep apnoea, and heavy alcohol use can cause false-positive results.

#### Primary Aldosteronism / Conn's Syndrome (Aldosterone Excess)

Most people have no symptoms. Your doctor will ask about:
- Resistant high blood pressure (difficult to control with medications)
- Low potassium (less than 3.5 mmol/L) — may cause muscle cramps or weakness
- Periodic muscle paralysis
- Headaches
- Palpitations
- Excessive thirst or urination

**Screening test:** Morning blood test for plasma aldosterone-renin ratio. This is not routinely needed for new-onset hypertension — only if it would change management. Certain medications (spironolactone, eplerenone, fludrocortisone) require specialist guidance before testing.

### Interpreting Results

- A mildly abnormal result with few or no symptoms may be a false positive. Your doctor may repeat the test after addressing possible causes (e.g., adjusting medications).
- A markedly abnormal result (2–3 times above the upper limit) with symptoms is clinically significant.

---

## Management

Management depends on what the assessment finds:

- **Malignant features on imaging:** Urgent referral to a general surgeon.
- **Incidental finding on another clinician's scan:** Your doctor will follow up to ensure no important findings are missed.
- **Not already under hospital specialist care:** Your doctor may refer you to an endocrinologist to review the imaging.
- **Blood tests suggesting Cushing's syndrome, phaeochromocytoma, or Conn's syndrome:** Referral to an endocrinologist.

All decisions about further investigation, referral, and management should be made in consultation with your doctor.
