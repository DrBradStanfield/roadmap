# Colorectal Symptoms

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of colorectal symptoms in adults — including rectal bleeding, altered bowel habit, unexplained weight loss, and abdominal pain — to determine the likely cause and appropriate next steps.

> **Note:** Rates of colorectal cancer (CRC) are increasing in people younger than 50 years, especially in Māori patients.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history asking about:

- Rectal bleeding
- Altered bowel habits — looser or more frequent stools persisting for more than 6 weeks are of more concern than constipation or fluctuating symptoms
- Unexplained weight loss
- Abdominal pain
- Previous bowel investigations (e.g., colonoscopy or CT colonography) and their results
- History of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)
- Personal or family history of colorectal cancer
- Medications (anticoagulants, antiplatelets, NSAIDs, PPIs, SSRIs, and statins can all affect bowel habits)
- Risk factors for *Clostridium difficile* infection (antibiotic exposure, PPI use, long-term care residence, immunocompromise, IBD flares)
- Co-morbidities that may affect suitability for investigations

Physical examination typically includes:

- Abdominal examination
- Rectal examination and possibly proctoscopy
- Weight measurement
- If faecal loading is suspected, re-examination after a short course of laxatives

Investigations your doctor will typically arrange:

- **For all patients:** FBC, creatinine, eGFR, electrolytes, ferritin, and iron studies
- **Consider also:** LFTs, tTG antibodies (coeliac screen), TSH
- **If diarrhoea or malabsorption:** CRP, stool culture, parasitology; faecal calprotectin if IBD is suspected; blood lipase and faecal elastase if pancreatic cause suspected; *C. difficile* stool testing if risk factors present

---

## The FIT Pathway

Most referrals for non-urgent colorectal symptoms are now triaged through a **faecal immunochemical test (FIT) pathway**. Your doctor will advise you that you may be asked to provide a stool sample — the FIT result guides whether colonoscopy or further specialist assessment is needed.

If your FIT is negative, you should re-present to your doctor if symptoms continue or worsen. If concerning symptoms persist 6 weeks after a negative FIT, your doctor will re-refer you for further investigation.

---

## When Urgent Specialist Assessment Is Needed

Your doctor will refer you urgently if:

**Rectal or abdominal mass:**
- A **rectal mass** on examination — urgent gastroenterology assessment, no imaging first
- An **abdominal mass** likely to have a colorectal cause — urgent surgical or gastroenterology assessment, often with urgent CT imaging

**Two-week category (high suspicion of colorectal cancer):**
- Known or suspected colorectal cancer on imaging, or a mass palpable on examination
- Unexplained rectal bleeding with iron deficiency anaemia (haemoglobin below normal range and low ferritin, after benign anal causes treated or excluded)
- Altered bowel habit (looser, more frequent, or both) for more than 6 weeks, with unexplained rectal bleeding, aged 50 or older

**Six-week category (significant concern):**
- Altered bowel habit (looser, more frequent, or both) for more than 6 weeks, aged 50 or older
- Altered bowel habit for more than 6 weeks, with unexplained rectal bleeding, aged 40 or older
- Unexplained rectal bleeding (benign causes excluded), aged 50 or older
- Unexplained iron deficiency anaemia (haemoglobin below normal, low ferritin, with other causes excluded)
- Imaging finding of a polyp larger than 5 mm
- Moderately increased family history risk (NZGG category 2), aged 40 or older, with altered bowel habit or rectal bleeding
- Potentially high family history risk (NZGG category 3), aged 25 or older, with altered bowel habit or rectal bleeding

> "Unexplained rectal bleeding" means bleeding that persists after benign anal causes (haemorrhoids, anal fissure, anal fistula, IBD, radiation proctitis, rectal prolapse) have been treated or excluded.

## Family History Risk Categories

Your doctor will assess your family history risk level:

**Category 2 — Moderately increased risk:**
- One first-degree relative with CRC diagnosed under age 55; or
- Two first-degree relatives on the same side of the family with CRC at any age (without category 3 features)

**Category 3 — Potentially high risk:**
- Two or more relatives on the same side with CRC, where at least one was diagnosed under 55, had multiple CRCs, or had a tumour suggesting Lynch syndrome (endometrial, ovarian, stomach, small bowel, renal pelvis, pancreas, or brain)
- Family history of adenomatous polyposis (FAP), Lynch syndrome, or other familial CRC syndrome
- First-degree relative plus two or more first- or second-degree relatives on the same side with CRC at any age
- First-degree relative with 10 or more adenomas by age 30

## Anaemia Thresholds (for Reference)

These are used in referral decisions:

- **Anaemia:** Male Hb <130 g/L; Female Hb <115 g/L (including pregnancy)
- **Low ferritin:** <20 micrograms/L in most adults; <100 micrograms/L in chronic inflammatory conditions (e.g., congestive heart failure)

---

## Other Possible Causes Your Doctor Will Consider

- Coeliac disease
- Constipation
- Diverticular disease
- Iron deficiency anaemia
- Hyperthyroidism
- Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD)
- Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS)
- Anal fissure or haemorrhoids
- Medication side effects
- Infective gastroenteritis / *C. difficile*
- Pancreatic causes

---

## Important

This information is for general understanding only. **Always discuss your symptoms and test results with your doctor.** Referral decisions, investigation choices, and management plans depend on your full clinical picture, which only your doctor can assess. If you are concerned about any symptoms — especially new rectal bleeding, unexplained weight loss, or a change in bowel habit — see your doctor promptly.
