# Lactation Insufficiency

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of low breast milk supply in breastfeeding mothers — including distinguishing true from perceived insufficiency, and keeping the baby well fed.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Care

Go to the emergency department or call for urgent help if:

- The infant has lost more than **12.5% of their body weight** in the first week of life

---

## Background

Breast milk volume relies on frequent and complete breast drainage. Robust milk production requires sufficient glandular tissue, intact nerve pathways and ducts, hormone production, and frequent effective milk removal.

There are two types of lactation insufficiency:

- **Primary** — the problem originates within the mother's body (e.g., insufficient milk-making tissue, previous breast surgery, severe postpartum haemorrhage, hormone or endocrine abnormalities)
- **Secondary** — external or infant-related factors interfering with lactation or causing a drop in supply. Most cases result from ineffective milk removal.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a breastfeeding history. Mothers with term infants generally nurse 8 to 12 times in 24 hours in the first week, decreasing to 7 to 9 times a day by four weeks.

Your doctor will ask about:
- When the baby first fed, and whether they fed well
- When the baby regained birth weight and subsequent weight gain
- Whether the baby is sleepy at the breast or wakes for all feeds
- The baby's urine and stool output (less than 6 to 8 wet nappies and around 2 mustard-like stools in 24 hours may suggest insufficiency in babies under 6 weeks)

### Factors That Can Affect Milk Supply

**Infant-related:**
- Preterm or unwell baby
- Birth events and medications
- Maternal and infant separation
- Jaundice or sleepiness
- Inability to latch successfully
- Tongue-tie
- Low suckling stimulation
- Limited or short feeds
- Cold stress or hypoglycaemia

**Maternal:**
- Birth events (postpartum haemorrhage, severe anaemia) or interventions
- Hypothyroidism, postpartum thyroiditis, or thyrotoxicosis
- Illness, pain, stress, or fatigue
- Unrelieved breast engorgement
- Retained placental products
- Psychosocial problems
- Smoking or alcohol
- Breast pump issues (inadequate frequency, poor technique, ill-fitting shields)

### Infant Examination

Your doctor may assess the baby for:
- Signs of dehydration: dry mucous membranes, lethargy, loose skin on limbs, sunken eyes and fontanelle, low urine output, dark or scant stools after day 4
- Poor growth: low muscle bulk, scrawny appearance
- Orofacial anomalies including tongue-tie (abnormally short lingual frenulum — release is required in extreme cases)

### Weight Assessment

Expected weight changes:
- Term infants normally lose less than 10% of birth weight in the first 3 to 5 days, and regain it by 1 to 2 weeks
- The infant should not continue to lose weight once the mother's breasts feel full by day 3 to 5

Expected average weight gain:

| Age | Expected weight gain per week |
|-----|-------------------------------|
| 0–3 months | 150–200 g |
| 3–6 months | 100–150 g |
| 6–12 months | 70–90 g |

### Maternal Breast Assessment

Your doctor may look for:
- Absence of breast growth during pregnancy or changes after birth
- No breast firmness before feeding / softness after feeding (note: breasts often feel softer from 6 weeks onward, even when supply is adequate)
- Damaged, inverted, or flat nipples
- Scarring from breast surgery (reduction or augmentation)
- Signs of insufficient glandular tissue (IGT): marked breast asymmetry, wide intra-mammary space, tubular-shaped breasts, bulbous areolae

### Is This Actually Low Supply?

Your doctor will consider whether the concern is perceived rather than true insufficiency. Common reasons mothers feel their supply is inadequate when it is actually normal:
- The baby is feeding frequently or suddenly increases feeds
- The baby doesn't settle after feeds or cries a lot
- Breasts feel soft
- Only small amounts can be expressed

Your doctor will also consider normal percentile variations in healthy infants, excessive crying from other causes, and feeding difficulties caused by:
- **Food protein-induced enteropathy** (cow's or soy milk protein allergy) — may cause mucous or bloody stools, vomiting, eczema
- **Gastro-oesophageal reflux disease (GORD)** — rare; associated with prematurity, poor growth, haematemesis, pronounced irritability. Note: physiological reflux is common and normal — medication is not recommended unless GORD is confirmed
- **Lactose overload** — can occur with very frequent feeding; causes gaseous, explosive, acidic stools

Your doctor may observe a feed and check latch and milk transfer.

## Management

Your doctor's goal is to increase milk production and transfer while keeping the baby well fed.

### Latch and Support

- Your doctor will help optimise the latch and may refer to a **lactation consultant** or lead maternity carer (LMC) if less than 6 weeks postpartum.
- Early lactation consultant assessment is especially recommended if a galactagogue or supplementary feeding is being considered.

### Infant Safety

- If there are concerns about infant hydration or acute illness, your doctor will arrange urgent paediatric assessment.
- If your baby has lost **10–12.5%** of birth weight in the first week, your doctor will seek paediatric advice.
- If your baby has lost **more than 12.5%**, emergency paediatric assessment is needed.

### Normal Newborn Feeding Behaviour

Your doctor may reassure you that:
- Babies normally feed 8 to 12 times in 24 hours
- Frequent feeding helps establish and maintain supply
- Hunger is rarely the cause of unsettled behaviour if weight gain has been normal
- As breastfeeding becomes established (weeks 3 to 12), breasts may not feel as full — this is normal
- The amount you can express is not a reliable indicator of your overall supply

### Maternal Wellbeing

- Adequate fluids, diet, and rest are important for milk production.
- Your doctor will check for postnatal depression and ensure you have psychological support.

### Breast Expression

Your doctor may consider:
- Hand expression or a combination of hand expression, breast massage, and electric pumping after feeds — this can increase both milk output and caloric content
- This is particularly useful for mothers with preterm infants in NICU, or those who are pump-dependent

Financial assistance for breast pumps may be available from Work and Income.

### Domperidone (Galactagogue)

Your doctor may consider domperidone — a medication that increases prolactin levels and can boost milk supply. It is not registered as a galactagogue in New Zealand, so its use is off-label.

- Some studies show it can increase milk supply, though evidence is not stronger than optimising breastfeeding frequency and technique alone
- Cardiac risk in lactating women is largely theoretical with no reports of sudden death in galactagogue studies
- Your doctor will discuss risks and benefits with you before prescribing

### Supplementary Feeding

If milk supply remains insufficient, supplementary feeding can be used alongside breastfeeding to support the baby's growth and wellbeing. Breastfeeding need not stop even if supply is limited.
