# Hyperlipidaemia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: October 2018.*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of high blood lipids (cholesterol and triglycerides) in adults, including when treatment is needed and what targets to aim for.

> This summary is general education, not personal medical advice. Discuss your own lipid results, cardiovascular risk and treatment options with your doctor.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Medical Attention Promptly

- Triglycerides > 11 mmol/L. Discuss this with your doctor promptly.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your lipid levels and cardiovascular risk before making any treatment decisions.

For screening, non-fasting blood lipids are used first. If triglyceride levels are > 1.7 mmol/L, fasting blood lipids are then arranged.

Before considering treatment, your doctor will typically:

- Measure non-fasting lipids on at least 2 occasions (not during an acute illness)
- Check for secondary causes: thyroid function (TSH), kidney function (creatinine), liver function (LFTs), fasting blood glucose or HbA1c, and a urine dipstick for protein

Cardiovascular risk is then calculated to guide management. CT calcium scoring and carotid ultrasound are not routinely recommended for asymptomatic individuals. CT calcium scoring improves prediction of cardiovascular risk compared with standard clinical risk prediction. It may be useful for selected patients with intermediate or high calculated CVD risk who are uncertain about their potential benefits from statins and other preventive treatments. It is usually not useful in people with known CVD, who already have a strong reason for preventive medication.

### Very High Risk Patients

Your doctor will place you in the high risk category, because of your clinical condition, if you have any of these very high risk features:

- Previous CVD event: angina, heart attack (MI), coronary intervention (PCI or CABG), TIA, ischaemic stroke, or peripheral vascular disease
- eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m² (severely reduced kidney function)
- Asymptomatic carotid or coronary disease
- Certain genetic lipid disorders: familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH), familial defective ApoB (FDB), or familial combined dyslipidaemia (FCH)
- Diabetes with overt nephropathy (albumin/creatinine ratio 30 mg/mmol or more, or urinary albumin 200 mg/L or more)

### Patients Aged ≥ 75 Years

Your doctor will consider cardiovascular risk alongside co-morbidities, risks and benefits of treatment, and life expectancy. Many patients aged ≥ 75 years would benefit from cardiovascular risk assessment.

In New Zealand, average life expectancy at age 75 years is 13.5 years for females and 11.9 years for males. At age 80 years it is 9.9 years for females and 8.8 years for males.

## Management

Your doctor will use shared decision-making for all treatment decisions, weighing your estimated 5-year cardiovascular risk against the benefits and potential harms of treatment.

### Treatment by Risk Level

| 5-Year CVD Risk | Approach |
|---|---|
| Below 5% | Generally managed well without drug treatment |
| 5%–15% | Shared decision-making about blood pressure and lipid-lowering drugs; if treatment started, aim for ≥ 40% LDL reduction |
| Over 15% or very high risk | Likely to benefit significantly from blood pressure and lipid-lowering medication |

### Lipid-Lowering Targets

- **5-year CVD risk ≥ 15% and very high risk patients:**
  - Aim for LDL cholesterol < 1.4 mmol/L.
  - Lipid-lowering drug treatment with this target is strongly recommended, in addition to dietary changes.
  - If the LDL target is not reached, your doctor will arrange a specialist lipid assessment.
- **CSANZ:** the Cardiac Society of Australia and New Zealand now recommends a target LDL of < 1.4 mmol/L for people with a 5-year CVD risk > 15%, with lipid-lowering treatment, in addition to dietary changes.

### Familial Hypercholesterolaemia

Your doctor may consider familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH) if:

- Total cholesterol > 8 mmol/L and LDL > 6 mmol/L
- Total cholesterol > 6 mmol/L and LDL > 4 mmol/L with a family history of premature cardiovascular disease (a first-degree relative who died or was hospitalised from heart attack or stroke before age 50)
- A family history of a confirmed genetic lipid disorder

FH affects approximately 1 in 500 people. If untreated, 50% of males will have had a heart attack by age 50, and 50% of females by age 60. Treatment is recommended from teenage years, as this significantly reduces the risk of ischaemic heart disease. Your doctor will arrange a specialist assessment if FH is suspected.

## Lifestyle and Dietary Changes

Lifestyle changes usually have a modest effect on LDL cholesterol, though a greater effect on triglycerides. Lifestyle changes aimed at reducing overall cardiovascular disease are important. Your doctor will recommend:

- **Weight loss** if relevant
- **Increased physical activity**
- **Smoking cessation**
- **Dietary changes:** The benefits of dietary changes to reduce saturated fat are controversial. Ministry of Health guidelines suggest giving dietary advice about reducing saturated fat. Replacing saturated fat with mono- and polyunsaturated fats is the most effective dietary approach to lowering LDL while maintaining or raising HDL

## Treatment for Specific Lipid Profiles

The right medication depends on your lipid profile and your cardiovascular risk assessment. International guidelines vary on the thresholds for starting medication in primary prevention. Given the level of risk, shared decision-making and your own choice are important.

### High Total Cholesterol

- In patients with a TC:HDL-C ratio ≥ 8 after lifestyle changes, lipid-lowering treatment is usually recommended regardless of combined CVD risk
- **Statins** are first-line treatment and have been shown to reduce cardiovascular illness and death
- In **primary prevention** (no previous CVD event), atorvastatin or simvastatin up to 40 mg are used
- In **secondary prevention** (existing CVD), atorvastatin up to 80 mg is used
- After a stroke or TIA, 80 mg atorvastatin is recommended regardless of pre-treatment cholesterol level

### Statin Adherence

Long-term adherence to statins is important but often poor. Around 4 in 10 patients are not taking statins regularly in the 12 months after an acute coronary event.

Your doctor will ensure you understand the proven long-term benefits of statins for reducing heart attacks, strokes, and death, particularly if you have a history of MI, stroke, or very high cardiovascular risk.

### Statin Intolerance

Statin intolerance, particularly muscle aches (myalgia), is over-diagnosed. Good evidence from placebo-controlled trials shows that most perceived statin side effects are not caused by the statin itself. Many, but not all, patients with possible side effects can be successfully rechallenged.

Your doctor may take the following steps:

- **Mild or moderate symptoms:**
  - Stop for 2–3 weeks, then restart at half the previous dose
  - If symptoms persist, repeat this step or switch to an alternate statin
  - Your doctor may consider rosuvastatin, which is generally better tolerated; 20 mg rosuvastatin is equivalent in LDL-lowering to 40 mg atorvastatin and 80 mg simvastatin, with fewer side effects
  - Note: Simvastatin 80 mg is associated with a high rate of muscle inflammation (myositis) and other statins are preferred for more effective LDL reduction
  - If lipid targets are not reached, your doctor will titrate back up to the maximum tolerated dose

- **Severe persistent myalgia:**
  - Stop the statin and measure creatine kinase (CK)
  - If CK > 1000 units/L, your doctor will arrange a specialist lipid assessment
  - If CK < 1000 units/L, the mild-to-moderate steps above are followed

### Second-Line Medications (Secondary Prevention)

Your doctor may consider the following if statin therapy alone is insufficient:

- **Ezetimibe:** added as a second-line option after a statin, at the highest tolerated statin dose, if the target is not reached. Usually started in secondary care or with specialist advice. It can be used with a statin, or in patients who cannot tolerate statins and have not reached their LDL goal. It is not usually recommended as the only LDL-lowering medication.
  - The IMPROVE-IT trial (2015) showed ezetimibe added to simvastatin reduced cardiovascular events by approximately 11%, but it did not reduce mortality.
- **Resins** (e.g., colestid): can be added to the highest tolerated statin dose; usually started with specialist advice
- **Fibrates** (e.g., bezafibrate, gemfibrozil): not used unless triglycerides are raised and HDL is low
- **PCSK9 inhibitors and LDL apheresis:** newer treatments that may be appropriate for some high risk patients
- If the target cannot be reached with the maximum tolerated treatment, your doctor will arrange a specialist lipid assessment

### High Fasting Triglycerides

High triglycerides may be associated with high alcohol intake, poorly controlled diabetes, or other secondary causes.

**Dietary measures are first line** and are often effective, limiting the need for drug therapy:

- Limit alcohol
- Reduce sugar intake
- Reduce total fat and calorie intake

Blood triglyceride levels are very diet-sensitive. Improvements can be seen within 2–3 weeks.

If fasting triglycerides ≥ 1.7 mmol/L on two occasions, your doctor will advise alcohol cessation, dietary changes, and better diabetes control where relevant.

If newly diagnosed diabetes with poor control and fasting triglycerides ≥ 10 mmol/L, your doctor may consider early insulin initiation and will recheck levels in 2–3 weeks.

If they are still ≥ 10 mmol/L, your doctor will arrange a specialist lipid assessment.

If triglycerides remain > 5 mmol/L after dietary measures, your doctor will arrange a specialist lipid assessment.

**Medications for high triglycerides:** your doctor may consider these in special situations, to prevent pancreatitis or reduce cardiovascular risk.

- Statins are used first if there is combined dyslipidaemia, as long as triglycerides are < 5 mmol/L
- If triglycerides remain > 2.3 mmol/L and HDL is low (< 1 mmol/L), a fibrate may be added

Patient dietary information: [Healthinfo: High Triglycerides](https://www.healthinfo.org.nz/High-triglycerides.htm)

### High HDL (> 2 mmol/L)

- A very high HDL is not necessarily protective
- If HDL is high, TC:HDL ratios should not be used to calculate cardiovascular risk. Your doctor will look at other risk factors instead

### Low HDL (< 0.7 mmol/L)

- Associated with increased cardiovascular risk and seen in patients with premature cardiovascular disease
- Lifestyle measures help raise HDL: smoking cessation, exercise, weight loss, and dietary changes
- If HDL remains < 0.5 mmol/L after lifestyle changes, your doctor will arrange a specialist lipid assessment
- If low HDL is the only risk factor, currently available medications do not improve cardiovascular outcomes

## Monitoring

Once treatment has been started, your doctor will monitor:

- **Non-fasting lipids every 6–12 months** until the agreed target has been reached
- **Annually** once the target is achieved
- **Compliance** at each visit, since non-compliance and statin intolerance are both common

Your doctor will also reassess all cardiovascular risk factors at each monitoring visit. The best outcomes come from reducing all risk factors together, not lipids alone.
