# Hyperlipidaemia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of high blood lipids (cholesterol and triglycerides) in adults, including when treatment is needed and what targets to aim for.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Medical Attention Promptly

- Triglycerides > 11 mmol/L — discuss with your doctor urgently as this significantly raises the risk of pancreatitis

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess your lipid levels and cardiovascular risk before making any treatment decisions.

For screening, non-fasting blood lipids are used first. If triglyceride levels are > 1.7 mmol/L, fasting blood lipids are then arranged.

Before considering treatment, your doctor will typically:

- Measure non-fasting lipids on at least 2 occasions (not during an acute illness)
- Check for secondary causes: thyroid function (TSH), kidney function (creatinine), liver function (LFTs), blood glucose or HbA1c, and a urine dipstick for protein

Cardiovascular risk is then calculated to guide management. CT calcium scoring and carotid ultrasound are not routinely recommended for asymptomatic individuals, though CT calcium scoring may help selected patients with intermediate or high calculated CVD risk who are uncertain about the benefits of preventive treatment.

### Very High Risk Patients

Your doctor will classify you as very high risk if you have any of:

- Previous CVD event — angina, heart attack (MI), coronary intervention (PCI or CABG), TIA, ischaemic stroke, or peripheral vascular disease
- eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73 m² (severely reduced kidney function)
- Asymptomatic carotid or coronary disease
- Certain genetic lipid disorders — familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH), familial defective ApoB (FDB), or familial combined hyperlipidaemia (FCH)
- Diabetes with overt nephropathy (albumin/creatinine ratio ≥ 30 mg/mmol or urinary albumin ≥ 200 mg/L)

### Patients Aged ≥ 75 Years

Your doctor will consider cardiovascular risk alongside co-morbidities, risks and benefits of treatment, and life expectancy. Many patients aged ≥ 75 years benefit from cardiovascular risk assessment. In New Zealand, average life expectancy at age 75 is approximately 13.5 years for females and 11.9 years for males.

---

## Management

Your doctor will use shared decision-making for all treatment decisions, weighing your estimated 5-year cardiovascular risk against the benefits and potential harms of treatment.

### Treatment by Risk Level

| 5-Year CVD Risk | Approach |
|---|---|
| Below 5% | Generally managed well without drug treatment |
| 5%–15% | Shared decision-making about blood pressure and lipid-lowering drugs; if treatment started, aim for ≥ 40% LDL reduction |
| Over 15% or very high risk | Likely to benefit significantly from blood pressure and lipid-lowering medication |

### Lipid-Lowering Targets

- **Very high risk patients:** LDL cholesterol target of < 1.4 mmol/L is strongly recommended, in addition to dietary changes
- **High CVD risk (> 15%):** CSANZ also recommends LDL < 1.4 mmol/L with lipid-lowering treatment

### Familial Hypercholesterolaemia

Your doctor may consider familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH) if:

- Total cholesterol > 8 mmol/L and LDL > 6 mmol/L
- Total cholesterol > 6 mmol/L and LDL > 4 mmol/L with a family history of premature cardiovascular disease (a first-degree relative who died or was hospitalised from heart attack or stroke before age 50)
- A family history of a confirmed genetic lipid disorder

FH affects approximately 1 in 500 people. If untreated, 50% of males will have had a heart attack by age 50, and 50% of females by age 60. Treatment is recommended from teenage years onwards and significantly reduces this risk. Your doctor will arrange a specialist assessment if FH is suspected.

---

## Lifestyle and Dietary Changes

Lifestyle changes have a modest but meaningful effect on LDL cholesterol and a greater effect on triglycerides. Your doctor will recommend:

- **Weight loss** if relevant
- **Increased physical activity**
- **Smoking cessation**
- **Dietary changes:** Reducing saturated fat and substituting it with mono- and polyunsaturated fats is the most effective dietary approach to lowering LDL while maintaining or raising HDL

---

## Treatment for Specific Lipid Profiles

### High Total Cholesterol

- In patients with a TC:HDL-C ratio ≥ 8 after lifestyle changes, lipid-lowering treatment is usually recommended regardless of combined CVD risk
- **Statins** are first-line treatment and are proven to reduce cardiovascular events and death
- In **primary prevention** (no previous CVD event), atorvastatin or simvastatin up to 40 mg are used
- In **secondary prevention** (existing CVD), atorvastatin up to 80 mg is used
- After a stroke or TIA, 80 mg atorvastatin is recommended regardless of pre-treatment cholesterol level

### Statin Adherence

Long-term adherence to statins is important but often poor — around 4 in 10 patients stop taking statins regularly within 12 months of an acute coronary event. Your doctor will ensure you understand the proven long-term benefits of statins for reducing heart attacks, strokes, and death, particularly if you have a history of MI, stroke, or very high cardiovascular risk.

### Statin Intolerance

Statin intolerance — particularly muscle aches (myalgia) — is over-diagnosed. Good evidence from placebo-controlled trials shows that most perceived statin side effects are not caused by the statin itself. Many patients can be successfully rechallenged.

Your doctor may take the following steps:

- **Mild or moderate symptoms:**
  - Stop for 2–3 weeks, then restart at half the previous dose
  - If symptoms persist, repeat this step or switch to an alternate statin
  - Rosuvastatin is generally better tolerated; 20 mg rosuvastatin is roughly equivalent in LDL-lowering to 40 mg atorvastatin or 80 mg simvastatin, with fewer side effects
  - Note: Simvastatin 80 mg is associated with a high rate of muscle inflammation (myositis) and other statins are preferred for more effective LDL reduction
  - If lipid targets are not reached, your doctor will titrate back up to the maximum tolerated dose

- **Severe persistent myalgia:**
  - Stop the statin and measure creatine kinase (CK)
  - If CK > 1000 units/L, your doctor will arrange a specialist lipid assessment
  - If CK < 1000 units/L, the mild-to-moderate steps above are followed

### Second-Line Medications (Secondary Prevention)

Your doctor may consider the following if statin therapy alone is insufficient:

- **Ezetimibe** — added as a second-line option after a statin. The IMPROVE-IT trial (2015) showed ezetimibe added to simvastatin reduced cardiovascular events by approximately 11%. Can be used in combination with a statin, or alone in patients who cannot tolerate statins. Usually started with specialist advice.
- **Resins** (e.g., colestid) — can be added to the highest tolerated statin dose; usually started with specialist advice
- **Fibrates** (e.g., bezafibrate, gemfibrozil) — not used unless triglycerides are raised and HDL is low
- **PCSK9 inhibitors and LDL apheresis** — newer treatments that may be appropriate for some very high risk patients

### High Fasting Triglycerides

High triglycerides may be associated with high alcohol intake, poorly controlled diabetes, or other secondary causes.

**Dietary measures are first line** and are often effective:

- Limit alcohol
- Reduce sugar intake
- Reduce total fat and calorie intake

Blood triglyceride levels are very diet-sensitive — improvements can be seen within 2–3 weeks.

If fasting triglycerides ≥ 1.7 mmol/L on two occasions, your doctor will advise alcohol cessation, dietary changes, and better diabetes control where relevant.

If newly diagnosed diabetes with poor control and fasting triglycerides ≥ 10 mmol/L, your doctor may consider early insulin initiation and will recheck levels in 2–3 weeks.

If triglycerides remain > 5 mmol/L after dietary measures, your doctor will arrange a specialist lipid assessment.

**Medications for high triglycerides:**

- Statins are used first if there is combined dyslipidaemia, as long as triglycerides are < 5 mmol/L
- If triglycerides remain > 2.3 mmol/L and HDL is low (< 1 mmol/L), a fibrate may be added

Patient dietary information: [Healthinfo — High Triglycerides](https://www.healthinfo.org.nz/High-triglycerides.htm)

### High HDL (> 2 mmol/L)

- A very high HDL is not necessarily protective
- If HDL is high, TC:HDL ratios should not be used to calculate cardiovascular risk — your doctor will look at other risk factors instead

### Low HDL (< 0.7 mmol/L)

- Associated with increased cardiovascular risk and seen in patients with premature cardiovascular disease
- Lifestyle measures help raise HDL: smoking cessation, exercise, weight loss, and dietary changes
- If HDL remains < 0.5 mmol/L after lifestyle changes, your doctor will arrange a specialist lipid assessment
- If low HDL is the only risk factor, currently available medications do not improve cardiovascular outcomes

---

## Monitoring

Once treatment has been started, your doctor will monitor:

- **Non-fasting lipids every 6–12 months** until the agreed target has been reached
- **Annually** once the target is achieved
- **Compliance** at each visit — non-compliance and statin intolerance are both common

Your doctor will also reassess all cardiovascular risk factors at each monitoring visit. The best outcomes come from reducing all risk factors together, not lipids alone.
