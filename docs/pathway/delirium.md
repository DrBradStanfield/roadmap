# Delirium

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the clinical approach to delirium in adults — assessment, identifying causes, management in the community, and when hospital care is required.

---

## When to Seek Emergency Care

Your doctor will arrange urgent hospital assessment if any of the following are present:

- Difficult to manage, aggressive, or wandering
- Acute delirium with no history of dementia and no identified cause
- Signs of sepsis, shock, significant metabolic disturbance, hypothermia, meningism, or new focal neurology (e.g. weakness on one side)

---

## What Is Delirium?

Delirium is an acute or subacute deterioration in mental functioning. It is a clinical syndrome (not a diagnosis in itself) that:

- Develops or worsens over hours to days
- Involves fluctuating alertness — the person may be over-aroused (agitated) or under-aroused (withdrawn)
- Is typically worse at night
- Has a cause that is usually multifactorial and often reversible

It is common in older people, particularly in hospital (15–40% of elderly inpatients) and aged-care settings. Recovery is often rapid, but complete resolution may take weeks. Delirium is sometimes the only sign of serious underlying illness.

### Delirium vs Dementia vs Depression

Your doctor will consider the "3 Ds":

| Feature | Delirium | Dementia | Depression |
|---------|----------|----------|------------|
| Onset | Sudden, often worse at twilight | Chronic and gradual | Often abrupt, linked to life events |
| Duration | Days to weeks | Months to years | Months to years |
| Progression | Rapid, fluctuating | Slow and even | Variable and uneven |

## How Your Doctor Assesses Delirium

### History

Your doctor will gather a detailed history — ideally involving family or carers — covering:

- Pre-existing cognitive impairment or mental illness
- Current medications (including herbal and complementary medicines), compliance, and recent changes
- Benzodiazepine, alcohol, and illicit drug use
- Symptoms of infection
- Bowel and bladder symptoms (constipation, urinary retention)
- Appetite, nutrition, hydration, and weight changes
- Recent falls, pain, and sleep pattern changes
- Risk of harm to self or others

### Signs and Symptoms

Someone with delirium may:

- Appear confused, forgetful, or inattentive
- Have fluctuating alertness — sometimes very agitated, other times withdrawn
- Be unsure of time or place
- Have hallucinations or delusions
- Have disturbed sleep patterns
- Be newly incontinent
- Seem like "a different person" compared to their usual self

### Examination and Cognitive Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Check attention and cognition using the **4AT** (a validated screening tool for delirium)
- Perform a thorough physical examination to look for the cause — there are usually several contributing factors

### Investigations

Your doctor may arrange:

- Oxygen saturation
- Blood sugar
- Blood tests: electrolytes, LFTs, calcium, FBC, CRP
- Thyroid function, vitamin B12, folate (if not checked in the last 3 months)
- Chest X-ray if indicated
- ECG if indicated
- CT head if indicated and the person can be safely managed in the community

**Note on urine tests:** A urine culture is not routinely needed unless there are specific signs of urinary infection (e.g. dysuria, urgency, frequency, or systemic features like fever or low blood pressure).

### Common Risk Factors

- Age over 65 years
- Pre-existing cognitive impairment, dementia, or brain damage
- Previous delirium
- Polypharmacy
- Dehydration and poor nutrition
- Infection or acute illness
- Pain
- Immobility
- Sleep deprivation
- Drug or alcohol use or withdrawal
- Sensory impairment (vision, hearing)
- Indwelling catheter

## Common Causes

### Medical

- Infection (e.g. pneumonia, urinary tract infection)
- Constipation, faecal impaction, or urinary retention
- Dehydration
- Acute coronary syndrome (heart attack can be painless in older people)
- Metabolic disturbance (electrolyte imbalance, renal or liver failure, acid-base disorder)
- Endocrine disorder (thyroid, parathyroid, hypoglycaemia, hyperglycaemia)
- Neurological (stroke, subdural haematoma, head injury, epilepsy, encephalitis, meningitis, space-occupying lesion)
- Hypoxia
- Hypothermia
- Drug or alcohol intoxication or withdrawal
- Vitamin deficiency (e.g. thiamine — Wernicke-Korsakoff syndrome)
- Recent surgery or procedure

### Medication-Related

Many medications can cause delirium. The most common culprits are anticholinergics, benzodiazepines, and opioids. Other medications that can contribute include:

- Sedatives and hypnotics (especially benzodiazepines)
- Opioid analgesics
- Anticholinergics: antihistamines, antispasmodics, tricyclic antidepressants, antipsychotics, amantadine, benztropine
- Cardiac: antiarrhythmics, digoxin, some antihypertensives (especially beta-blockers)
- Gastrointestinal: H2-antagonists, proton-pump inhibitors, prochlorperazine, metoclopramide
- Miscellaneous: antiepileptics, corticosteroids, dopaminergic antiparkinsonians, lithium, serotonergic drugs, antibiotics (occasionally, e.g. high-dose beta-lactams)

### Psychosocial

- Unfamiliar environment
- Grief
- Sleep deprivation
- Emotional stress

## Management

### Non-Pharmacological (First-Line)

Your doctor will use supportive measures as the primary approach — especially for patients whose symptoms are not causing significant distress. These measures alone can prevent delirium and improve function. Remember **"PINCHES ME Kindly"**:

- **P** — Pain relief
- **I** — Infection: prevent or treat
- **N** — Nutrition
- **C** — Constipation: prevent or treat
- **H** — Hydration
- **E** — Exercise: regular walking with supervision if needed
- **S** — Sleep: maximise at night
- **M** — Medication: avoid or reduce contributing medications where possible
- **E** — Environment: orient to a quiet, familiar setting; comfortable temperature; low noise; sensory aids (hearing aids, clock, calendar, night light); involve family or carers
- **Kindly** — be calm, patient, and mindful of emotional needs

### Treating Underlying Causes

Your doctor will:

- Treat reversible underlying causes where possible
- Stop or reduce medications contributing to confusion (with caution around withdrawal, especially benzodiazepines)

### Managing Agitation

Non-pharmacological de-escalation is always tried first — reassurance, involving family or carers, reducing environmental triggers.

Medication is only considered when:
- Psychotic symptoms, agitation, or distress are present
- There is risk of harm
- De-escalation has been ineffective

Your doctor will use the lowest effective dose for the shortest duration and will have a clear plan for tapering and stopping.

#### Medication — No Known or Suspected Lewy Body Dementia or Parkinson's Disease

**Haloperidol** is the preferred antipsychotic:
- Adults: 0.5–1 mg (up to 2 mg in younger adults) orally or subcutaneously, once or twice daily
- Older adults (especially frail): 0.25 mg orally or subcutaneously, once or twice daily
- Prescribed for a short period and tapered after symptom resolution (typically within a week)

Benzodiazepines are only used if haloperidol is ineffective, or if delirium is caused by alcohol or benzodiazepine withdrawal. They increase the risk of falls and confusion and should not be stopped abruptly if long-standing.

#### Medication — Known or Suspected Lewy Body Dementia or Parkinson's Disease

**Important:** Haloperidol and most other antipsychotics are **contraindicated** in Lewy body dementia and Parkinson's disease — they can cause severe and often fatal neuroleptic sensitivity reactions. Dopamine-blocking antiemetics (e.g. metoclopramide) should also be avoided.

If medication is needed, your doctor will consider:
- **Quetiapine** 12.5–25 mg orally once or twice daily (with extreme caution)
- **Lorazepam** 0.25–0.5 mg (up to 1 mg in younger adults) orally or IM, once or twice daily
- In emergencies: lorazepam IM 0.5–1 mg

Your doctor will seek specialist advice if unsure.

## Follow-Up

Your doctor will always arrange follow-up for patients managed at home:

- **Mild symptoms, known cause, no safety concerns:** review in 3–4 days; return sooner if symptoms worsen or new concerns arise
- **Unknown cause or moderate symptoms:** next-day follow-up via POAC (Primary Options for Acute Care) and additional community supports if available
- **Symptoms not settling or no cause found:** hospital assessment arranged

### After Delirium Resolves

Your doctor will:
- Review any decisions made during the delirium (e.g. capacity assessments, legal documents)
- Reassess cognition — delirium can unmask previously unrecognised dementia, and there may be lasting cognitive or psychological effects
