# Physical Assault

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways. Last reviewed: January 2025.*

This pathway covers adults (aged 18 years or over) who have experienced physical assault. For people under 18 years, see the child abuse and neglect pathway. Related pathways cover family violence, elder abuse and neglect, recent sexual assault, and non-fatal strangulation and/or suffocation.

> **Always discuss your injuries and your safety with your doctor.** This information is a clinical reference, not a substitute for individual medical advice.

---

## When to Seek Urgent Medical Attention

**Call 111 or go to the emergency department if you have a significant injury, such as:**
- Significant eye trauma, e.g. a penetrating eye injury or significant blunt trauma to the eye.
- Significant head injury, e.g. reduced level of consciousness (a lower Glasgow Coma Scale score), a possible skull fracture, or a severe headache.
- Injury to deeper structures, e.g. tendons, joints, or nerves.
- Glass injuries. These need an X-ray and thorough exploration.
- Penetrating trauma, e.g. a stab wound or gunshot wound.

**Tell your doctor straight away if:**
- Someone tried to strangle you (sometimes called "choking"). Strangulation carries a high risk of serious injury and a significantly increased risk of future homicide. Your doctor will follow the non-fatal strangulation and/or suffocation pathway.
- You are at risk of serious assault. With your consent, your doctor may notify the police and/or seek emergency refuge support.
- You are at risk of suicide or self-harm. Your doctor may request an urgent mental health assessment at hospital. If you are in immediate danger, call 111 or go to the emergency department.

---

## Background

- Your GP's role after a physical assault includes:
  - assessing and recording your injuries.
  - checking for strangulation.
  - identifying family violence or elder abuse, and whether children are involved.
  - managing injuries and any psychological effects.
  - referring you to, or giving you information about, support agencies.
- Careful recording of injuries supports your care, and the notes may be used in legal processes, including a criminal investigation.
- A full medical forensic assessment is not part of general practice. It needs specially credentialed practitioners and equipment.
- Māori are overrepresented as victims of physical and/or sexual intimate partner violence (IPV). The lifetime risk for women of physical and/or sexual IPV is 49% for Māori and 31% for NZ European. From a Māori perspective, all forms of violence are a violation of mana and tapu.

## Assessment

### History

Your doctor may ask about:
- The time and date of the assault.
- Anyone else present, or at the address at the time, including children or other family members.
- How the injury happened.
- Whether this has happened before, and whether you think it is increasing.
- What clothing you were wearing.
- Any use of drugs or alcohol.
- Pre-existing conditions (e.g. eczema) and any medications you take.
- Your current symptoms.

Your doctor will specifically ask whether anyone tried to strangle you.

### Your choice

It is your choice whether to take any further action. You should not feel pressured.
- You can choose whether to make a police report or seek counselling.
- You can do so if or when you want to.

### Cultural safety

Your doctor should respect tikanga Māori (inherited values and customs). For example:
- The head is the most tapu part of the body. Your doctor must ask permission before touching and examining it.
- Objects such as pounamu are taonga. Any need to touch them should be discussed first.
- Things that are tapu are kept separate from things that are noa, e.g. not sitting on things associated with the head, such as pillows.

### Safety and risk

Your doctor will consider your safety, especially if the assault was by a partner, was family-related, or involved elder abuse. Questions may include:
- Where is the abuser now?
- Are you afraid of the abuser?
- Are you afraid to go home or stay home?
- Has the violence become more frequent or more severe?
- Are you pregnant?
- Has anyone physically abused the children? Have children seen violence in the home?
- Has the abuser ever tried to choke or strangle you or anyone else?
- Does the abuser make threats of homicide or suicide?
- Is there alcohol or substance abuse?
- Is there a gun in the house?

### Recording injuries

Accurate records matter because you or the police may request your medical records if charges are considered.
- Your doctor will note the time and date of the examination.
- With your consent, your doctor may take photographs, if secure storage is available. If the police are involved, police photography is recommended.
- Your doctor may mark injuries on a body diagram and keep it in your records.

## Management

**Your doctor may:**
- Follow the non-fatal strangulation and/or suffocation pathway if strangulation occurred.
- Follow the family violence pathway if there are safety concerns about children, and may seek advice from Oranga Tamariki if unsure about the risk to children.
- With your consent, notify the police and/or seek emergency refuge support if you are at risk of serious assault.
- Request an urgent mental health assessment at hospital if you are at risk of suicide or self-harm.
- Arrange emergency department or other hospital assessment for a significant injury (see above). Otherwise, treat any wounds with standard wound care.
- Complete ACC injury documentation.
- Arrange assault or abuse community support.

### Clinical notes and reports

- If the police request a copy of your clinical notes or a report, your doctor will ask for your consent first.
- Notes may be used in court as a factual professional report, not an expert opinion. GPs are not experts in forensic medicine and are not expected to give an expert opinion on the findings.
