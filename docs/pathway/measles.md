# Measles

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the diagnosis, management, and prevention of measles — including assessment of immunity, diagnostic testing, isolation requirements, contact management, and MMR vaccination.

> **Important:** Measles is a notifiable disease. A single suspected case requires an immediate public health response. This information is for educational purposes — always discuss your situation with your doctor.

---

## About Measles

Measles is a highly contagious viral illness that can be life-threatening. The virus is airborne-spread, or less commonly via direct contact with respiratory secretions or droplets.

### Classic Presentation

**Prodromal phase** (begins 10–14 days after exposure, range 7–21 days):
- Fever, cough, coryza (runny nose), conjunctivitis (red eyes)

**Rash phase** (3–5 days after first symptoms):
- Generalised maculopapular rash starting on the face (hairline, behind the ears), spreading downward to neck, trunk, and the rest of the body
- Fever is always present at the time of rash onset
- The rash may be absent or atypical in immunosuppressed patients or those previously vaccinated
- Can be harder to detect on darker skin

**Convalescent phase:**
- Rash resolves after about 7 days; cough may persist for around 10 days

### Infectious Period

The infectious period starts **4 days before the rash appears** and ends **4 days after rash onset** — a total of 9 days.

### Koplik's Spots

Koplik's spots are pathognomonic (definitively diagnostic) for measles:
- White-grey spots on the buccal mucosa (inside the cheeks opposite the molar teeth), described as "grains of salt on a red background"
- Appear 1–2 days before the rash and may disappear as the rash develops
- Present in 50–70% of cases if examined at the right time

## Who Is at Risk of Severe Disease

Anyone can develop severe measles, but the following groups are at highest risk:

- Infants younger than 12 months
- Immunosuppressed people (see below)
- Non-immune pregnant people (and up to 6 weeks postpartum)
- Non-immune children younger than 5 years, particularly if severely malnourished with vitamin A deficiency
- Māori and Pacific people
- Socioeconomically deprived people

**Immunosuppressed patients include those:**
- With primary or acquired immunosuppressive conditions (cancer, leukaemia, lymphoma, immunoglobulin deficiencies, organ or bone marrow transplant recipients)
- Receiving cancer chemotherapy or radiotherapy
- On systemic high-dose steroids (adults: >40 mg/day for >1 week, or >20 mg/day for >14 days; children: >2 mg/kg/day for ≥1 week or >1 mg/kg/day for 1 month), or who have received high-dose steroids in the past 3 months
- On DMARDs (disease-modifying antirheumatic drugs) or biologics
- On TNF-alpha inhibitors
- With HIV or AIDS

> Measles in immunocompromised patients is a medical emergency. Immunosuppressed patients may still be susceptible even if fully vaccinated or previously immune.

---

## Complications

Complications of measles include:

- **Otitis media** (7–9% of cases)
- **Pneumonia** (1–6% of cases)
- **Croup**
- **Encephalitis** (1 per 1,000 cases — fatal in 15%; 1 in 3 survivors have permanent brain damage). Signs include: altered mental status, personality/behaviour changes, confusion, speech or movement problems, focal neurological deficits, seizures.
- **Miscarriage or premature delivery** in pregnant patients (frequency unknown)
- **Diarrhoea** (8% of cases)

## Assessing Immunity

Your doctor will assess your immunity status as follows:

**Presumed immune if you:**
- Have documentation of two doses of measles-containing vaccine, given at least 4 weeks apart and both after your first birthday
- Were born or living in New Zealand before 1 January 1969 (presumed immune from exposure to the wild virus), or outside New Zealand before 1 January 1969 (unless living in a country where an earlier year applies)
- Have evidence of a previous measles infection confirmed by PCR testing
- Have a positive measles-specific IgG serology result

**Susceptible-partially vaccinated if you:**
- Have had only one measles-containing vaccine dose after your first birthday (including infants aged 12–15 months who have received one MMR)

**Susceptible-non-immune (born from 1 January 1969) if you:**
- Have not received any measles-containing vaccine
- Were given MMR before age 12 months (MMR0) only
- Received your first MMR dose (MMR1) within the last 4 weeks
- Have negative or equivocal measles-specific IgG serology results

## Differential Diagnosis

Conditions that can resemble measles include:

- **Post-MMR vaccination reaction** — rash occurs in ~5% of patients, develops 5–12 days after vaccination, symptoms are usually significantly milder than measles. Note: recent MMR vaccination can give a positive measles PCR result due to detection of vaccine strain.
- Rubella
- Roseola infantum
- Human parvovirus
- Drug reactions (especially to antibiotics)
- Scarlet fever
- Kawasaki disease
- Scalded skin syndrome
- Mycoplasma pneumonia

## If Measles Is Suspected

**A single suspected case of measles requires an immediate public health response.** Your doctor will:

1. Isolate you immediately on arrival — away from other patients, in a separate room with the door closed
2. Notify the Medical Officer of Health
3. Arrange diagnostic testing (nasopharyngeal PCR swab — most accurate from day 0 to day 7 after rash onset)

**What to expect while awaiting results:**
- You will need to isolate at home until confirmed not to be a case (even if a vaccine reaction is suspected)
- PCR results take up to 2–3 days
- If positive: stay in isolation until end of day 4 after rash onset; Public Health will contact you and confirm your release date
- If negative: your doctor will advise you that you can end isolation
- Do not travel to a sample collection centre — your doctor will arrange for the swab to be taken at the practice

---

## Management of Confirmed Measles

Your doctor will:

- Notify Public Health immediately
- Arrange isolation
- Provide supportive treatment and monitor for complications
- Seek specialist advice if you are at increased risk (infants, immunocompromised, pregnant, at risk of vitamin A deficiency)

**For transport to hospital if complications develop:**
- Do not use public transport
- If using private transport, wait outside the hospital and call ahead — staff will come out to meet you and take you directly to isolation

**Vitamin A:** Vitamin A levels fall during measles. For patients with vitamin A deficiency (fat malabsorption, malnutrition, recently arrived from a low or middle income country), vitamin A supplementation may be indicated — your doctor will seek specialist advice. Note: standard multivitamin preparations do not contain sufficient dosage of vitamin A for this purpose.

## If You Are a Close Contact of a Measles Case

**Close contact** means any of the following during the infectious period:
- Face-to-face contact of any duration
- Household contact
- Being in a confined indoor space with an infectious case, or being in that space within 1 hour after the case left

**Casual contact** means a lower level of exposure where risk cannot be fully determined — casual contacts do not need to quarantine but should monitor for symptoms for 21 days.

### What to Do as a Close Contact

If you believe you are a close contact, contact your doctor rather than presenting to the clinic without warning. Your doctor will:

- Confirm whether you are an identified contact (you may have received a letter from Public Health)
- If you have symptoms or a rash, arrange testing and advise immediate isolation
- If you are non-immune, discuss post-exposure prophylaxis (PEP)

### Post-Exposure Prophylaxis (PEP)

**Immunoglobulin PEP** (for high-risk contacts — infants under 12 months, immunosuppressed, non-immune pregnant):
- Must be given within 6 days of exposure to be effective
- Your doctor will contact Public Health to arrange this
- MMR vaccination must be deferred for at least 6 months after immunoglobulin

**MMR vaccine PEP** (for other non-immune or partially vaccinated contacts):
- Offered to those who have not received immunoglobulin, up until any quarantine or restriction period starts
- If given within 72 hours of initial exposure, may provide some protection against infection and severe illness
- Does not alter your quarantine period but may reduce the risk of severe disease

### Quarantine and Restrictions

Your doctor or Public Health will provide specific advice. In general:

- **Non-immune close contacts** (no evidence of immunity): Quarantine at home from day 7 to day 14 after last exposure; return to normal activities on day 15 if asymptomatic. If immunoglobulin was given, quarantine until day 19 after last exposure (immunoglobulin may extend the incubation period).
- **Partially vaccinated close contacts** (one documented MMR dose after first birthday): No quarantine required, but restricted from high-risk settings (schools, childcare, healthcare settings, international travel) from day 7 to day 14 after last exposure. Wear a mask if seeking healthcare.
- **Immune or casual contacts:** No restrictions; no quarantine required.

For all contacts, for 21 days after exposure:
- Watch for measles symptoms
- Phone your practice ahead of any appointment to let them know you are a measles contact
- Wear a well-fitting mask if attending in person

## MMR Vaccination

MMR (measles-mumps-rubella) vaccine is the most effective way to prevent measles. Two doses are required for full protection, given from age 12 months and at least 4 weeks apart.

**Who should receive MMR:**
- All children at 12 months and 15 months (scheduled doses)
- Anyone born after 1 January 1969 without documented evidence of 2 doses after age 12 months
- Catch-up doses are available — there is no harm in receiving additional doses if your vaccination history is uncertain

**Historical gaps to be aware of:**
- People born 1969–1992 in New Zealand may have had only one dose (possibly before age 12 months)
- People born 1992–2004 may have missed their second dose

**MMR is a live vaccine — it cannot be given to:**
- Pregnant people (avoid pregnancy for 4 weeks after vaccination)
- Immunocompromised people

**MMR is safe** when breastfeeding.

**For infants 4–11 months travelling internationally:** An early dose (MMR0) may be considered, but two further doses are still needed after age 12 months.

**If you decline vaccination:** Your doctor will provide written information about recognising and responding to measles.
