# Parotid Gland Disorders

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of parotid gland swelling in adults, including infection, stones, and tumours.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

Contact your doctor promptly or go to the emergency department if you have:

- Rapidly worsening facial swelling with fever and feeling severely unwell
- Suspected abscess formation (very tender, fluctuant swelling)
- Difficulty swallowing or breathing due to neck swelling
- Facial nerve weakness or paralysis (drooping of the face)
- A neck lump alongside the facial swelling (possible cancer spread)
- An unexplained salivary or neck mass that is larger than 1 cm and has persisted longer than 3 weeks, is growing, or is associated with hoarseness, difficulty swallowing, ear or throat pain, or a blocked nose — these are criteria for urgent cancer assessment

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- How long the swelling has been present
- Whether the swelling affects one side or both sides of the face
- Whether the swelling or pain gets worse when eating (suggests a blocked duct or stone)

Your doctor will examine:

- Whether the swelling involves the whole gland or is a distinct lump within it (focal vs generalised)
- Whether one side or both sides are affected (unilateral vs bilateral)
- Whether other salivary glands are involved
- Whether there is any discharge from the salivary duct opening (located opposite the second upper molar tooth)
- Whether there are any systemic symptoms such as fever, dry eyes, or dry mouth

**What the pattern of swelling suggests:**

- **Bilateral swelling** usually points to a systemic condition — such as Sjögren syndrome, diabetes, or alcohol dependency — or a viral infection such as mumps (more common in people aged 10–29 who may have lower immunisation rates)
- **Unilateral swelling** involving the whole gland is usually sialoadenitis (inflammation of the gland), which may or may not involve a stone. Only about 15% of cases are caused by stones, and these are usually not visible on standard X-ray
- **Focal swelling** (a distinct lump within the gland) is usually a tumour. Around 60% are benign and 40% are malignant. Your doctor will assess the facial nerve and check for any lumps in the neck

**Investigations your doctor may arrange:**

- For suspected Sjögren syndrome: blood tests checking extractable nuclear antigens (ENA)
- For suspected tumour: fine needle aspiration (FNA) — a procedure where a thin needle collects a small tissue sample for analysis
- For recurrent episodes of swelling: a non-contrast CT scan (plain X-ray and ultrasound have limited value as parotid stones are usually not visible on these)
- For a first episode of acute sialoadenitis with a clinically obvious stone: imaging is generally not needed initially

## Management

### Acute Sialoadenitis (Infection of the Gland)

Acute sialoadenitis is usually a bacterial infection caused by oral bacteria or *Staphylococcus aureus*.

**Your doctor may consider:**

- Prescribing antibiotics — typically **flucloxacillin**, or **erythromycin** if you have a penicillin allergy. The antibiotic may be adjusted once swab results are available
- Recommending gentle massage of the gland — using forward strokes from the ear towards the corner of the mouth
- Advising you to stay well hydrated, take pain relief, and rest
- Recommending sucking on something sour throughout the day (such as a lemon wedge or sour lolly) — this stimulates saliva flow and can help clear the duct

If you are feeling very unwell or an abscess is suspected, your doctor will arrange urgent specialist assessment for intravenous antibiotics or surgical drainage.

If symptoms have not fully resolved within 7–10 days, your doctor will refer you for further specialist assessment.

### Bilateral Swelling

Your doctor will focus on treating the underlying cause. If Sjögren syndrome is suspected, a referral to a rheumatologist is usually arranged.

**Sjögren syndrome** is an autoimmune condition that typically affects the parotid glands and causes dry eyes and dry mouth. It is often associated with other autoimmune conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis or systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE).

### Suspected Tumour

Your doctor will arrange fine needle aspiration (FNA) and refer you to an ear, nose, and throat (ENT/ORL) specialist for further assessment.

### Chronic or Recurrent Episodes

If you have had repeated episodes of parotid infection or swelling, your doctor will refer you to a specialist. Surgery may be considered.

### Ongoing Unexplained Swelling

If swelling persists without a clear explanation, your doctor will arrange specialist assessment.
