# Submandibular Gland Disorders

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers assessment and management of submandibular (under-jaw) gland swelling in adults.

---

## Urgent Assessment — When to Seek Same-Day Care

Go to your doctor or emergency department promptly if you have:

- Submandibular swelling with signs of systemic infection (fever, feeling very unwell, difficulty swallowing or breathing)
- A salivary stone that has not passed after 48 hours

These situations may require same-day specialist (ear, nose and throat) review.

---

## Red Flags for Cancer — Tell Your Doctor

Contact your doctor if you have an unexplained neck or salivary gland mass **plus any** of the following:

- Mass larger than 1 cm lasting more than 3 weeks
- Mass that is growing
- Previous head or neck cancer (including skin cancer)
- Facial weakness or palsy
- Any new unexplained symptoms: hoarseness, difficulty swallowing, throat or ear pain, blocked nose or ear

These features may indicate high suspicion of cancer and require prompt specialist referral.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider the most likely cause of the swelling by asking about:

- **Pain and swelling at mealtimes** — this pattern suggests a salivary stone (sialolithiasis) causing sialoadenitis. Stones block the submandibular duct, obstructing saliva flow. Symptoms typically come and go; ongoing blockage can cause infection and gland damage.
- **Growth history** — a slow-growing, painless, localised swelling is more likely to be a tumour. Around 60% of submandibular tumours are malignant, 40% benign.

Your doctor will examine inside the mouth to look for:

- Discharge from the submandibular duct opening (located under the tongue) — a swab may be taken
- A palpable stone in the duct

### Investigations

Your doctor may consider:

- **Fine needle aspiration (FNA)** if a tumour is suspected
- **Non-contrast CT scan** if swelling is recurrent — this is the preferred imaging to detect stones (plain X-ray or ultrasound may also detect stones, as 70–80% are radio-opaque)
- No imaging is needed for a clinically obvious stone or a first episode of sialoadenitis

---

## Management

### Acute Sialoadenitis (Salivary Stone / Infected Gland)

Your doctor may consider:

- **Pain relief** and advice to stay well hydrated
- **Sialogogue stimulation** — sucking a lemon wedge or sour lolly throughout the day can stimulate saliva flow and help the stone pass on its own
- If the gland is infected, **antibiotics** are usually prescribed: flucloxacillin is first-line (or erythromycin if you have a penicillin allergy). The antibiotic choice may be adjusted once swab results return.
- Infection is typically bacterial, caused by oral bacteria or *Staphylococcus aureus*

### When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor will arrange referral to an ear, nose and throat (ORL/ENT) surgeon if:

- You are systemically unwell with acute infection — urgent same-day assessment
- A stone has not passed after 48 hours — urgent assessment
- Swelling is not resolving after 7–10 days of treatment — non-urgent referral
- You have had chronic or recurrent infections — non-urgent referral, to consider gland removal
- A tumour is suspected — non-urgent referral, alongside FNA
