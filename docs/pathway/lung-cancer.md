# Lung Cancer

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment, investigation, and management of suspected or confirmed lung cancer in primary care — from initial presentation through to follow-up and supportive care.

---

## Background

Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death in New Zealand. Smoking cessation improves outcomes at any stage, even after diagnosis.

Lung cancer can occur in non-smokers. The incidence of non-smoking-related lung cancer is increasing, particularly in women and East Asian people.

Māori and Polynesian people have higher rates of lung cancer. Lung cancer is the leading cause of all cancer deaths in Māori women.

Survival, morbidity, and equity can all be improved by **early detection** in primary care through risk assessment, improved health literacy in at-risk communities, and prompt referral of suspicious cases.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Assessment Immediately

If any of the following are present, your doctor will arrange an ambulance transfer to the emergency department without delay:

- **Massive haemoptysis** — more than 100 mL blood, or coughing blood with cardiovascular instability
- **Signs of airway obstruction** — stridor or respiratory distress
- **Superior vena cava obstruction** — swelling of the face or neck with fixed jugular venous pressure
- **Spinal cord compression**

These are medical emergencies. Do not wait for a clinic appointment if you or someone with you develops these symptoms.

---

## Risk Factors

Your doctor will assess for the following risk factors for lung cancer:

- Current or previous smoking history
- Occupational exposure — most importantly asbestos
- Personal history of any cancer, including previous lung or head and neck cancer
- Family history of a first-degree relative with lung cancer
- Pre-existing lung disease — particularly COPD, interstitial lung disease, or tuberculosis
- Passive smoking
- Heavy marijuana use
- Long-term exposure to air pollution
- Age over 40 years
- Māori or Pacific ethnicity

---

## Symptoms to Discuss with Your Doctor

Symptoms of lung cancer are often nonspecific and may resemble other, more common conditions. Your doctor should be informed of:

- Unexplained or persistent cough lasting more than 3 weeks
- Coughing up blood (haemoptysis) — any amount, especially in those aged over 40
- Chest or shoulder pain
- Shortness of breath
- Hoarseness
- Weight loss of more than 10%
- Fatigue
- Unresolved chest infection
- Symptoms that might suggest spread of cancer — for example, bone pain, confusion, abdominal symptoms, or skin changes
- Venous thromboembolism (blood clot in leg or lung)

---

## High-Suspicion Features

Your doctor will have a **high index of suspicion** for lung cancer if any of the following are present — early detection significantly increases the chance of survival:

**Any haemoptysis** — especially persistent or unexplained haemoptysis in patients aged over 40 years

**Persistent (more than 3 weeks) or unexplained:**
- Cough
- Dyspnoea (breathlessness)
- Chest or shoulder pain
- Hoarseness
- Abnormal chest signs on examination
- Digital clubbing (rounded fingertips)
- Cervical or supraclavicular lymphadenopathy (swollen neck or collarbone lymph nodes)
- Weight loss
- Features suggesting cancer spread — e.g., to brain, bone, or liver

**Or any of the following:**
- Unexplained change in existing symptoms in someone with an underlying respiratory condition
- Previous chest X-ray or imaging that was suspicious, including new pleural effusion, pleural mass, or slowly resolving consolidation

---

## Clinical Signs

Signs your doctor may find on examination include:
- Abnormal chest signs — persistent pleural effusion, persistent localised wheeze
- Weight loss over 10%
- Finger clubbing
- Persistent enlarged cervical or supraclavicular lymph nodes
- Horner's syndrome
- Signs suggestive of cancer spread
- Paraneoplastic syndromes

---

## Investigations

Your doctor will arrange the following initial tests. These are not steps you can take independently — clinical assessment and ordering of tests requires your doctor's involvement.

### Chest X-ray

The **first-line investigation** for suspected lung cancer. An urgent X-ray will be arranged if:
- High suspicion of lung cancer (as above)
- Symptoms are worsening, or a chronic cough has not responded to 3 weeks of treatment

**Important:** A normal chest X-ray does not exclude lung cancer. If clinical concern persists, specialist assessment is still required.

### Blood Tests

Your doctor will arrange:
- Full blood count (FBC)
- Electrolytes and creatinine
- Corrected calcium
- Liver function tests
- CRP
- Coagulation studies

### Spirometry

Community spirometry will be arranged if available.

### Further Imaging

If the chest X-ray is abnormal or there is high clinical suspicion, your doctor will refer you to a respiratory/lung cancer service. The specialist team will then organise further investigations such as:
- CT chest
- PET CT

---

## Management

### Emergency Referral

If any red flag features are present, your doctor will arrange emergency department transfer by ambulance.

### Urgent Specialist Referral

If the chest X-ray is abnormal or suspicious, or if there is high clinical suspicion of lung cancer, your doctor will:

- Record your **ECOG Performance Status** (a measure of your functional ability — required for the referral form)
- Ensure you are informed that investigations for possible lung cancer are being arranged
- Send an **urgent non-acute respiratory referral** annotated with "high suspicion of lung cancer"
- Arrange follow-up to ensure full resolution of symptoms or chest X-ray findings

The lung cancer service will then take over, organising further investigations and treatment planning.

### Smoking Cessation

Smoking cessation is recommended for **all** patients with lung cancer, regardless of age or disease stage. Evidence shows improved outcomes for those who quit compared to those who continue to smoke. Your doctor can assist with this.

### Psychosocial Support

At diagnosis and at every follow-up visit, your doctor should assess your emotional wellbeing:
- Screening for depression and anxiety
- Discussing levels of emotional distress — for both you and your carer or family
- Identifying key areas of concern

---

## After Diagnosis — Ongoing Care

If you are diagnosed with lung cancer, your GP will provide ongoing coordinated care alongside your specialist team.

### Symptom and Side-Effect Management

At each visit, your doctor will ask about:
- Cancer-related symptoms — pain, bowel changes, and others
- Side-effects or complications from treatment

### Psychosocial Assessment at Every Visit

At every appointment, your doctor should ask how you are feeling emotionally. This includes:
- Screening for depression and anxiety
- Discussing how you feel about your treatment
- Listening to your fears and concerns about treatment and prognosis
- Providing access to an oncology nurse, where appropriate
- Arranging counselling if needed
- Checking in on how your carer and family are coping

### Support for You and Your Family

Your doctor can help you access support by:
- Asking about your support network and who helps with practical issues (transport, work, childcare)
- Providing information about support services and peer support programmes

### Lifestyle Recommendations

Your doctor will recommend:
- **Smoking cessation** — at every visit, regardless of stage
- **Healthy lifestyle** — alcohol moderation, regular exercise, good nutrition
- **Advance Care Planning** — discussing your wishes for future care, particularly if your prognosis is uncertain

### Financial Support

If financial concerns are present, your doctor can help you access:
- Work and Income NZ:
  - Disability allowance
  - Disability certificate for counselling
  - Jobseeker Support (for new cancer diagnoses meeting criteria — the entire planned treatment period can be covered on a single medical certificate)
  - Supported Living Payment
- Ministry of Health — Carer Support

### Specialist Referral for Supportive Care

Your doctor may refer you to:
- **Cancer Society of New Zealand** — support and information
- **Hospice or community palliative care team** — when appropriate for symptom management and end-of-life planning

---

## Key Points

- Lung cancer is the leading cause of cancer death in New Zealand — early detection saves lives.
- Symptoms are often nonspecific. A high index of suspicion and early chest X-ray are essential.
- A normal chest X-ray does not rule out lung cancer — further specialist assessment is still needed if clinical concern remains.
- All investigation and management must go through your doctor. Do not delay if you have red flag symptoms.
- Smoking cessation improves outcomes at any stage, even after diagnosis.
- Māori and Pacific peoples are at higher risk — culturally appropriate care and early engagement matter.
