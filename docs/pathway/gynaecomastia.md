# Gynaecomastia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of gynaecomastia — benign enlargement of breast glandular tissue in males — across all age groups.

---

## What is Gynaecomastia?

Gynaecomastia is benign enlargement of breast glandular tissue in males. It occurs due to:

- Hormone or hormone-like stimulants.
- Hormone imbalance (increased ratio of estrogen to androgen activity).
- Hormone sensitivity disorders.

It must be distinguished from breast cancer in males (far less common) and from **pseudo-gynaecomastia** (accumulation of subareolar fat without glandular proliferation — common in obesity).

Gynaecomastia can be bilateral or unilateral.

---

## Who Gets It?

| Group | What to Know |
|---|---|
| **Infants** | >50% of male infants are born with enlarged breasts from maternal estrogen; resolves within 2–3 weeks |
| **Adolescents** | 85–90% resolve spontaneously within 6 months to 2 years. May persist into adulthood (persistent pubertal gynaecomastia) |
| **Middle-aged to older males** | Caused by increasing body fat relative to lean mass and gradually declining testosterone (idiopathic) |

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Attention

The following features may suggest **breast cancer** and require prompt medical assessment:

- Discrete breast or axillary lump, ulceration, skin dimpling, or breast distortion.
- Persistent nipple eczema, ulceration, or recent (<3 months) nipple retraction or distortion.
- Unilateral, blood-stained, clear, or serous nipple discharge (green or yellow discharge is not a red flag).
- Breast inflammation or infection not responding to treatment — especially without fever or abnormal bloods.
- Repeated presentations about the same breast symptoms, other than pain.

**Note:** About 1% of breast cancer occurs in men. Men can have mammograms.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider your age and take a full history. They will ask about:

- Onset and duration of breast enlargement (one or both sides).
- Breast or testicular pain or tenderness.
- Alcohol intake and liver disease.
- Symptoms of hypogonadism (sexual dysfunction, altered body hair, small testes) or hyperthyroidism.
- Medications (many can cause gynaecomastia — see below).
- Non-prescribed substances and substances of abuse.

### Medications That Can Cause Gynaecomastia

Your doctor will review your medications carefully. Implicated drugs include:

- **Estrogens / estrogenic action:** diethylstilbestrol, phytoestrogen-containing herbal remedies, cosmetics with estrogen, phenytoin, clomifene. Possibly tea tree oil and lavender oil products.
- **Testosterone synthesis inhibitors:** metronidazole, ketoconazole, spironolactone, chemotherapy, GNRH agonists (leuprorelin, goserelin).
- **Testosterone action inhibitors:** cyproterone, finasteride, H2 receptor antagonists, proton pump inhibitors (PPIs).
- **Androgens causing high estrogen:** excessive testosterone replacement therapy.
- **Prolactin-raising medications:** antipsychotics, tricyclic antidepressants, domperidone, metoclopramide, verapamil.
- **Antiretrovirals** (mechanism unclear).
- **Antimicrobials:** isoniazid, ketoconazole, metronidazole.
- **Cardiovascular medications:** ACE inhibitors, spironolactone, digoxin, amiodarone, calcium channel blockers, methyldopa.

### Substances Associated with Gynaecomastia

- Alcohol.
- Amphetamines.
- Cannabis.
- Heroin.
- Methadone.
- Phytoestrogens (some herbal medicines).

### Physical Examination

Your doctor will:

- Perform a bilateral breast examination to confirm true gynaecomastia versus pseudo-gynaecomastia and check for red flags.
  - In true gynaecomastia: a ridge of glandular tissue is felt symmetrical to the nipple-areolar complex.
  - In pseudo-gynaecomastia: no resistance until the nipple.
- Examine the testes.
- Measure height, weight, and calculate BMI.

### Underlying Causes Your Doctor Will Assess For

- Alcohol (liver damage, inhibits testosterone).
- Illicit anabolic steroids or exogenous hormones.
- Illicit substances (cannabis, amphetamines, methadone, heroin).
- Obesity (associated with both pseudo- and true gynaecomastia).
- Liver cirrhosis (signs: hard liver edge, splenomegaly, spider naevi, palmar erythema, jaundice, ascites, peripheral oedema, bruising, leuconychia).
- Hyperthyroidism.
- Hypogonadism, including Klinefelter's syndrome.
- Haemochromatosis causing secondary hypogonadotrophic hypogonadism.
- Chronic kidney disease.
- Malnutrition.
- Neoplasms: testicular tumours (Leydig's cell), hCG-secreting tumours (lung, gastric, renal, hepatoma), adrenal tumours.

### Investigations

Your doctor will decide which investigations are needed. Most cases have a benign cause and do not require extensive testing.

**Hormone tests may include:**
- Estradiol.
- Testosterone (morning fasting sample: 7–9 am).
- Beta-hCG and alpha-fetoprotein (tumour markers).
- Luteinising hormone (LH):
  - LH high + testosterone low → testicular failure.
  - LH and testosterone both low → likely secondary (pituitary) hypogonadism.
  - LH and testosterone both high → androgen resistance or gonadotrophin-secreting tumour (rare).
- Prolactin — only if specific indications are met (e.g., secondary hypogonadism with testosterone <5 nmol/L and normal LH); not routinely indicated for gynaecomastia alone.

**Other bloods:** Renal function, liver function tests (LFTs), TSH.

**Imaging:**
- Breast imaging — only if red flag features for breast cancer are present.
- Testicular ultrasound — if testicular abnormality on examination, raised beta-hCG or alpha-fetoprotein, normal/low LH, or increased estradiol.
- Chest X-ray — only if lung lesion suspected.

## Management

Management depends on age group and underlying cause. All management decisions should be made with your doctor.

### Infants and Adolescents

- Most adolescent gynaecomastia is physiological and resolves spontaneously — your doctor will provide reassurance and review every 6 months.
- Gynaecomastia that is marked, occurs in slim boys, or in boys not yet in puberty is more likely to have an underlying cause and requires specialist assessment.
- If androgen insensitivity or a similar developmental disorder is identified, specialist referral is arranged.

### Underlying Cause Identified

If your doctor finds an underlying cause, they will manage that directly. Examples:

- Hypogonadism (including Klinefelter's) — see Testosterone Deficiency pathway.
- Hyperthyroidism.
- Liver cirrhosis.
- Chronic kidney disease.
- Alcohol or other drug use.
- Illicit anabolic steroid use.
- Haemochromatosis.
- Malnutrition.
- Obesity.
- Neoplasm.

### Eliminating Triggers

Where possible, your doctor will:
- Remove or substitute the offending medication.
- Replace androgen in cases of testicular failure.

### SERM Treatment (Tamoxifen)

If triggers cannot be removed and gynaecomastia persists, your doctor may seek specialist endocrinology advice regarding tamoxifen (a selective estrogen receptor modulator, SERM):

- Evidence shows partial regression in 50–80% of patients and complete remission in 20–80% after 3 months of treatment.
- Less effective if gynaecomastia is long-standing (>1 year), as fibrosis may have occurred.
- A reasonable option for adolescents with pubertal gynaecomastia persisting >6 months with substantial pain, tenderness, or embarrassment.
- May also be considered for idiopathic tender gynaecomastia persisting >3 months.
- Tamoxifen is not formally approved for this indication — your doctor will explain the off-label status.

### Hypogonadism

If hypogonadism is suspected, your doctor will refer for specialist endocrinology assessment before initiating testosterone replacement therapy (TRT):

- Subnormal testosterone is common and does not always indicate true hypogonadism.
- Starting TRT without specialist guidance may worsen gynaecomastia (excess testosterone is converted to estrogen).

### Middle-Aged to Older Males

- Your doctor will provide information and reassurance in the first instance.
- If testosterone deficiency is present and clinically indicated, management will be discussed.

### Pseudo-gynaecomastia

- If an underlying cause is found, it will be treated.
- Weight loss is the primary intervention.
- Surgery is a last resort if all other measures fail and the patient meets referral criteria.

### Surgery

Surgery is considered only after all other management options have been exhausted and the patient requests it. Your doctor will discuss what is realistic:

- Surgery is infrequently offered in the public system.
- Referrals are declined if: the patient is smoking or vaping nicotine, or BMI >30.
- Clinical photographs must be submitted with the referral.
- Cases are reviewed and prioritised — may be declined based on capacity.
