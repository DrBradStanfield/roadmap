# Chlamydia

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of Chlamydia trachomatis infection — including who to test, specimen collection, treatment, contact tracing, and when specialist referral is needed.

*Note: This pathway uses the terms male, female, men, and women in a cis-gendered way to refer to genital anatomy. It recognises that gender identity may not match the sex assigned at birth.*

---

## Background

### What Is Chlamydia?

- Caused by **Chlamydia trachomatis**, which infects the anogenital region including the endocervix, urethra, rectum, and occasionally the pharynx and eye.
- **Up to 75% of infections are asymptomatic.**
- Incubation period: usually 1–2 weeks, but can be 2 days to 2 months.
- Chlamydia can persist for months to > 2 years if untreated; reinfection is common.
- Transmission is through contact with infected genital or anal secretions — occurs in both symptomatic and asymptomatic infection.

---

## Who Gets Tested

Your doctor will offer chlamydia testing to patients:

- At higher risk of a sexually transmitted infection (STI):
  - Young people, men who have sex with men (MSM), Māori, Pacific peoples
  - Multiple sexual contacts, recent change in sexual contact, sexual partner with an STI, sexually active and younger than 30 years
- With signs or symptoms suggestive of chlamydia
- Before termination of pregnancy (TOP)
- Before intrauterine device (IUD) insertion if at risk of STIs
- With suspected epididymo-orchitis or pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)
- With a history of sexual assault or intimate partner violence
- Opportunistically during cervical cancer screening, antenatal care, contraception advice, or travel medicine consultations

---

## Symptoms

Up to 75% of people with chlamydia have no symptoms. When symptoms occur, they may include:

- **Urethral symptoms** (about 50% have none): dysuria, penile urethral discharge
- **Cervical symptoms** (about 75% have none): abnormal vaginal discharge, postcoital or intermenstrual bleeding
- **Anorectal symptoms** (often absent): anal pain, tenesmus, discharge, rectal bleeding, irritation, change in bowel function, painful defecation
- **Ocular symptoms**: conjunctivitis, eye inflammation
- **Pharyngeal symptoms** (usually absent)
- Pelvic pain — may suggest pelvic inflammatory disease
- Joint pain and rash — may suggest reactive arthritis
- Unilateral scrotal pain and swelling — may suggest epididymitis or epididymo-orchitis

---

## Complications of Untreated Chlamydia

If chlamydia is not treated, it can cause:

- Pelvic inflammatory disease (PID)
- Chronic pelvic pain
- Epididymo-orchitis and prostatitis
- Recurrent urethritis
- Ectopic pregnancy, premature rupture of membranes, preterm delivery, low-birth-weight infants
- Infertility
- Reactive arthritis
- Perihepatitis
- Conjunctivitis or uveitis

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will examine you if you are symptomatic and may arrange a full sexual health check at the same visit. Examination may include checking temperature, bimanual or speculum examination, urine pregnancy test, penile and testicular examination, throat, skin, and joints as clinically indicated.

---

## Investigations

Your doctor will arrange testing at the site(s) of suspected infection based on sexual activity.

**If asymptomatic and concerned about a specific recent sexual event:** your doctor may test at presentation and repeat after the recommended interval:
- Chlamydia, gonorrhoea, and trichomoniasis — retest 2 weeks after last unprotected sexual activity
- HIV and syphilis — retest 3 months after last unprotected sexual activity
- High-risk HIV exposure — test at 6 weeks and again at 3 months

**Specimen types your doctor may use:**

- **Patients with a vagina:** self-collected vaginal PCR/NAAT swab (preferred) or first-catch urine (if vaginal swab declined)
- **Patients without a vagina:** first-catch urine (first 20–30 mL of urinary stream, preferably ≥ 1 hour after last void)
- **Anyone who has anal sex or anorectal symptoms:** anorectal PCR/NAAT swab
- **Men who have sex with men (MSM):** first-catch urine + oropharyngeal swab + anorectal swab (all three, regardless of reported sexual practices or condom use, as asymptomatic infection is common)
- **Suspected conjunctivitis:** eye PCR/NAAT swab
- **Suspected pharyngitis:** throat PCR/NAAT swab

---

## Treatment

Your doctor will treat according to the clinical situation:

### Uncomplicated Genital, Pharyngeal, or Eye Infection

- **First-line:** doxycycline 100 mg orally, twice a day for 7 days
- **Alternative:** azithromycin 1 g orally as a single dose — only if doxycycline is contraindicated or adherence is highly likely to be poor

### Anorectal Infection (Asymptomatic)

- **First-line:** doxycycline 100 mg orally, twice a day for 7 days
- **Alternative:** azithromycin 1 g orally immediately and repeated in one week — only if doxycycline is contraindicated or adherence is highly likely to be poor

### Symptomatic Anorectal Infection

Management is complex. Your doctor will seek specialist sexual health advice or refer for acute sexual health assessment.

### Pregnancy or Breastfeeding

- **Pregnancy:** azithromycin 1 g orally as a single dose
- **Breastfeeding:** azithromycin 1 g orally as a single dose

### Co-Infection with Gonorrhoea

Your doctor will give ceftriaxone 1 g by intramuscular injection (single dose) plus doxycycline 100 mg orally twice daily for 7 days.

If there is a symptomatic rectal co-infection, your doctor will seek specialist sexual health advice.

### Complications (PID or Epididymo-orchitis)

Your doctor will follow the relevant pathway for pelvic inflammatory disease or epididymo-orchitis.

---

## Preventing Reinfection and Transmission

### Abstinence or Condom Use

Your doctor will advise you to abstain from sex (including oral sex) or use condoms:
- Until 7 days after you have completed treatment
- Until 7 days after your sexual contacts have been treated

### Contact Tracing

All sexual contacts from the **last 3 months** should be notified. Most people choose to tell contacts themselves; your doctor can provide written information to help.

Your doctor will advise that:
- Sexual contacts should have a sexual health check
- Immediate treatment of contacts is not routine unless the last contact was within the past 2 weeks, or the contact is symptomatic, unlikely to wait for results, or unlikely to return for treatment
- Contacts should abstain from sex or use condoms until their results are available and for 1 week from the start of treatment

### One-Week Review

Your doctor will typically review you in 1 week to:
- Discuss test results
- Check symptoms have resolved
- Confirm you completed and tolerated the medication
- Check for any risk of reinfection
- Confirm contacts have been notified

## Doxy-PEP (Doxycycline Post-Exposure Prophylaxis)

Doxy-PEP is a strategy to reduce the risk of bacterial STIs (particularly chlamydia and syphilis) in eligible people. It is **not effective** against gonorrhoea in New Zealand due to local antibiotic resistance.

### Who May Be Eligible

Your doctor will discuss doxy-PEP proactively if you are **assigned male at birth, have sex with men, and** have been diagnosed in the past 12 months with either:
- Syphilis, OR
- Two bacterial STIs (chlamydia or gonorrhoea)

Your doctor may also consider it if you are assigned male at birth, have sex with men, and:
- Have an upcoming period of high STI risk
- Have concurrent male partners and partners at risk of pregnancy
- Are Māori or Pacific (due to inequities in syphilis rates)

### How It Works

- A single 200 mg dose of doxycycline taken within 72 hours of unprotected sex significantly reduces the risk of chlamydia and syphilis (off-licence indication)
- Each dose covers the preceding 72 hours; maximum 3 doses per week
- 3-monthly follow-up appointments are required while on doxy-PEP
- A full sexual health check is required before starting and every 3 months

### Important Notes

- Doxy-PEP is **not suitable** for the prevention of gonorrhoea in New Zealand
- It is **not suitable** for people who are already a known contact of syphilis, gonorrhoea, or chlamydia — these are managed differently
- Do not share doxycycline with others
- If you have sexual contact with someone known or suspected to have an STI, seek medical attention even while on doxy-PEP
- If a syphilis serology result comes back reactive while on doxy-PEP, your doctor will seek specialist advice, as doxy-PEP may affect test results

---

## Test of Cure

If you are pregnant or have been treated for anorectal chlamydia, your doctor will arrange a test of cure:
- Best performed at least 4 weeks after completing treatment
- A self-collected PCR/NAAT swab is recommended

---

## Follow-Up Screening

Your doctor will arrange:
- Re-screening in the third trimester of pregnancy
- Re-screening 3 months after any chlamydia infection to detect reinfection
- Testing for other STIs if not already done

---

## When Specialist Advice Is Needed

Your doctor will seek sexual health specialist advice or refer for acute sexual health assessment if:
- Symptomatic anorectal infection is present
- The clinical situation is complicated
- You have an allergy or standard treatments are contraindicated
- Screening and treatment of sexual contacts cannot be provided in primary care
