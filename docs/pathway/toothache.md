# Toothache

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to toothache and orofacial pain — assessing the likely cause and determining urgency.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department immediately if any of the following are present:

- Airway compromise with or without stridor
- Raised tongue or floor of mouth
- Severe facial swelling
- Severe trismus (reduced mouth opening — cannot open mouth more than 2 cm)

These features may indicate Ludwig's angina (a rapidly spreading infection of the neck spaces from the jaw to the chest) or another life-threatening infection with risk of airway obstruction and sepsis.

---

## Background

Toothache is a common description of acute pain in the orofacial region, which may also be due to non-dental reasons.

- **Dental causes** include tooth and/or mucosal pathology — tooth decay, lost filling, infection, trauma, swelling, ulceration, erupting teeth.
- **Non-dental causes** include referred pain from temporomandibular joint disease (TMD), ear or maxillary sinus infection, or musculoskeletal pain from the neck or shoulders.
- Trigeminal neuralgia may present as tooth pain, but its brief, stabbing quality helps distinguish it from toothache.

Tooth pain:
- Is commonly unilateral, dull, and aching
- Is often associated with sensitivity to hot and cold food or fluids, and/or increased pain at night
- Is often worsened by pressure on the tooth, such as chewing
- Is sometimes poorly localised and felt at a distance from the offending tooth
- Can range from mild to severe, and may have a "nagging" or "nauseating" quality

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

**Type of pain:**
- Pain on eating hot, cold, or sweet food and drink:
  - Short and sharp pain with a stimulus only — suggests reversible pulpitis (inflammation of the dental pulp that can be restored)
  - Dull ache persisting after the stimulus is removed — suggests irreversible pulpitis, infected root canal, or periodontitis (irreversible pulpitis requires root canal treatment or extraction)
- Pain on biting — suggests abscess or cracked tooth
- Tender swelling — suggests abscess
- Pain related to a recent extraction — consider post-operative pain or alveolar osteitis (dry socket)
- Pain in upper teeth worsening as the head is tilted — consider sinus infection
- Intense, brief, sharp, unilateral pain lasting seconds — consider trigeminal neuralgia

**Associated symptoms:**
- Malaise or fever
- Clicking or painful jaw joint — may indicate temporomandibular joint dysfunction
- Trismus (restricted mouth opening)
- Difficulty breathing or swallowing
- Gum inflammation, swelling, bleeding, or recession
- Sensitive teeth
- Halitosis
- Dysphagia

**Precipitating events:**
- Recent dental procedure (especially extraction) — consider alveolar osteitis or dry socket
- History of trauma — fractures of teeth or fillings can cause pulpitis

**Physical examination includes:**
- Signs of airway compromise (stridor, difficulty breathing or swallowing, elevated or firm tongue)
- Signs of dehydration
- Facial swelling — including assessment for spreading odontogenic infection (systemic features: pallor, sweating, tachycardia, temperature above 38°C)
- Oral cavity — gum swelling, redness, recession, abscess, tenderness, caries, broken teeth, ulcers
- Alveolar osteitis: empty extraction socket with exposed bone and surrounding inflammation
- Fever and lymphadenopathy
- Trismus — if mouth opening is less than 2 cm, always assess for airway compromise
- Sinus or facial tenderness

## Possible Causes

### Pulpitis
- Reversible: short, sharp pain triggered by hot/cold/sweet — pulp vitality can be restored with treatment
- Irreversible: dull ache persisting after the stimulus — requires root canal therapy or extraction

### Infected Root Canal with Acute Apical Periodontitis
- Dull ache persisting after removal of stimulus
- Antibiotics are not indicated — definitive treatment is root canal therapy or extraction by a dentist

### Dental Abscess
- Intense, persistent, throbbing pain with redness and swelling adjacent to a tooth (intra-oral or extra-oral)
- Pain on biting; may have tender swelling

### Alveolar Osteitis (Dry Socket)
- Occurs after a recent tooth extraction — loss of the protective blood clot with exposed bone
- Severe pain radiating to the jaw, ear, or eye; halitosis; bad taste in the mouth
- Resolves in 2 to 3 weeks

### Cracked Tooth Syndrome
- Pain on biting; sensitivity to thermal change (especially cold) in a posterior tooth
- Can be difficult to visualise

### Dead Tooth (Non-Vital Tooth)
- Not sensitive to hot, cold, or sweet food or drink

### Maxillary Sinusitis
- Tender sinus; pain in upper teeth worsening as the head is tilted forward

### Trigeminal Neuralgia
- Intense, brief, sharp, unilateral pain lasting 1 to several seconds

### Other Orofacial Pain
- Temporomandibular joint (TMJ) dysfunction — clicking or painful jaw joint
- Referred pain from neck or facial muscles
- Infected gums or gingivitis
- Sore areas under dentures

## Management

> **Note:** Intravenous antibiotics are not indicated for dental abscess.

### If red flags are present
If any red flag is present, this is a medical emergency. Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance immediately.

### Pain relief

Your doctor may consider the following analgesia:

**Adults:**
- An oral NSAID (non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug) is preferred for acute dental pain — for example, **ibuprofen** 400 mg every 8 hours, unless contraindicated
- If an NSAID is contraindicated, **paracetamol** 1 g every 4 to 6 hours is the preferred alternative
- Combining an NSAID and paracetamol typically provides better pain relief than either alone
- Opioids such as **codeine** may be considered if the above combinations are insufficient, though evidence on efficacy is variable

**Children:**
- **Paracetamol** 15 mg/kg/dose (maximum 1 g per dose) every 4 to 6 hours (maximum 60 mg/kg daily)
- Can be combined with **ibuprofen** 5 to 10 mg/kg/dose every 8 hours (maximum 3 doses per day)

### By cause

**Pulpitis:**
- Avoid foods that provoke pain (hot, cold, sweet, or sour)
- Antibiotics are ineffective for pulpal pain
- See a dentist as soon as practical

**Dental abscess:**
- Requires urgent dental assessment — drainage and removal of the infection source is the definitive treatment
- Antibiotics are only appropriate if there are signs of systemic illness or significant immunocompromise
- Oral antibiotics are not appropriate in the absence of spreading infection or systemic upset — they do not prevent severe complications
- If there will be a delay in seeing a dentist, your doctor may consider:
  - **Adults (non-penicillin allergic):** Amoxicillin 500 mg orally three times a day for 3 days (an initial loading dose of 1 g can be given)
  - **Adults with severe penicillin allergy** (e.g., anaphylaxis, urticaria, angioedema): Metronidazole 400 mg orally three times a day for 5 days, or erythromycin 800 mg orally twice a day for 5 days
  - **Children (non-penicillin allergic):** Amoxicillin 15 to 30 mg/kg (maximum 1 g per dose) three times a day for 3 days
  - **Children aged 11 years or younger with penicillin allergy:** Metronidazole 7.5 mg/kg (maximum 400 mg per dose) three times a day for 5 days, or erythromycin 20 mg/kg/dose (maximum 800 mg per dose) twice a day for 5 days

**Infected root canal with acute apical periodontitis:**
- Avoid foods that provoke pain
- Antibiotics are not indicated
- Urgent dental assessment for root canal treatment or extraction

**Dead tooth (non-vital tooth):**
- Dental assessment required — not urgent unless symptomatic

**Post-surgical pain (after a dental procedure):**
- See the treating dental practitioner or surgeon urgently
- Opioid analgesia should not be prescribed before review with the treating clinician

**Alveolar osteitis (dry socket):**
- Book urgent assessment with treating dental practitioner or surgeon
- Opioid analgesia should not be provided before review with the treating clinician
- Antibiotics are not indicated
- Management may include flushing the socket with warm sterile saline and inserting a dry socket paste dressing (e.g., Alveogyl) if available; saline mouthwashes are also advised

**Non-dental orofacial pain:**
- Maxillary sinusitis — treat the sinus infection
- Temporomandibular joint dysfunction — specialist management
- Trigeminal neuralgia — specialist management
- Referred pain from neck or facial muscles — assess and manage underlying cause
