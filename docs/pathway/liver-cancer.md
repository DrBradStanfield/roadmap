# Liver Cancer

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) and primary intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma — from initial assessment through to management and supportive care.

See also: Chronic Hepatitis B, Chronic Hepatitis C, Fatty Liver (MASLD), Cirrhosis

---

## Background

Most primary liver cancers arise in patients with chronic liver disease. The risk of HCC can be significantly reduced by diagnosing and treating the underlying liver conditions early. Early detection through surveillance of at-risk patients substantially improves survival.

HCC is the second most common cause of cancer death in men in the Asia-Pacific region. In New Zealand, around 300 people are diagnosed per year, with numbers rising ~10% annually — making it the most rapidly rising cause of cancer death. Average survival after diagnosis is less than 12 months, largely because most cases are found at an advanced stage after symptoms appear.

- **90% of HCC arises in patients with cirrhosis.** HCC can also occur in chronic hepatitis B or non-alcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) without cirrhosis.
- **Hepatitis B** accounts for nearly half of HCC cases.
- **Hepatitis C** accounts for approximately one third of HCC cases. HCC can develop after successful HCV treatment if cirrhosis was present before treatment.

---

## Who Is at Risk

Your doctor will assess for known risk factors. HCC usually occurs in people with at least one of:

- **Hepatitis B infection**, especially if:
  - Maori, Pacific Islander, or Asian ethnicity
  - Male over 40 years, or female over 50 years
  - Family history of HCC
  - From a region with aflatoxin exposure (e.g., South China, Southeast Asia)
- **Cirrhosis of any cause** (including hepatitis B, hepatitis C, or alcoholic cirrhosis)
- **Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD)**, especially with:
  - Obesity and type 2 diabetes
  - High NAFLD Fibrosis Score
- **Haemochromatosis**

## Symptoms

HCC often has no symptoms early on. Tumours typically only cause symptoms when large or causing complications. If you are experiencing any of the following — especially with known liver disease — discuss with your doctor:

- Upper abdominal discomfort
- Abdominal swelling or a hard lump
- Right shoulder pain (can be referred pain from the diaphragm)
- Back pain
- Jaundice (yellowing of the skin or eyes)
- Dark urine, pale stools, or itching (signs of obstructive jaundice)
- Easy bruising
- Reduced appetite or nausea
- Unexplained weight loss
- Unusual tiredness or confusion

## Assessment

Your doctor will carry out a structured assessment. This is not something to self-manage — these steps require clinical evaluation.

### History and examination

Your doctor will look for signs of liver disease on examination, including:
- Spider naevi or palmar erythema
- Jaundice
- Enlarged or tender liver, or palpable masses
- Abdominal pain or distension
- Flapping tremor or confusion (signs of encephalopathy)
- Fever

### Blood tests

Your doctor may arrange:

- **Hepatitis B serology** (HBsAg; if positive, HBV DNA) — especially for Maori, Pacific Islander, or Asian patients
- **Hepatitis C serology** (Anti-HCV antibody; reflex HCV RNA if positive) — especially in older adults or those with past injecting drug use
- **Alpha-fetoprotein (AFP)** — a tumour marker raised in many HCC cases
- **Full blood count** — low platelets can indicate advanced cirrhosis
- **Liver function tests** — low albumin / high bilirubin suggest advanced cirrhosis; elevated ALT/AST suggest active inflammation; elevated ALP/GGT may suggest alcohol or fatty liver
- **Urea and creatinine** — renal function matters if contrast CT is being planned

### Liver ultrasound

A liver ultrasound is usually the first imaging step. If a suspicious lesion is found, your doctor will discuss it with a radiologist or liver specialist and may arrange confirmatory imaging:

- Urgent four-phase contrast CT scan of the liver (for lesions > 1 cm), or
- Urgent contrast MRI scan of the liver

A standard or non-contrast CT of the abdomen is not adequate for HCC diagnosis.

### Psychosocial check

If HCC is diagnosed, your doctor should ask about emotional wellbeing, support networks, and any practical concerns (e.g., transport, finances, work).

## Management

Management of liver cancer is complex and always involves a multidisciplinary specialist team. Your GP coordinates care but the key decisions are made by specialists. Below is an overview of what may be involved.

### When urgent or emergency assessment is needed

Seek emergency assessment if:
- **Tumour rupture or bleeding** is suspected — requires acute surgical assessment
- **Acute decompensation of liver disease** (variceal bleeding, severe jaundice, large-volume ascites, or encephalopathy) — requires acute medical assessment
- **Pain not controlled by analgesia**, especially after a procedure

### Non-urgent specialist referral

If HCC is suspected on imaging but hospitalisation is not immediately needed, your doctor will refer urgently to a gastroenterology or hepatology specialist (liver cancer team).

If you have known risk factors but current investigations are negative, your doctor may refer for routine HCC surveillance.

### Optimising liver health

In patients with chronic liver disease and a diagnosis of HCC, your doctor will address:

- **Treating the underlying liver condition:**
  - Antiviral therapy if hepatitis B with detectable HBV DNA
  - Direct-acting antiviral therapy if hepatitis C with detectable HCV RNA
- **Alcohol:** Complete abstinence is recommended to prevent further liver damage
- **Vaccination:** Hepatitis A, hepatitis B, pneumococcus, and annual influenza — best given early, as the immune response weakens as cirrhosis progresses
- **Diabetes:** Optimal glucose management
- **Weight and activity:** Manage obesity; encourage regular physical activity
- **Nutrition:** Most patients need a high protein, high energy diet to preserve muscle mass. Dietitian referral may be offered.
- **Smoking:** Cessation of tobacco and nicotine
- **Medications:** Your doctor will review for drug interactions and potential dose reductions, especially in cirrhosis

### Treatment options

The type of treatment depends on lesion characteristics, liver function, and overall health. A multidisciplinary team will discuss options, which may include:

- **Thermal ablation** (microwave, radiofrequency, or cryoablation) — destroys cancer cells with heat or cold; requires general anaesthetic
- **Irreversible electroporation (IRE)** — uses electrical pulses to destroy cancer cells; requires general anaesthetic
- **Transarterial chemoembolisation (TACE)** — delivers chemotherapy directly to the tumour via a catheter; requires sedation
- **Selective internal radiation therapy (SIRT)** — radiation-containing microspheres delivered via catheter; not publicly funded but available privately
- **Targeted drug therapy** — used in advanced disease; not publicly funded but available privately
- **Clinical trials** — patients may be eligible for trials of immunotherapy or cancer-blocking medications; trials are free, with travel and accommodation support if needed
- **Surgical resection** — most suitable for early-stage disease with preserved liver function
- **Liver transplant**

### Psychosocial support

Living with a liver cancer diagnosis is difficult. Your doctor can help coordinate support:

- Psychological support referral if you need counselling, stress management, or emotional support
- Specialist cancer support services through the hospital liver team
- Lifestyle and wellbeing approaches: acupuncture, mindfulness, yoga, healthy eating, exercise, massage
- Financial assistance: Work and Income NZ may help with prescription costs, health practitioner costs, and travel or accommodation costs

### End-stage disease and advance care planning

If the cancer is at an advanced stage, your doctor will discuss palliative care. Where prognosis is uncertain or poor, advance care planning conversations are important — covering your wishes for future care.

---

## Key Points for Patients

- Liver cancer is often silent early on — surveillance of at-risk patients (especially those with cirrhosis or chronic hepatitis B) is the best way to catch it at a treatable stage.
- If you have risk factors, ask your doctor whether you should be on a surveillance programme.
- All management decisions involve specialists. Your GP helps coordinate and support you through the process.
- Early referral matters — outcomes are significantly better when caught before symptoms develop.
