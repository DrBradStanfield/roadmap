# Humerus Fractures

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to fractures of the humerus (upper arm bone), including fractures of the humeral neck (near the shoulder) and humeral shaft (the main bone).

---

## When to Seek Urgent Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance if you have:

- Severe deformity or an open (skin-breaking) fracture
- Loss of sensation, numbness, or weakness in the arm, hand, or fingers after injury (may suggest nerve injury)
- Signs of poor blood supply to the arm: pale, cold, or pulseless hand
- High-energy trauma (e.g., motor vehicle accident, fall from height)
- Suspected non-accidental injury in a child

---

## Background

- Humerus fractures are common in older people with osteoporosis, often caused by a fall onto an outstretched hand (FOOSH) or a direct blow to the arm or shoulder.
- In younger people, they are more often caused by high-energy trauma.
- There are two main types: **humeral neck fractures** (near the shoulder) and **humeral shaft fractures** (mid-arm).

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Take a history including how the injury happened (mechanism of injury)
- Examine the arm for range of movement, nerve function, and blood supply
- Pay particular attention to the **axillary nerve** (injured in about 1 in 6 humeral neck fractures) and **radial nerve** (injured in about 1 in 9 humeral shaft fractures)
- Check for associated injuries to the shoulder, elbow, and forearm
- Arrange X-rays (AP and lateral views) to determine the type and degree of fracture displacement

**Fracture types your doctor may identify:**

- **Humeral neck fracture** — classified using the Neer system into 2, 3, or 4 part fractures, depending on how many bone fragments are separated by more than 1 cm or 45°
- **Humeral shaft fracture** — described by location (proximal, middle, distal), pattern (oblique, transverse, spiral, comminuted), and whether the skin is intact

In children, your doctor will consider whether the fracture pattern is consistent with the reported injury.

---

## Management

Your doctor will determine the right treatment based on the fracture type and severity.

### Fractures Requiring Hospital or Specialist Assessment

Your doctor will arrange urgent orthopaedic assessment if:

- The fracture involves the joint surface
- The greater tuberosity is displaced more than 5 mm
- The surgical neck is displaced more than 1 cm or angulated more than 45°
- There is a fracture of the anatomical neck
- There are multiple fractures of the proximal humerus
- There is a humeral shaft fracture

### Extra-Articular and Minimally Displaced Fractures

Your doctor may consider:

- A collar and cuff for support
- Follow-up X-rays at 7 days, 4 weeks, and 2 months
- Gentle pendular movements and wall-climbing exercises from day 7
- Stopping immobilisation at 4 weeks and allowing active arm movement
- Referral for physiotherapy to support early mobilisation
- Return to manual work is typically expected around 3 months after injury
- Referral to a fracture clinic or orthopaedic surgeon if there is any uncertainty

### Greater Tuberosity Fracture (Minimally Displaced — 5 mm or Less)

Your doctor may consider the following staged approach:

**Weeks 0–2:**
- Rest, ice, compression, and elevation (RICE)
- Adequate pain relief
- Broad arm sling until acute symptoms settle, then begin mobilisation

**Weeks 2–6:**
- Physiotherapy referral
- Passive shoulder exercises
- Active wrist and elbow exercises
- Wean off the sling by week 4
- Repeat X-rays if there is concern about stability

**Weeks 6–12:**
- X-ray at 6 weeks to check for displacement
- Once passive range is achieved, begin active shoulder exercises in all planes
- Orthopaedic advice if movement or healing is a concern

**Week 12 onwards:**
- Continue strengthening and return to normal activities
- Full recovery often takes 12 months or more
- Return to full range of motion is unlikely — most people can expect shoulder abduction of around 100–110°

### Surgical Neck of Humerus Fracture

Your doctor may consider:

- A shoulder immobiliser for impacted fractures, or a collar and cuff for minimally displaced or angulated fractures
- Review at 7 days with X-ray and gentle wrist and elbow movements
- Review at 4 weeks with X-ray, stopping immobilisation and allowing active movement
- Review at 2 months with X-ray; return to manual work typically at 3 months
- Orthopaedic advice if any displacement is found at follow-up

### Additional Considerations

- Home help and community supports may be arranged if you are elderly or living alone
- If this fracture occurred from a minor fall (minimal trauma), your doctor may assess you for **osteoporosis** to help prevent future fractures
- If rehabilitation is slow, your doctor may consider referral for specialist orthopaedic review
