# Ingrown Toenail

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of ingrown toenails (onychocryptosis), from conservative measures through to surgical treatment.

---

## Background

- Commonly affects adolescents and young adults, often male
- Generally occurs in the big toe, but can occur in lesser toes
- Painful and chronic — can affect lifestyle and daily activities
- May become infected

**Common causes:** poor cutting technique, hereditary nail shape, hyperhidrosis (excessive sweating), trauma, footwear, or external pressure

---

## Warning Signs — See Your Doctor Promptly

Tell your doctor or seek urgent advice if:

- The toe looks "sausage"-like, hot, and very swollen — this may suggest osteomyelitis (bone infection)
- You have diabetes, lymphoedema, or poor circulation in your feet — surgery carries a higher risk of infection and complications in these conditions
- You are on blood thinners (anticoagulants)

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will ask about:

- Whether you have arterial disease, lymphoedema, or diabetes-related neuropathy
- What may have caused the ingrown toenail (nail cutting technique, nail shape, footwear)
- Whether you are on blood thinners

Your doctor will determine the stage:

- **Stage 1** — redness, swelling, and pain on pressure around the nail plate
- **Stage 2** — increased symptoms, local infection, some discharge
- **Stage 3** — further increase in symptoms, granulation tissue, and thickening of the skin fold alongside the nail

Your doctor will also check circulation to the foot. If osteomyelitis or an inflammatory condition is suspected, an X-ray may be requested.

---

## Management

Management depends on the stage and your individual risk factors.

### Stage 1

Your doctor may consider conservative management:

- Removal of the nail spike with nail nippers (local anaesthetic usually not required)
- Advice on nail care: cut the nail straight across, not short at the corners (unless the nail is horseshoe-shaped, in which case corners are removed to relieve pressure)
- Daily rinsing with saline (e.g., 2 teaspoons of table salt in 500 mL warm water) and application of antiseptic solution

### Stage 2 or 3

Your doctor may consider:

- Conservative removal of the nail spike
- If the nail spike removal has not worked, **chemical matrixectomy** — a minor procedure to permanently prevent regrowth of the problem part of the nail. This uses either:
  - **Phenol** (80% solution) — applied with a cotton-tipped applicator
  - **Sodium hydroxide** (10% solution) — an alternative chemical

  The procedure involves:
  1. Local anaesthetic (digital ring block — xylocaine injected around the toe)
  2. Cutting and removing the offending nail strip down to the base
  3. Applying the chemical to destroy the nail matrix (root)
  4. Applying a neutraliser to stop the chemical reaction
  5. Dressing the toe

### If You Have Diabetes, Lymphoedema, or Poor Circulation

Surgery poses a higher risk. Your doctor may:

- Discuss your care with a more experienced colleague
- Request a diabetes active foot disease assessment if you have diabetes
- Monitor you closely after any procedure until fully healed

### If Osteomyelitis Is Suspected

If the toe appears "sausage"-like, hot, and very swollen, your doctor will seek orthopaedic advice before proceeding.

---

## After Chemical Matrixectomy — What to Expect

Your doctor will advise:

- Take analgesia (pain relief) if needed
- Elevate the foot for the first 12–24 hours
- Keep the toe dry until surgical dressings are removed
- After 24–48 hours, soak the foot for 10 minutes per day in Epsom salts or saline (2 teaspoons of table salt in 500 mL warm water)
- Dry the toe and cover with a non-stick dry dressing, changing it daily after bathing
- Consider Betadine solution after a few days to help minimise infection risk
- If there is persistent bleeding, apply pressure and elevate the foot
- Follow-up is typically at 2 days to 1 week, and again at 2–3 weeks
- The toe may take up to 6 weeks to fully heal

**Note on infection:** Redness, swelling, and some discharge are normal after chemical matrixectomy and are often mistaken for infection. Antibiotics (cream or oral) are not routinely needed and are not supported by evidence for routine post-procedure care.
