# Long Term Glucocorticoids (Steroids)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the safe use, monitoring, and withdrawal of long-term glucocorticoid (steroid) therapy.

---

## Background

Glucocorticoids such as prednisone, dexamethasone, and hydrocortisone — as well as high doses of inhaled glucocorticoids — can suppress the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis.

Key facts:

- HPA axis suppression usually occurs when prolonged steroids are given at doses **> 5 mg prednisone per day for > 3 weeks** (although age, frailty, and duration of therapy are also important).
- Physiological steroid equivalents: **prednisone 5 mg = hydrocortisone 20 mg = dexamethasone 0.5 mg** per day.
- High-dose inhaled glucocorticoids (> 1500 micrograms beclometasone or > 750 micrograms fluticasone daily) may also suppress the HPA axis.
- Recovery of normal HPA axis function may take up to 12 months; some patients may never fully recover.
- Long-term glucocorticoids do not cause clinically evident hypothyroidism but do lower TSH levels.

---

## Starting Long-Term Steroids

Your doctor will:

- Only start long-term oral steroids when a definite clinical diagnosis has been made.
- Consider the risks and benefits, your age, and any co-existing conditions (especially diabetes, hypertension, or liver impairment).

---

## Side Effects to Be Aware Of

Long-term corticosteroid use can cause:

- Insomnia
- Weight gain
- Skin thinning, easy bruising, delayed wound healing
- Gastrointestinal ulceration
- High blood pressure
- Muscle weakness
- Immunosuppression (increased infection risk)
- New-onset diabetes, or reduced blood glucose control
- Psychiatric disturbances (mania, delirium, depression) — more likely with pre-existing mood disorders, excessive alcohol intake, or prednisone doses above 40 mg
- Reactivation of latent infections

---

## Drug Interactions

Your doctor will check for important drug interactions. These include:

- Increased risk of GI bleed or ulceration with NSAIDs (e.g. ibuprofen, naproxen)
- Enhanced anticoagulant effects with warfarin
- Reduced effectiveness of diabetes medications
- Worsening of low potassium (hypokalaemia) with digoxin, diuretics, or beta-2 agonists
- Impaired immune response to vaccines

---

## Monitoring

Your doctor will monitor:

- Blood pressure
- Weight
- Blood glucose
- Bone density (DEXA scan)

## Education: What You Need to Know

### Sick day and stress dosing

Long-term steroid use suppresses your adrenal glands. During illness, trauma, or surgery, your body cannot produce the extra cortisol it normally would. This means you may need extra steroid doses — sometimes called "stress steroids" or "sick day steroids."

Your doctor should give you written advice about this. General guidance:

- **Mild illness or injury:** Double your usual steroid dose for 1 to 2 days, then return to your normal dose once improving.
- **Moderate or severe illness** (e.g. pneumonia, severe vomiting or diarrhoea, or surgery): You may need parenteral hydrocortisone 50–100 mg by injection, repeated every 6–8 hours. This may require hospital admission.

### Signs of adrenal insufficiency to watch for

If your steroid dose is reduced below the physiological equivalent (prednisone 5 mg/day or equivalent), or if you are unwell or having surgery, watch for symptoms of adrenal insufficiency:

- Nausea
- Fatigue
- Occasional vomiting
- Light-headedness

Tell your doctor promptly if these occur.

### MedicAlert

Your doctor may recommend registering with MedicAlert so that healthcare staff are aware of your steroid use in an emergency.

### Do not stop steroids suddenly

Never stop your steroids abruptly without medical guidance. If you become unwell, contact your doctor — your dose may need to be temporarily increased.

## Steroid Withdrawal and Tapering

Your doctor will guide you through gradually reducing your steroid dose. The rate of taper depends on your underlying condition.

### Suggested dose reduction regimen

| Current dose | Reduce by |
|---|---|
| Prednisone > 60 mg/day | 10 mg every 1–2 weeks |
| Prednisone 20–60 mg/day | 5 mg every 1–2 weeks |
| Prednisone 10–20 mg/day | 2.5 mg every 1–2 weeks |
| Prednisone 5–10 mg/day | 1 mg every 1–2 weeks |

If your underlying condition flares, steroids may need to be temporarily increased and then reduced more gradually.

### When you reach prednisone 5 mg (or equivalent)

At this stage your doctor will either continue a very slow taper, or follow a formal protocol to check whether your adrenal glands have recovered:

1. A blood test (0800 plasma cortisol) is taken 24 hours after your last steroid dose.
2. If cortisol levels are low (≤ 300 nmol/L), a **Synacthen test** will be arranged — this is done by an endocrinology department, not a standard laboratory.
3. Results guide next steps:
   - **Normal Synacthen result:** Steroids can be reduced to zero (e.g. by 1 mg prednisone per week). No further testing needed; you no longer require stress steroids or MedicAlert.
   - **Abnormal Synacthen result (two options):**
     - Stay on prednisone 5 mg/day and repeat Synacthen in 3 months.
     - Switch to hydrocortisone (shorter-acting) to allow earlier HPA recovery. A typical dose is 15 mg in the morning and 5 mg at 3 pm. Lower doses may be tried but watch for steroid insufficiency symptoms.

4. If the Synacthen test remains persistently abnormal, your doctor will gradually lower your hydrocortisone dose to encourage your adrenal glands to restart secreting cortisol naturally.

---

## When to See a Specialist

Your doctor will refer you to an endocrinologist if:

- A Synacthen test is required (arranged through endocrinology)
- You are unable to stop steroids despite following the withdrawal protocol
