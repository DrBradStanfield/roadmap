# Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder (PTSD)

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of PTSD in adults and children following exposure to a traumatic event.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Help

If you or someone you know is at risk of suicide or self-harm, seek urgent medical help immediately — go to your nearest emergency department or call emergency services.

---

## Background

PTSD refers to four clusters of symptoms that persist for more than 1 month after exposure to an event generally accepted as traumatic:

- **Re-living the traumatic event** — recurrent intrusive memories, flashbacks, vivid nightmares, and intense emotional or physical reactions (such as sweating, heart palpitations, or panic) when reminded of the event
- **Avoiding reminders of the event** — deliberately avoiding activities, places, people, thoughts, or feelings associated with the trauma
- **Negative changes in mood and thought processes** — dissociative amnesia, inability to experience positive emotions, loss of interest in activities, detachment or estrangement from others
- **Being overly alert or wound up** — sleeping difficulties, irritability, anger, poor concentration, being easily startled, constantly on guard for danger

Strong feelings of anxiety, distress, and agitation are common in the first days after a traumatic event. The vast majority of people will recover normal functioning with little or no intervention. PTSD is only diagnosed when these symptoms persist beyond 1 month.

Be aware that the onset of PTSD may be delayed for months or even years after the event.

---

## What Counts as a Traumatic Event?

PTSD follows exposure to events such as:

- Actual or threatened death
- Serious injury or accident
- Childhood or domestic abuse
- Sexual violence
- War, torture, or conflict
- Health-related trauma (e.g., difficult childbirth, admission to intensive care)

Note: Distress relating to other events commonly described as "traumatic" — such as failing an exam, divorce, or job loss — is better described as an adjustment or reaction disorder rather than PTSD.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will consider PTSD when psychological symptoms cause functional impairment lasting more than 1 month after a traumatic event. Assessment typically includes:

### Screening Questions

If you have been through a traumatic event, your doctor may ask whether you answer "yes" to 3 or more of these questions (a positive screen):

- Have you had nightmares about the event, or thought about it when you didn't want to?
- Do you try hard not to think about the event, or go out of your way to avoid situations that remind you of it?
- Are you constantly on guard, watchful, or easily startled?
- Have you felt numb or detached from others, activities, or your surroundings?
- Have you felt guilty or unable to stop blaming yourself or others for the event or any problems it caused?

Your doctor will not screen until at least 1 month has passed after the event, because most people recover naturally with time.

### Symptoms in Children

PTSD in children can look different from adults. Signs may include:

- Sleep disturbance or nightmares
- Regressive behaviours — acting younger than their age (e.g., thumb-sucking, bedwetting)
- Avoiding things related to the event (e.g., not wanting to get in the car)
- Physical symptoms such as headaches or stomach-aches
- Repetitive play involving trauma themes
- Fear of the dark or separation anxiety
- Oppositional behaviour or temper tantrums
- Hyperactive or disorganised behaviour
- Marked decline in academic performance
- Worry about dying at an early age

### Risk Factors Your Doctor Will Consider

**High-risk populations:**
- Military veterans
- Emergency responders
- Sexual assault survivors
- Domestic violence survivors
- Children
- Refugees and asylum seekers

**Factors before the trauma:**
- Childhood adversity or past history of trauma, abuse, or neglect
- Psychiatric history or family history of anxiety or PTSD
- Chronic stress or multiple stressors at the time of the event
- Personality traits such as introversion or pessimism

**Factors during the trauma:**
- Pre-existing mental health problems at the time of the event
- Poor coping skills or significant dissociation
- The severity, proximity, and nature of the trauma

**Factors after the trauma:**
- Poor social support — this is the single biggest risk factor
- Victim blaming (by others or by the person themselves)
- Failure to access effective treatment

### Co-Morbid Conditions

Your doctor will also check for conditions that commonly occur alongside PTSD:

- Suicide risk
- Substance abuse
- Depression
- Traumatic brain injury
- Increased cardiovascular risk
- Fatty liver disease
- Sleep disturbance or obstructive sleep apnoea
- Smoking-related conditions
- Gastric upset

### Complex PTSD

If you have experienced prolonged or repeated traumatic events (such as multiple incidents of childhood abuse, prolonged domestic violence, or torture), your doctor may consider complex PTSD. This involves all the features of PTSD plus:

- Severe difficulties regulating emotions
- Persistent feelings of worthlessness, shame, guilt, or failure related to the trauma
- Persistent difficulties sustaining relationships or feeling close to others

Complex PTSD has different care needs and your doctor will tailor their approach accordingly.

## Management

### Reassurance in the First Month

Your doctor will reassure you that:

- An acute stress reaction to trauma in the first month is normal and can involve many PTSD-like symptoms
- Most acute stress reactions do not go on to become PTSD
- PTSD has a predictable course and often resolves with treatment

Note: Post-trauma psychological debriefing is no longer recommended — there is no evidence it helps and it may be harmful. Specific treatment for PTSD is only indicated when symptoms persist beyond 1 month.

### Psychological Therapy

Trauma-focused psychological therapy is the **first-line treatment** for PTSD — it is more clinically effective and more cost-effective than medication alone.

Your doctor may refer you to:

- **Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT)** — effective even many years after the event
- **Eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing (EMDR)** — may also improve co-existing depression
- Mental health services or community counselling
- Employee assistance programmes (EAP) if you are employed
- Support services at your tertiary education provider if you are a student

### Lifestyle and Self-Care

As an adjunct to treatment, your doctor will encourage:

- Regular physical activity and a healthy diet
- Good sleep habits — keeping a consistent routine, avoiding caffeine and alcohol before bed, and engaging in relaxing activities in the evening
- Avoiding alcohol, recreational drugs, and excessive caffeine, which can worsen symptoms
- Exploring new hobbies and maintaining social connections

### Medications

Your doctor may consider medication as a second-line or adjunct treatment, particularly if there is significant depression alongside PTSD. Drug therapy is always second line — it is less effective than psychological therapy alone, and combining both has not been shown to be more effective than either alone.

Your doctor may consider:

- **SSRIs** — escitalopram, sertraline, or venlafaxine are the agents with most evidence. Any drug trial should last at least 8 to 12 weeks. PTSD often responds more slowly to antidepressants than depression does. If beneficial, treatment is usually continued for 12 months before gradual reduction.
- **Prazosin** (unapproved indication) — an alpha-adrenergic blocker with mixed evidence for treating nightmares and sleep disturbance. Used with caution if there is a risk of orthostatic hypotension; not stopped suddenly due to risk of rebound hypertension.
- **Low-dose quetiapine** (not approved) — 12.5 to 25 mg at night may be considered for significant sleep disturbance.
- **Benzodiazepines are not recommended** for PTSD.

Your doctor will always seek mental health specialist advice before starting pharmacotherapy for PTSD.

### When Specialist Referral Is Needed

Your doctor may refer you for specialist assessment if:

- There is a high risk of suicide
- The case is complex or severe
- You are experiencing postpartum PTSD
- There are significant substance abuse problems alongside the PTSD
