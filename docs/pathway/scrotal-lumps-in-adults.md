# Scrotal Lumps in Adults

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the initial approach to scrotal lumps and swelling in adults aged 15 years and over — assessing the likely cause and determining urgency.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Go to the emergency department or call an ambulance immediately if any of the following are suspected:

- **Testicular torsion** — sudden, severe testicular pain requiring surgery within 12 hours to save the testicle
- **Testicular cancer** — a new, firm, painless swelling involving the testicle itself
- **Strangulated inguinal hernia** — a bowel-containing hernia that cannot be pushed back and has become trapped

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

**Pain characteristics:**
- Location, onset (sudden or gradual), severity, and radiation (e.g., to the abdomen or flank)
- What makes it better or worse; whether it occurs at rest or with movement
- Pain that starts suddenly and is severe suggests torsion; pain that builds gradually is more typical of infection

**Swelling characteristics:**
- When the lump first appeared and whether it has grown
- Whether it extends into the groin
- Whether it changes in size when standing, coughing, straining, or lying down
- Whether it can be pushed back (reduced) when lying down

**Associated symptoms:**
- Nausea or vomiting (associated with testicular torsion and strangulated hernia)
- Urethral discharge, urinary frequency, or burning on urination (suggests infection)
- Fever (more common with infection; torsion is usually afebrile)
- Abdominal pain, back pain, weight loss, night sweats (may suggest cancer)
- Parotid (cheek) swelling (associated with mumps orchitis)

**Relevant history:**
- Previous scrotal trauma, testicular surgery, or undescended testis
- Sexual history including anal intercourse
- Risk factors for testicular cancer: undescended testis (cryptorchidism), family history of testicular cancer, Klinefelter's syndrome, or past history of testicular cancer

**Physical examination** typically includes:

- Examination standing and lying flat
- Checking for tenderness, redness, warmth, and extent of swelling
- Assessing whether the upper border of the swelling is palpable (can "get above" it)
- Checking the position and lie of each testis (a high-riding or transverse testis suggests torsion)
- Testing the cremasteric reflex (stroking the inner thigh causes the testis to retract — absent in torsion)
- Checking for pain relief on elevating the scrotum (Prehn's sign — does not relieve pain in torsion)
- Transillumination: shining a light through the scrotum — cystic swellings (hydrocele, epididymal cyst) glow; solid masses and hernias generally do not
- Checking for a varicocele (feels like "a bag of worms," more prominent when standing or straining)
- Examining the skin of the scrotum for skin conditions
- Feeling for enlarged lymph nodes in the groin

## Possible Causes

### Testicular Torsion

- Most common between ages 10–25 but can occur at any age
- Sudden onset of severe testicular pain, often with nausea and vomiting
- Pain may radiate to the abdomen
- Scrotum may be swollen and red on the affected side
- Testis may sit higher than usual or lie transversely; cremasteric reflex typically absent
- Intermittent torsion causes short episodes of severe pain that resolve on their own — still requires urgent assessment

### Torsion of Testicular Appendage

- More gradual onset than testicular torsion
- Pain localised to the upper pole of the testis
- Testis position and cremasteric reflex are usually normal
- A small blue dot ("blue-dot sign") may be visible at the upper part of the scrotum in about 1 in 5 cases
- Surgery is minor and shortens the duration of pain

### Testicular Cancer

- Peak age 20–40 years
- Usually a painless, firm swelling involving the body of the testis — though it can occasionally be painful
- Risk factors: undescended testis, family history, past testicular cancer, Klinefelter's syndrome
- About 1 in 10 cases present with signs of spread: neck lump, cough, abdominal pain or lump, leg swelling, weight loss
- May also cause hormonal effects such as gynaecomastia (breast tissue enlargement)

### Epididymo-Orchitis (Infection)

- Inflammation or infection of the epididymis and testis
- Usually gradual onset of unilateral testicular pain, with or without urethral discharge or painful urination
- May be caused by sexually transmitted infections (STIs) or urinary tract infections (UTIs); sometimes no organism is identified
- Fever may be present
- Associated with urinary tract procedures, systemic illness, immunosuppression, or mumps

### Inguinal Hernia

- A portion of bowel or tissue pushes through a weakness in the abdominal wall into the groin or scrotum
- **Uncomplicated hernia:** An intermittent or reducible lump in the groin or scrotum; more prominent when standing; can usually be pushed back when lying down
- **Irreducible (trapped) hernia:** Fixed, firm mass that cannot be reduced; may cause bowel obstruction (nausea, vomiting, abdominal bloating)
- **Strangulated hernia (emergency):** Tender, red or discoloured skin over the lump; hard surrounding tissue; signs of bowel obstruction — requires immediate surgical assessment

### Hydrocele

- A collection of fluid around the testicle, causing painless swelling confined to the scrotum
- Transilluminates brightly (light passes through)
- The testis is palpable within or adjacent to the swelling
- In adults, a new hydrocele may occasionally be a sign of underlying testicular cancer

### Epididymal Cyst or Spermatocele

- Small, smooth lump in or along the epididymis, separate from the testis
- Fluctuant (soft) and transilluminates
- Most common in men over 40
- Generally benign — can be due to trauma, inflammation, or ductal blockage

### Varicocele

- Enlarged veins of the spermatic cord — similar to varicose veins
- Non-tender "bag of worms" swelling above the testis, more prominent when standing or straining (Valsalva manoeuvre)
- Predominantly left-sided
- May cause a dull aching or "dragging" sensation
- A new right-sided or bilateral varicocele warrants investigation to rule out renal cancer

### Viral Orchitis (e.g., Mumps)

- Occurs in 15–20% of post-pubertal males with mumps
- Typically starts 5–10 days after the parotitis (cheek swelling)
- Abrupt onset of fever (39–41°C), severe testicular pain, swelling, and redness
- Usually unilateral; sterility is rare

### Trauma or Haematoma

- Usually follows a clear history of injury, though swelling may appear days to weeks later

### Anal Fistula

- Suspect if swelling extends toward or from the anal area
- Often painful and red; may have discharge, painful bowel motions, or fever

### Scrotal Skin Conditions

- Many patients presenting with a "scrotal lump" have a skin condition rather than a deeper swelling
- Examples include cutaneous cysts, genital warts, molluscum contagiosum, angiokeratoma, or (rarely) squamous cell carcinoma of the scrotal skin

### Scrotal Oedema

- Generalised swelling of the scrotal skin — causes include insect bite, nephrotic syndrome, or idiopathic scrotal oedema (often with itch)

## Management

Management depends on the identified or suspected cause:

- **Suspected testicular torsion:** Emergency surgical assessment — surgery is required within 12 hours to preserve the testicle. Your doctor will refer you immediately without waiting for investigations.
- **Suspected appendage torsion:** Your doctor may consider urgent urology referral — surgery shortens the duration of pain.
- **Suspected testicular cancer:** Your doctor will consider urgent scrotal ultrasound and referral to urology without waiting for results. Blood tests for tumour markers (beta-HCG, AFP, LDH) may also be requested.
- **Suspected strangulated inguinal hernia:** Emergency surgical assessment.
- **Epididymo-orchitis:** Your doctor will consider antibiotic treatment and may arrange STI testing and a urine test (MSU). If symptoms don't improve after 7–10 days, your doctor may consider ultrasound.
- **Uncomplicated inguinal hernia:** Your doctor will follow a hernia-specific pathway.
- **New hydrocele in adults:** Your doctor will consider urgent scrotal ultrasound (within 2 weeks) to rule out an underlying testicular cause.
- **Varicocele (new, right-sided or bilateral):** Your doctor will consider a kidney ultrasound to exclude renal cancer.
- **Smaller benign masses (varicocele, hydrocele, epididymal cyst):** Your doctor will likely reassure you that these are not dangerous. For discomfort, scrotal support and simple analgesia such as paracetamol or NSAIDs (e.g., ibuprofen) may be suggested.
- **Large benign scrotal mass (>5 cm) affecting quality of life:** Your doctor may consider a non-urgent urology referral if you are requesting surgery, following ultrasound confirmation.
- **Suspected squamous cell carcinoma of scrotal skin:** Your doctor will consider dermatology review.
- **Viral orchitis (mumps):** Your doctor will offer supportive care including over-the-counter pain relief and local measures, and will follow the appropriate viral pathway.
