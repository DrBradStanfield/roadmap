# Bowel Obstruction in Palliative Care

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of bowel obstruction in adults receiving palliative care. Bowel obstruction is common in certain cancers and requires specialist expertise to manage well.

---

## What Is Bowel Obstruction?

Bowel obstruction means the normal movement of intestinal contents is blocked or significantly slowed. It is:
- Often caused by cancer (malignant bowel obstruction) but can have non-cancer causes.
- Usually complex and requiring specialist palliative care involvement.
- Dynamic — it can partially resolve then worsen again, requiring ongoing reassessment.

The goal is to identify the cause and relieve the obstruction where possible.

---

## Symptoms

Presentation varies depending on the location and severity of the obstruction. Common symptoms include:

- **Constipation** or changed bowel habit
- **Nausea and vomiting** — in high (proximal) obstruction, vomiting is frequent and early; in low obstruction, vomiting is later and may be foul-smelling (feculent)
- **Abdominal pain** — constant pain from tumour or nerve involvement, and/or cramping colic in mechanical obstruction
- **No passage of wind** (complete obstruction)
- **Fatigue and loss of appetite**
- **Diarrhoea or faecal incontinence** — can occur with faecal overload

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will:

- Take a history and examine the abdomen (looking for distension, tenderness, altered bowel sounds, or a palpable mass).
- Perform a rectal examination if appropriate to exclude faecal impaction.
- Determine whether the obstruction is **partial** (some gas or faeces passing) or **complete** (nothing passing).
- Consider the possible cause:

**Cancer-related causes:**
- Most commonly colorectal or ovarian cancer; also melanoma, stomach, pancreatic, endometrial, bladder, or breast cancer.
- Can be due to: direct tumour compression, intraluminal tumour, peritoneal disease (carcinomatosis), or nerve involvement.
- High bowel obstruction: sudden vomiting, often with little nausea, occurs soon after eating, undigested food in vomit, central cramping pain.
- Low bowel obstruction: lower abdominal distension and pain, less frequent vomiting (possibly feculent).

**Non-cancer causes:**
- Chronic constipation, medications, post-surgical adhesions, dehydration, biochemical abnormalities (e.g. hypercalcaemia), or intestinal motility disorders (pseudo-obstruction).
- Pseudo-obstruction: usually partial obstruction with reduced bowel sounds and no colic.

- Consider whether investigations are appropriate based on the patient's overall condition and goals of care. These may include blood tests, abdominal X-ray, or CT scan.

- Discuss **goals of care** with the patient and family — considering their wishes, prognosis, and whether active treatment or comfort care is the priority.

---

## Management

**Important:** Seek specialist palliative care support early. Malignant bowel obstruction is complex and dynamic.

### Surgical Options

Surgery may benefit some patients — particularly those with a longer prognosis, good general condition, and a potentially reversible obstruction. Your doctor will weigh the likely benefits against the risks.

Surgery is generally not recommended if the patient has:
- Widespread peritoneal disease (carcinomatosis)
- Poor general condition
- Significant ascites (abdominal fluid)
- Previous abdominal or pelvic radiotherapy
- Multiple levels of obstruction
- Low albumin (nutritional marker)

Surgical options include stenting (for localised obstruction) or a venting gastrostomy (for persistent vomiting from upper obstruction).

If surgery is not appropriate, your doctor will discuss goals of care, preferred place of care, and will seek specialist palliative care advice on next steps.

### Symptom Management

Regardless of whether surgery is pursued, your doctor will:

- Explore your concerns about symptoms (fear and anxiety are common).
- Provide frequent mouth care.
- Administer medications **subcutaneously** (under the skin) via a syringe driver, since oral absorption is unreliable when vomiting.
- Review and rationalise medications — some regular medications may need to be paused.

### Dexamethasone (steroid)

For malignant bowel obstruction, a short five-day course of subcutaneous **dexamethasone** (4–8 mg daily) may be tried to reduce inflammation and swelling around the obstruction. It is stopped after 5 days regardless of response.

### Managing Partial Obstruction

When the obstruction is partial, the aim is to stimulate bowel movement. Your doctor will start a continuous subcutaneous infusion (syringe driver) with:
- A **prokinetic** (e.g. metoclopramide) to stimulate bowel movement — only if there is no colic.
- An **antiemetic** (e.g. levomepromazine) if nausea is a problem.
- An **opioid** if pain is present.

Monitoring is ongoing. If colic develops, the prokinetic is stopped. If bowel function doesn't improve, the dose may be increased.

You may be able to continue: small amounts of oral intake (low-residue diet) and laxatives.

### Managing Complete Obstruction

If the obstruction becomes complete, the approach changes to **bowel rest**. Your doctor will:
- Stop: prokinetic medications, laxatives, and opioid antagonists.
- Start a syringe driver with:
  - An **antisecretory agent** (e.g. hyoscine butylbromide) to reduce intestinal secretions and colic.
  - An **antiemetic** (e.g. haloperidol) for nausea.
  - An **opioid** if pain is present.
- Minimise oral intake, or continue small amounts if tolerated and preferred.

Regular review continues. If improvement occurs, the approach may shift back toward bowel stimulation. If the patient deteriorates, a transition to last days of life care will be discussed.

---

## Transition to Last Days of Life

If appropriate, your doctor will guide a transition to last days of life care, focused entirely on comfort.

---

## When Specialist Palliative Care Advice is Sought

Your doctor will seek specialist palliative care support:
- Early in the management of malignant bowel obstruction.
- When considering hospice admission.
- If the obstruction progresses from partial to complete.
- If the patient's condition deteriorates.
- If surgery is being considered.
