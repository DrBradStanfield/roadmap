# Scleritis and Episcleritis

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers two distinct causes of red eye — scleritis (serious, requires medical assessment) and episcleritis (benign and self-limiting).

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Care

See your doctor urgently if you have:

- A deeply painful, intensely red eye (suggests scleritis)
- Dark patches visible through the white of the eye (suggests necrotising scleritis — needs urgent treatment)
- Known connective tissue or autoimmune disease (e.g. rheumatoid arthritis, lupus) and a painful red eye

---

## Background

### Scleritis

- Inflammation of the sclera (the white of the eye), which may also involve the cornea and the layer beneath it.
- Causes severe, deep aching pain — usually bad enough to wake you from sleep or prevent sleep. Pain may radiate to the face and the area around the eye.
- Vision may be blurred.
- Often associated with underlying systemic autoimmune or connective tissue diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis, Wegener granulomatosis, systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE), vasculitis, and inflammatory bowel disease.
- If untreated, can damage eye structures and cause permanent vision loss. The most severe form is necrotising scleritis.

### Episcleritis

- A benign inflammation of the episclera — the thin, superficial layer over the sclera.
- Causes redness, irritation, and mild pain only. Vision is unaffected.
- Self-limiting: resolves on its own within approximately 3 weeks, though it can recur.
- Usually has no identifiable cause, but can occasionally be associated with autoimmune conditions.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will differentiate between scleritis and episcleritis based on your history and examination.

**History your doctor will ask about:**

- Severity of pain — scleritis causes severe, aching pain that may disrupt sleep; episcleritis causes only mild discomfort
- Whether you have a known autoimmune or connective tissue disease
- How quickly the redness and pain developed

**Examination findings:**

- Scleritis produces a deep, dusky or violaceous (violet-tinged) redness of the eyeball — not the eyelids
- Episcleritis produces a more superficial redness
- In scleritis: redness may be generalised or involve a sector of the eye; dark areas of uvea may be visible through the sclera in the most severe (necrotising) form
- In both conditions: eye movements are normal, with no protrusion of the eye (proptosis)
- In episcleritis: vision is usually normal, though sometimes slightly blurred
- In scleritis: vision may be blurred; there may be photophobia (light sensitivity); the condition can affect both eyes

---

## Management

### Scleritis

Your doctor will refer you for acute ophthalmology assessment. While awaiting specialist review, your doctor may consider:

- **Ibuprofen** SR 800 mg twice a day for 1–2 weeks (high-dose oral NSAID)
- As an alternative, **oral prednisone** at moderate dose (typically 0.5 mg/kg), tapered over 1–2 weeks
- Topical eye drops are of little, if any, benefit for scleritis

If necrotising scleritis is suspected (dark areas visible through the sclera), urgent ophthalmology referral is needed.

### Episcleritis

Episcleritis does not require referral. Your doctor may recommend:

- Reassurance — it resolves on its own within approximately 3 weeks
- **Artificial tears** to relieve discomfort
- **Ibuprofen** (oral NSAID) if needed for symptom relief

If symptoms worsen, your doctor will reassess for the possibility of scleritis.
