# Malaria

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of malaria in returned travellers, and prophylaxis for those planning travel to endemic areas.

---

## Important Note

All patients with suspected malaria need specialist assessment. Treatment for malaria is **only available in hospital pharmacies** — even uncomplicated malaria requires infectious disease physician approval. This information is to help you understand what your doctor is considering, not to guide self-treatment.

---

## Who Is at Risk

Malaria is a mosquito-borne infection caused by *Plasmodium* parasites. The main species affecting travellers:

- ***P. falciparum*** — the most dangerous; most common in sub-Saharan Africa
- ***P. vivax*** — less severe but can relapse months later from dormant liver forms; common in Asia, Oceania, South America
- ***P. ovale*** — similar to vivax; can also relapse
- ***P. malariae*** — generally milder
- ***P. knowlesi*** — rare; found mainly in Malaysian Borneo; can be severe

Young children and pregnant women are particularly susceptible to severe malaria.

---

## Prevention (Prophylaxis for Travellers)

If you are travelling to a malaria-endemic area, speak to your doctor before departure. No antimalarial medicine is completely effective — avoiding mosquito bites from dusk to dawn remains essential.

**Protective measures include:**
- Wearing long, light-coloured clothing
- Using insect repellent
- Sleeping under treated mosquito nets
- Avoiding perfume and aftershave (which attract mosquitoes)

**Prophylaxis medicines commonly used:**

| Medicine | Notes |
|---|---|
| Doxycycline | Funded (off-label use) |
| Atovaquone + proguanil (Malarone) | Unfunded |
| Mefloquine | Off-label, unfunded; available from travel clinics |
| Tafenoquine | Off-label, unfunded; available from travel clinics |
| Hydroxychloroquine | Only effective in Haiti |

- Recommendations vary by country and region — your doctor will advise based on your specific destination and risk profile.
- Migrants returning to visit family in malarious areas should still take prophylaxis, as prior immunity is lost after living in non-endemic countries.
- If travelling to a remote high-risk location, discuss emergency standby treatment (e.g. Riamet/artemether-lumefantrine) with your doctor.
- Seek medical care promptly for any fever during travel or after returning from a malarious area.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care Immediately

If a returned traveller has any of the following features of **severe malaria**, call an ambulance or go to the emergency department:

- Reduced level of consciousness or confusion (impaired GCS)
- Generalised weakness — unable to sit, stand, or walk without help
- Seizures
- Jaundice (yellowing of skin/eyes)
- Low oxygen saturation (below 92%) or fast breathing (more than 30 breaths/minute in adults)
- Significant bleeding or signs of clotting problems
- Shock

Severe malaria is usually caused by *P. falciparum* but can also occur with *P. knowlesi*, and rarely *P. vivax* or *P. malariae*.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will always consider malaria in any febrile patient who has returned from an endemic area, even if malaria prophylaxis was taken — prophylaxis does not guarantee protection.

**Incubation period:**
- Typically 7–14 days after exposure
- May be longer if prophylaxis was taken
- *P. vivax* and *P. ovale* can relapse months after return (dormant liver stage)

**Symptoms of malaria** (non-specific — can resemble many infections):
- Fever (classically cyclical, but cycles are often not obvious)
- Malaise and fatigue
- Headache
- Muscle aches (myalgia)
- Nausea and vomiting
- Diarrhoea
- Abdominal pain
- Backache
- Delirium (in more severe cases)

Signs in uncomplicated malaria may include fever, fast heart rate, mild fast breathing, pallor, and enlarged spleen.

Note: Lymph node swelling and joint effusions are not typical of malaria — if present, your doctor will investigate for an alternative cause.

---

## Investigations

Your doctor will only arrange investigations if you appear clinically stable. If there is high suspicion and you appear unwell, you will be sent directly for specialist assessment rather than waiting for test results.

**Tests for malaria:**
- "Malaria parasites" blood test — this requests thick and thin blood films (the gold standard). If the first test is negative but suspicion remains high, repeat testing is recommended.

**Other blood tests typically ordered:**
- Full blood count (FBC)
- CRP (inflammation marker)
- Renal function and electrolytes
- Liver function tests (LFTs)
- Blood cultures

**Additional tests depending on symptoms:**
- Stool specimen — if diarrhoea is present (travel history noted on form)
- Urine test — if urinary symptoms
- Mosquito-borne viral PCR (dengue, Zika, chikungunya) — if illness is within the first 5 days

---

## Management

Your doctor will refer all patients with suspected malaria for specialist assessment:

- **Adults:** Acute infectious diseases assessment
- **Children:** Acute paediatric medical assessment

This is because malaria can deteriorate rapidly and can be fatal. Treatment medicines are only stocked in hospital pharmacies and require infectious disease physician prescription — even for uncomplicated cases.

Malaria is also a **notifiable disease** — your doctor is required by law to report both suspected and confirmed cases to Public Health.
