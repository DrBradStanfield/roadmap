# Opioid Reduction Management

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway applies to people who are reducing their use of prescribed opioids. Opioid reduction is a gradual, supervised process — do not stop or reduce your dose without discussing it with your doctor first.

---

## Important — Speak to Your Doctor First

Reducing opioids without medical supervision can cause serious withdrawal symptoms and a high risk of relapse. **Your doctor will create an individualised plan for you.** This information is provided to help you understand what that process involves.

---

## Background

The main aim of managed opioid withdrawal is to reduce the physical and psychological discomfort that comes from stopping opioid use. Withdrawal needs to be a gradual process, with the dose slowly reduced over time. When it is rushed or not well managed, the risk of relapse is very high.

**Timing of withdrawal symptoms:**

- Short-acting opioids (e.g., codeine, morphine, oxycodone): symptoms typically begin 8–12 hours after the last dose
- Long-acting opioids (e.g., methadone): symptoms typically begin 24–48 hours after the last dose

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess you thoroughly before starting a reduction plan. This includes:

**Drug history:**
- What opioid you are taking, why it was prescribed, and how long you have been on it
- Your current dose and whether the dose has increased over time
- Whether you use immediate-release or slow-release formulations
- Any other medications, including gabapentinoids, benzodiazepines, muscle relaxants, or medicinal cannabis

**Pain and contributing factors:**
- History of the pain problem and what non-drug approaches have already been tried
- Any co-existing conditions such as depression, anxiety, history of trauma, or chronic disease
- Other substance use (e.g., alcohol, tobacco, benzodiazepines, cannabis)

**Signs of physical dependence:**

People on long-term opioids who have developed dependence may report that they:
- Feel they cannot cope without the medication
- Feel unwell, anxious, or have insomnia when they reduce the dose
- Feel the medication has less effect over time
- Have taken extra doses during stressful times or gradually increased the dose
- Make sure they always have access to their opioids

**Signs of opioid addiction:**

Opioid addiction involves a problematic pattern of use with significant distress or impairment, plus 2 or more of the following in the past year:
- Using larger amounts or over a longer period than intended
- Obtaining opioids from multiple sources (different doctors or illicit supply)
- A persistent desire to cut down
- Spending a lot of time obtaining or recovering from use
- Strong cravings to use opioids
- Failing to fulfil work, home, or study roles
- Continuing use despite relationship problems
- Giving up social or recreational activities
- Continuing use despite physical or psychological harm

**Opioid-induced adrenal insufficiency:**

Long-term opioid use can suppress the adrenal glands (estimated to affect around 15% of people on opioids for 3–6 months or more). Your doctor may check a morning cortisol blood test if you have symptoms such as fatigue, nausea, vomiting, weight loss, dizziness, or muscle aches — particularly if you are unwell, injured, or facing surgery.

If your cortisol level is low, you may be at risk of an adrenal crisis during acute illness or surgery. The treatment is to slowly taper the opioid to allow the body to resume normal cortisol production.

## Management

Your doctor will direct and supervise your reduction plan. The information below describes what that plan may involve.

### Assessing who can reduce in general practice

Opioid reduction in general practice is appropriate when:
- The opioids are prescribed (not illicit)
- There is a supportive home environment
- The patient is engaged and motivated
- There are no major mental health concerns
- There is no other dependence or substance use issue

If opioid addiction is present, or if the situation is more complex, your doctor will refer you to a specialist addiction service.

### The reduction plan

A good therapeutic relationship between you and your doctor improves outcomes. Expect an open, non-judgemental discussion about your opioid use, the benefits and risks, and what is realistic.

Your doctor may consider:

- **An opioid contract** — a formal written agreement between you and your doctor about how the reduction will be managed
- **Stopping versus tapering:** People on low doses for short periods may be able to stop quickly. People on higher doses or long-term treatment will need a gradual taper
- **Rate of tapering:** Typically a 10% reduction in the total daily dose every one to two weeks, though this is flexible and agreed with you. Slower rates (e.g., 5% reductions) may suit some people
- **Dose timing:** Reducing the dose at the time of day when pain is least severe (e.g., starting with the morning dose)
- **Pause option:** You can ask to pause the reduction for a few days if it becomes too difficult
- **Switching opioids:** If you switch from one opioid to another (e.g., from a fentanyl patch to oral morphine), your doctor will use an equivalence calculator and reduce the equivalent dose by at least 25–50% for safety
- **Dispensing frequency:** More frequent dispensing (e.g., weekly) is preferred during reduction. Daily pharmacy pick-up may be used if there is a risk of overdose
- **Avoiding benzodiazepines:** These must not be co-prescribed during withdrawal, as they significantly increase the risk of breathing problems. If you are also taking benzodiazepines, these may need to be withdrawn first

**Long-term effects of opioids your doctor will discuss with you:**
- Drowsiness, cognitive effects, poor balance, and fall risk
- Breathing problems (respiratory depression)
- Hormonal effects — including reduced testosterone, erectile dysfunction, osteoporosis
- Opioid-induced hyperalgesia — paradoxically increased pain with long-term use, where the opioids worsen sensitivity to pain. In this case, increasing the dose makes things worse; tapering is the treatment

### Managing withdrawal symptoms

Your doctor may prescribe medications to help with specific withdrawal symptoms. These may include:

| Symptom | What your doctor may consider |
|---|---|
| Agitation | Quetiapine 25–50 mg (maximum 200 mg in 24 hours), or chlorpromazine 10–50 mg |
| Diarrhoea | Loperamide 2 mg capsules |
| Nausea or vomiting | Metoclopramide 10 mg three times daily |
| Gut cramps or spasms | Hyoscine butylbromide 10–20 mg |
| Sleep difficulty | Quetiapine 50 mg at night, or promethazine 25–50 mg at night |
| Raised blood pressure, pulse, or anxiety | Clonidine patch (100 micrograms/24 hours, replaced weekly) |

*Note: Quetiapine is typically used for a maximum of 3 weeks in this setting. Benzodiazepines are not appropriate for opioid withdrawal.*

**Typical opioid withdrawal symptoms** (what to expect during the process):
- Anxious or low mood
- Nausea, vomiting, or diarrhoea
- Muscle aches and cramps
- Abdominal and back pain
- Runny nose or watery eyes
- Sweating
- Fast heart rate
- Restlessness and agitation
- Yawning, fatigue, insomnia
- Cravings

### Safety — naloxone

Your doctor may consider prescribing naloxone (a medication that reverses opioid overdose) to keep at home during the reduction process, especially if there is a risk of accidental overdose. Gabapentinoids (e.g., pregabalin, gabapentin) taken alongside opioids increase overdose risk.

### Driving

Withdrawal symptoms and medications used to manage them may make it unsafe to drive. The opioids themselves may also impair driving. Discuss this with your doctor.

### Monitoring

Your doctor will typically review you monthly, or more often if needed. At each visit, they will:
- Check your blood pressure and pulse
- Review withdrawal symptoms and adjust medications if needed
- Consider urine drug screening to check for other substance use
- Encourage you to return any unused opioid medication to the pharmacy

### Relapse prevention

Relapse is common — this is not a failure, and support is available. Your doctor may:
- Offer ongoing counselling or refer you to a specialist service
- Address co-existing mental health conditions (depression, anxiety)
- Encourage non-drug approaches (see below)

**Non-pharmacological support:**
- Anxiety management and stress reduction techniques
- Sleep hygiene
- Relaxation and breathing exercises
- Mindfulness and meditation
- Online CBT programmes (e.g., [Sleepio](https://www.sleepio.com/) for insomnia)

---

## When Specialist Referral is Needed

Your doctor will seek specialist addiction input if:
- You show features of opioid addiction (not just dependence)
- You are not suitable for reduction in general practice
- You are unable to follow the reduction plan
- You need advice on a specific withdrawal regime (e.g., codeine, tramadol)
- You are also taking benzodiazepines
- Your morning cortisol level is low and there is concern about causes beyond opioid-related suppression
