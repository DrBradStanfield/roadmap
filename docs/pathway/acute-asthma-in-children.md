# Acute Asthma in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of acute asthma in children aged 1 to 11 years.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Emergency Care

Call an ambulance immediately if your child has:

- Deterioration despite maximal treatment
- Signs of respiratory failure (exhaustion, confusion, unable to talk or feed, silent chest, cyanosis)
- Signs of exhaustion
- Suspected anaphylaxis (swollen tongue or throat, collapse, pale and floppy)

---

## Background

Acute asthma is a sudden worsening of breathing due to narrowing of the airways. Māori and Pacific children are more likely to be hospitalised or die due to asthma compared to non-Māori — early and adequate treatment matters.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will assess severity first — if the child appears severely or life-threateningly unwell, treatment begins before further assessment.

### Severity Classification

| Feature | Mild | Moderate | Severe | Life-threatening |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Appearance | Normal; able to talk; mild wheeze | Normal; able to talk; moderate wheeze | Agitated or lethargic; 1–2 words only; tripod posturing | Exhausted, confused, unable to talk or feed; silent chest; cyanosis |
| Respiratory rate | Normal | Normal or slightly increased | Significantly increased | Markedly increased (or decreased if exhausted) |
| Heart rate | Normal | Normal or mildly fast | Fast | Markedly fast or slow |
| Work of breathing | Normal | Mildly increased | Obviously increased (recessions, tracheal tug, nasal flaring) | Markedly increased or poor effort if exhausted |
| Oxygen saturation | ≥ 92% | ≥ 92% | < 92% | < 92% |
| Peak flow (if used) | ≥ 50% predicted | ≥ 50% predicted | 30–50% predicted | < 30% predicted |

Note: wheeze intensity alone is not a reliable indicator of severity. General appearance, mental state, and work of breathing are the most important signs.

### History

Your doctor will ask about:

- Possible anaphylaxis (sudden onset, swollen tongue or throat, collapse, pale and floppy)
- Possible inhaled foreign body (abrupt onset after choking, focal wheeze, unilateral reduced breath sounds)
- Features that suggest asthma: expiratory wheeze, chest tightness, shortness of breath, cough — especially if triggered by exercise, cold air, or allergens, worse at night, or recurrent
- Previous hospitalisations, ICU admissions, or need for oral steroids for asthma
- Major co-morbidities (e.g., heart disease)
- Other possible causes: hyperventilation (anxiety), stridor (croup, anaphylaxis), vocal cord dysfunction, aspiration, congenital airway disorders

### Examination

Your doctor will assess:

- Respiratory rate, heart rate, oxygen saturation, temperature (see normal ranges by age below)
- Work of breathing: recessions, tracheal tug, nasal flaring, accessory muscle use
- Chest auscultation (wheeze, air entry)

**Normal physiological ranges by age:**

| Age | Heart rate (beats/min) | Respiratory rate (breaths/min) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 year | 105–150 | 20–45 |
| 2 years | 95–150 | 20–40 |
| 4 years | 80–150 | 17–30 |
| 6 years | 75–140 | 16–30 |
| 8 years | 70–130 | 16–30 |
| 10 years | 60–130 | 15–25 |
| 12 years | 65–120 | 15–25 |

## Management

Your doctor will manage based on assessed severity. Do not underestimate severity — parents, patients, and doctors can all underestimate how serious an episode is.

### Life-threatening Asthma

Your doctor will:

1. Call an ambulance immediately (priority one, intensive care paramedic).
2. Give high-flow oxygen (15 L/min) via mask.
3. Seek immediate paediatric specialist phone advice.
4. Give continuous nebulised salbutamol 2.5–5 mg (or salbutamol MDI 6 puffs of 100 mcg via spacer every 5 minutes) until ambulance arrives.
5. Add ipratropium bromide — nebulised 0.25 mg or MDI 4 puffs of 20 mcg via spacer, repeated every 20 minutes for up to three doses.
6. If suspected anaphylaxis or imminent arrest: give IM adrenaline 0.01 mL/kg of 1:1000 solution (min 0.1 mL, max 0.5 mL), may repeat after 5–10 minutes.
7. Give steroid — IV hydrocortisone 4 mg/kg (max 100 mg), or if no IV access, oral prednisolone/prednisone 1 mg/kg (max 40 mg) immediately.
8. If IV access available and skills allow, consider IV salbutamol bolus (10 mcg/kg, max 500 mcg) or IV magnesium sulfate — only after paediatric specialist advice.
9. Monitor continuously and reassess after each dose; the child must be transferred to hospital even if improving.

### Severe Asthma

Your doctor will:

1. Call an ambulance (priority one, intensive care paramedic).
2. Give high-flow oxygen (> 10 L/min) to maintain saturations > 92%.
3. Seek immediate paediatric specialist phone advice.
4. Give salbutamol every 20 minutes until respiratory distress settles — MDI 6 puffs of 100 mcg via spacer, or nebulised 2.5–5 mg with oxygen.
5. Add ipratropium bromide — MDI 4 puffs of 20 mcg via spacer, or nebulised 250 mcg with salbutamol, repeated every 4 hours until improved.
6. Give oral prednisolone/prednisone 1 mg/kg (max 40 mg) immediately.
7. Monitor continuously and reassess; the child must be transferred to hospital even if improving.

### Moderate Asthma

Your doctor will:

1. Give salbutamol MDI 6 puffs of 100 mcg via spacer.
2. Reassess severity every 15–20 minutes for 1 hour; repeat salbutamol every 20 minutes if still moderate.
3. If aged ≥ 5 years, give prednisolone/prednisone 1 mg/kg (max 40 mg) immediately. Oral steroids are not usually required for moderate wheeze in children under 5.
4. If worsening or not improving after 1 hour, manage as severe and call an ambulance.
5. If stable at reassessment and no ongoing signs of moderate/severe/life-threatening asthma, the child may be able to go home, with follow-up arranged within 1 week (or 24–48 hours if needed).
6. Discuss stepwise home management:
   - Mild symptoms (exercise or waking): 2 puffs salbutamol via spacer as needed.
   - Wheeze with persisting difficulty breathing: 6 puffs salbutamol via spacer, one at a time. Seek medical help if reliever doesn't work, wears off within 2–3 hours, or attack continues 6+ hours.
   - Extreme breathlessness (cannot speak or walk): Call an ambulance; give 6 puffs via spacer every 6 minutes while waiting.
7. Provide the Asthma and Respiratory Foundation NZ Child Asthma Action Plan — available in English, te reo Māori, Samoan, and Tongan.
8. Continue oral steroids 1 mg/kg (max 40 mg) once daily for 3–5 days (not usually needed in preschoolers).
9. Check inhaler technique and barriers to adherence. Assess for social risk factors (household smoking, inadequate heating).

### Mild Asthma

Your doctor will:

1. Give 1–2 puffs of salbutamol (or terbutaline) via metered-dose inhaler with spacer.
2. Reassess after 15–20 minutes; increase treatment if needed.
3. Once stabilised, educate patient and family on stepwise home management and action plan (same as above).
4. If the episode has lasted several days and child is aged ≥ 5 years, consider prednisolone/prednisone 1 mg/kg (max 40 mg) once daily for 3–5 days. Not recommended for most mild episodes.
5. Arrange follow-up to review ongoing long-term management.
6. Check inhaler technique and barriers to adherence.
7. Assess for social risk factors (household smoking, inadequate heating, transport barriers).

### Using a Spacer Correctly

- Prime with 10 "waste" puffs before first use (not needed after first use or if washed with detergent).
- If child cannot seal lips around spacer, use a close-fitting face mask.
- Shake the inhaler vigorously before each puff.
- Allow 6 breaths from the spacer after each puff.
