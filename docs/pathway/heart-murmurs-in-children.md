# Heart Murmurs in Children

*Source: Auckland Region HealthPathways*

This pathway covers the assessment and management of heart murmurs detected in children, including distinguishing innocent murmurs from those requiring further investigation.

---

## Red Flags — Seek Urgent Medical Assessment

Seek immediate medical attention if any of the following are present:

- **Central cyanosis** (blue discolouration of lips or tongue)
- Abnormally strong or weak pulses
- Symptoms of cardiac dysfunction — breathlessness or sweating with feeding

---

## Background

Heart murmurs are very common in children:

- More than 80% of children have an innocent heart murmur at some point in childhood, most commonly between ages 3 and 4 years.
- All innocent heart murmurs are made louder by states of increased cardiac output — fever, pain, fear, and exercise.
- Underlying structural heart problems are uncommon and more likely to be found in infants under 12 months of age.
- Congenital heart disease affects around 1% of live births, most occurring without a family history.
- Acyanotic lesions (such as ventricular septal defect (VSD), atrial septal defect (ASD), patent ductus arteriosus (PDA), aortic coarctation, pulmonary or aortic stenosis) are more common than cyanotic lesions (such as tetralogy of Fallot, transposition of the great arteries, single ventricle variants).
- More complex lesions are often diagnosed during pregnancy on ultrasound, but a normal fetal ultrasound does not rule out congenital heart disease.
- Heart murmurs detected in older children and adolescents are less likely to represent congenital heart disease.

---

## Assessment

Your doctor will take a history including:

- Checking for red flags (see above)
- Symptoms that may suggest an underlying heart problem:
  - Cyanosis or "dusky" spells, persistent breathlessness, or frequent respiratory infections
  - Sweatiness with feeds (in babies)
  - Reduced exercise tolerance compared to peers (in older children)
  - Exertional syncope (fainting during activity)
  - Poor growth
  - Note: exertional chest pain and palpitations are common in normal children and are relatively poor discriminators
- Past history of illness consistent with rheumatic fever
- Family history of congenital heart disease in a first-degree relative
- Whether the child has dysmorphic features

Physical examination typically includes:

- Growth measurements
- Vital signs including blood pressure
- Pulses
- Heart sounds, listening for any abnormal extra sounds
- Oxygen saturations
- Features of the murmur:
  - **Grade** of systolic murmur (1–6 scale):
    - Grade 1 — barely audible
    - Grade 2 — soft but easily audible
    - Grade 3 — moderately loud but no thrill
    - Grade 4 — louder with a thrill present
    - Grade 5 — audible with stethoscope just touching the chest
    - Grade 6 — audible without a stethoscope
  - Where the murmur is loudest
  - Radiation (e.g., to the back or axilla)
- Hepatomegaly (enlarged liver)

---

## Management

Your doctor may consider the following based on the child's age and findings:

- **Newborns under 4 weeks:** Urgent discussion with a neonatologist or paediatrician is needed.
- **Children 4 weeks or older with red flags:** Acute paediatric medical assessment is required.
- **Children with concerning features:** Your doctor will refer for non-acute paediatric assessment if any of the following are present:
  - Murmur first noted before 3 months of age
  - Murmur not previously investigated in a child with dysmorphic features or a family history of congenital heart disease in a first-degree relative
  - Loud systolic murmur — grade 3/6 or louder
  - Any diastolic murmur
  - Abnormal heart sounds (e.g., a click or abnormal splitting of the second heart sound)
  - Harsh murmur quality
  - Murmur radiating to the back or axilla
- **Murmur persisting beyond age 10 years:** Your doctor may consider an ECG to help detect asymptomatic small atrial septal defects (ASDs), which may not be picked up by clinical criteria alone. If the ECG is abnormal or if symptoms have developed, paediatric assessment will be arranged.
- **Reassurance:** If no concerning features are present, your doctor will reassure you that most murmurs are innocent — particularly those only heard when the child is tachycardic (e.g., during fever, pain, fear, or exercise).
- **Follow-up:** If the murmur is first heard when a child is unwell, your doctor will arrange a follow-up examination once the child has recovered.
