# Bone Health: Osteoporosis, Bone Density Scans and Fracture Prevention

## In short

- Osteoporosis means bones thin enough to break easily. It usually causes no symptoms until a bone breaks [8][10].
- A bone density (DXA or DEXA) scan gives a T-score: -2.5 or lower is osteoporosis; between -1.0 and -2.5 is osteopenia [2][34].
- Guidelines base decisions on your 10-year fracture risk (often FRAX), not the T-score alone [1][2][4].
- A bone broken in a minor fall from age 50 points to osteoporosis by itself [4].
- Heavy, progressive strength training and impact exercise help bone, and balance exercise cuts falls [4][31][42].
- Calcium from food is preferred. Supplements do little to prevent fractures in healthy people at home, though experts disagree on details [19][20][42].
- Bisphosphonates and denosumab cut fractures, and serious side effects are rare. Never stop denosumab without a plan: fractures can follow fast [15][32][35][36].
- The Health Roadmap suggests a bone density scan for women from 50 and men from 70 (the health tool).

## Get help now

- Severe hip, groin or leg pain after a fall or injury, being unable to stand or put weight on the leg, or tingling or loss of feeling in the hip or leg: call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) and do not drive yourself [10][11].
- After a fall, if the person may have hurt their head, neck, back or hip, or cannot get up: call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) and do not try to lift them yourself [12].
- Swelling of the face, lips or tongue, throat tightness or trouble breathing after a bone medicine injection: call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [15].
- Chest pain or signs of a stroke (face drooping, arm or leg weakness, trouble speaking), especially while on romosozumab: call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [16].

## What it is

Bone is constantly broken down and rebuilt. From middle age, and fast after menopause, breakdown wins, so bones thin [8][43].

Often the first sign is a broken wrist, hip or spine bone after a minor fall. Back pain, a hunched posture or lost height can come from small spine fractures [8][10]. A broken hip is the most serious: it needs hospital care straight away, and many people never regain their old strength [11].

**Risk factors** include older age, menopause, a parent with a hip fracture, low body weight, smoking, more than 14 units of alcohol a week, steroid tablets (5 mg or more of prednisolone a day for over 3 months), two or more falls in a year and a past fragility fracture [1][10].

## Your numbers

**T-score.** A DXA scan compares your bone density with a young adult's:

| T-score | Meaning |
|---|---|
| -1.0 or higher | Normal |
| Between -1.0 and -2.5 | Osteopenia (low bone mass) [34] |
| -2.5 or lower | Osteoporosis [2] |

**10-year fracture risk (FRAX).** FRAX turns age, sex, risk factors and bone density into a 10-year chance of a major fracture [1][4]. NZ doctors may use FRAX or a Garvan score [8][46].
- Australia: scan at a major fracture risk of 10% or more. Treat at a T-score of -2.5 or lower, or -1.5 to -2.5 plus a major risk of at least 20% or a hip risk of at least 3% [4].
- NZ: a hip T-score of -2.5 or lower, or a 10-year hip risk of 3% or more, is the usual line for treatment [46].
- UK: consider a scan at a 10-year risk of 10% or more (an expert-consensus line), then decide on treatment together [1][2].

**The Health Roadmap's bone density card** (the health tool):
- It suggests a DEXA scan for women from 50 and men from 70. With no sex on record, the card does not show.
- After a normal result, the next scan is due in 5 years. After osteopenia, in 2 years, the plan's default interval.
- An osteoporosis result opens a follow-up card. Once it is completed, the plan sets the next scan 12 months later.

NICE and RACGP advise against a repeat scan within 2 years, except in unusual cases [3][4]. In women 67 and over, 10% developed osteoporosis about 17 years after a normal or mildly low result, 4.7 years after moderate and 1.1 years after advanced osteopenia [18].

**Kidney function (eGFR).** The denosumab label warns of severe, sometimes fatal, low calcium with an eGFR below 30 [15], a level the plan labels severely decreased (the health tool). Alendronate and zoledronic acid are not advised with a creatinine clearance below 35 mL/min [13][14].

**Protein.** The plan's daily target is 1.2 g per kg of ideal body weight, or 1.0 g with an eGFR below 45 (the health tool). RACGP suggests 1.0 to 1.2 g per kg for older adults [5].

**Vitamin D level.** The US Institute of Medicine says 20 ng/mL (50 nmol/L) or more covers most people [27]; RACGP aims above 50 nmol/L [5]; the American Geriatrics Society wants 30 ng/mL (75 nmol/L) in older adults [28]. This is not settled.

## What helps

**Load your bones.** RACGP advises progressive, moderate to vigorous strength training at least twice a week plus impact exercise, and finds little bone benefit from walking or cycling [4]. With a spine fracture or high fracture risk, exercise should be supervised and adapted, with safe lifting taught and no fast, weighted or deep forward bending or twisting of the spine [4].

**Prevent falls.** Exercise cut falls in older people at home by 23%, balance and functional exercise by 24%, and mixed programmes probably by 34% [31]. Tai chi may cut falls by about 19%, but the evidence is low certainty [31]. The NHS adds good shoes, no loose rugs, eye checks and a medicine review [12].

**Calcium: food first.** Australia advises 1000 mg a day for adults and 1300 mg for women over 50 and men over 70 [5]. The US intake runs from 700 to 1300 mg a day by age [27]. Yet most studies found no link between dietary calcium and fractures [26]. About 4 serves of dairy a day gives most people enough [9].

**Calcium supplements:**
- The USPSTF advises against 1000 mg or less of calcium with 400 IU or less of vitamin D for postmenopausal women at home [19].
- A 2026 review of 69 trials found calcium plus vitamin D cut fractures by about 9%, judged too small to matter, with little effect on falls [20].
- RACGP advises calcium and vitamin D for frail older people in care, and on osteoporosis medicine if diet gives under 1300 mg a day [5].

**Supplement risks.** In one large trial, calcium with vitamin D raised kidney stones by 17% [24]. An Auckland analysis found 31% more heart attacks with calcium supplements [25]. Researchers still disagree about the heart risk. Vegan women had 55% higher hip fracture risk than meat eaters, but not if they took calcium and vitamin D [30]. More on the [calcium page](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/calcium-benefits-best-forms-dosing-and-side-effects).

**Vitamin D.** The recommended intake is 600 IU a day to age 70 and 800 IU from 71 [27]. In VITAL, 2000 IU a day for 5.3 years did not cut fractures in 25,871 adults [21]. An 81-trial review found no effect on fractures or falls [22]. A pooled analysis found 800 IU a day or more somewhat helpful from 65, so not all data agree [23]. VITAL did not select people with low vitamin D [21]; it is still advised for frail older people and anyone at risk of deficiency [46]. Huge doses harm: 500,000 IU once a year raised falls by 15% and fractures by 26% in women 70 and over [29]. More on the [vitamin D page](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/vitamin-d-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects).

**Other supplements.** Vitamin K2 (MK-7) for 3 years did not change bone density in osteopenia [41]. See the pages on [vitamin K](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/vitamin-k-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [collagen](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/collagen-benefits-best-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [magnesium](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/magnesium-benefits-best-forms-dosing) and [boron](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/boron-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects).

**Stop smoking and keep alcohol low** [10].

## Medicines

**Who is offered treatment.** NICE suggests it from 50 or after menopause with a past hip or spine fragility fracture, a T-score of -2.5 or lower, or -1.5 or lower plus a risk factor such as steroid use [2]. RACGP treats a fragility fracture from 50 as presumed osteoporosis, without waiting for a scan [4].

**Calcium and vitamin D alongside.** Low blood calcium must be corrected before alendronate, zoledronic acid or denosumab [13][14][15]. The zoledronic acid label advises at least 1200 mg of calcium and 800 to 1000 IU of vitamin D a day for osteoporosis [14]; the denosumab label, 1000 mg of calcium and at least 400 IU of vitamin D [15]. So keep taking prescribed vitamin D with a bone medicine, though it does little alone.

**Bisphosphonates** (alendronate, risedronate, zoledronic acid) are first choice in the US and NZ [7][9].
- Alendronate: in higher-risk women it cut clinical spine fractures by 55% and hip fractures by 51%. In lower-risk women it may cut spine fractures; the hip effect was unclear [32].
- Zoledronic acid, once a year, cut spine fractures from 10.9% to 3.3% and hip fractures from 2.5% to 1.4% over 3 years. Serious atrial fibrillation (an irregular heartbeat) was more common: 50 women against 20 [33]. In 2000 Auckland women 65 or over with osteopenia, an infusion every 18 months cut fractures by about a third [34].
- Label doses: alendronate tablets, 70 mg once a week or 10 mg daily, swallowed with 180 to 240 mL of plain water 30 minutes before food, drink or other medicines, staying upright for 30 minutes [13]. Zoledronic acid (Reclast), 5 mg by drip over at least 15 minutes, once a year [14]; NZ often spaces doses 12 to 24 months apart [9][46]. It can cause a flu-like illness and eye inflammation [14].

**Denosumab** (Prolia) cut spine fractures from 7.2% to 2.3% and hip fractures from 1.2% to 0.7% over 3 years [35]. The label dose is 60 mg by injection under the skin every 6 months [15].

**Stopping denosumab safely.** Bone loss rebounds when it stops: spine fractures went from 1.2 to 7.1 per 100 people a year, often several at once [36]. The label warns against stopping, skipping or delaying a dose and advises moving to another bone medicine [15]. RACGP advises a bisphosphonate for at least 12 months [4]. Spine fractures can start 7 months after the last dose, so the follow-on medicine often starts when the next injection was due, about 6 months after the last [4][15]. Plan it before the last dose.

**Bone-building medicines,** for very high risk, followed by a bisphosphonate [7]:
- Romosozumab (Evenity) cut fractures more than alendronate, but serious heart events were more common (2.5% against 1.9%) [37]. It has a boxed warning for heart attack and stroke and should not start within a year of either. The dose is 210 mg by injection monthly for 12 doses [16].
- Teriparatide (Forteo): 20 mcg by daily injection, beyond 2 years only if risk stays high [17].

**Hormone therapy** helps bone but is rarely the main treatment. It may suit women within 10 years of menopause with symptoms and high fracture risk [9].

**Rare risks.**
- Atypical thigh-bone fracture: risk rises with years of bisphosphonate use and falls quickly after stopping. Over 3 years, 149 hip fractures were prevented per 2 atypical fractures in white women; 91 per 8 in Asian women [38]. Report new thigh, hip or groin pain [13][15].
- Jaw osteonecrosis (jawbone that fails to heal): about 0.05% after 5 years and 0.18% after 10, and the extra risk fades within 6 to 9 months of stopping [39]. A dental check may come first; good mouth care matters [15].

**Drug holidays.** A break is usually considered only in lower-risk people, after about 5 years of tablets or 3 years of yearly infusions [40][46][48]. People with a past spine fracture, a new fracture during treatment, or a hip T-score still at -2.5 or lower usually continue [46][47][48]. Stopping alendronate after 5 years raised clinical spine fractures (5.3% against 2.4%) but not other fractures [40]. Continuing halved non-spine fractures in women whose hip T-score stayed at -2.5 or lower [47]. After 3 years of zoledronic acid, risk on stopping was low with a hip T-score above -2.5, no recent fracture and at most one risk factor [48]. Breaks never apply to denosumab.

## Brad's view (reviewed 9 Nov 2026)

- **Load matters most.** In his [2026 NASA post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/nasa-found-how-to-stop-osteoporosis), Brad argues that heavy resistance training, not pills, rebuilds meaningful bone density [42].
- **Start easy, then go heavy.** He suggests 50 hops a day as a free first step (it did not help the spine), then heavy deadlifts, squats and presses, tested in older women with low bone density, taught first by a trainer [42].
- **Calcium from food, not pills.** He notes supplements have not cut fractures in the general population, warns of the heart-attack signal, and advises dairy, leafy greens and fish [42][44]. If your doctor put you on calcium, talk with them before stopping [42]. Vegan women may need supplements [44].
- **Vitamin D: enough, not more.** He cites 600 IU a day to 70 and 800 IU from 71, and warns that high doses lowered wrist bone density in a trial [42][45].
- **Supplements are not the main event.** In 2025 he took vitamin D3, K2 and magnesium but called exercise the main driver [43]; his 2026 post stresses load [42].
- **Scans.** He cites the USPSTF: screen women from 65 and younger postmenopausal women at higher risk; decide case by case for men [43].
- **Medicines.** If loading is not enough, or with osteoporosis, he sees bisphosphonates as the next step: a shared decision, with dental care sorted first [42][43].
- **Plan cards.** The micronutrient card's vitamin D3 and K2 are for cognition, not bone (the health tool).

## Who needs extra care

- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Alendronate and zoledronic acid stop when pregnancy is found; pregnancy must be ruled out before denosumab [13][14][15].
- **Kidney disease.** See the eGFR notes; ask before any bone medicine [13][14][15].
- **Long-term steroids.** Ask for a fracture risk check if you take steroid tablets for over 3 months [1].
- **Men.** The USPSTF found too little evidence to advise screening men [6]; NICE suggests assessing men from 75, or from 50 with risk factors [1]. Alendronate, zoledronic acid and denosumab are approved for men with osteoporosis; denosumab also for men on hormone treatment for prostate cancer [13][14][15]. The ACP suggests a bisphosphonate first and denosumab second, on low-certainty evidence [7].
- **Heart disease or stroke.** Romosozumab carries a heart warning; zoledronic acid raised serious atrial fibrillation [16][33].
- **Warfarin.** The plan's micronutrient card asks you to check with your doctor before starting or stopping vitamin K2 (the health tool).
- **Under 18.** Talk to their doctor.

## When to see your doctor

- You broke a bone in a minor fall or bump, especially after 50: ask for a bone check [4][8].
- Back pain not easing after a few weeks, a curving spine, a hunched posture or getting shorter [10].
- A recent fall with pain that is not easing: the same day [10]. Any fall, or worry about balance: ask for a falls check, including a medicine review [12].
- You are a woman of 65 or over or a man of 75 or over: ask about your fracture risk [1].
- New thigh, hip or groin pain on a bisphosphonate or denosumab [13][15].
- On alendronate: chest pain, new or worse heartburn, or pain or trouble swallowing. Stop it and call your doctor straight away [13].
- On denosumab: muscle spasms, twitches or cramps, or numbness or tingling in the fingers, toes or around the mouth, which can mean low calcium. Call your doctor straight away [15].
- A denosumab dose is overdue, or you want to stop it [15].
- Jaw pain, a loose tooth or a mouth sore that will not heal on a bone medicine [15].

## Common questions

**Should I have a bone density scan?** It depends on age, sex and risk factors; see the table. The Health Roadmap suggests one for women from 50 and men from 70.

**I'm scared of jaw problems on a bisphosphonate.** The risk is about 1 in 2000 after 5 years [39]. The hip fractures these medicines prevent are far more common [38].

**Can I take a break from my bisphosphonate?** Possibly, if your risk is lower; see Drug holidays above. Decide with your doctor [40][46][47][48].

**Can I just stop denosumab?** No. Stopping without a follow-on medicine can lead to several spine fractures [15][36].
