# Weight: Obesity, BMI, Fat Loss and Weight-Loss Drugs

*Sources: NICE NG246, TA875 and TA1026, NHS, healthdirect, USPSTF 2018, ACP 2026, the FDA, US labels for Zepbound and Wegovy, the NZ Mounjaro data sheet, and primary trials. Brad's view comes from the Health Roadmap algorithm and his posts.*

## In short

- BMI is a quick screen, not a verdict. Your waist tells you more about the fat that harms health: try to keep it under half your height [1, 10].
- Fat loss needs eating less energy than you burn. No single diet wins for everyone; pick one you can keep [2].
- Strength training and enough protein help preserve muscle while you lose weight [20].
- GLP-1 based medicines (semaglutide, tirzepatide) led to average weight loss of about 15 to 21% in their main trials, and semaglutide lowered a combined count of heart attacks, strokes and heart deaths in people with heart disease and overweight but no diabetes [12, 14, 15].
- Weight usually comes back when these medicines stop, so most people need them long term [13].
- **Brad's view:** his plan suggests discussing a weight medicine at lower BMI levels than guidelines do, alongside diet, exercise and sleep, with tirzepatide preferred (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

## Get help now

- Severe stomach pain that does not go away, sometimes spreading to your back, while on a GLP-1 or tirzepatide medicine: stop the medicine and get urgent care; if the pain is severe, call your emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK). This can be pancreatitis [21].
- Swelling of your face, lips, tongue or throat, trouble breathing or swallowing, or fainting after a dose: stop the medicine and call your emergency number now (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [21].
- Pain in the upper belly with fever, yellow skin or eyes, or pale clay-coloured stools: get seen urgently today; if the pain is severe or you feel very unwell, call your emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK). These can be signs of gallbladder disease [21].
- Vomiting or diarrhoea so bad you cannot keep fluids down, with dizziness or very little urine: get urgent care, because the fluid loss can injure your kidneys; if you are confused or faint, call your emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [21].
- Severe constipation with a swollen belly, vomiting, or not passing wind: get urgent care (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK). Blockage of the bowel has been reported on these medicines [21].
- On insulin or a sulfonylurea, blood sugar can drop dangerously [21]. If someone with diabetes is unconscious, having a seizure, or cannot swallow safely, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK). Do not give food or drink by mouth.

## What it is

Obesity means carrying enough body fat to raise the risk of illness. Doctors screen with BMI, the body mass index: weight in kg divided twice by height in metres [1].

Where the fat sits matters as much as how much there is. Fat around the middle raises the risk of type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure and heart disease [1]. A pooled analysis of 31 studies found waist-to-height ratio picked out these risks better than BMI or waist size alone [10].

Higher weight is linked with type 2 diabetes, heart disease, stroke, high blood pressure, some cancers, osteoarthritis and fatty liver disease. The NHS estimates obesity is likely to shorten life by 2 to 10 years [6]. In 3.9 million never-smokers without chronic disease, death rates were lowest at a BMI of 20 to 25 [11].

Losing even 5 to 10% of your body weight can improve your health [6].

**Metabolic syndrome** means three of five risk factors, none required: a large waist, blood pressure of 130/85 or more, triglycerides of 150 mg/dL (1.7 mmol/L) or more, HDL cholesterol under 40 mg/dL (1.0 mmol/L) in men or 50 (1.3) in women, and fasting blood sugar of 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) or more [26, 27]. Drug treatment for raised triglycerides, low HDL, raised blood sugar or known high blood pressure also counts [26]. Waist lines vary by ethnicity; the US uses over 102 cm (men) and 88 cm (women) [27]. Treatment is mainly lifestyle change, plus medicines for each part where needed [28].

## Your numbers

**BMI bands in Brad's plan** (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026):

| BMI | Waist-to-height ratio | What the plan calls it |
|---|---|---|
| Under 18.5 | any | Underweight |
| 18.5 to 24.9 | any | Normal |
| 25 to 29.9 | under 0.5 | Normal (no central fat) |
| 25 to 29.9 | 0.5 or more | Overweight |
| 25 to 29.9 | not measured | Overweight; the plan asks you to measure your waist |
| 30 to 34.9 | any | Obese, class I |
| 35 to 39.9 | any | Obese, class II |
| 40 or more | any | Obese, class III |

**Waist-to-height ratio:** waist in centimetres divided by height in centimetres. A ratio of 0.5 or more means more risk from belly fat. The plan rounds the ratio to two decimal places, so it counts as raised from about 0.495 times your height (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). NICE uses the same 0.5 line for adults with a BMI under 35, of either sex, any ethnicity, and with high muscle mass [1].

To measure: find the bottom of your ribs and the top of your hip bone, wrap the tape midway between them (just above the belly button), breathe out normally, then read it [1].

**Ideal body weight:** the plan uses the Peterson formula with a target BMI of 24 for men and 22 for women, never below 30 kg. With no sex on record it shows none (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Daily protein target:** 1.2 g per kg of ideal body weight, or 1.0 g per kg if your eGFR is below 45 (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**When the plan raises a weight medicine** (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). Medicine cards need a known age of 18 or over; with no birth year on record the plan asks for it instead:

- BMI of 30 or more: always.
- BMI above 28 and below 30: unless your waist is healthy and no marker below is raised.
- BMI of 25 to 28: only with at least one raised marker.
- BMI under 25: never.

Raised markers:

- HbA1c of 38.8 mmol/mol (5.7%) or more.
- Triglycerides of 150 mg/dL (about 1.7 mmol/L) or more.
- Blood pressure: systolic 130 or more, or diastolic 80 or more.
- The plan's cholesterol marker at or above its risk-enhancing line: ApoB 130 mg/dL (1.3 g/L), non-HDL cholesterol 190 mg/dL (about 4.9 mmol/L), or LDL cholesterol 160 mg/dL (about 4.1 mmol/L).
- Waist-to-height ratio of 0.5 or more.

The cholesterol lines come from the 2018 AHA/ACC cholesterol guideline, which uses them for statin decisions, not weight medicine (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

## What helps

**Eat less energy than you burn, in a way you can keep.** NICE advises any approach that keeps you in an energy deficit (low-fat, low-carbohydrate or other), with a balanced diet long term. It advises against restrictive, unbalanced diets, which fail in the long run and can harm [2]. Better eating can help your cholesterol and diabetes risk even when the scale does not move [2]. See the diet guideline.

**Lose steadily.** The NHS suggests a steady loss of 0.5 to 1 kg each week [6].

**Move most days.** NICE says most people may need 45 to 60 minutes of moderate activity a day to prevent obesity, and people who have lost weight may need 60 to 90 minutes a day to keep it off [2]. See the exercise guideline.

**Help preserve your muscle.** Any weight loss takes some muscle with it. A 2025 joint advisory from four US societies calls for resistance training and a suitable diet to preserve muscle and bone during GLP-1 treatment [20]. The protein target above gives a daily amount; the [protein powder](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/protein-powders-benefits-types-dosing-and-side-effects) and [creatine](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/creatine-benefits-best-forms-dosing-and-side-effects) entries cover supplements.

**Sleep, stress and alcohol.** The 2025 advisory lists sleep, mental stress and substance use in the lifestyle check before a GLP-1 [20]. Alcohol is high in calories, and the NHS advises cutting down [6]. See the sleep guideline.

**Weight-loss supplements:** see the [weight-loss supplements entry](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/weight-loss-supplements-benefits-evidence-and-safety) before buying any.

**Lifestyle and diabetes risk.** In the Diabetes Prevention Program, a program aiming for 7% weight loss and 150 minutes of activity a week cut new cases of diabetes by 58% against placebo; metformin cut them by 31% [19].

## Medicines

All weight medicines are used with a reduced-calorie diet and more activity [3, 21, 22].

**GLP-1 based medicines.** These mimic gut hormones that regulate appetite, and they slow stomach emptying. Semaglutide acts on the GLP-1 receptor. Tirzepatide acts on both the GIP and GLP-1 receptors [21, 22].

- **Semaglutide (Wegovy injection; Ozempic is the diabetes brand).** In STEP 1, adults without diabetes lost 14.9% of their body weight over 68 weeks on 2.4 mg a week, against 2.4% on placebo [12]. In SELECT, 17,604 people with heart disease and a BMI of 27 or more, without diabetes, had fewer combined heart attacks, strokes and heart deaths on semaglutide: 6.5% against 8.0% over about 40 months [15]. In STEP 9, people with obesity and knee osteoarthritis had larger falls in knee pain than on placebo [16].
- **Tirzepatide (Zepbound; Mounjaro is the diabetes brand, and in New Zealand also licensed for weight).** In SURMOUNT-1, adults without diabetes on 5, 10 or 15 mg a week lost 15.0%, 19.5% or 20.9% of their weight over 72 weeks, against 3.1% on placebo [14]. The US label also covers moderate to severe sleep apnoea with obesity [17, 21].

**Label doses (US prescribing information).** Both start low and step up slowly to reduce stomach side effects.

- **Zepbound (tirzepatide) injection:** 2.5 mg once a week for 4 weeks, then 5 mg. The dose may rise in 2.5 mg steps, at least 4 weeks apart. Maintenance for weight is 5, 10 or 15 mg once a week; 15 mg is the maximum [21].
- **Wegovy (semaglutide) injection:** 0.25 mg once a week for 4 weeks, then 0.5 mg, 1 mg and 1.7 mg, each for 4 weeks. Maintenance for weight is 2.4 mg (recommended) or 1.7 mg once a week. If 2.4 mg is tolerated for at least 4 weeks and more weight loss is needed, the dose may rise to a maximum of 7.2 mg once a week [22].
- **Wegovy (semaglutide) tablets:** 1.5 mg once a day for 30 days, then stepped up every 30 days to a maintenance dose of 25 mg once a day [22].

**Stopping.** In the STEP 1 extension, people who stopped semaglutide regained two-thirds of their lost weight within a year, and their blood pressure, blood sugar and cholesterol drifted back [13]. In SURMOUNT-4, people switched to placebo regained 14.0% of body weight in a year; those staying on tirzepatide lost a further 5.5% [24]. In SURMOUNT-MAINTAIN, stepping down from 10 or 15 mg to 5 mg held weight 16.6% below baseline, against 21.9% on the full dose and 9.9% on placebo. People who regained over half their lost weight could restart full tirzepatide: 25% did on 5 mg, 8% on the full dose and 67% on placebo [25]. NICE says to review tirzepatide if under 5% of starting weight is lost after 6 months on the highest tolerated dose [5].

**Side effects and safety (from the US labels unless cited otherwise):**

- Nausea, diarrhoea, vomiting, constipation, indigestion and reflux are the most common. They are worst when the dose goes up [21].
- Rats given these medicines developed thyroid C-cell tumours. It is unknown whether this happens in people. Do not use them if you or a relative has had medullary thyroid cancer, or if you have MEN 2 [21].
- Pancreatitis, gallbladder disease and kidney injury from dehydration have been reported [21]. A meta-analysis of 76 trials found gallbladder and bile duct disease more common on GLP-1 medicines, more so at higher doses and with longer use. In the 13 weight-loss trials the risk was 2.29 times that of the comparison group [18].
- Resting heart rate rose by 1 to 4 beats a minute on Wegovy in weight trials [22].
- They slow stomach emptying, so tell your surgeon or anaesthetist before any procedure that needs anaesthesia or deep sedation [21].
- Tirzepatide can make birth control pills less reliable. Switch to a non-pill method, or add a barrier method, for 4 weeks after starting and after each dose increase [21].
- Avoid compounded, online or copycat versions. The FDA does not review them for safety, effectiveness or quality, fakes may hold the wrong ingredients, and harms, some needing hospital care, may be related to dosing errors. Fill a doctor's prescription at a licensed pharmacy [33].

**Other medicines.** NICE also lists liraglutide, a daily injection, and orlistat, a capsule that blocks fat absorption [3]. Orlistat can be prescribed at a BMI of 30, or 28 with risk factors, and is sold at a lower dose in pharmacies [3].

**Diabetes medicines in Brad's plan.** SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin) and metformin are diabetes medicines that the plan adds after a GLP-1. The plan describes SGLT2 inhibitors as having proven heart and kidney protection in some populations (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Surgery.** NICE suggests a referral for weight-loss surgery at a BMI of 40 or more, or 35 to 39.9 with a significant condition that weight loss could improve, among other criteria, and a faster assessment at 35 or more with recent-onset type 2 diabetes [3]. US and international surgical societies (2022) recommend it at a BMI of 35 or more with or without other conditions, and suggest it at 30 to 34.9 with metabolic disease, or from 27.5 in Asian people [30].

## Brad's view (reviewed 2 Nov 2026)

**He raises weight medicine at lower lines than guidelines.** Most guidelines consider these medicines at a BMI of 30 or more, or 27 or more with a weight-related condition; NHS England sets higher lines [4, 5]. The American College of Physicians (ACP, 2026) at BMI 27 to 30 requires type 2 diabetes, abnormal blood fats, high blood pressure, sleep apnoea or heart disease [29]. Brad's plan suggests discussing them at a BMI of 30 or more, above 28 unless the waist is healthy, and from 25 when a marker is raised, alongside diet, exercise and sleep with no waiting period. No guideline supports these lower lines, and a doctor may not agree to prescribe below the licensed ones (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Waist over BMI.** A BMI of 25 to 29.9 with a waist under half your height counts as normal in his plan, and by itself does not trigger weight advice (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**His medicine order.** Tirzepatide first, semaglutide as the alternative. Then a higher dose, or a switch to tirzepatide; then an SGLT2 inhibitor; then metformin (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). He calls muscle loss the most important side effect of any weight-loss strategy, and says it is the same on these medicines as with dieting or surgery ([end of obesity post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/we-are-witnessing-the-end-of-obesity)).

**Protect muscle the plain way.** He advises resistance training a couple of times a week and enough protein, and names creatine as the one supplement worth adding; he warns against grey-market muscle peptides and SARMs ([lean and ripped post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/were-all-about-to-become-lean-and-ripped-new-evidence)).

**Plan to stay on it.** Because weight returns on stopping, he suggests patients who reach their target weight stay on tirzepatide, and says a lower dose may be enough for many to hold their weight ([end of obesity post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/we-are-witnessing-the-end-of-obesity)).

**Doctors should do it properly.** He expects a doctor to assess BMI, waist and other conditions, explain long-term use, and follow patients closely, not prescribe after a short online questionnaire. He judges the gallstone risk real but modest, and the fears about thyroid cancer, pancreatitis and depression overblown on current evidence ([nuanced answer post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/should-you-use-ozempic-glp-1-weight-loss-meds-nuanced-answer)).

**His own low dose.** Brad takes tirzepatide 2.5 mg a week, the starting dose, to quiet food noise and lower his blood pressure. He says this is off-label, against current guidelines, not formally studied, and not a recommendation for anyone else (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Microdosing is unproven.** He notes no studies of small GLP-1 doses in healthy adults without obesity or diabetes, and warns that even a small dose may cause unplanned weight and muscle loss, a concern in older adults ([microdosing post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/microdosing-tirzepatide-ozempic-glp1-for-longevity)).

**Retatrutide.** It is not approved and is legally available only in trials. He warns against buying it online and choosing your own dose, and expects it may be used to reach a healthy weight and clear liver fat, then swapped for tirzepatide, because it raises heart rate and its heart-outcome data are not in yet ([end of obesity post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/we-are-witnessing-the-end-of-obesity)).

**Diet.** He finds no best diet for everyone: diets that lead at six months tend to even out by one to two years, and none of these medicines replaces a good diet, regular exercise and good sleep ([end of obesity post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/we-are-witnessing-the-end-of-obesity)).

## Who needs extra care

- **Pregnancy and planning pregnancy.** Weight loss is not recommended in pregnancy. Stop semaglutide (Wegovy) at least 2 months before a planned pregnancy, because it stays in the body a long time [22]. The US Zepbound label says to stop tirzepatide when a pregnancy is recognised and to tell your doctor if you plan to become pregnant [21]; the New Zealand data sheet says to stop it if you wish to become pregnant [23]. Arrange stopping with your prescriber before you try to conceive. NICE says not to use tirzepatide, semaglutide or liraglutide in pregnancy [3].
- **Breastfeeding.** Breastfeeding is not recommended on Wegovy tablets, as an added ingredient passes into milk. For the injections, effects on the baby are unknown; weigh the benefits with your doctor [21, 22].
- **People of South Asian, Chinese, other Asian, Middle Eastern, Black African or African-Caribbean background.** Health risks start at a lower BMI. NICE uses overweight from 23 and obesity from 27.5 [1].
- **High muscle mass.** BMI can overstate fat; use your waist-to-height ratio [1].
- **Age 65 and over.** NICE advises reading BMI with care, since a slightly higher BMI may protect older people [1]. Muscle loss during weight loss matters more at this age ([microdosing post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/microdosing-tirzepatide-ozempic-glp1-for-longevity)).
- **Reduced kidney function.** Dehydration from vomiting or diarrhoea on these medicines can injure the kidneys; drink fluids and report persistent symptoms [21]. Brad's plan lowers the protein target when eGFR is below 45 (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
- **Diabetes on insulin or a sulfonylurea, or with diabetic retinopathy.** Watch for low blood sugar, as your doctor may lower those doses, and have your eyes followed if you have retinopathy [21].
- **Past pancreatitis or severe stomach emptying problems.** Tell your doctor before starting; Zepbound is not recommended in severe gastroparesis [21].
- **Eating disorders.** See your GP if you have, or think you may have, an eating disorder such as binge eating [6].
- **Under 18.** Brad's plan gives no suggestions to anyone under 18 and tells them to talk with their doctor (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

## When to see your doctor

- You keep losing weight without changing your diet or exercise. It can be a sign of stress or a serious illness. With other symptoms, go as soon as possible [7].
- Your BMI is in the overweight or obese range and you want help, your waist is more than half your height, or you have prediabetes, diabetes, high blood pressure, joint pain, high cholesterol or sleep apnoea [6].
- Before starting, changing or stopping a weight medicine.
- On a GLP-1 or tirzepatide medicine: nausea, vomiting or diarrhoea that does not settle; a lump or swelling in your neck, hoarseness or trouble swallowing; vision changes if you have type 2 diabetes [21]; or a racing heartbeat at rest [22].

## Common questions

**I lift weights and my BMI says overweight. Is that right?** Maybe not. BMI can overstate fat in people with a lot of muscle [1]. Measure your waist: if it is under half your height, Brad's plan counts a BMI of 25 to 29.9 as normal (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**How do I lose fat without losing muscle?** Lose slowly, lift weights, and eat enough protein [6, 20].

**Which weight-loss medicine should I ask about?** Brad's plan prefers tirzepatide, with semaglutide as the alternative (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). Your doctor weighs your other conditions, cost and access.

**What doses do people use to microdose?** There is no tested microdose. Zepbound's label calls 2.5 mg a starting dose only [21]. Brad takes 2.5 mg a week off-label, not as a recommendation (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Is a GLP-1 safe if I have reflux?** Reflux and indigestion are common side effects on the Zepbound label, though not contraindications [21]. Discuss it with your doctor first, along with any hiatus hernia or swallowing problems.

**What happens if I stop?** Most regain much of it: two-thirds within a year in STEP 1 [13].

**My ideal weight looks too low for my build.** The plan's ideal weight uses a BMI of 24 for men and 22 for women, inside the lowest-mortality ranges it cites from large studies: 23 to 26 for men, 20 to 23 for women (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). It is a guide; your waist-to-height ratio shows whether your current weight carries risk.
