# Heart Disease Risk: Calcium Score, Risk Scores and Prevention

This guide covers the risk of a first heart attack or stroke caused by narrowed arteries: how doctors estimate it, what a coronary calcium score adds, and what lowers the risk. Cholesterol drugs and blood pressure treatment have their own guides, High Cholesterol and High Blood Pressure; this one links to them rather than repeating them.

## In short

- Heart attacks and most strokes come from plaque in the artery wall. LDL and other ApoB particles cause it; blood pressure, smoking, diabetes and excess weight speed it up [1][2].
- Most guidelines estimate your chance of a heart attack or stroke over the next 5 or 10 years with a risk calculator: PREVENT in the US, SCORE2 in Europe, QRISK3 in the UK, the PREDICT-based equations in New Zealand and the AusCVDRisk calculator in Australia [3][4][5][6][7].
- A coronary artery calcium (CAC) score is a low-dose CT scan that finds hardened plaque. Guidelines use it when a treatment decision is uncertain, not as a test for everyone [8][9][20].
- Aspirin is no longer started routinely to prevent a first heart attack. It still matters after a heart attack, stroke or stent [9][10][11].
- Brad's view: control each risk factor to its own target, with ApoB below 0.5 g/L, rather than steering by a calculated risk score; if every risk factor is already controlled, a calcium score would not change the plan ([Soon We'll Forget What Heart Attacks Are](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/soon-well-forget-what-heart-attacks-are)).

## Get help now

- Chest pain that feels tight, heavy or squeezing, or spreads to the arm, neck or jaw, or comes with sweating or severe breathlessness: call your emergency number now (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK). [12]
- Possible stroke: face drooping, arm or leg weakness or numbness, trouble speaking, or vision loss that has not recovered. Call your emergency number now (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).
- Blood pressure of 180 systolic or more, or 120 diastolic or more, with symptoms such as chest pain, shortness of breath, severe headache or vision changes: seek immediate medical attention (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).

A reading of 180 or more systolic, or 120 or more diastolic, without symptoms is still dangerously high and urgent (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

## What it is

Atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD) is the umbrella term for heart attack, angina, stroke and narrowed leg arteries caused by plaque.

**What drives it.** Cholesterol can only enter the artery wall inside particles that carry apolipoprotein B (ApoB): LDL, VLDL remnants and Lp(a). Each of these particles carries one ApoB, so ApoB counts the particles [2]. A European consensus panel reviewed genetic studies, cohorts and trials covering more than 2 million people and concluded that the evidence "unequivocally establishes that LDL causes ASCVD" [1]. The longer and higher the exposure, the greater the risk [1].

**What adds to it.** Raised blood pressure, smoking, diabetes, chronic kidney disease, a family history of early heart disease and a high Lp(a) all raise the risk on top of cholesterol [9][13]. Lp(a) is mostly inherited, and a high level is a risk factor even when LDL is very low [13].

**Plaque in people with no risk factors.** In the PESA study, 1,779 middle-aged Spanish adults had none of the usual risk factors, yet 49.7% already had plaque or coronary calcium on imaging. LDL was linked to plaque even at levels called normal, including in a subgroup with optimal blood pressure, glucose and cholesterol [14].

**Metabolic syndrome.** This is a cluster of raised waist size, raised triglycerides, low HDL, raised blood pressure and raised fasting glucose. Three of the five qualify [15]. It travels with a higher risk of heart disease and type 2 diabetes, and the US prevention guideline counts it as a risk-enhancing factor [9][15].

**Smoking.** Stopping helps quickly. In the Framingham cohorts, heavy smokers who quit had a lower rate of cardiovascular disease within 5 years than those who kept smoking, though their risk stayed above that of never-smokers for longer [16].

**Lp(a) units.** Labs print Lp(a) in nmol/L, mg/dL or mg/L. The plan grades in nmol/L and treats 75 nmol/L as about 30 mg/dL and 125 nmol/L as about 50 mg/dL; a mg/L result is ten times the mg/dL figure (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). The US prevention guideline and the European consensus use 50 mg/dL or 125 nmol/L as the line for a risk-enhancing level [9][13]. One test is usually enough, because the level is set mainly by your genes [8][13].

**A calcium score is not a plan number.** The plan does not record a CAC score or change any card because of one (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). If you have one, the next section explains how guidelines read it.

## What helps

**The risk factors themselves.** Nearly everything that prevents a first heart attack works through the same short list: lower ApoB, lower blood pressure, no smoking, normal blood sugar and a healthy waist. Diet, exercise and sleep have their own guidelines (Diet, Exercise, Sleep). The cholesterol and blood pressure guides cover those two factors in depth.

**Know your risk the way your doctor will.** Guidelines start with a risk calculator. Each one takes age, sex, smoking, blood pressure and cholesterol, and most add diabetes. Some add kidney function, ethnicity, deprivation or family history [3][4][5][6][7].

- **PREVENT (US).** Built from more than 6 million US adults aged 30 to 79, it predicts heart attack, stroke and heart failure, includes kidney function, and leaves out race [3]. The 2026 ACC/AHA cholesterol guideline uses it to place adults in bands: borderline 3% to below 5%, intermediate 5% to below 10%, and high 10% or more over 10 years [8].
- **SCORE2 and SCORE2-OP (Europe).** SCORE2 gives 10-year risk for people aged 40 to 69 and is calibrated to four European risk regions; SCORE2-OP covers people over 70 [4][17]. The same risk factors give very different results across regions [4].
- **QRISK3 (UK).** Derived from 7.89 million patients aged 25 to 84 in English general practice, it adds family history, ethnicity, deprivation and conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis and atrial fibrillation [5]. NICE asks doctors to use it for people aged 25 to 84 without heart disease [18].
- **PREDICT (New Zealand).** Derived from about 400,000 primary care patients, it gives 5-year risk and includes ethnicity and deprivation. The study found that the older US equations overestimated risk in New Zealand patients [6].
- **AusCVDRisk (Australia).** The 2023 Australian guideline gives 5-year risk: high 10% or more, intermediate 5% to below 10%, low below 5% [7].

Calculators are not used for people already known to be at high risk, such as those with familial hypercholesterolaemia or moderate to severe kidney disease [7][18].

**When a calcium score helps.** A CAC scan is a non-contrast CT of the heart. It reports calcium in the coronary arteries in Agatston units and often as a percentile against people of the same age and sex.

- In the MESA cohort of 6,814 adults followed for about 11 years, 10-year event rates with a score of 0 ranged from 1.3% to 5.6%, and with a score above 300 from 13.1% to 25.6%, across age, sex and ethnic groups. Each doubling of the score carried a 14% higher relative risk [19].
- The 2019 US prevention guideline said a score was reasonable for adults at intermediate risk, and some at borderline risk, when the statin decision was still uncertain. A score of zero could move a person down a risk group and let treatment wait; a score of 100 or more, or at the 75th percentile or above, moved them up [9].
- The 2026 ACC/AHA cholesterol guideline recommends a scan selectively for men over 40 and women over 45 at borderline or intermediate risk. Any calcium supports an LDL goal below 100 mg/dL, with lower goals as the score rises [8].
- European guidance treats CAC, or carotid plaque on ultrasound, as an optional tool to sharpen risk around a treatment threshold (a weaker, Class IIb recommendation) [20].
- Screening everyone does not yet have proof of benefit. In DANCAVAS, 46,611 Danish men aged 65 to 74 were invited to a screening package that included a CAC scan, or not. After 5.6 years, deaths were 12.6% in the invited group and 13.1% in the control group, a difference that was not statistically significant [21].

## Medicines

Every way of lowering LDL tested so far lowers heart attacks in proportion to how far LDL falls [1]. Statins, ezetimibe, PCSK9 inhibitors and blood pressure medicines: their choice, doses and side effects are in High Cholesterol and High Blood Pressure.

**Statins by risk level.** The US Preventive Services Task Force recommends a statin for adults aged 40 to 75 with at least one risk factor (high cholesterol, diabetes, high blood pressure or smoking) and a 10-year risk of 10% or more, and a selective offer at 7.5% to below 10%. For adults 76 and over it found the evidence insufficient [22]. NICE offers a statin from a 10-year QRISK3 score of 10% and says a lower score need not rule one out if the person prefers it [18].

**Aspirin for a first heart attack.** The picture has changed.

- The USPSTF says the decision to start low-dose aspirin at age 40 to 59 with a 10-year risk of 10% or more should be individual, since the net benefit is small. It recommends against starting it at 60 or older [10].
- In ASPREE, 19,114 healthy adults aged 70 or older (65 for Black and Hispanic people in the US) took 100 mg of aspirin or placebo. Aspirin did not significantly lower cardiovascular disease, and major bleeding was higher: 8.6 against 6.2 events per 1,000 person-years [11].
- The 2019 US prevention guideline says aspirin might be considered for select adults aged 40 to 70 at higher risk but not at higher bleeding risk, and should not be used routinely over 70 or in anyone at higher bleeding risk [9].
- After a heart attack, stroke or stent, aspirin or another antiplatelet is a different decision. Do not stop it without your doctor ([We Were Wrong About Aspirin](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/we-were-wrong-about-aspirin-new-evidence)).

Blood thinners have their own guide, Blood Thinners. If you take one, check with your doctor or pharmacist before adding aspirin or any supplement.

## Brad's view (reviewed 2 Nov 2026)

**No risk calculator in the plan.** The roadmap grades each marker against its own threshold and does not calculate a 5-year or 10-year risk with PREVENT, SCORE2, QRISK3 or PREDICT (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). Brad's approach is to control every major risk factor: smoking, blood pressure, diet, exercise, weight and ApoB above the level we were born with ([Soon We'll Forget What Heart Attacks Are](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/soon-well-forget-what-heart-attacks-are)). He notes that over 99% of people who had a first heart attack in a 9.3-million-person study had at least one risk factor above optimal beforehand ([99% of Heart Attacks Had This Prior Warning](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/99-of-heart-attacks-had-this-prior-warning)).

**Lower targets than most guidelines.** The plan aims for ApoB below 0.5 g/L, LDL below 1.4 mmol/L and non-HDL below 1.6 mmol/L for anyone on treatment, lower than the ESC/EAS 2019 ApoB goal for very high risk, based on the Ez-PAVE target trial and PESA (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). Blood pressure targets are below 120/80 under 65 and below 130/80 from 65, based on SPRINT and ESPRIT (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). He takes a low-dose statin plus ezetimibe himself to keep LDL below 55 mg/dL, which he calls highly aggressive for primary prevention ([Debunked $6 Pill Found to Reduce Heart Disease](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/debunked-6-pill-found-to-reduce-heart-disease)).

**The calcium score.** If the risk factors are already controlled, Brad sees no reason to get a CAC score, because it would not change the plan; the scan's job is to settle whether to treat when that is uncertain, and guidelines say not to rescan once treatment starts ([Soon We'll Forget What Heart Attacks Are](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/soon-well-forget-what-heart-attacks-are)). He points out that the scan sees only hardened plaque, that soft plaque usually triggers the clot, and that a zero score does not mean zero plaque. A CT coronary angiogram with dye, or an ultrasound of the neck and groin arteries, can find plaque the calcium scan misses, but he prefers measurements that point at a cause before plaque forms ([The One Number I'd Measure to Avoid a Heart Attack](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/the-one-number-id-measure-to-avoid-a-heart-attack)). He also says that in the right patient a calcium score can reveal plaque no blood test would catch ([99% of Heart Attacks Had This Prior Warning](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/99-of-heart-attacks-had-this-prior-warning)).

**The one number.** If he could measure only one thing, Brad would choose waist-to-height ratio, kept below 0.5, with ApoB a very close second and HbA1c and blood pressure next ([The One Number I'd Measure to Avoid a Heart Attack](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/the-one-number-id-measure-to-avoid-a-heart-attack)).

**Lp(a).** Test it once. If it is high, he aims for a lower LDL, under 40 to 50 mg/dL in his clinic, usually with lifestyle, a low-dose statin and ezetimibe, and sometimes considers aspirin when Lp(a) and other risk factors are high ([Lipoprotein(a) Is a Cheap, One-Time Blood Test](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/lipoproteina)). The plan's elevated-Lp(a) card lists the modifiable risk factors to control: lipids, blood pressure, BMI, HbA1c and medicines (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Aspirin.** Daily low-dose aspirin without a past heart attack or stroke has lost its support in older adults, with a clear bleeding cost; after a heart attack, stroke or stent it stays ([We Were Wrong About Aspirin](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/we-were-wrong-about-aspirin-new-evidence)).

## Who needs extra care

- **Under 18.** The plan is built for adults. For anyone younger, talk with their doctor (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Talk to your doctor or midwife before starting, stopping or changing any heart, blood pressure or cholesterol medicine.
- **Older adults.** NICE treats people aged 85 or older as at higher risk on age alone, since QRISK3 stops at 84 [18]. SCORE2-OP covers people over 70 [17]. In adults 70 and older, aspirin raised major bleeding without significantly lowering heart events [11].
- **Reduced kidney function.** Chronic kidney disease raises heart risk; the US guideline counts an eGFR of 15 to 59 as a risk-enhancing factor, and PREVENT includes eGFR in its estimate [3][9]. Australian guidance does not run the calculator for moderate to severe kidney disease: those people are high risk already [7]. Ask your doctor or pharmacist before any new medicine or supplement.
- **Diabetes.** SCORE2 was built for people without diabetes, and the Australian calculator adds diabetes-specific measures such as HbA1c and time since diagnosis [4][7].
- **Familial hypercholesterolaemia.** Calculators are not used, because risk is high from birth [7][18].
- **Ethnicity.** Māori, Pacific and Indian patients in New Zealand had higher predicted risk than Europeans, and Chinese and other Asian patients lower [6]. New Zealand starts assessment earlier for Māori, Pacific and South Asian people: from 30 for men and 40 for women [23]. Australia starts at 30 for First Nations people [7].
- **Severe mental illness.** New Zealand advises assessment from age 25 [23].

## When to see your doctor

- You are in your country's age range for assessment (see the table below), or younger with diabetes, a family history of early heart disease or another risk factor, and have never had your risk assessed [7][9][23].
- A parent, brother or sister had a heart attack or stroke young. The US guideline uses before 55 in men and before 65 in women [9].
- Your ApoB, LDL, blood pressure or HbA1c is above the plan's line, or your Lp(a) has never been tested.
- You have had a calcium score or CT angiogram and want to know what it means for treatment.
- You take aspirin without a past heart attack or stroke and are not sure you still need it. Do not stop aspirin prescribed after a heart attack, stroke or stent on your own.
- New chest discomfort, breathlessness or reduced exercise tolerance that comes and goes. If it is severe or lasting, use the emergency steps above.

## Common questions

**My calcium score is above the percentile for my age and sex. What now?**
Guidelines read a score of 100 or more, or at the 75th percentile or above, as a reason to start or step up cholesterol-lowering treatment [8][9]. In the 2026 US guideline, any calcium supports an LDL goal below 100 mg/dL, with lower goals at higher scores [8]. Brad's plan already aims lower, for ApoB below 0.5 g/L (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). Take the report to your doctor; High Cholesterol covers the medicines.

**Should I get a calcium score?**
Guidelines suggest one when you are at borderline or intermediate risk and unsure whether to start treatment [8][9]. It is not advised as a test for everyone [21]. Brad's view is that if your risk factors are already controlled, the scan would not change anything ([Soon We'll Forget What Heart Attacks Are](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/soon-well-forget-what-heart-attacks-are)).

**My score is zero. Am I safe?**
A zero score goes with a low 10-year risk in MESA [19], and guidelines allow treatment to wait in some people with a zero score [9]. It does not apply to people with very high LDL, diabetes, current smoking, or early heart disease in close relatives [9]. Brad notes that zero does not mean no plaque ([The One Number I'd Measure to Avoid a Heart Attack](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/the-one-number-id-measure-to-avoid-a-heart-attack)).

**What is my 10-year heart risk?**
The plan does not calculate one. Your doctor can run the calculator your country uses (see the table above). What changes the result is the same list the plan tracks: blood pressure, cholesterol, smoking, diabetes and, in some tools, kidney function [3][4][5].

**My cardiologist says my risk is low. Does the plan agree?**
Possibly not, and both can be right. A calculator estimates the next 5 or 10 years; the plan grades each marker against lower lifetime targets (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). PESA found plaque in people with LDL in the normal range [14].

**Do I need aspirin to prevent a first heart attack?**
For most people, no. Starting it at 60 or older is not recommended, and under 60 it is an individual decision at higher risk [10]. Ask your doctor, who can weigh your bleeding risk.

**Does a family history of early heart disease mean I should be screened?**
Yes, it is a reason to have your risk assessed earlier and to test Lp(a) [9][13][23]. Brad says a strong family history warrants more aggressive monitoring however your other numbers look ([99% of Heart Attacks Had This Prior Warning](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/99-of-heart-attacks-had-this-prior-warning)).

**What does metabolic syndrome mean for my heart?**
It means three or more of: a large waist, high triglycerides, low HDL, raised blood pressure and raised fasting glucose [15]. It raises heart risk, and each part can be treated. Weight and Type 2 Diabetes and Prediabetes cover the details.

**I had bypass surgery or a stent. Which supplements are safe?**
You are in secondary prevention, and your cardiologist's plan comes first. Many supplements interact with blood thinners: check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting any. Brad advises checking with your doctor first before omega-3 if you have atrial fibrillation or take a blood thinner (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
