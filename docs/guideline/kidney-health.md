# Kidney Health: eGFR, CKD and Protecting the Kidneys

*Written from KDIGO 2024, NICE NG203, New Zealand and Australian guidance, NHS pages, FDA labels and trials, plus Brad's posts and the Health Roadmap algorithm.*

## In short

- Kidney function is checked with two tests: a blood test for creatinine, which is turned into an eGFR (estimated glomerular filtration rate), and a urine test for albumin, reported as the albumin-to-creatinine ratio (ACR) [1].
- Chronic kidney disease (CKD) means a kidney problem that lasts at least 3 months. One low result is not enough to diagnose it [1][2].
- Early CKD usually causes no symptoms, so it is found by testing [7].
- Only about 2 in 100 people with CKD reach kidney failure; heart disease and stroke are among the main causes of death with CKD [7].
- Blood pressure control, diabetes control, less salt, exercise and not smoking all help [1]. Medicines that protect the kidneys include ACE inhibitors, ARBs and SGLT2 inhibitors [1].
- Some painkillers (NSAIDs such as ibuprofen), potassium supplements and some herbal products can harm damaged kidneys [1][8].
- Brad's view: the plan lowers its protein target and drops potassium advice when eGFR falls below 45 (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

## Get help now

- Blood pressure of 180/120 or higher with chest, back or belly pain, shortness of breath, numbness or weakness, a change in vision, difficulty speaking or a sudden severe headache: call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK). With no symptoms, rest and recheck after 5 minutes; if it is still 180/120 or higher, call your doctor (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
- For chest pain or signs of a stroke (face drooping, arm or leg weakness or numbness, trouble speaking, vision loss, even if it passes, or a sudden severe headache), call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).
- On an ACE inhibitor or ARB: for trouble breathing or swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [20].
- On an SGLT2 inhibitor (such as empagliflozin or dapagliflozin): with nausea, vomiting, belly pain, unusual tiredness or laboured breathing, stop the medicine and seek medical attention immediately, even if your blood sugar is normal; if you are very unwell or confused, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [17].

## What it is

Your kidneys filter waste from your blood, balance salt, potassium and water, and help control blood pressure. Labs estimate the filtering rate (GFR) from blood creatinine, a waste product of muscle, with an equation that includes age and sex. That estimate is the eGFR [1][10].

KDIGO, the international kidney guideline group, defines CKD as abnormal kidney structure or function, present for at least 3 months, with implications for health. It is classified by cause, by GFR category (G1 to G5) and by albuminuria category (A1 to A3) [1]. An eGFR below 60 lasting 3 months or longer counts, and so does persistent albumin in the urine even with a normal eGFR [1].

CKD is common. A global analysis for 2017 put its prevalence at 9.1% [1]. Common causes are high blood pressure, diabetes, high cholesterol, kidney inflammation (glomerulonephritis), inherited polycystic kidney disease, blocked urine flow and long-term use of some medicines such as lithium and NSAIDs [7].

A lower eGFR and a higher ACR each raise the risk of kidney failure, heart disease and death, and together they multiply it, so guidelines grade risk on both numbers [1][2].

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is different: kidney function drops suddenly, over hours or days, often from dehydration, infection, low blood pressure or medicines. It can be serious, but many people recover within weeks [9]. A single low eGFR may reflect AKI rather than CKD [1].

## Your numbers

The Health Roadmap labels eGFR like this, in mL/min/1.73m² (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026):

| eGFR | Plan label | KDIGO 2024 category [1] |
|---|---|---|
| 70 or more | Normal | G1 (90 or more) or part of G2 |
| 60 to 69 | Low Normal | G2, mildly decreased |
| 45 to 59 | Mildly to Moderately Decreased | G3a |
| 30 to 44 | Moderately to Severely Decreased | G3b |
| 15 to 29 | Severely Decreased | G4 |
| Below 15 | Kidney Failure | G5 |

- The two top bands are the tool's own. KDIGO calls 60 to 89 mildly decreased (G2), and neither G1 nor G2 is CKD without another marker of kidney damage, such as albumin in the urine (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
- **Below 45:** the plan's daily protein target drops from 1.2 to 1.0 g per kg of ideal body weight, and the potassium-rich food advice is no longer shown (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
- **Which eGFR the plan uses:** it calculates one with the race-free CKD-EPI 2021 equation from your creatinine, age and sex, and compares it with the eGFR printed on your lab report. The newer one wins; on the same day, the lab's wins. With no sex on record it does not calculate one, but a printed eGFR still counts. eGFR values based on cystatin C are recorded but not used (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
- **Creatinine units:** labs in New Zealand, Australia and the UK report µmol/L; US labs report mg/dL. Divide µmol/L by 88.4 to get mg/dL [1].

### Urine albumin (ACR)

The plan records urine ACR but does not grade it. KDIGO and NICE use these categories [1][2]:

| Category | ACR, mg/mmol | ACR, mg/g | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| A1 | below 3 | below 30 | Normal to mildly increased |
| A2 | 3 to 30 | 30 to 300 | Moderately increased |
| A3 | above 30 | above 300 | Severely increased |

A raised ACR needs confirming on an early-morning urine sample [1][2]. Exercise, a urine infection, menstruation and blood in the urine can push it up for a while [1].

### Things that move creatinine without kidney damage

Creatinine comes from muscle, so muscle and diet shift the eGFR. NICE asks adults not to eat meat in the 12 hours before the test, and to read the result with caution in bodybuilders, protein supplement users, after an amputation and in muscle-wasting conditions [2]. KDIGO lists high-protein diets and creatine supplements among the reasons a creatinine eGFR can mislead, and suggests a cystatin C test in those cases [1].

## What helps

KDIGO asks for a whole-person plan aimed at slowing CKD and preventing its complications, heart disease above all [1].

**Blood pressure.** Raised pressure strains the small blood vessels in the kidneys [7]. Targets differ (see "Where guidelines differ").

**Diabetes.** High blood glucose damages the kidneys' filters [7]. Several diabetes medicines also protect the kidneys (see "Medicines").

**Salt.** KDIGO suggests under 2 g of sodium a day (about 5 g of salt) for people with CKD [1]. The NHS gives a limit of 6 g of salt a day, about a teaspoon [8].

**Diet.** KDIGO advises a varied diet with more plant foods than animal foods and fewer ultra-processed foods, with a renal dietitian to tailor sodium, potassium, phosphorus and protein [1].

**Protein.** KDIGO suggests 0.8 g per kg of body weight a day in CKD G3 to G5, and advises avoiding more than 1.3 g per kg a day in adults whose CKD is at risk of getting worse [1]. NICE advises against low-protein diets, below 0.6 to 0.8 g per kg a day [2]. See the [protein powders page](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/protein-powders-benefits-types-dosing-and-side-effects) for supplements.

**Potassium and salt substitutes.** Damaged kidneys may not clear potassium well, and blood potassium can rise too high [8]. Potassium salt substitutes can help cut sodium, but not if you are pregnant, have kidney disease or diabetes, have been told to limit potassium, or take a potassium-sparing diuretic, a potassium supplement, an ACE inhibitor or an ARB, unless your doctor agrees; the plan suggests one only with a current eGFR of 45 or more (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). The NHS advises people with CKD to avoid potassium supplements [8]. See the [potassium page](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/potassium-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects).

**Exercise and weight.** KDIGO recommends at least 150 minutes a week of moderate activity, or as much as your heart and fitness allow, less sitting, and weight loss for people with obesity [1].

**Tobacco.** KDIGO advises people with CKD not to use tobacco products [1].

**Vaccines.** Australian guidance recommends influenza, pneumococcal, COVID-19 and shingles vaccines for adults with CKD [3].

**Painkillers and supplements.** The NHS advises people with kidney disease to avoid over-the-counter NSAIDs such as ibuprofen unless a health professional advises them [8]. KDIGO asks doctors to review and limit over-the-counter medicines and herbal or dietary products that may harm the kidneys [1].

**Illness.** If you are vomiting or have diarrhoea, keep drinking enough that your urine stays pale [9]; with a fluid limit, on dialysis or passing very little urine, follow your own team's fluid advice instead [8]. New Zealand guidance tells people to pause some medicines while acutely unwell: sulfonylureas, ACE inhibitors, diuretics, metformin, ARBs, NSAIDs and SGLT2 inhibitors [4]. Ask your doctor for a written plan, including when to restart [1].

## Medicines

Most medicines for CKD are chosen by your urine ACR, your eGFR and whether you have diabetes [1][2].

**ACE inhibitors and ARBs** (for example lisinopril, ramipril, losartan, candesartan). KDIGO recommends one for people with CKD and a raised ACR, with or without diabetes, and advises against combining an ACE inhibitor with an ARB [1]. NICE offers one to adults with CKD and hypertension and an ACR above 30 mg/mmol, to adults without diabetes from an ACR of 70, and to adults with diabetes from an ACR of 3 [2]. Blood pressure, creatinine and potassium are checked within a few weeks of starting or changing the dose [1]. Their labels warn of high potassium and of swelling of the face and throat, and say not to use potassium supplements or salt substitutes without asking your doctor [20][21]. They can harm an unborn baby; stop them as soon as pregnancy is found [20]. This guide gives no doses for them in reduced kidney function: check with your doctor or pharmacist.

**SGLT2 inhibitors** (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin). KDIGO recommends one for adults with type 2 diabetes and CKD with an eGFR of 20 or more, and for adults with CKD and an ACR of 20 mg/mmol or more, or heart failure [1]. In EMPA-KIDNEY (6,609 people with CKD, with or without diabetes), empagliflozin lowered kidney disease progression or cardiovascular death (13.1% vs 16.9% over a median 2 years; hazard ratio 0.72) [11]. In DAPA-CKD (4,304 people), dapagliflozin lowered the main kidney and cardiovascular outcome (9.2% vs 14.5%; hazard ratio 0.61), with similar effects with or without type 2 diabetes [12]. A small, early fall in eGFR is expected when one is started and is generally not a reason to stop [1][4]. The labels warn of ketoacidosis, genital infections and fluid loss [16][17].

**Finerenone** (a non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonist). US-approved for CKD with type 2 or type 1 diabetes and some heart failure, not for CKD without diabetes [18]. In FIDELIO-DKD (5,734 people) it lowered the main kidney outcome (17.8% vs 21.1%; hazard ratio 0.82); more people stopped it because of high potassium (2.3% vs 0.9%) [14]. In FIND-CKD (1,584 adults with CKD and albuminuria but no diabetes, all on an ACE inhibitor or ARB), finerenone slowed the yearly fall in eGFR (3.3 vs 4.0 mL/min/1.73m² a year) and lowered a combined kidney and heart outcome (hazard ratio 0.77); high potassium was the most common adverse event (17.0% vs 13.3% with placebo) [29].

**GLP-1 medicines.** In FLOW (3,533 people with type 2 diabetes and CKD), weekly semaglutide lowered major kidney disease events by 24% (hazard ratio 0.76) and death from any cause by 20% [13].

**Statins.** KDIGO recommends a statin, or a statin with ezetimibe, for adults aged 50 or over with an eGFR below 60 who are not on dialysis [1].

**Aspirin.** After a heart attack, stroke or stent, take it as your doctor directs; it is not for preventing a first event (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). KDIGO recommends low-dose aspirin in CKD only for people with established heart or blood vessel disease [1].

**Blood thinners.** Doses of many anticoagulants depend on kidney function [1]. This guide gives none: check with your doctor or pharmacist.

**Metformin.** Its label says not to use it with an eGFR below 30, not to start it between 30 and 45, and to weigh continuing it if eGFR falls below 45. It may need pausing around iodinated contrast scans [19].

**Medicines to be careful with.** Long-term NSAID use may speed CKD, and even short-term use can lower GFR for a while [2]; the ibuprofen label tells people with kidney disease to ask a doctor first [22]. Long-term lithium can damage the kidneys [7]. Many medicines need a dose change at low eGFR, so tell every prescriber and pharmacist about your kidney function [1].

## Brad's view (reviewed 2 Nov 2026)

- **The eGFR bands.** The plan uses its own Normal (70 or more) and Low Normal (60 to 69) bands, then KDIGO's names and cut points from 60 down, and states that an eGFR below 60 kept up for 3 months or more is consistent with CKD (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
- **Protein.** The plan aims for 1.2 g of protein per kg of ideal body weight a day, and 1.0 g per kg when eGFR is below 45, to ease the load on the kidneys (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). In his [protein post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/the-protein-obsession-is-going-horribly-wrong), Brad writes that a slightly higher protein intake, such as 1.2 g per kg, is probably safe and even beneficial, including for people with milder kidney disease, but he advises his patients to stay away from the high end, such as 2.2 g per kg [25]. The Health Roadmap adds that people with CKD stages 3 to 5 should ask their doctor about protein intake (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
- **Potassium.** The potassium card (3,500 to 5,000 mg a day from food) shows only with an eGFR of 45 or more, because impaired kidneys may not clear extra potassium safely (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). In his [salt post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/completely-wrong-about-salt-new-study), Brad says potassium-rich food matters for balancing sodium, while noting the risk of too much potassium in kidney disease [26].
- **Creatine.** In his [creatine post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/why-creatine-is-the-one-supplement-all-longevity-researchers-are-taking), Brad explains that creatine raises blood creatinine, which can make kidney function look worse than it is, that the evidence suggests creatine is not a problem with normal kidney function, and that for patients with significant kidney disease he reduces the dose or stops it [23]. In his [post for older adults](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/older-adults-seniors-creatine-supplements), he advises people with chronic kidney disease or on dialysis to discuss creatine with their doctor first [24].
- **Testing.** In his [blood tests post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/5-livesaving-blog-tests), Brad lists kidney function (sodium, potassium and creatinine) among the tests every adult should have, with prompt follow-up of an abnormal result [28].
- **Kidney-protecting medicines.** Brad takes empagliflozin 6.25 mg a day, below the usual 10 mg dose, mainly to protect his kidneys, because high blood pressure strains them over time; this is off-label, not formally studied and not a recommendation for anyone else (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). The plan's SGLT2 inhibitor card describes cardiovascular and kidney protection as proven "in some populations" (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). In his [tirzepatide post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/why-i-take-low-dose-tirzepatide-zepbound), Brad notes that FLOW's kidney benefit was unrelated to weight change [27].

## Who needs extra care

- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Neither ACE inhibitors nor ARBs are used in pregnancy [20][21]. A creatinine eGFR is less reliable in pregnancy [2]. KDIGO asks doctors to review every medicine for harm to a baby in people with CKD who could become pregnant [1].
- **Older adults.** KDIGO suggests less intensive blood pressure lowering with frailty, a high risk of falls or dizziness on standing, and higher protein and calorie targets with frailty or muscle loss [1].
- **Diabetes.** People with diabetes should be tested every year [2][3]. Several medicines protect both kidneys and heart in diabetes (see "Medicines").
- **Higher-risk groups.** CKD is more common in people who are Black or of South Asian origin [7]. Australian guidance has its own testing schedule for First Nations people [3].
- **Low eGFR and medicines.** Check with your doctor or pharmacist before any new medicine or supplement [1][9].
- **Under 18.** Kidney tests in children are judged differently; talk to their doctor [1].

## When to see your doctor

- **Today, urgently:** much less urine than usual, severe thirst, vomiting, swollen feet or legs, confusion or drowsiness, or breathlessness when lying down. These can be signs of acute kidney injury [9].
- A first eGFR below 60: NICE repeats it within 2 weeks [2].
- A raised urine ACR, so it can be confirmed on a morning sample [1][2].
- Blood in your urine, swollen ankles, feet or hands, tiredness or breathlessness that does not settle [7].
- In people with CKD, an eGFR that drops by more than 20% from one test to the next, beyond normal variation [1].
- Before taking NSAIDs, potassium supplements, a salt substitute or herbal remedies if you have kidney disease [8][22].
- For the blood tests your doctor asks for after starting or changing an ACE inhibitor, ARB or finerenone [1][18].
- Guidelines suggest specialist kidney care for, among others, an eGFR below 30, an ACR above 70 mg/mmol, or an ACR above 30 mg/mmol in urine that also contains blood [1][2].

## Common questions

**What is a normal eGFR for my age?** The plan calls 70 or more Normal and 60 to 69 Low Normal (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). KDIGO calls 90 or more normal and 60 to 89 mildly decreased, which is not CKD without other signs of kidney damage [1]. The equation includes age, so the same creatinine gives a lower eGFR as you get older (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Cystatin C or creatinine: which is more accurate?** Creatinine is the usual first test. KDIGO recommends adding cystatin C when the creatinine eGFR is likely to be wrong and the result matters for a decision [1]. Equations using both markers were more accurate than creatinine alone in the study behind the 2021 equations [10]. The plan itself uses the creatinine eGFR (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Does creatine harm the kidneys?** In healthy people, an expert position stand found up to 30 g a day for 5 years safe and well tolerated [15]. Creatine raises blood creatinine, so the creatinine eGFR can read low without real damage [1][23]. KDIGO's list of supplements with possible kidney harm includes creatine, based on case reports [1]. With kidney disease, talk to your doctor first [23][24]. For creatine and muscle, see the [creatine page](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/creatine-benefits-best-forms-dosing-and-side-effects).

**Is protein powder safe with kidney disease?** It adds to your total protein, which KDIGO suggests keeping at 0.8 g per kg a day in CKD G3 to G5 and below 1.3 g per kg if your CKD may progress [1]. The plan's own target is 1.0 g per kg of ideal body weight below an eGFR of 45 (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). See the [protein powders page](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/protein-powders-benefits-types-dosing-and-side-effects).

**Which supplements are safe at my eGFR?** There is no single list. KDIGO advises limiting herbal and over-the-counter products that may harm the kidneys; excess vitamin C, for example, can deposit crystals in them [1]. The NHS advises avoiding potassium supplements with CKD [8]. Each supplement page covers its kidney safety; bring your list to your doctor or pharmacist.

**Can I take ibuprofen?** Ask first. NSAIDs can lower kidney function, and long-term use may speed CKD [2]. The NHS advises people with kidney disease to avoid them unless a health professional advises otherwise [8].

**Can I take an ACE inhibitor or ARB with reduced kidney function?** Often yes; KDIGO advises continuing them even when eGFR falls below 30 [1]. The choice, dose and blood tests are for your doctor. Check with your doctor or pharmacist.
