# Osteoarthritis: Knee and Hip Pain, Exercise and Treatment

## In short

- Osteoarthritis is the most common type of arthritis. It causes pain and stiffness, most often in the knees, hips and small joints of the hands [6][7].
- Doctors usually diagnose it from your symptoms and an examination. Most people do not need an X-ray [3].
- Exercise is a core treatment for everyone with osteoarthritis [1][2][3], and weight loss for people with overweight and knee or hip osteoarthritis [2][3].
- Topical anti-inflammatory gels (topical NSAIDs) are a first medicine for knee osteoarthritis. Paracetamol gives little benefit in trials [3][13][14].
- Glucosamine and hyaluronic acid injections are not recommended by most current guidelines [2][3][4].
- Joint replacement is an option when pain and stiffness badly affect your life and other treatment has not worked [3][20].
- Brad's view: move more, not less; aerobic exercise such as cycling or walking, and for people with overweight, weight loss, including GLP-1 medicines where suitable [30].
- Rheumatoid arthritis, psoriatic arthritis and gout are different conditions; this guide does not cover them.

## Get help now

- A hot, swollen or red joint with a fever, or feeling hot, cold or shivery, or sudden severe pain in one joint that you cannot move or stand on, may be a joint infection: get medical help the same day, and if you cannot be seen quickly or you feel very unwell, go to an emergency department or call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [25].
- After a fall or injury, severe hip pain, being unable to walk or put weight on the leg, or tingling or loss of feeling in the hip or leg: go to an emergency department or call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK), and do not drive yourself [26].
- After an injury, a leg that looks twisted or bent, or bone showing through the skin: call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [27].
- After a knee replacement, leg pain and swelling with chest pain or difficulty breathing may be a blood clot in the lungs: call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [28].
- Vomiting blood while taking an anti-inflammatory such as ibuprofen, with feeling faint, dizzy, confused or unwell, black stools or tummy pain: call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [22][29].
- Face, lip or tongue swelling, wheezing or trouble breathing, or collapse after taking or applying an anti-inflammatory: stop it and call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [22][23].
- If you have taken more paracetamol (acetaminophen) than the label allows, get help straight away even if you feel well: call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or a poisons centre [24].
- For chest pain or signs of a stroke (face drooping, arm or leg weakness or numbness, trouble speaking, vision loss, even if it passes, or a sudden severe headache), call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).

## What it is

In osteoarthritis, the smooth cartilage that covers the ends of the bones in a joint breaks down. Bony growths can form, and the joint can become swollen, painful and stiff [7].

A global analysis estimated that 595 million people had osteoarthritis in 2020, with the knee the most common site [6]. Risk rises with age, past joint injury, obesity, family history and female sex [7].

**Symptoms.** Pain that gets worse the more you use the joint, stiffness, and trouble moving the joint. Some people also notice swelling, tenderness or a grating or crackling feeling [7]. Symptoms can come and go, and do not always worsen; some people improve [7].

**Diagnosis.** NICE advises diagnosing osteoarthritis without imaging in people aged 45 or over who have joint pain linked to activity and either no morning stiffness or stiffness that lasts 30 minutes or less [3]. Imaging is kept for unusual features such as a recent injury, a hot swollen joint, or signs of infection or cancer [3]. Treatment follows your symptoms and function, not imaging [3].

## Your numbers

No blood test or scan number diagnoses osteoarthritis [3]. Two numbers in the Health Roadmap matter for treatment: your weight, and your kidney function.

**Weight.** For people with overweight or obesity, NICE says any weight loss is likely to help, and losing 10% of body weight is likely to be better than 5% [3]. The plan's weight cards and its weight-medicine card follow your BMI, waist and metabolic markers, not your joints (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026); the weight guideline covers them.

**Kidney function (eGFR).** Anti-inflammatory tablets can harm the kidneys; NICE asks doctors to weigh kidney harm before prescribing them [3]. The Health Roadmap labels eGFR (in mL/min/1.73m²) in these bands (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026):

| eGFR | Plan label |
|---|---|
| 70 or more | Normal |
| 60 to 69 | Low Normal |
| 45 to 59 | Mildly to Moderately Decreased |
| 30 to 44 | Moderately to Severely Decreased |
| 15 to 29 | Severely Decreased |
| Below 15 | Kidney Failure |

The plan does not link these bands to any pain medicine.

## What helps

Every guideline starts with exercise, weight management where it applies, and information about the condition [1][2][3][4].

**Exercise.** NICE recommends exercise tailored to each person, such as muscle strengthening and aerobic fitness [3]. In a 2024 Cochrane review of 139 trials in knee osteoarthritis, exercise probably improved physical function and may have improved pain compared with usual care, though the authors judged these gains of uncertain clinical importance; exercise may increase adverse events compared with no treatment [8]. In a 2026 Cochrane review of 18 mostly small hip trials, exercise may make little or no difference to pain against an attention control; against usual care it probably helped pain and function slightly, but probably not by a clinically meaningful amount [9]. Guidelines still recommend it for the hip [2][3]. A 2025 network meta-analysis of 217 trials (15,684 people) in knee osteoarthritis found aerobic exercise was most likely to be the best type for pain, function, walking and quality of life, with moderate certainty [10].

**Expect some discomfort at first.** NICE advises that joint pain may rise when you start exercising, and that regular exercise still helps the joint [3].

**Weight loss.** The US and Australian guidelines strongly recommend weight loss for people with knee or hip osteoarthritis and overweight [2][4]. In the 18-month IDEA trial in adults with overweight and knee osteoarthritis, diet plus exercise (an average loss of 10.6 kg) gave less pain and better function than exercise alone [11]. In the STEP 9 trial, over 68 weeks, people with obesity and knee osteoarthritis who took weekly semaglutide lost 13.7% of body weight, against 3.2% with placebo, and their knee pain score fell 41.7 points on a 100-point scale, against 27.5 points with placebo [12]. Pain also fell in the placebo group, which had the same diet and activity counselling [12].

**Walking aids and braces.** The US guideline strongly recommends a cane, and a knee brace for some people with knee osteoarthritis, when the joint makes walking or stability hard [2]. NICE advises against routine insoles, braces or supports unless the joint is unstable and exercise alone is not enough [3].

**Tai chi and talking therapy.** The US guideline strongly recommends tai chi for knee and hip osteoarthritis, and conditionally recommends cognitive behavioural therapy [2].

**Not offered by NICE:** acupuncture, TENS, ultrasound or laser therapy [3].

**Supplements.** Each has its own page: [glucosamine, chondroitin and other joint supplements](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/glucosamine-and-joint-supplements-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [collagen](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/collagen-benefits-best-forms-dosing-and-side-effects) and [turmeric and curcumin](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/turmeric-curcumin-benefits-best-forms-dosing-and-side-effects).
- **Glucosamine.** NICE does not offer it, and the US guideline strongly recommends against it for knee, hip and hand osteoarthritis, because the lowest-bias trials show no important benefit [2][3]. In the large GAIT trial, glucosamine, chondroitin and the two together did not reduce knee pain more than placebo overall [19].
- **Chondroitin.** A Cochrane review of mostly low-quality trials found a small to moderate short-term pain benefit [18]. The US guideline strongly recommends against it for knee and hip, alone or with glucosamine, but conditionally for it in hand osteoarthritis [2].
- **Fish oil and vitamin D.** The US guideline recommends against both for osteoarthritis, conditionally [2].

## Medicines

Medicines are used alongside exercise and weight management, at the lowest dose that works, for the shortest time [3].

**Topical NSAIDs (anti-inflammatory gels).** NICE offers a topical NSAID first for knee osteoarthritis, and the US guideline strongly recommends one [2][3]. In a Cochrane review, topical diclofenac and ketoprofen gave good pain relief beyond a dummy gel for a minority of people, mostly in knee osteoarthritis [14]. An over-the-counter diclofenac sodium 1% gel label directs 4 g to each knee (or 2 g to each hand) 4 times a day, on no more than 2 body areas at once, for up to 21 days unless your doctor directs otherwise; it may take up to 7 days to work [23]. It carries the same bleeding, heart and stroke warnings as the tablets [23].

**Capsaicin cream.** The US guideline conditionally recommends topical capsaicin for knee osteoarthritis; the Australian guideline suggests not using it [2][4].

**Anti-inflammatory tablets (oral NSAIDs).** Examples are ibuprofen, naproxen and celecoxib. The US guideline strongly recommends them and calls them the first oral medicine of choice [2]. NICE considers them when topical medicines fail, with a stomach-protecting medicine [3]. OARSI advises against any oral NSAID for people with heart disease or frailty [1]. The labels warn that NSAIDs (except aspirin) raise the risk of heart attack, heart failure and stroke, and can cause severe stomach bleeding, more so from age 60, with past ulcers, or with blood thinners or steroids [22]. An over-the-counter ibuprofen 200 mg label allows 1 caplet every 4 to 6 hours, 2 if 1 does not work, and no more than 6 caplets in 24 hours unless a doctor directs [22]. Ask a doctor first if you have high blood pressure, heart disease, kidney disease, liver cirrhosis, asthma or past stomach problems, take a diuretic, or take aspirin for heart attack or stroke [22].

**Paracetamol (acetaminophen).** In a Cochrane review of 10 trials, paracetamol gave only minimal improvement in pain and function in knee and hip osteoarthritis, and abnormal liver tests were more common than with placebo, though the authors were less certain of this and of its clinical importance [13]. NICE does not routinely offer it, except for occasional short-term use when other medicines are unsuitable [3]. The US guideline still recommends it, conditionally, for short-term use when NSAIDs are unsuitable [2]. One extra-strength 500 mg label allows 2 tablets every 6 hours and no more than 6 tablets in 24 hours; its liver warning applies above 4,000 mg in 24 hours, with other paracetamol products, or with 3 or more alcoholic drinks a day [24].

**Steroid injections into the joint.** NICE considers them when other medicines fail or to help you exercise, and says the relief lasts only about 2 to 10 weeks [3]. A Cochrane review found any benefit fades over time, with no evidence of an effect at 6 months [15]. In a 2-year trial, injections every 3 months caused more cartilage loss than saline and did not improve pain more [16]. One hospital found rapidly worsening hip damage within months of a hip steroid injection in 5.4% of people, more often after high (80 mg or more) or repeat doses [36]. An injection in the 3 months before replacing that joint may be linked to more infection after surgery [37].

**Hyaluronic acid injections.** NICE does not offer them [3]. The US guideline recommends against them, conditionally for the knee and strongly for the hip [2]. In a meta-analysis of 89 trials, the large trials with blinded assessment showed a clinically irrelevant effect on pain, and the injections increased serious adverse events [17].

**Platelet-rich plasma (PRP) and stem cell injections.** The US guideline strongly recommends against both for knee and hip osteoarthritis [2].

**Other medicines.** The US guideline conditionally recommends duloxetine and tramadol for some people [2]. NICE does not offer strong opioids, because their risks outweigh their benefits [3], and OARSI strongly advises against opioids [1].

**Surgery.** NICE considers referral for joint replacement when symptoms badly affect your life and non-surgical treatment has not worked or is unsuitable. It does not exclude people because of age, smoking, other conditions or BMI, though risks vary with them [3]. In England in 2021, two in three local health bodies had a BMI policy for these operations; where they restricted access, most often at a BMI of 40 [38]. In a trial of 100 people eligible for knee replacement, surgery plus 12 weeks of non-surgical care gave more pain relief and better function at 12 months than that care alone, but more serious adverse events [20].

## Brad's view (reviewed 2 Nov 2026)

- **Move more, not less.** In his [knee arthritis exercise post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/new-study-reveals-the-best-exercise-to-treat-knee-arthritis), Brad argues that the old "wear and tear" advice to rest arthritic joints is wrong, that a sedentary life is linked to more arthritis, and that exercise helps people who already have knee arthritis [30].
- **Aerobic, low-impact exercise.** In the same post he highlights the 2025 network meta-analysis that ranked aerobic exercise (walking, cycling and swimming) best, notes that it gives no data on running, and says that for most of his patients he recommends something low-impact, such as a second-hand exercise bike at home [30]. The plan's exercise card asks everyone for at least 150 minutes of moderate cardio a week, or 75 of vigorous, plus 2 to 3 resistance sessions; it has no arthritis version (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
- **Weight loss and GLP-1 medicines.** Brad writes that he now routinely prescribes GLP-1 medicines to his patients with overweight and knee arthritis [30]. In his [cartilage post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/lizard-venom-regrows-irreplaceable-cartilage), he describes a 20-person, 24-week pilot study in which semaglutide plus hyaluronic acid injections went with thicker knee cartilage on MRI than the injections alone, and stresses that it was a pilot needing larger, longer trials [32]. In the plan, weight medicine is suggested from your BMI and metabolic markers, with tirzepatide preferred; joint pain is not one of its triggers (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
- **Metformin is not for everyone.** In his [metformin post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/arthritis-breakthrough-from-a-common-6-drug), Brad covers a 6-month trial in which metformin eased knee pain in people with overweight more than placebo [21][31]. For people without diabetes he weighs that against evidence that it may blunt fitness gains from exercise and lower testosterone in men, and decides with each patient [31].
- **Supplements.** In his [collagen post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/the-collagen-peptides-for-skin-story-is-changing), Brad notes a modest fall in osteoarthritis pain in pooled trials, but that knee pain improved no more with collagen than with milk protein when the protein was matched [33]. The plan's collagen card is about skin, not joints (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). He tends to steer patients away from curcumin supplements, though he notes a meta-analysis found it seemed to help knee arthritis pain and function, and warns of reports of liver injury [34]. In a trial of 74 people with knee arthritis, fisetin did not improve pain, function or the joint [35].

## Who needs extra care

- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Ask a health professional before using any pain medicine. Ibuprofen and diclofenac labels warn against use at 20 weeks of pregnancy or later unless a doctor directs it [22][23].
- **Older adults.** The stomach-bleeding risk with NSAIDs is higher from age 60 [22].
- **Heart disease, stroke or high blood pressure.** Ask a doctor before an NSAID (see Medicines) [22].
- **Reduced kidney function.** Ask your doctor or pharmacist before an NSAID tablet or gel [22][23].
- **Blood thinners or steroids.** NSAIDs add to the risk of stomach bleeding [22]. If you take warfarin, ask your doctor or pharmacist before taking paracetamol regularly [24].
- **Liver disease or regular alcohol.** Paracetamol labels warn of liver damage, and diclofenac gel carries a liver warning too [23][24].
- **Under 18.** Talk to their doctor. The diclofenac gel label says it is not known whether it works or is safe under 18 [23].

## When to see your doctor

- Joint pain or stiffness that keeps coming back, so your doctor can confirm the diagnosis [7].
- Morning stiffness that lasts more than 30 minutes, which can point to another type of arthritis, or symptoms or joint shape that worsen quickly [3][7].
- Vomiting blood that has stopped, with no other symptoms: stop the anti-inflammatory and get an urgent same-day appointment [29].
- Black or bloody stools, or stomach pain that does not settle, while taking an NSAID [22].
- Pain that gets worse or lasts more than 10 days on an over-the-counter tablet, or more than 21 days on diclofenac gel, or new redness or swelling in the joint [22][23][24].
- After a knee replacement: throbbing or cramping leg pain, a fever, pus from the wound, or knee redness or swelling that is getting worse, the same day [28].
- When your plan is not working, or pain and stiffness are badly affecting your life, to discuss other options, including referral for joint replacement [3].

## Common questions

**Is walking or exercise bad for arthritic knees?** No. Every guideline recommends it, and trials show it eases pain a little and improves function [3][8][10].

**Is running bad for my knees?** Brad notes the 2025 meta-analysis has no running data, and suggests low-impact exercise such as cycling for most of his patients [30].

**What do knee osteoarthritis trials show for collagen?** Brad notes that in a trial that matched protein, knee pain improved no more with collagen than with milk protein [33].

**I have a meniscus tear and arthritis. Do I need keyhole surgery?** Usually not. NICE does not offer arthroscopic washout or trimming for osteoarthritis, and the Australian guideline advises against arthroscopic meniscus surgery for knee osteoarthritis unless the knee is locking [3][4].

**Can cartilage grow back?** Osteoarthritis cannot be cured, though symptoms can improve [7]. In a small pilot Brad describes, semaglutide went with thicker cartilage on MRI; larger trials are needed [32].
