# Dementia: Lowering Your Risk, Memory Problems and Alzheimer's Treatment

*Written from NICE, WHO and US guidance, FDA labels, NHS pages and trials, plus Brad's posts and the Health Roadmap algorithm.*

## In short

- Dementia is a decline in memory and thinking that is bad enough to affect daily life. Alzheimer's disease is the most common cause. Mild cognitive impairment (MCI) is a milder stage in which daily life is still intact [1][4].
- New memory problems are worth checking. NICE advises a history from you and from someone who knows you well, plus tests for reversible causes [1].
- Sudden confusion is different. It may be delirium, which needs urgent care [12][55].
- Lowering high blood pressure cut mild cognitive impairment in one large trial and dementia in another. Trials of exercise, diet and brain training show small or mixed effects, though WHO still recommends physical activity [2][17][18][19].
- Cholinesterase inhibitors and memantine give small benefits. The anti-amyloid antibodies slow early Alzheimer's modestly and can cause brain swelling and bleeding, more often in people with two copies of APOE e4 [37][39][40][42][8][9].
- No supplement has been shown to prevent or treat dementia in good trials [2][46][49].
- Brad's view: keep blood pressure and LDL cholesterol low for years, watch the shingles vaccine evidence closely, and use an Alzheimer's blood test only when there are symptoms [60][61][62][64].

## Get help now

- Someone suddenly becomes confused, or suddenly cannot stay alert or follow a conversation: this may be delirium, which can be life threatening, so call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [12][55].
- Signs of a stroke (face drooping, arm weakness, slurred or confused speech), even if they pass: call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [14].
- After a head injury, if the person cannot stay awake, has a fit, new weakness or numbness, trouble walking, speaking or understanding, or a change in behaviour: call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [13].
- On lecanemab or donanemab, a new headache, confusion, dizziness, vision change, nausea, trouble walking or a seizure may be brain swelling (ARIA): go to the nearest emergency department right away or call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK), and say which medicine you take [8][9].
- During an antibody infusion, tell the staff at once about any swelling, rash or breathing trouble. After you leave, trouble breathing or swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat may be a severe allergic reaction: call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [8][9][68].

## What it is

In dementia, memory, thinking and judgement decline over months to years. Alzheimer's disease is the most common type; others include vascular, Lewy body and frontotemporal dementia [1].

**Mild cognitive impairment.** Thinking is worse than expected for age, but daily life is intact [4]. In a pooled analysis, about 5 to 10% of people with MCI progressed to dementia each year, and most had not progressed even after 10 years [32].

**Delirium** is a sudden change in attention and awareness. It is common in older people in hospital, often missed, and serious [55]. A short bedside check, the 4AT, picks up about 88% of cases [57].

**Risk.** The rate of new dementia in Europe and North America fell by about 13% per decade over 25 years [16]. The 2024 Lancet Commission named 14 risk factors that can be changed: low education, hearing loss, high LDL cholesterol, depression, head injury, inactivity, diabetes, smoking, high blood pressure, obesity, heavy drinking, social isolation, air pollution and untreated vision loss [15]. Its estimate of how much dementia they explain comes from observational data, not trials [15].

## Your numbers

**Memory tests.** NICE lists several brief tests, such as the Mini-Cog and 6CIT [1]. In its first study, the MoCA detected 90% of people with MCI, against 18% for the older MMSE [36]. Tests differ by country.

**Blood and urine tests.** NICE advises tests to rule out reversible causes of poor memory before referral, and a brain scan unless dementia is well established and its type is clear [1].

**Blood pressure.** In SPRINT MIND, aiming for a systolic below 120 mmHg did not significantly cut dementia (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.67 to 1.04), but it cut mild cognitive impairment (HR 0.81) [17]. The trial stopped early with fewer cases than expected [17]. In a village trial of 33,995 adults in rural China, more intensive blood pressure care cut dementia by 15% (risk ratio 0.85) [18]. The Health Roadmap's target is below 120/80 under age 65 and below 130/80 from 65 (the health tool); the blood pressure guide has more.

**LDL cholesterol and HbA1c.** High midlife LDL and diabetes are among the 14 risk factors [15]. The plan's cholesterol and diabetes cards apply as usual; the high cholesterol and type 2 diabetes guides cover them.

**Alzheimer's blood tests (p-tau217, p-tau181).** In people with memory symptoms seen in primary and specialist care, a p-tau217 test identified Alzheimer's with high accuracy [35]. The Alzheimer's Association's 2025 guideline, for specialist memory care only, says a test with at least 90% sensitivity and 75% specificity can triage, and one with at least 90% for both can replace a PET scan or spinal fluid test [5]. It warns that many commercial tests miss these marks, and the tests are not for people without symptoms [5].

**APOE e4.** Before an antibody, the labels and specialist recommendations advise APOE testing, because people with two copies have more brain swelling and bleeding [6][7][8][9].

**Homocysteine.** The plan can store a homocysteine result but has no rule for it (the health tool). In 11 trials of about 22,000 people, B vitamins lowered homocysteine but did not change thinking [46].

## What helps

**WHO's 2019 advice for people with normal thinking.** It strongly recommends physical activity and stopping smoking. It conditionally suggests a Mediterranean-style diet, less harmful drinking and cognitive training. It strongly advises against vitamins B and E, omega-3 and multi-nutrient supplements [2].

**What trials show.** A Cochrane review of 12 short trials in healthy older adults found no cognitive benefit from aerobic exercise [19]. The 3-year MIND diet trial found no difference against a control diet [22]. Two-year programmes combining diet, exercise, brain training and heart checks gave small gains on thinking scores in FINGER and US POINTER [20][21]. None of these trials tested dementia itself.

**Hearing.** Hearing loss is a risk factor [15]. In the 3-year ACHIEVE trial of 977 adults aged 70 to 84, hearing aids did not slow cognitive decline overall; the effect differed between a higher-risk group and healthy volunteers, a secondary finding [23].

**Shingles vaccine.** In Wales, people just old enough to qualify for the vaccine were compared with people just too old. Vaccination cut new dementia diagnoses by 3.5 percentage points over 7 years [24]. A similar Australian study found eligibility cut them by 1.8 points over 7.4 years [25]. Neither was a randomised trial. A pooled analysis of observational studies found no significant link (OR 0.84, 95% CI 0.50 to 1.43) [26].

**Diabetes medicines.** In a meta-analysis of heart-protective glucose-lowering trials, the drugs together did not significantly cut dementia (OR 0.83). GLP-1 medicines did (OR 0.55); SGLT2 inhibitors did not (OR 1.20) [27]. In people who already had early Alzheimer's, oral semaglutide did not slow decline over 2 years [28].

**Not protective in trials:** daily aspirin in healthy older adults [29]; oestrogen plus progestin from age 65, which raised risk [30].

**Medicines that can blur thinking.** NICE asks doctors to cut medicines with strong anticholinergic effects [1]. In one cohort, the highest long-term use went with 54% more dementia (HR 1.54) [31].

**If you have MCI.** The US neurology guideline found no good evidence for any medicine, and says 6 months of exercise is likely to improve thinking scores [4]. In 11 trials, aerobic exercise raised MMSE scores by about 1 point [33].

**Living with dementia.** NICE offers group cognitive stimulation therapy for mild to moderate dementia, and not acupuncture, ginseng, vitamin E or herbal products [1]. To prevent delirium, combined non-drug care in hospital (moving, eating and drinking, sleep, glasses and hearing aids, orientation) cut delirium by 44% and falls by 64% [56]. After a diagnosis, NICE advises early talks about planning ahead (power of attorney, future care wishes), how dementia affects driving, and training and support for carers [1].

**Changes in behaviour.** NICE advises non-drug approaches first, with antipsychotics only for severe distress or risk of harm [1]. In trials, newer antipsychotics in dementia raised the risk of death over 10 to 12 weeks (3.5% against 2.3%) [58].

**Supplements.** Each has a page with the evidence and safety notes: [ginkgo](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/ginkgo-biloba-benefits-best-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [omega-3](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/omega-3-fish-oil-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [B vitamins](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/b-vitamins-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [vitamin E](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/vitamin-e-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [multivitamins](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/multivitamins-benefits-evidence-dosing-and-side-effects), [lion's mane](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/lions-mane-and-chaga-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [phosphatidylserine](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/phosphatidylserine-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [huperzine A](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/huperzine-a-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects) and [lithium](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/lithium-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects). In brief:
- **Ginkgo.** In GEM, 3,069 older adults took 120 mg twice a day; it did not prevent dementia [49]. In GuidAge it did not prevent Alzheimer's in people with memory complaints [50].
- **Omega-3.** It did not slow decline over 5 years in AREDS2 [47], or in mild to moderate Alzheimer's at 2 g a day of DHA [48].
- **B vitamins.** They lowered homocysteine but did not change thinking in pooled trials [46].
- **Lion's mane.** A 16-week trial in 30 adults with MCI improved a thinking score, which fell after stopping [52]. A small pilot in mild Alzheimer's reported a better MMSE [53]. Both are too small to rely on.
- **Phosphatidylserine.** Memory rose in all groups, placebo included; only people with low starting scores did better on it [54].
- **Huperzine A.** Some benefit in small trials, but only one was of adequate quality and size [51]. It blocks the same enzyme as donepezil, rivastigmine and galantamine, and the donepezil label expects added effects with drugs of this kind, so do not combine them without your doctor [10][51].

## Medicines

**Cholinesterase inhibitors (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine).** NICE offers them for mild to moderate Alzheimer's and for Lewy body dementia, but not for frontotemporal dementia [1]. In a Cochrane review, donepezil improved the MMSE by about 1 point out of 30, and more people had side effects (72% against 65%) [37]. In the AD2000 trial it did not delay moving into care (42% against 44% at 3 years) [38]. For MCI, the US neurology guideline says doctors may choose not to offer them; over 3 years, donepezil did not stop progression to Alzheimer's [4][34]. The donepezil label starts at 5 mg tablets once a day in the evening, rising to 10 mg after 4 to 6 weeks [10]. It warns of a slow heart rate, fainting, stomach bleeding (more so with NSAIDs) and nausea, vomiting or diarrhoea, most often after a dose increase [10].

**Memantine.** A Cochrane review found a small benefit in moderate to severe Alzheimer's and probably none in mild disease [39]. NICE uses it alone when inhibitors are unsuitable, or added to one in moderate or severe disease [1]. The tablet label starts at 5 mg once a day and rises weekly, in 5 mg steps, to 20 mg a day; the target is lower in severe kidney disease [11]. Common side effects are dizziness, headache, confusion and constipation [11].

**Anti-amyloid antibodies (lecanemab, donanemab).** These clear amyloid from the brain. They are for MCI or mild dementia due to Alzheimer's, after amyloid is confirmed, with an MRI before treatment and at set times during it [8][9].
- In Clarity AD, over 18 months, the CDR-SB score (0 to 18; higher is worse) rose 1.21 points with lecanemab and 1.66 with placebo [40]. Infusion reactions hit 26.4%, and brain swelling (ARIA-E) 12.6% [40]. In longer follow-up, ARIA-E reached 34.5% in people with two copies of APOE e4 [41].
- In TRAILBLAZER-ALZ 2, over 76 weeks, donanemab slowed decline, with a CDR-SB difference of 0.67 points in people with low or medium tau [42]. ARIA-E occurred in 24.0% (52 people had symptoms), and 3 deaths were linked to treatment [42]. A slower starting schedule lowered ARIA-E to 15.6%, against 24.2% [43].
- Most ARIA causes no symptoms, but it can be serious or rarely fatal [6][8]. Blood thinners and clot-busting drugs may raise the risk of brain bleeding [9]. The lecanemab recommendations advise against it for people who need anticoagulants [6].
- Lecanemab is given by drip every 2 weeks, or by a weekly injection under the skin, which the 2026 label allows from the start; donanemab by drip every 4 weeks, stopping once amyloid is cleared [8][9].
- Both are approved in the US [6][7]. Access and funding differ by country; a memory specialist can say what is offered where you live.

**Vitamin E.** In one trial of mild to moderate Alzheimer's, 2,000 IU a day slowed decline in daily function by 3.15 points on a 78-point scale [44]. NICE advises against it [1]. A meta-analysis linked 400 IU a day or more to higher death rates [45]. The evidence conflicts, and this guide does not recommend it.

## Brad's view (reviewed 9 Nov 2026)

- **Most risk is worth tackling.** In his [dementia risk post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/breaking-reduce-dementia-risk-45-percent), Brad covers the Lancet Commission's 14 risk factors and its estimate that tackling them could cut dementia by up to 45% [59].
- **Blood pressure below 120.** In his [blood pressure and dementia post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/scientists-just-found-an-easy-way-to-cut-dementia-risk-by-15), Brad says most people should aim for a systolic below 120 to protect the brain as well as the heart [60].
- **Lower LDL for decades.** In his [cholesterol and dementia post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/dementia-rates-plunged-in-study-of-1-million-people), Brad notes that LDL-lowering trials found no significant cut in dementia, but argues that a 1-million-person genetic study points to protection from lifelong lower LDL [61].
- **The shingles vaccine.** In his [shingles vaccine post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/shingles-vaccine-dementia), Brad calls the Wales study the strongest evidence yet, about a 20% relative cut in diagnoses, while noting it covered people aged 79 to 80 given the older live vaccine [62].
- **Diabetes medicines.** In his [GLP-1 and SGLT2 post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/hard-to-believe-dementia-effects-found), Brad says the link with lower dementia comes mostly from people with type 2 diabetes, and trials are needed [63]; semaglutide later failed in early Alzheimer's [28].
- **Blood tests only with symptoms.** In his [Alzheimer's blood test post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/weapon-against-alzheimer-s-just-got-fda-approved), Brad explains that the FDA-cleared p-tau181 test is for people 55 and older with symptoms. A negative result was right about 98% of the time, but a positive one only about 22%, so it needs a PET scan or spinal fluid test to follow [64].
- **Coffee and sleep.** Brad notes that 1 to 2.5 cups of caffeinated coffee a day went with about 19% less dementia in a large observational study, with no link for decaf [65]. Sleep apnoea was linked to 43% more dementia, and he stresses that it is treatable once diagnosed [67].
- **Omega-3 and B vitamins.** Brad thinks omega-3 may help the brain only when B vitamin status is good, and he still takes about 1 g a day, though a 2026 observational study found faster decline in omega-3 users [66]. The plan's omega-3 card offers up to 1 g a day as his judgement and notes WHO's advice against these supplements (the health tool). The plan's micronutrient card cites a COSMOS analysis reporting about 2 years less cognitive ageing on a multivitamin (the health tool).

## Who needs extra care

- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Ask a doctor before starting any medicine or supplement in this guide.
- **Under 18.** Memory problems in a child or teenager need their own doctor.
- **Two copies of APOE e4.** Brain swelling and bleeding on antibodies are more common [8][9][41].
- **Blood thinners.** They may raise the bleeding risk with antibodies; the lecanemab recommendations advise against it if you need an anticoagulant [6][9].
- **Slow heart rate or fainting.** Donepezil can slow the heart or cause fainting [10].
- **Severe kidney disease.** The memantine label lowers the target dose [11].
- **Lewy body or Parkinson's disease dementia.** Antipsychotics can worsen movement problems and sometimes cause severe reactions, so they need specialist advice. Frontotemporal dementia also needs different medicines [1].
- **Under 65, or a strong family history.** Young-onset dementia sometimes has a genetic cause; ask about referral to a specialist [1].

## When to see your doctor

- Memory or thinking problems that are new or getting worse, or that family members notice. Bring someone who knows you well [1][12].
- Confusion or memory getting worse over days to weeks after a fall or head knock, or while on a blood thinner: get seen urgently, the same day, as it may be a slow bleed on the brain [13][69].
- Hives alone after an antibody infusion: contact the infusion team the same day [9].
- Mood changes, low mood or poor sleep alongside memory problems, or loud snoring with daytime sleepiness [15][67].
- Before buying an Alzheimer's blood test or APOE test, to talk about what a result would mean [5][64].
- If you take several medicines, to review any that may affect memory [1].
- If dementia is still suspected after reversible causes are checked, ask about referral to a memory service [1].
- New agitation, distress or hallucinations: pain or delirium may be the cause [1].

## Common questions

**Is forgetting names normal ageing?** Problems that are new, worsening or noticed by others are worth checking [1].

**Should I get an Alzheimer's blood test?** Only with symptoms and through a doctor. A negative result is reassuring; a positive one needs more testing [5][64].

**Are lecanemab and donanemab worth it?** They slow early Alzheimer's modestly, with real risks and frequent MRI scans [40][42]. A specialist can help weigh this.
