# Blood Thinners: Warfarin, Eliquis and Other Anticoagulants

*From guidelines, FDA labels, NIH pages, trials, Brad's posts and the Health Roadmap algorithm. No doses or stopping schedules: your doctor or pharmacist sets those.*

## In short

- "Blood thinner" covers anticoagulants (warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, heparin), which slow clotting, and antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel), which stop platelets clumping [15].
- Anticoagulants are used to cut the risk of stroke in atrial fibrillation, to treat and prevent clots in the legs or lungs, and with mechanical heart valves [15][17].
- For most people with atrial fibrillation, US and UK guidelines prefer the newer tablets (often called DOACs) over warfarin [1][2]. Warfarin is still the choice for mechanical heart valves [1].
- Bleeding is the main risk. Know the warning signs, and never stop a blood thinner on your own: stopping early raises the risk of a clot or stroke [18][21].
- Warfarin needs regular INR blood tests and a steady vitamin K intake. The newer tablets need no INR tests, but kidney function is checked [1][2][17].
- Many medicines and supplements interact: NSAID pain relievers, aspirin, some antibiotics, regular paracetamol on warfarin, vitamin K2, vitamin E and some herbs. Check with your pharmacist before adding anything [2][17][24][30].
- Brad's view: aspirin only after a heart attack, stroke or stent, as your doctor directs; on warfarin, check with your clinic before starting or stopping vitamin K2 (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

## Get help now

- For chest pain, sudden shortness of breath or signs of a stroke or bleeding in the brain (face drooping, arm or leg weakness or numbness, trouble speaking, vision loss, even if it passes, or a sudden severe headache), call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).
- On a blood thinner, if you took too much, or for bleeding that is severe or will not stop, vomiting blood or vomit that looks like coffee grounds, coughing up blood, or black tar-like or red stools [18], call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).
- On a blood thinner, after a fall or injury, especially a blow to the head, get checked by a doctor straight away; if a headache, dizziness or weakness follows, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [18].
- On a blood thinner, after a spinal or epidural injection or a spinal tap: for back pain, tingling, numbness or weakness in the legs or feet, or loss of bowel or bladder control, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [18].

## What it is

Blood thinners lower the chance of a new clot forming. They do not dissolve a clot you already have, but they can stop it growing [15].

**Anticoagulants** act on the clotting proteins in the blood [15]:

- **Warfarin** (brands include Coumadin, Jantoven and Marevan) is a vitamin K antagonist: it blocks the liver's use of vitamin K to make clotting factors [13][17].
- **Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs)** block one clotting factor directly. Apixaban (Eliquis), rivaroxaban (Xarelto) and edoxaban (Savaysa, Lixiana) block factor Xa; dabigatran (Pradaxa) blocks thrombin [18][19][20][21].
- **Heparins**, such as enoxaparin, are injections, often used around surgery, in pregnancy or at the start of treatment [3][15].

**Antiplatelets** stop platelets clumping. Aspirin and clopidogrel (Plavix) are taken mainly after a heart attack, stroke or stent, or for artery disease in the legs [15][22].

**Who takes an anticoagulant?** The main reasons, as the labels list them [17][18][19]:

- **Atrial fibrillation (AF).** An irregular heart rhythm in which clots can form in the heart and travel to the brain. In meta-analyses, AF goes with about 2.4 times the risk of stroke [1].
- **Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE).** A clot in a deep vein, usually in the leg, or one that has travelled to the lungs. Blood thinners treat it and lower the chance of another [3].
- **Prevention after hip or knee replacement**, for a set time [18][19].
- **Mechanical heart valves**, where warfarin is used [1][17].

## Your numbers

**INR (for warfarin).** The INR (international normalised ratio) measures how long your blood takes to clot, on a scale that is the same in every lab. The higher the number, the slower your blood clots. Warfarin is adjusted to keep the INR in a target range, most often 2 to 3 for atrial fibrillation and for clots in the legs or lungs [1][17]. Some mechanical heart valves need a higher range [17]. Your doctor or anticoagulation clinic sets your range and your dose. Tests are frequent at the start and after any change, then spaced out once the INR is steady [17].

The INR moves with new medicines, changes in diet, alcohol and illness, such as diarrhoea or eating less [16][17][30]. An INR below your range means less protection against clots; above it means a higher chance of bleeding [17]. If yours is out of range, your clinic will advise; do not change your dose yourself.

**Kidney function (for DOACs).** The kidneys clear these drugs, so kidney function matters instead of the INR [1]. NICE asks for a review at least once a year of the need for anticoagulation, and of kidney function when it is reduced [2].

The Health Roadmap shows your eGFR (mL/min/1.73m²) in these bands (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026):

| eGFR | Roadmap label |
|---|---|
| 70 or more | Normal |
| 60 to 69 | Low normal |
| 45 to 59 | Mildly to moderately decreased |
| 30 to 44 | Moderately to severely decreased |
| 15 to 29 | Severely decreased |
| Below 15 | Kidney failure |

Drug labels use creatinine clearance instead, from a formula such as Cockcroft-Gault; it is not the same number as the eGFR on your blood test [21].

## What helps

**Take it exactly as prescribed.** Every DOAC label carries a boxed warning that stopping early, without another anticoagulant in its place, raises the risk of clots and stroke [18][21]. Never double a missed dose to catch up [16][18]. For warfarin, call your doctor [16]; for a DOAC, follow your leaflet or ask your pharmacist. Do not lower your dose yourself.

**Tell everyone who treats you**, including your dentist and pharmacist, and talk to your prescriber before any surgery or dental work [18].

**On warfarin, keep vitamin K steady.** Vitamin K reverses warfarin's effect, so sudden changes in how much you eat can push the INR up or down [13][17]. You do not need to avoid green vegetables; eat a steady amount and avoid sudden big changes [17]. Do not start or stop vitamin K or K2 supplements without your anticoagulation clinic. Low-dose vitamin K tablets to steady a swinging INR rest on weak evidence, and US chest physicians suggest against routine use [33].

**Lower the bleeding risks you can change.** NICE lists uncontrolled high blood pressure, poor INR control on warfarin, other medicines (antiplatelets, SSRI antidepressants and NSAIDs), harmful drinking and treatable causes of anaemia [2]. On warfarin, ask your doctor how much alcohol is safe [16].

## Medicines

**Warfarin.** Its boxed warning is that it can cause major or fatal bleeding [17]. Rare serious effects include skin or tissue death and small cholesterol clots breaking off; tell your doctor about painful or discoloured skin or toes [17]. Its effect can be reversed with vitamin K and, in serious bleeding, with a clotting-factor concentrate [1].

**DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban).** A meta-analysis of the four main AF trials (71,683 people) compared them with warfarin [7]. The relative risk was 0.81 for stroke or clots elsewhere in the body, 0.48 for bleeding in the brain, 0.90 for death from any cause and 1.25 for bleeding from the gut, so somewhat more gut bleeding.

Keep dabigatran capsules in their original package, away from moisture. It has its own reversal drug, idarucizumab [20].

**Interactions that matter:**

- **Other blood thinners and NSAIDs.** Aspirin, clopidogrel and NSAIDs such as ibuprofen and naproxen add to bleeding risk [18]. NSAID labels say the chance of serious stomach bleeding is higher if you take a blood thinner [24]. Take aspirin only if your doctor prescribed it with your blood thinner.
- **Warfarin's long list.** Many antibiotics, antifungals, heart drugs and herbal products change its effect. Its label asks for more INR checks when any medicine is started or stopped, even a short antibiotic course [17].
- **Paracetamol (acetaminophen) on warfarin.** Regular use can raise the INR. In one study the odds of an INR above 6 rose with the dose, and were 10 times higher at 9,100 mg a week or more [30]. Tell your clinic if you take it most days.
- **DOAC interactions.** Some medicines raise DOAC levels and bleeding risk, such as some azole antifungals (ketoconazole, itraconazole), some HIV medicines (ritonavir) and, with dabigatran, the heart rhythm drug dronedarone [18][19][20]. Others, such as rifampicin, carbamazepine and St John's wort, lower them, raising the clot risk [18][19].

**Supplements.** Some botanicals, such as garlic and ginkgo, can cause bleeding on their own and may add to warfarin's effect [17]; nattokinase may too, so do not combine it with a blood thinner unless your doctor agrees. Others, such as coenzyme Q10, St John's wort and ginseng, may weaken it [17]. Vitamin K2 (MK-7) is potent: in volunteers on a warfarin-type drug, 10 mcg a day lowered the INR enough to matter in some, and 45 mcg a day cut the average INR by about 40% [11]. Fish oil may have antiplatelet effects at high doses, though 3 to 6 g a day did not significantly change the INR in a small trial of people on warfarin [12][14]. Vitamin E supplements raised the risk of a bleeding stroke (relative risk 1.22) in a meta-analysis of trials in the general population [31], and turmeric raised the INR in a case report [32]. Each supplement has its own page: [vitamin K](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/vitamin-k-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [fish oil](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/omega-3-fish-oil-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [vitamin E](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/vitamin-e-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [turmeric](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/turmeric-curcumin-benefits-best-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [garlic](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/garlic-benefits-best-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [nattokinase](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/nattokinase-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects) and [ginkgo](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/ginkgo-biloba-benefits-best-forms-dosing-and-side-effects).

**Antiplatelets.** In a meta-analysis of trials in people who already had heart or artery disease, aspirin cut serious vascular events from 8.2% to 6.7% a year. In people without disease, the gain was small and had to be weighed against more major bleeding [10]. Clopidogrel's label warns that it works less well in some people for genetic reasons, and that stopping it early raises the risk of heart events [22].

**An anticoagulant plus aspirin?** In AUGUSTUS (4,614 people with AF after a heart attack or stent), aspirin raised major or clinically relevant bleeding from 9.0% to 16.1% without fewer heart events [8]. In AFIRE (2,236 people with AF and stable coronary disease), rivaroxaban alone prevented events as well as rivaroxaban plus an antiplatelet, with less major bleeding; the trial stopped early for more deaths on the combination [9]. The US guideline now recommends an anticoagulant alone, not with an antiplatelet, for AF with stable coronary disease beyond a year after a stent or bypass, unless a stent has clotted before [1]. In AQUATIC (872 people on an anticoagulant with stable coronary disease after a stent), adding aspirin raised deaths and major bleeding [37]. Recent stents are different, so do not stop aspirin or clopidogrel without your cardiologist.

**Procedures.** Your doctor or pharmacist will advise whether and when to pause a blood thinner, and when to restart. For the DOACs, the pause rests on the drug, your kidney function and the procedure's bleeding risk [1]; in the PAUSE study (3,007 people with AF), a standard plan built on these gave low rates of major bleeding and stroke [6]. Many people do not need "bridging" injections: in the BRIDGE trial of people with AF stopping warfarin, skipping bridging was as safe for clots and caused less major bleeding (1.3% vs 3.2%) [5]. US chest physicians strongly advise against bridging in AF, and for staying on warfarin when a pacemaker or defibrillator is fitted [4]. Mechanical valves and recent stroke are exceptions with their own plans [1]. Never stop on your own before a procedure.

## Brad's view (reviewed 2 Nov 2026)

- **No anticoagulant rule.** The Health Roadmap has no rule on which anticoagulant to use or how to manage it (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026); your doctor decides.
- **Aspirin.** Aspirin is for people after a heart attack, stroke or stent, as their doctor directs; the roadmap does not use it to prevent a first event (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). In his [aspirin post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/we-were-wrong-about-aspirin-new-evidence), Brad says not to stop prescribed aspirin because of his article; talk to your doctor first [27]. In his [deprescribing post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/when-your-meds-start-making-you-sick), he describes stopping aspirin in an older patient with no past heart attack: the bleeding risk outweighed the modest benefit [28].
- **Vitamin K2 and warfarin.** The roadmap's micronutrient card asks anyone taking warfarin (Coumadin, Marevan) or another vitamin K antagonist to check with their doctor or anticoagulation clinic before starting or stopping vitamin K2, because it can change the INR (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). In his [vitamin K2 trial post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/does-vitamin-k2-actually-protect-your-heart-new-trial), Brad calls for real caution with K2 on warfarin, and notes that this is a warfarin issue: aspirin and the newer blood thinners are not affected [25]. In a [later post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/why-arent-cardiologists-prescribing-vitamin-k2), he calls it the safety point he checks with every patient, and says some specialists advise avoiding K2 entirely on warfarin [26].
- **Fish oil.** The roadmap suggests up to 1 g a day of EPA and DHA combined, and says to check with your doctor first if you have atrial fibrillation or take a blood thinner (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). In his [omega-3 post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/omega-3-breakthrough-cuts-major-heart-events-by-43), Brad says he stays away from the higher doses linked to atrial fibrillation [29].

## Who needs extra care

- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Warfarin can harm an unborn baby; its label says not to use it in pregnancy, except for women with mechanical heart valves at high risk of clots [17]. The US atrial fibrillation guideline says DOACs should not be used in pregnancy or breastfeeding, because there are not enough data on their safety [1]; the apixaban label advises against breastfeeding [18]. Warfarin was not found in breast milk in a small study; its label weighs breastfeeding case by case [17]. If you could become pregnant, plan with your doctor before you conceive.
- **Older adults.** Bleeding risk rises with age, and NSAID and aspirin labels say the chance of stomach bleeding is higher from 60 [23][24]. Even so, NICE says not to withhold anticoagulation only because of age or a risk of falls [2].
- **Reduced kidney function.** Kidney function guides which blood thinner is chosen, and its dose [1][21]. Warfarin's label asks people with reduced kidney function to have the INR checked more often [17].
- **Liver disease.** Apixaban is not recommended in severe liver disease [18]; tell your doctor about any liver problem.
- **Mechanical heart valves.** Warfarin, not a DOAC: dabigatran must not be used, and apixaban is not recommended [1][18][20].
- **Antiphospholipid syndrome**, a clotting disorder. DOACs are not recommended in the triple-positive form; NICE uses heparin then warfarin for clots in that group [3][18].
- **Cancer.** Clots with active cancer need a plan weighing the tumour site, bleeding risk and cancer-drug interactions [3].
- **Under 18.** Children's treatment is different; talk to their doctor.

## When to see your doctor

You may bruise more easily on a blood thinner. These can be signs of bleeding: contact your doctor or get medical help right away [18].

- Unexpected bruising, bleeding gums, nosebleeds that happen often, or periods heavier than usual.
- Red, pink or brown urine.
- Unexpected pain, swelling or joint pain.

Call your doctor or anticoagulation clinic, not as an emergency, for:

- An INR out of range, or a missed or doubled dose of warfarin [16].
- If you are pregnant or planning a pregnancy: warfarin can harm a baby early in pregnancy, so contact your doctor promptly [17].
- Before starting or stopping any medicine or supplement, including vitamin K2, fish oil and herbal products (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026) [17].
- Before any surgery, dental work or procedure, in good time [18].
- For a regular review of your blood thinner and kidney function [1][2].

## Common questions

**Can I take vitamin K2 or eat natto on warfarin?** K2 supplements only with your clinic's agreement. Natto is very rich in MK-7, and researchers say people on warfarin should avoid it [36]. Even small amounts of MK-7 can lower the INR [11]. Keep your intake steady either way [13]. The newer tablets do not work through vitamin K [18][19][20][21].

**Does fish oil raise bleeding risk with Eliquis?** High doses can affect platelets. A prescription high-dose omega-3 (icosapent ethyl) raised bleeding in its main trial, more so with aspirin, clopidogrel or warfarin, and its label asks for bleeding checks with any blood thinner [35].

**Which supplements interact with apixaban or rivaroxaban?** St John's wort lowers their levels [18][19]. Supplements that affect platelets or clotting, such as garlic, ginkgo, nattokinase, turmeric, vitamin E and high-dose fish oil, may add to bleeding; the evidence comes mostly from warfarin [14][17][31][32]. Ask your pharmacist before starting one.

**Do I still need aspirin if I'm on a blood thinner?** Often not; it depends on why you take it (see "An anticoagulant plus aspirin?" above). Ask your cardiologist before stopping either.
