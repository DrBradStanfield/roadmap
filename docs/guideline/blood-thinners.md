# Blood Thinners: Warfarin, Eliquis and Other Anticoagulants

*Written from US and UK guidelines, FDA drug labels, NIH pages and primary trials, plus Brad's posts and the Health Roadmap algorithm. It gives no doses for these medicines: your doctor or pharmacist sets those.*

## In short

- "Blood thinner" covers two groups. Anticoagulants (warfarin, apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban, heparin) slow the clotting process. Antiplatelets (aspirin, clopidogrel) stop platelets clumping [15].
- Anticoagulants are used to cut the risk of stroke in atrial fibrillation, to treat and prevent clots in the legs or lungs, and with mechanical heart valves [15][17].
- For most people with atrial fibrillation, US and UK guidelines prefer the newer tablets (often called DOACs) over warfarin [1][2]. Warfarin is still the choice for mechanical heart valves [1].
- Bleeding is the main risk. Know the warning signs, and never stop a blood thinner on your own: stopping early raises the risk of a clot or stroke [18][21].
- Warfarin needs regular INR blood tests and a steady vitamin K intake. The newer tablets need no INR tests, but kidney function is checked [1][2][17].
- Many medicines and supplements interact, including NSAID pain relievers, aspirin, vitamin K2 and some herbs. Check with your pharmacist before adding anything [2][17][24].
- Brad's view: aspirin belongs after a heart attack, stroke or stent, as your doctor directs, not to prevent a first event; and if you take warfarin, check with your doctor or anticoagulation clinic before starting or stopping vitamin K2 (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

## Get help now

- For chest pain or signs of a stroke or bleeding in the brain (face drooping, arm or leg weakness or numbness, trouble speaking, vision loss, even if it passes, or a sudden severe headache), call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).
- On a blood thinner, for bleeding that is severe or will not stop, vomiting blood or vomit that looks like coffee grounds, coughing up blood, or black tar-like or red stools, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [18].
- On a blood thinner, after a fall or injury, especially a blow to the head, get checked by a doctor straight away; if a headache, dizziness or weakness follows, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [18].
- On a blood thinner, after a spinal or epidural injection or a spinal tap: for back pain, tingling, numbness or weakness in the legs or feet, or loss of bowel or bladder control, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [18].

## What it is

Blood thinners lower the chance of a new clot forming. They do not dissolve a clot you already have, but they can stop it growing [15].

**Anticoagulants** act on the clotting proteins in the blood [15]:

- **Warfarin** (brands include Coumadin, Jantoven and Marevan) is a vitamin K antagonist. It blocks the vitamin K-dependent steps the liver uses to make clotting factors [13][17].
- **Direct oral anticoagulants (DOACs)** block one clotting factor directly. Apixaban (Eliquis), rivaroxaban (Xarelto) and edoxaban (Savaysa, Lixiana) block factor Xa; dabigatran (Pradaxa) blocks thrombin [18][19][20][21].
- **Heparins**, such as enoxaparin, are injections, often used around surgery, in pregnancy or at the start of treatment [3][15].

**Antiplatelets** stop platelets clumping. Aspirin and clopidogrel (Plavix) are the common ones. They are taken mainly by people who have had a heart attack, a stroke, a stent or artery disease in the legs [15][22].

**Who takes an anticoagulant?** The main reasons, as the labels list them [17][18][19]:

- **Atrial fibrillation (AF).** An irregular heart rhythm in which clots can form in the heart and travel to the brain. In meta-analyses, AF goes with about 2.4 times the risk of stroke [1].
- **Deep vein thrombosis (DVT) or pulmonary embolism (PE).** A clot in a deep vein, usually in the leg, or one that has travelled to the lungs. Blood thinners treat it and lower the chance of another [3].
- **Prevention after hip or knee replacement**, for a set time [18][19].
- **Mechanical heart valves**, where warfarin is used [17][20].

## Your numbers

**INR (for warfarin).** The INR (international normalised ratio) measures how long your blood takes to clot, on a scale that is the same in every lab. The higher the number, the slower your blood clots. Warfarin is adjusted to keep the INR in a target range, most often 2 to 3 for atrial fibrillation and for clots in the legs or lungs [1][17]. Some mechanical heart valves need a higher range [17]. Your doctor or anticoagulation clinic sets your range and your dose. Tests are frequent at the start and after any change, then spaced out once the INR is steady [17].

The INR moves with new medicines, changes in diet, alcohol and illness [16][17]. Smoking goes with poorer INR control [1]. An INR below your range means less protection against clots; above it means a higher chance of bleeding [17]. If yours is out of range, your clinic will tell you what to do. Do not change your dose yourself.

**Kidney function (for DOACs).** The newer tablets need no INR test. Kidney function matters instead, because the kidneys clear these drugs [1]. NICE asks for the need for anticoagulation, and kidney function in people with reduced kidney function, to be reviewed at least once a year [2].

The Health Roadmap shows your eGFR (mL/min/1.73m²) in these bands (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026):

| eGFR | Roadmap label |
|---|---|
| 70 or more | Normal |
| 60 to 69 | Low normal |
| 45 to 59 | Mildly to moderately decreased |
| 30 to 44 | Moderately to severely decreased |
| 15 to 29 | Severely decreased |
| Below 15 | Kidney failure |

Drug labels judge kidney function differently. They use creatinine clearance, worked out from a formula such as Cockcroft-Gault, which is not the same number as the eGFR on your blood test [21]. Kidney function can change the choice of drug and its dose. This guide gives no doses: check with your doctor or pharmacist.

## What helps

**Take it exactly as prescribed.** Every DOAC label carries a boxed warning that stopping early, without another anticoagulant in its place, raises the risk of clots and stroke [18][21]. If you miss a dose of warfarin, do not double up the next day; call your doctor [16]. For a missed DOAC dose, follow your medicine leaflet or ask your pharmacist.

**Tell everyone who treats you.** That includes your dentist, pharmacist and anyone planning a procedure. They should talk to the doctor who prescribed it before any surgery or dental work [18].

**On warfarin, keep vitamin K steady.** Vitamin K reverses warfarin's effect, so sudden changes in how much you eat can push the INR up or down [13][17]. You do not need to avoid green vegetables. Eat a normal, balanced diet with a consistent amount of vitamin K, and avoid drastic changes, such as suddenly eating large amounts of leafy greens [17]. The [vitamin K page](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/vitamin-k-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects) covers foods and supplements.

**Lower the bleeding risks you can change.** NICE lists uncontrolled high blood pressure, poor INR control on warfarin, other medicines (antiplatelets, SSRI antidepressants and NSAIDs), harmful drinking and treatable causes of anaemia [2]. The [high blood pressure guide](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/high-blood-pressure) covers targets. On warfarin, ask your doctor how much alcohol is safe [16].

**Check before you add anything.** That means prescription medicines, over-the-counter pain relievers and supplements (see "Medicines" below) [2][17].

## Medicines

This guide gives no doses for warfarin or the DOACs, including in reduced kidney function.

**Warfarin.** It needs regular INR tests and has many drug and food interactions [1]. Its boxed warning is that it can cause major or fatal bleeding [17]. Rare but serious effects include death of skin or other tissue, and small cholesterol clots breaking off into the circulation; tell your doctor about painful or discoloured skin or toes [17]. Its effect can be reversed with vitamin K and, in serious bleeding, with a clotting-factor concentrate [1].

**DOACs (apixaban, rivaroxaban, dabigatran, edoxaban).** A meta-analysis of the four main AF trials (71,683 people) compared them with warfarin [7]:

- stroke or clots elsewhere in the body: relative risk 0.81
- bleeding in the brain: 0.48
- death from any cause: 0.90
- bleeding from the gut: 1.25, so somewhat more gut bleeding.

Their labels warn about stopping early and about bleeding into the spine after spinal or epidural procedures [18][21]. Dabigatran capsules commonly caused indigestion and other stomach upsets in trials, and must be kept in their original package to protect them from moisture [20]. Specific reversal agents exist: idarucizumab for dabigatran, and andexanet alfa for apixaban and rivaroxaban [18][20].

DOACs are not for everyone. Dabigatran must not be used with a mechanical heart valve, and apixaban is not recommended with one [18][20]. DOACs are not recommended in triple-positive antiphospholipid syndrome, a clotting disorder [3][18].

**Interactions that matter** [2][17][18]:

- **Other blood thinners and NSAIDs.** Aspirin, clopidogrel and NSAIDs such as ibuprofen and naproxen add to bleeding risk [18]. NSAID labels say the chance of serious stomach bleeding is higher if you take a blood thinner [24].
- **SSRI antidepressants** add to bleeding risk [2].
- **Warfarin's long list.** Many antibiotics, antifungals, heart drugs and herbal products change its effect [17]. Check every new medicine.
- **DOAC interactions.** Some drugs lower DOAC levels; St John's wort is one [18].

**Supplements.** Some botanicals, such as garlic and ginkgo, can cause bleeding on their own and may add to warfarin's effect. Others, such as coenzyme Q10, St John's wort and ginseng, may weaken it [17]. Vitamin K2 (MK-7) is potent: in healthy volunteers on a warfarin-type drug, 10 mcg a day lowered the INR enough to matter in some people, and 45 mcg a day cut the group's average INR by about 40% [11]. Fish oil may have antiplatelet effects at high doses, though 3 to 6 g a day did not significantly change the INR in a small trial of people on warfarin [12][14]. Each supplement has its own page: [vitamin K](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/vitamin-k-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [omega-3](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/omega-3-fish-oil-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [nattokinase](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/nattokinase-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects) and [ginkgo](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/ginkgo-biloba-benefits-best-forms-dosing-and-side-effects).

**Antiplatelets.** In a meta-analysis of trials in people who already had heart or artery disease, aspirin cut serious vascular events from 8.2% to 6.7% a year. In people without disease, the gain was small and had to be weighed against more major bleeding [10]. Clopidogrel is used after a heart attack, stroke or stent and in artery disease of the legs [22]. Its label warns that it works less well in people with two inactive copies of the CYP2C19 gene, and that it raises bleeding risk [22]. Stopping it early raises the risk of heart events [22].

**An anticoagulant plus aspirin?** Adding aspirin to an anticoagulant raises bleeding. In AUGUSTUS (4,614 people with AF after a heart attack or stent), aspirin raised major or clinically relevant bleeding from 9.0% to 16.1% without fewer heart events [8]. In AFIRE (2,236 people with AF and stable coronary disease), rivaroxaban alone was as good as rivaroxaban plus an antiplatelet at preventing events and caused less major bleeding; the trial stopped early because more people died on the combination [9]. The US guideline now recommends an anticoagulant alone, not with an antiplatelet, for AF with stable coronary disease beyond a year after a stent or bypass, unless a stent has clotted before [1]. Recent stents are different, so do not stop aspirin or clopidogrel without your cardiologist.

**Procedures.** Your doctor or pharmacist will advise whether, when and for how long to pause a blood thinner, and when to restart. For the DOACs, the US guideline bases the pause on the drug, your kidney function and the bleeding risk of the procedure [1]. In the PAUSE study of 3,007 people with AF on a DOAC, a standard plan built on those factors gave low rates of major bleeding and stroke [6]. Many people do not need "bridging" injections: in the BRIDGE trial of people with AF stopping warfarin, skipping bridging was as safe for clots and caused less major bleeding (1.3% vs 3.2%) [5]. US chest physicians strongly advise against bridging in AF [4]. Mechanical valves and recent stroke are exceptions with their own plans [1]. Never stop on your own before a procedure.

## Brad's view (reviewed 2 Nov 2026)

- **No anticoagulant rule.** The Health Roadmap has no rule on which anticoagulant to use or how to manage it (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). For those questions this guide summarises the guidelines, and your doctor decides.
- **Aspirin.** Aspirin is for people after a heart attack, stroke or stent, as their doctor directs; the roadmap does not use it to prevent a first event (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). In his [aspirin post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/we-were-wrong-about-aspirin-new-evidence), Brad says that if your doctor prescribed aspirin after a heart attack, stroke or stent, you should not stop it on the strength of his article, and should talk to your doctor first [27]. In his [deprescribing post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/when-your-meds-start-making-you-sick), he describes stopping aspirin in older patients with no past heart attack, because the bleeding risk outweighed the modest benefit [28].
- **Vitamin K2 and warfarin.** The roadmap's micronutrient card asks anyone taking warfarin (Coumadin, Marevan) or another vitamin K antagonist to check with their doctor or anticoagulation clinic before starting or stopping vitamin K2, because it can change the INR (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). In his [vitamin K2 trial post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/does-vitamin-k2-actually-protect-your-heart-new-trial), Brad calls for real caution with K2 on warfarin, and notes that this is a warfarin issue: aspirin and the newer blood thinners (apixaban, dabigatran, rivaroxaban) are not affected [25]. In a [later post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/why-arent-cardiologists-prescribing-vitamin-k2), he calls it the safety point he checks with every patient, and says some specialists advise avoiding K2 entirely on warfarin [26].
- **Fish oil.** The roadmap suggests up to 1 g a day of EPA and DHA combined, and says to check with your doctor first if you have atrial fibrillation or take a blood thinner (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). In his [omega-3 post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/omega-3-breakthrough-cuts-major-heart-events-by-43), Brad says he stays away from the higher doses linked to atrial fibrillation [29].

## Who needs extra care

- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Warfarin can harm an unborn baby; its label says not to use it in pregnancy, except for women with mechanical heart valves at high risk of clots [17]. The US atrial fibrillation guideline says DOACs should not be used in pregnancy or breastfeeding, because there are not enough data on their safety [1]; the apixaban label advises against breastfeeding [18]. If you could become pregnant, plan with your doctor before you conceive.
- **Older adults.** Bleeding risk rises with age, and NSAID and aspirin labels say the chance of stomach bleeding is higher from 60 [23][24]. Even so, NICE says not to withhold anticoagulation only because of age or a risk of falls [2].
- **Reduced kidney function.** Kidney function guides which blood thinner is chosen, and its dose [1][21]. Warfarin's label asks people with reduced kidney function to have the INR checked more often [17]. This guide gives no doses: check with your doctor or pharmacist.
- **Liver disease.** Apixaban is not recommended in severe liver disease [18]; tell your doctor about any liver problem.
- **Mechanical heart valves.** Warfarin, not a DOAC [1][18][20].
- **Antiphospholipid syndrome.** DOACs are not recommended in the triple-positive form; NICE uses heparin then warfarin for clots in that group [3][18].
- **Cancer.** Clots linked to active cancer need a plan that weighs the tumour site, bleeding risk and interactions with cancer drugs [3].
- **Under 18.** Children's treatment is different; talk to their doctor.

## When to see your doctor

Call your doctor or anticoagulation clinic soon (not an emergency) for [18]:

- Unexpected bruising, bleeding gums, nosebleeds that happen often, or periods heavier than usual.
- Red, pink or brown urine.
- Unexpected pain, swelling or joint pain.
- An INR out of range, or a missed or doubled dose of warfarin [16].
- Before starting or stopping any medicine or supplement, including vitamin K2, fish oil and herbal products (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026) [17].
- Before any surgery, dental work or procedure, in good time [18].
- If you are pregnant or planning a pregnancy [17].
- For a regular review of your blood thinner and kidney function [1][2].

## Common questions

**Can I take vitamin K2 or eat natto on warfarin?** Only with your anticoagulation clinic's agreement. MK-7, the form in natto and most K2 supplements, lowered the INR at doses as low as 10 mcg a day in some volunteers on a warfarin-type drug [11]. A lower INR means less protection against clots. Keep your intake steady either way [13]. The newer tablets work on clotting factors directly, not through vitamin K [18][19][20][21]. See the [vitamin K page](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/vitamin-k-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects).

**Does fish oil raise bleeding risk with Eliquis?** Fish oil can affect platelets at high doses [14]. Reviews and package inserts report no clinically significant bleeding from omega-3s, but the inserts for prescription omega-3s ask for periodic checks in people on anticoagulants [14]. Brad's roadmap says to check with your doctor first if you take a blood thinner (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). See the [omega-3 page](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/omega-3-fish-oil-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects).

**Which supplements interact with apixaban or rivaroxaban?** St John's wort can lower apixaban levels [18]. Supplements that affect platelets or clotting, such as garlic, ginkgo and nattokinase, may add to bleeding with any blood thinner [17]. Ask your pharmacist before starting one.

**How long must I stop before a procedure?** Your doctor or pharmacist will advise. It depends on the drug, your kidney function and how much the procedure could bleed [1][6]. Most people with AF do not need bridging injections [4][5]. Do not stop on your own.

**Do I still need aspirin if I'm on a blood thinner?** Often not, but it depends on why you take it. For AF with stable coronary disease, the US guideline recommends the anticoagulant alone, because adding aspirin raises bleeding [1][9]. After a recent stent the plan is different. Ask your cardiologist before stopping either.

**I have AF. Warfarin or a newer drug?** For most people, US and UK guidelines prefer a DOAC [1][2]. Against warfarin, DOACs lowered stroke, bleeding in the brain and death, with somewhat more gut bleeding [7]. Warfarin remains the choice with a mechanical valve or moderate to severe rheumatic mitral stenosis [1].

**How often is the INR checked, and what range?** Often at first, then less often once it is steady [17]. The usual range is 2 to 3 for AF and clots; some valves need higher [1][17]. Your clinic sets yours.

**Does nattokinase thin the blood?** It may affect clotting, and case reports describe serious bleeding, including with aspirin. Do not combine it with a blood thinner unless your doctor agrees. See the [nattokinase page](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/nattokinase-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects).

**I bruise easily on a blood thinner. Is that normal?** Some bruising can happen. Tell your doctor about bruising you cannot explain, bleeding gums, frequent nosebleeds or heavier periods [18]. For severe bleeding, see "Get help now".
