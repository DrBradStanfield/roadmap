# Exercise: Clinical Exercise Guidance

*Sources: AHA 2024 Scientific Statement on Resistance Exercise (Paluch et al, DOI: 10.1161/CIR.0000000000001189), US Physical Activity Guidelines 2018, WHO 2020, Schoenfeld et al meta-analyses, 2025 UK Biobank dose-response study, PURE study (Lear 2017)*

This guideline covers exercise prescription for cardiovascular health, mortality reduction, and disease prevention — organised by what the evidence actually shows rather than by generic guideline categories.

---

## Minimum Effective Dose

### Baseline guideline

**Guideline position:** The US Physical Activity Guidelines (2018) and WHO (2020) recommend ≥150 min/week of moderate-intensity aerobic activity OR ≥75 min/week of vigorous-intensity activity, plus ≥2 days/week of muscle-strengthening activities [Piercy 2018, WHO 2020].

**Brad's position:** Aligns as a minimum. The algorithm recommends "150+ min cardio + 2-3 resistance sessions/week" (`exercise` suggestion, always-show). However, recent evidence shows this understates the benefit of vigorous activity and overestimates the minimum dose needed for health benefits.

### Even small amounts matter

Recent evidence demonstrates that structured exercise is not required for significant mortality reduction:

**VILPA (Vigorous Intermittent Lifestyle Physical Activity):** A UK Biobank study of 25,000+ adults who did no formal exercise found that just 3-4 bouts of vigorous lifestyle activity per day (e.g., brisk stair climbing, carrying heavy shopping, playing with children — totalling ~6 min/day) was associated with 38-40% lower all-cause and cancer mortality, and 48-49% lower cardiovascular mortality [Stamatakis 2022].

**Exercise snacks:** Brief bouts of vigorous activity (1-2 min stair climbs, cycling sprints) yield significant improvements in VO2max and metabolic health, even without structured exercise sessions.

**Brad's position:** This evidence is clinically important for patients who say "I don't have time to exercise." Even small amounts of daily vigorous activity provide substantial mortality reduction. The chatbot should emphasise that something is dramatically better than nothing.

**References:**
- Stamatakis 2022, VILPA and mortality, Nature Medicine (DOI: 10.1038/s41591-022-02100-x)
- Piercy 2018, Physical Activity Guidelines for Americans, JAMA (DOI: 10.1001/jama.2018.14854)

## Cardiorespiratory Exercise

### Vigorous activity is far more effective than moderate

**Guideline position:** The US PAG 2018 equates 150 min moderate with 75 min vigorous (2:1 ratio). This is outdated.

**2025 UK Biobank evidence:** A wearable-based study found vigorous activity is 4-9x more effective than moderate activity — not 2x as traditionally assumed:
- All-cause mortality: vigorous is **4.1x** more effective per minute
- Heart disease mortality: **7.8x** more effective
- Type 2 diabetes prevention: **9.4x** more effective

This demolishes the simple 2:1 equivalency. 15 minutes of vigorous activity provides more health benefit than 60 minutes of moderate activity.

**Brad's position:** Aligns strongly. Patients able to exercise vigorously should be encouraged to do so — the returns per minute of effort are dramatically higher. However, moderate activity still provides substantial benefits and is appropriate for patients who cannot exercise vigorously (elderly, deconditioned, cardiovascular limitations).

**References:**
- 2025 UK Biobank vigorous dose-response study, Nature Communications (DOI: 10.1038/s41467-025-63475-2)

### Cardiorespiratory fitness predicts mortality

**VO2max as a predictor:** Cardiorespiratory fitness (measured by VO2max or metabolic equivalents) is one of the strongest predictors of all-cause mortality — stronger than smoking, diabetes, or hypertension as individual risk factors [Kodama 2009].

**PURE study:** In 130,000 people across 17 countries, higher physical activity consistently reduced all-cause mortality regardless of income level or country [Lear 2017].

**Brad's position:** Aligns. VO2max improvement should be a goal of exercise, not just "minutes per week." Vigorous interval training is the most time-efficient way to improve VO2max.

**References:**
- Kodama 2009, cardiorespiratory fitness and mortality, JAMA (DOI: 10.1001/jama.2009.681)
- Lear 2017, PURE study, physical activity and mortality, Lancet (DOI: 10.1016/S0140-6736(17)31634-3)

## Resistance Training Prescription

### Why resistance training is a cornerstone health intervention

Resistance training reduces:
- **All-cause mortality** by 15% (any RT), up to 27% at optimal dose (~60 min/week) [Shailendra 2022]
- **CVD mortality** by 19% [Shailendra 2022]
- **Cancer mortality** by 14% [Shailendra 2022]
- **Type 2 diabetes risk** — continuous improvement with dose, no plateau (L-shaped) [Momma 2022]

Muscular strength independently predicts **31% lower all-cause mortality** in a meta-analysis of ~2 million people [Garcia-Hermoso 2018].

The dose-response for health outcomes is **J-shaped**: most mortality benefits are captured in the first ~30-60 min/week of resistance training. Additional volume provides diminishing returns for mortality reduction but continues to benefit hypertrophy and strength [Momma 2022].

**Brad's position:** Resistance training should be treated as a first-line health intervention, not just a fitness pursuit. The algorithm recommends 2-3 resistance sessions per week (`exercise` suggestion).

### Volume (sets per muscle per week)

**Guideline position (Schoenfeld 2017 meta-analysis):** There is a graded dose-response relationship between weekly resistance training volume and muscle hypertrophy — more sets produce more growth. However, the curve shows steep initial gains that flatten quickly:
- 1-4 sets/muscle/week: ~64% of maximum hypertrophy gains
- 5-9 sets/muscle/week: ~84% of maximum gains
- 10+ sets/muscle/week: near-optimal for most people

For strength: 81% of strength gains come from just 1-4 sets per exercise per week. Volume matters less for strength than for hypertrophy [Schoenfeld 2019].

**Brad's position:** For health (not competitive bodybuilding), the minimum effective dose matters most. Even 1-4 sets/muscle/week provides substantial strength and hypertrophy benefits. The 2026 meta-regression (Pelland et al) confirmed diminishing returns for both hypertrophy and strength with increasing volume. Start patients at a manageable volume and progress gradually.

**Practical targets:**
- **Health-focused minimum:** 30-60 min/week total RT (captures most mortality benefit) [Momma 2022]
- **Hypertrophy-focused:** ~10 sets/muscle/week, split across 2+ sessions
- **Maintenance:** As little as 1/9 of building volume can maintain muscle mass for 8+ months [Bickel et al]

**References:**
- Schoenfeld 2017, volume dose-response meta-analysis, J Sports Sci (DOI: 10.1080/02640414.2016.1210197)
- Schoenfeld 2019, volume and hypertrophy vs strength RCT, Med Sci Sports Exerc (DOI: 10.1249/MSS.0000000000001764)
- Pelland 2026, resistance training dose-response meta-regressions, Sports Med (DOI: 10.1007/s40279-025-02344-w)
- Shailendra 2022, resistance training and mortality, Am J Prev Med (DOI: 10.1016/j.amepre.2022.03.020)
- Momma 2022, muscle-strengthening and NCD risk, Br J Sports Med (DOI: 10.1136/bjsports-2021-105061)

### Frequency

**Guideline position (Schoenfeld 2016):** Training each major muscle group at least 2x/week produces superior hypertrophy compared to 1x/week.

**Updated evidence (Schoenfeld 2019):** When total weekly volume is equated, training frequency does not significantly impact hypertrophy. Individuals can select frequency based on preference and schedule, provided total volume is maintained.

**Brad's position:** Aligns. The algorithm recommends "2-3 resistance sessions/week." The key message for patients is: split your weekly volume across at least 2 sessions per muscle group. Whether that's a full-body routine 3x/week or an upper/lower split 4x/week matters less than consistency and total volume.

**References:**
- Schoenfeld 2016, training frequency and hypertrophy, Sports Med (DOI: 10.1007/s40279-016-0543-8)
- Schoenfeld 2019, frequency and hypertrophy when volume equated, J Sports Sci (DOI: 10.1080/02640414.2018.1555906)

### Load and rep ranges

**Guideline position (Schoenfeld 2017, 2021):** Muscle hypertrophy occurs across a wide range of loads — from light (<60% 1RM) to heavy (>60% 1RM) — when sets are taken close to failure. The traditional "rep continuum" (heavy = strength, moderate = hypertrophy, light = endurance) is an oversimplification. Heavy loads (>60% 1RM) do specifically optimise 1-rep max strength.

**Brad's position:** This is clinically important because it means patients can build muscle with whatever equipment and loads they have access to. Bodyweight exercises, resistance bands, light dumbbells, and machines can all stimulate hypertrophy when performed with sufficient effort (close to failure). This removes the barrier of needing heavy weights or gym access.

**References:**
- Schoenfeld 2017, low vs high load meta-analysis, J Strength Cond Res (DOI: 10.1519/JSC.0000000000002200)
- Schoenfeld 2021, repetition continuum re-examination, Sports (DOI: 10.3390/sports9020032)

### Proximity to failure

**Evidence (Grgic 2022):** Training to complete muscular failure is not required for strength or hypertrophy gains. However, sets should be performed within 1-3 reps of failure (RPE 7-9) for adequate stimulus. In trained individuals, training to failure may provide a modest hypertrophy advantage.

**Brad's position:** For most patients, stopping 1-2 reps short of failure is sufficient and reduces injury risk and recovery demands. Patients new to resistance training should learn proper form before increasing intensity.

**References:**
- Grgic 2022, training to failure meta-analysis, J Sport Health Sci (DOI: 10.1016/j.jshs.2021.01.007)

### Rest periods

**Evidence:** 2-3 minutes between sets allows greater volume and performance per set, optimising hypertrophy stimulus. Shorter rest periods (30-60 seconds) can be used when time is limited but may reduce per-set performance.

**Brad's position:** For health-focused patients, rest periods are flexible. Longer rest enables more effective sets. For time-constrained patients, antagonist supersets (e.g., chest press followed by rows) allow productive work during rest periods without reducing performance.

## Power Training

### Muscle power predicts mortality better than strength

**2025 Mayo Clinic study:** In a study of 4,000 people followed for 10 years, muscle power (the speed of force generation) predicted mortality better than muscle strength alone. The ability to generate force quickly — assessed by tests like sit-to-stand speed and stair climbing — declines faster with age than absolute strength.

**Brad's position:** Exercise programmes should include power-oriented movements, not just slow, controlled lifts. Practical examples: brisk sit-to-stands, stair climbing at pace, medicine ball throws, jump squats (for those able). This is especially important for older adults at risk of falls — power determines the ability to recover balance.

**Functional preservation:** A 2025 ACSM study found that 70-year-olds who resistance train maintain physical function (sit-to-stand, stair climbing) at levels comparable to untrained 20-year-olds. This challenges the "inevitable decline" narrative.

**Algorithm link:** The algorithm's `exercise` suggestion recommends resistance training but does not specifically mention power training. This is a candidate for enhancement.

**References:**
- Araújo 2025, muscle power vs strength as mortality predictor, Mayo Clin Proc 100(8):1319-1331 (DOI: 10.1016/j.mayocp.2025.02.015)

## Exercise for Specific Conditions

### Blood pressure

**Guideline position:** The 2025 AHA/ACC blood pressure guideline recommends exercise for BP management. A 2024 meta-analysis (84 RCTs) found that isometric exercise (wall squats, planks, isometric handgrip) may produce the strongest blood pressure reductions — approximately 7.5 mmHg systolic reduction, with wall squats showing outsized effects in some studies.

**Brad's position:** Aligns. Isometric exercise is underutilised for blood pressure management. Wall squats (4 × 2 min holds with 2 min rest, 3x/week) are a simple, equipment-free intervention. This complements the algorithm's `low-salt` and `high-potassium` suggestions. Aerobic exercise also reduces BP (~5-7 mmHg) but isometric may be more effective per minute of effort.

**Algorithm link:** BP suggestions (`bp-stage1`, `bp-stage2`). Exercise is mentioned in stage 1 text but isometric training is not specifically recommended — candidate for enhancement.

**References:**
- 2024 Nature Reviews, isometric exercise and blood pressure meta-analysis (DOI: 10.1038/s41440-024-01998-9)

### Knee osteoarthritis

**Guideline position (2025 BMJ meta-analysis, 217 RCTs, 15,684 participants):** Aerobic exercise (walking, cycling, swimming) is the most effective exercise modality for knee osteoarthritis pain and function. This represents a paradigm shift from the old "rest and protect" approach. Low physical activity actually increases arthritis risk (U-shaped relationship) — sedentary behaviour worsens joint health.

**Brad's position:** Aligns strongly. Patients with knee OA should be encouraged to exercise, not avoid it. Walking and cycling are first-line interventions. Resistance training also helps by strengthening the muscles supporting the joint. The chatbot should counter the common misconception that exercise worsens arthritis.

**References:**
- 2025 BMJ, exercise for knee osteoarthritis network meta-analysis (DOI: 10.1136/bmj-2025-085242)

### Type 2 diabetes (HbA1c)

**Evidence (Jansson 2022):** Resistance training reduces HbA1c by a weighted mean of **-0.39%** (20 RCTs, n=1,172), comparable to aerobic training. Greater strength gains predict greater HbA1c reductions. RT and aerobic exercise are equally effective — the best exercise for diabetes is whichever the patient will do consistently.

**Brad's position:** Aligns. The algorithm's HbA1c suggestions (`hba1c-prediabetic`, `hba1c-normal`) mention lifestyle changes but don't specifically recommend resistance training for glycaemic control. This evidence supports adding RT as a specific recommendation for patients with prediabetes or diabetes.

**Algorithm link:** `hba1c-prediabetic`, `hba1c-diabetic`. Candidate for adding specific RT recommendation.

**References:**
- Jansson 2022, RT and HbA1c meta-analysis, BMJ Open Diabetes Res Care (DOI: 10.1136/bmjdrc-2021-002595)

### Bone density (osteoporosis prevention)

**Evidence (Massini 2022):** Resistance training improves bone mineral density at the hip (+0.64%) and spine (+0.62%) in older adults. Typical effective protocol: 4-5 exercises (upper + lower body), 2-3 sets of 8-12 reps at 70-90% 1RM, 3x/week, for ≥12 weeks.

**Brad's position:** Aligns. RT is a key non-pharmacological intervention for osteoporosis prevention, particularly important for postmenopausal women and older men. The algorithm's `screening-dexa` suggestion triggers bone density screening but does not specifically link to RT as an intervention.

**References:**
- Massini 2022, RT and bone mineral density meta-analysis, Healthcare (DOI: 10.3390/healthcare10061129)

### Cognitive function

**Evidence (Coelho-Junior 2022):** Resistance training improves cognitive function in both cognitively healthy and cognitively impaired older adults (SMD = 0.40). Improvements in working memory (0.44), verbal learning/memory (3.01), and spatial memory (0.63). Effective protocol: ≥12 weeks, 2-3x/week, ~45 min/session.

**Brad's position:** Aligns. This complements the COSMOS trial evidence for multivitamin/mineral supplementation and cognitive function. Exercise + adequate nutrition is the strongest non-pharmacological strategy for cognitive preservation.

**References:**
- Coelho-Junior 2022, RT and cognitive function meta-analysis, Aging Ment Health (DOI: 10.1080/13607863.2020.1857691)

### Mental health (depression and anxiety)

**Evidence:** Resistance training significantly alleviates depressive symptoms (effect size ~-0.94 from 29 RCTs) and reduces anxiety. In older adults (≥60 years): depression SMD -1.13, anxiety SMD -1.33. RT is at least as effective as many other lifestyle interventions for depression.

**Brad's position:** Aligns. Exercise (both aerobic and resistance) should be recommended as a first-line intervention for mild-moderate depression and anxiety, alongside other treatments. The algorithm does not currently have mental health suggestions — this is an area for potential expansion.

## The Plaque Paradox

### High-volume exercise and coronary plaque

**2026 Master@Heart study (Circulation):** High-volume, high-intensity endurance athletes (marathon runners, cyclists) have approximately **6x more coronary artery plaque** than low-volume exercisers, as measured by wearable-verified exercise data. However, despite the increased plaque burden, high-volume exercisers have the **lowest all-cause mortality**.

**Brad's position:** This finding reinforces the case for aggressive LDL-C and ApoB lowering — not for reducing exercise. High-volume exercise is strongly protective for mortality, but the increased plaque burden highlights that even very fit individuals are not immune to atherosclerosis. The solution is to keep exercising AND address atherogenic lipoproteins aggressively (Brad's target: LDL-C <55 mg/dL for essentially everyone). Exercise cannot outrun a high ApoB. Patients should not reduce exercise volume because of plaque concerns — they should ensure their lipids are optimally managed alongside their training.

High-volume endurance athletes with symptoms (chest pain, palpitations) should still be evaluated.

**References:**
- 2026 Master@Heart study, Circulation (DOI: 10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.125.077117)

## Protein Requirements for Exercise

### The dose-response for protein and muscle

Exercise science research often cites 1.6 g/kg/day as the target for maximising muscle protein synthesis during resistance training (Morton 2018, BJSM). However, a newer meta-analysis found the dose-response for lean body mass **rapidly diminishes after 1.3 g/kg/day** — most muscle benefits are captured below 1.6 g/kg [DOI: 10.1093/nutrit/nuaa104].

For strength specifically, the threshold is even lower — 81% of strength gains come from just 1-4 sets per exercise per week, and protein supplementation beyond ~1.2 g/kg shows minimal additional strength benefit.

### Source matters as much as quantity

The mortality data strongly favours plant protein sources (legumes, nuts, pea protein) over animal protein (especially red meat and dairy). High animal protein in under-65s was associated with 74% higher all-cause mortality and 4x cancer mortality via IGF-1/mTOR activation (Longo 2014). Plant protein was not associated with these risks. Fish protein is the exception among animal sources — associated with lower mortality. See the Diet guideline's Feature 4 section for the full evidence.

### Brad's position

**Target: 1.2 g/kg of ideal body weight per day.** Brad previously targeted 1.6 g/kg but reduced to 1.2 g/kg because the evidence doesn't show meaningful additional benefit above ~1.3 g/kg for most people. And 1.2 g/kg is already a challenging target to achieve from primarily plant-based sources — which is where the protein should come from.

**Source:** Prioritise plant-based proteins — legumes, nuts, and pea protein powder (low in methionine, the amino acid Longo's research flags as promoting cancer growth). Fish is the best animal source. Limit red and processed meat.

**Resistance training is essential:** Protein intake without resistance training shows minimal muscle benefits. The combination of RT + adequate protein is the most potent non-pharmacological strategy against sarcopenia.

**CKD adjustment:** Algorithm reduces to 1.0 g/kg for eGFR < 45.

**Algorithm link:** `protein-target` suggestion. See the Diet guideline's Feature 4 for full protein evidence including cancer, kidney, and source data.
