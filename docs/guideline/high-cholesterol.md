# High Cholesterol: LDL, ApoB, Statins and Lowering It

*This guide educates; it is not personal medical advice. Your own doctor knows your history; if this guide and your Health Roadmap plan disagree, the plan wins.*

## In short

- LDL particles cause the plaque behind most heart attacks and strokes. The evidence for cause, not just association, comes from genetics, cohorts and many drug trials [Borén 2020].
- ApoB counts those particles directly. It is the best single blood marker, then non-HDL cholesterol, then LDL cholesterol [Sniderman 2019].
- Lowering LDL lowers risk in proportion to how far it falls: each 1 mmol/L (39 mg/dL) drop is linked to about 20% fewer cardiovascular events [ESC/EAS 2019].
- Diet helps but has a ceiling. Statins are the first medicine; ezetimibe, bempedoic acid and PCSK9 drugs add more lowering.
- Most muscle aches people feel on a statin also happen on a placebo [CTT 2022; Howard 2021].
- Test Lp(a) once. It is mostly inherited and adds risk at any LDL [Kronenberg 2022].
- Brad's view: aim low and start early. The plan's targets are ApoB below 0.5 g/L, LDL below 1.4 mmol/L and non-HDL below 1.6 mmol/L, lower than most guidelines set for people without heart disease (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

## Get help now

- Chest pain, tightness or squeezing, especially if it spreads to your arm, neck or jaw, or comes with breathlessness, sweating or nausea: call your emergency number now (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK). Do not drive yourself [NHS heart attack; Gulati 2021].
- Signs of stroke such as face drooping, arm weakness or slurred speech, even if they pass within minutes: call your emergency number now (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [NHS stroke].
- Sudden, severe belly pain that spreads to your back, especially if your triglycerides are known to be very high: call your emergency number or go to the emergency department now (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK). This can be acute pancreatitis [NHS pancreatitis].

## What it is

Cholesterol is a fat your body needs for cell walls and hormones. In the blood it travels inside particles called lipoproteins.

- **LDL, VLDL, remnants and Lp(a)** each carry one ApoB protein. They can enter the artery wall and get stuck there, which starts plaque [Borén 2020; Sniderman 2019].
- **HDL** carries no ApoB, which is why non-HDL cholesterol leaves it out [Sniderman 2019].
- **Triglycerides** are a different fat, carried mostly in VLDL. Very high levels raise the risk of pancreatitis [Nordestgaard 2016].

"High cholesterol" usually means too many ApoB particles. It causes no symptoms, and plaque can grow silently for decades [Ibanez 2021]. In the PESA study of 4,184 middle-aged adults without symptoms, about half of those with no standard risk factors already had plaque or coronary calcium, and the odds rose with each 10 mg/dL of LDL [Fernández-Friera 2017; Ibanez 2021].

**Common causes.** Diet and genes set most of your level. Before treatment, guidelines check for causes that raise lipids on their own, such as heavy drinking, uncontrolled diabetes, an underactive thyroid, liver disease and nephrotic syndrome [NICE NG238].

**Familial hypercholesterolaemia (FH)** is an inherited condition that keeps LDL high from birth. About 1 in 311 people have it [Hu 2020]. An LDL above 5 mmol/L (190 mg/dL) should prompt the question [Nordestgaard 2016]. If you have FH, ask your doctor whether your close relatives should be tested.

## Your numbers

These are the Health Roadmap plan's lines. The plan grades in mmol/L and g/L; mg/dL figures are shown beside them. A lipid panel does not need fasting for most people [Nordestgaard 2016].

**The plan uses one marker, the best you have:** ApoB first, then non-HDL, then LDL. Non-HDL is total cholesterol minus HDL.

**On-treatment targets** (the value should be below these):

| Marker | Target |
|---|---|
| ApoB | below 0.5 g/L (50 mg/dL) |
| LDL cholesterol | below 1.4 mmol/L (55 mg/dL) |
| Non-HDL cholesterol | below 1.6 mmol/L (62 mg/dL) |

The LDL and non-HDL targets are also the plan's "Optimal" line. A value at the target counts as above it.

**Bands above the target:**

| Marker | Borderline from | High from | Very high from |
|---|---|---|---|
| ApoB | 0.5 g/L (50 mg/dL) | 0.7 g/L (70 mg/dL) | 1.0 g/L (100 mg/dL) |
| LDL | 3.36 mmol/L (130 mg/dL) | 4.14 mmol/L (160 mg/dL) | 4.91 mmol/L (190 mg/dL) |
| Non-HDL | 4.14 mmol/L (160 mg/dL) | 4.91 mmol/L (190 mg/dL) | 5.69 mmol/L (220 mg/dL) |
| Total cholesterol | 5.17 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) | 6.21 mmol/L (240 mg/dL) | none |

Between the target and Borderline, LDL and non-HDL read "Above optimal". ApoB has no such band: its Borderline starts at its target.

**HDL:** low below 1.03 mmol/L (40 mg/dL) in men and below 1.29 mmol/L (50 mg/dL) in women. With no sex on record the plan grades no HDL.

**Triglycerides:** borderline from 1.69 mmol/L (150 mg/dL), high from 2.26 mmol/L (200 mg/dL), very high from 5.65 mmol/L (500 mg/dL). The plan marks very high triglycerides urgent: they carry a risk of pancreatitis, and its card says this needs immediate medical attention.

**Lp(a):** normal below 75 nmol/L (about 30 mg/dL); borderline 75 to 125 nmol/L; elevated from 125 nmol/L (about 50 mg/dL). NZ, Australian and UK labs often print mg/L, a tenth of mg/dL. The conversion between mass and particle units is approximate.

## What helps

These steps lower ApoB particles and protect the heart in other ways. They work with medicines, not instead of them.

- **Swap saturated fat for unsaturated fat.** Butter, fatty meat and coconut oil for olive oil, nuts, seeds and oily fish. In a Cochrane review of long-term trials, cutting saturated fat lowered combined cardiovascular events (risk ratio 0.83), with little or no effect on deaths [Hooper 2020]. See the diet guideline.
- **Eat the cholesterol-lowering foods together.** In a controlled feeding trial, a "portfolio" of plant sterols, soy protein, viscous fibre and almonds lowered LDL by 28.6%, against 30.9% with lovastatin 20 mg [Jenkins 2003]. Eaten at home for six months, the drop was about 13% to 14% [Jenkins 2011].
- **Soluble fibre.** Psyllium at a median of about 10 g a day lowered LDL by 0.33 mmol/L and ApoB by 0.05 g/L across 28 trials [Jovanovski 2018]. Oats, beans and lentils add more.
- **Move, and stop smoking.** Guidelines encourage physical activity and quitting smoking for everyone, whatever their risk [Nelson 2024]. See the exercise guideline.
- **For high triglycerides,** weight loss, less alcohol and better blood sugar control can bring levels down a long way [NZ Ministry of Health 2018].

**Supplements.** Brad's supplement entries cover cholesterol-lowering supplements, psyllium, red yeast rice and berberine. NICE does not advise plant stanols or sterols to prevent heart disease [NICE NG238].

## Medicines

Medicines are chosen by your overall risk, your LDL or ApoB, and how far it needs to fall. Doses below are from US prescribing information; your doctor sets yours.

### Statins (first line)

Statins block cholesterol production in the liver. Across 26 trials, each 1 mmol/L drop in LDL lowered major vascular events (rate ratio 0.78) and all-cause deaths by 10%, with no rise in cancer [CTT 2010].

- Common choices: atorvastatin calcium 10 to 80 mg once daily [Lipitor label]; rosuvastatin calcium 5 to 40 mg once daily [Crestor label]; pravastatin, pitavastatin and simvastatin.
- Approximate LDL lowering: rosuvastatin 5 mg or atorvastatin 20 mg about 40%; rosuvastatin 20 mg about 55%; rosuvastatin 40 mg about 63% [NZ Ministry of Health 2018].
- Each step up in dose adds less LDL lowering than the first [NZ Ministry of Health 2018], and the most intensive regimens carry slightly more muscle symptoms [CTT 2022].

**Muscle symptoms.** In 19 double-blind trials, muscle pain or weakness was reported by 27.1% of people on a statin and 26.6% on placebo. Only about 1 in 15 of the reports in the first year were caused by the statin, and after year 1 there was no clear excess [CTT 2022]. In the SAMSON trial, 60 people who had quit statins took statin, placebo and empty months in random order: symptoms were the same on statin and placebo, and half were back on a statin six months later [Howard 2021]. True muscle damage is rare. The labels list age 65 or over, an underactive thyroid, kidney impairment, some interacting drugs and higher doses as risk factors, and advise reporting unexplained muscle pain, tenderness or weakness promptly [Crestor label; Lipitor label]. If symptoms persist, options include a lower dose, a different statin, or stopping and trying again once symptoms settle [NZ Ministry of Health 2018].

**Diabetes.** Statins raise the chance of developing type 2 diabetes a little (odds ratio 1.09, about one extra case per 255 people treated for four years), far less than the heart attacks they prevent [Sattar 2010].

**Interactions.** Large amounts of grapefruit juice (over 1.2 litres a day) and several drugs raise statin levels [Lipitor label]. Tell your pharmacist about every medicine and supplement.

### Ezetimibe

Ezetimibe blocks cholesterol absorption in the gut. The dose is 10 mg once daily [Zetia label]. Added to simvastatin after a recent heart attack or unstable angina, it lowered LDL to 1.4 mmol/L and reduced the main composite outcome over seven years (32.7% against 34.7%), with similar side effects [IMPROVE-IT]. A moderate-dose statin plus ezetimibe was as good as a high-dose statin over three years, got more people under 1.8 mmol/L, and fewer people stopped or cut the dose because of side effects (4.8% against 8.2%) [RACING 2022]. In a Swedish registry, ezetimibe added within 12 weeks of a heart attack was linked to fewer later events than late or no ezetimibe [Leosdottir 2025].

### Bempedoic acid

Bempedoic acid blocks liver cholesterol production earlier in the pathway statins act on. The dose is 180 mg once daily [Nexletol label]. In people who could not take statins, it lowered major cardiovascular events (11.7% against 13.3%) over about 40 months, with more gout and gallstones [CLEAR Outcomes]. The label warns of raised uric acid and tendon rupture, and limits simvastatin to 20 mg and pravastatin to 40 mg alongside it [Nexletol label].

### PCSK9 drugs

- **Evolocumab** and alirocumab are injections. On top of a statin, evolocumab lowered LDL by 59% [FOURIER]. Evolocumab is 140 mg every 2 weeks or 420 mg once a month [Repatha label]. In people with heart disease it cut LDL to a median 0.78 mmol/L and lowered events (9.8% against 11.3%) with no rise in diabetes or memory problems [FOURIER]. In people with atherosclerosis or diabetes but no previous heart attack or stroke, it lowered first major events over five years (6.2% against 8.0%) [VESALIUS-CV].
- **Inclisiran** is an injection given at 0 and 3 months, then every 6 months (284 mg) [Leqvio label]. It lowered LDL by about half in two trials [ORION-10 and ORION-11]. Those trials measured LDL, not heart attacks.
- **Enlicitide** is a PCSK9 tablet, approved in the US in July 2026. It lowers LDL about as much as the injections but is not yet shown to prevent heart attacks ([Lp(a) post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/lpa-cholesterol-study-shocks-cardiologists-now-what)).

**Is very low LDL safe?** Trials that pushed LDL below 0.8 mmol/L (30 mg/dL) have shown continued benefit and no clear short-term harm [Karagiannis 2021; FOURIER]. Whether it is linked to new diabetes, or possibly haemorrhagic stroke, is still debated, and long-term data are needed [Karagiannis 2021].

### Triglyceride medicines

For very high triglycerides, weight, alcohol and blood sugar come first. Fibrates lower triglycerides, and limited trial evidence suggests they may reduce heart events. Levels of 11 mmol/L or more may need treatment whatever the overall risk, because of pancreatitis [NZ Ministry of Health 2018].

## Brad's view (reviewed 2 Nov 2026)

**Targets.** Brad's plan aims for ApoB below 0.5 g/L (50 mg/dL), LDL below 1.4 mmol/L (55 mg/dL) and non-HDL below 1.6 mmol/L (62 mg/dL) (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). For ApoB that is lower than the ESC/EAS 2019 goal of below 65 mg/dL for very high risk, based on the Ez-PAVE trial of LDL targets and the PESA study (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). Brad notes it was run only in South Korea, and only 60.8% of the intensive group reached the target ([cholesterol war post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/the-cholesterol-war-is-over-heres-who-won)). He aims for an LDL below 55 mg/dL himself because PESA showed plaque building even at an LDL of 60 mg/dL ([statin panic post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/the-30-year-statin-panic-is-finally-over)). With his patients, he has an open discussion about aiming for 55 rather than 70 ([cholesterol war post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/the-cholesterol-war-is-over-heres-who-won)).

**Measure ApoB.** The plan shows only the best marker you have: ApoB, then non-HDL, then LDL (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). Brad would rather people knew their ApoB than their LDL, because ApoB is a particle count and the count does the damage ([heart attacks post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/soon-well-forget-what-heart-attacks-are)).

**The order of medicines.** Brad's steps are a low-dose statin, then ezetimibe, then a higher statin dose or a stronger statin, then a PCSK9 inhibitor; never skip ezetimibe to go straight to a high dose (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). After ezetimibe, the plan also suggests bempedoic acid, alongside the dose step (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). His preferred starting statins are low-dose water-soluble ones: pravastatin 20 mg, rosuvastatin 5 mg or pitavastatin 1 to 2 mg (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). He holds that a low-dose statin plus ezetimibe beats a high-dose statin alone, with fewer side effects (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). He takes a low-dose statin himself because most of the lowering comes at low doses while muscle risk climbs at high doses ([statin panic post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/the-30-year-statin-panic-is-finally-over)): pravastatin 20 mg at night plus ezetimibe 10 mg, though young and healthy, to prevent plaque as he ages ([ezetimibe post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/ezetimibe-is-the-most-underrated-weapon-against-heart-disease)).

**Side effects.** Brad's reading is that most muscle symptoms on statins are nocebo, true muscle injury is rare, and the small diabetes risk is outweighed by fewer heart attacks for people at raised risk ([statin panic post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/the-30-year-statin-panic-is-finally-over)). He argues the biggest risk for most people is stopping a statin, or never starting, because of something they read online ([statin panic post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/the-30-year-statin-panic-is-finally-over)).

**Diet first, never diet alone.** He advises most patients to follow a Mediterranean diet, and notes that diet's LDL lowering has a ceiling that leaves a gap for most people at meaningful risk ([statin panic post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/the-30-year-statin-panic-is-finally-over)). The plan's food card lists oats, walnuts, almonds, ground flaxseed, edamame and extra-virgin olive oil, plus psyllium husk, and it stays on alongside medicines (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Lp(a).** The plan follows the 2026 ACC/AHA advice to measure Lp(a) at least once in all adults (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). When it is high, the plan drives every other risk factor down: lipids, blood pressure, blood sugar and weight (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). Brad aims for ApoB below 0.5 g/L in that case and is quicker to add a PCSK9 inhibitor, which is not licensed to lower Lp(a) ([Lp(a) post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/lpa-cholesterol-study-shocks-cardiologists-now-what)).

**Calcium scores.** If your risk factors are already controlled, Brad sees no reason for a coronary calcium score, because it would not change anything ([heart attacks post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/soon-well-forget-what-heart-attacks-are)).

**When every step is taken.** If you take, or cannot tolerate, a statin, ezetimibe, bempedoic acid and a PCSK9 inhibitor, have tried or ruled out a higher dose, and are still above target, the plan shows no further card. Brad's reason: nothing else can clinically be done (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

## Who needs extra care

- **Pregnancy and planning pregnancy.** The FDA removed its strongest warning against statins in pregnancy in 2021 but still advises most people to stop once they know they are pregnant; an exposure before pregnancy is recognised is unlikely to harm the baby [FDA 2021]. NICE is stricter: stop statins 3 months before trying to conceive [NICE NG238]. Talk to your doctor before trying.
- **Breastfeeding.** US labels advise against breastfeeding on a statin [Crestor label; Lipitor label]. Ask your doctor whether to pause the statin or keep it and not breastfeed [FDA 2021].
- **Reduced kidney function.** Kidney impairment raises the risk of statin muscle problems, and the rosuvastatin label limits the dose in severe impairment [Crestor label]. Check with your doctor or pharmacist about the right statin and dose.
- **People of Asian ancestry** reach higher rosuvastatin levels; the label advises starting at 5 mg once daily [Crestor label].
- **Age 75 and over.** Statins lower major vascular events at every age, but the direct evidence is thinner after 75 in people without known artery disease [CTT 2019]. The USPSTF calls the evidence insufficient for starting a statin after 75 in people without heart disease [USPSTF 2022]. Frailty, other illnesses and other medicines shape the choice.
- **Under 18.** The Health Roadmap plan is for adults (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). A child or teenager with high cholesterol, or a family history of FH, should see their doctor.
- **People on many medicines.** Gemfibrozil, ciclosporin and several antiviral drugs raise rosuvastatin levels [Crestor label]. Ask your pharmacist.

## When to see your doctor

**Same day (in the UK, NHS 111):**
- Severe muscle pain, cramps or weakness on a statin, especially with a fever or red or brown urine. This can be muscle damage [NHS atorvastatin].
- Yellow skin or eyes on a statin [NHS atorvastatin].
- Triglycerides of 5.65 mmol/L (500 mg/dL) or more. The plan marks this urgent and says it needs immediate medical attention (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Book a visit:**
- You have never had a lipid panel, or not for five years (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
- Your LDL is 4.91 mmol/L (190 mg/dL) or more, or a close relative had a heart attack or stroke young. Ask about FH.
- You have never had ApoB or Lp(a) measured (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
- You are on treatment and above target, or a side effect makes you want to stop. Do not stop on your own; there are options.
- You are planning a pregnancy while on a statin.

**Recheck.** New Zealand advice is a non-fasting check at 6- to 12-month intervals until the agreed target is reached, then once a year [NZ Ministry of Health 2018].

## Where guidelines differ

Brad's plan uses one set of targets for every adult on treatment. Most guidelines tie the decision to treat, and the target, to your calculated risk.

| | Risk tool | Start a statin (no heart disease) | LDL goal, no heart disease | LDL goal with heart disease |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Brad's plan** | None; uses the lipid marker | Suggests a statin when the marker is at or above target | Below 1.4 mmol/L (55 mg/dL); ApoB below 0.5 g/L | Same |
| **New Zealand** (2018) | 5-year risk calculator | Strongly advised at 15% or more; discuss at 5% to 15%; also if total to HDL ratio is 8 or more | 40% or more reduction (5% to 15% risk); below 1.8 mmol/L at 15% or more | Drug treatment advised; targets of about 1.6 to 1.8 mmol/L |
| **Australia** (2023) | Aus CVD Risk calculator, 5-year | Prescribe at 10% or more; consider at 5% to under 10% | Not set in the summary | Not covered (primary prevention guideline) |
| **United States** (ACC/AHA 2026; USPSTF 2022) | 10-year risk | USPSTF: ages 40 to 75 with a risk factor and risk of 10% or more; selectively at 7.5% to under 10% | Below 70 mg/dL (1.8 mmol/L) at high risk; below 100 mg/dL (2.6 mmol/L) at borderline or intermediate risk | Very high risk: below 55 mg/dL (1.4 mmol/L), non-HDL below 85 mg/dL, and a 50% or more reduction |
| **United Kingdom** (NICE 2023) | QRISK3, 10-year | Atorvastatin at 10% or more | More than 40% fall in non-HDL | LDL 2.0 mmol/L or less, or non-HDL 2.6 mmol/L or less |

Sources: NZ Ministry of Health 2018; Nelson 2024; ACC/AHA 2026; USPSTF 2022; NICE NG238.

**Other differences:**
- **Europe** (ESC/EAS 2019) sets LDL below 1.4 mmol/L with a 50% fall at very high risk and below 1.8 mmol/L at high risk; it suggests below 1.0 mmol/L for a second event within two years. Its ApoB goals are below 65, 80 and 100 mg/dL at very high, high and moderate risk [ESC/EAS 2019].
- **Lower LDL targets.** The Ez-PAVE trial randomised 3,048 people with heart disease to an LDL target below 55 mg/dL or below 70 mg/dL; events at three years were 6.6% against 9.7% [Ez-PAVE].
- **ApoB.** ACC/AHA 2026 suggests checking ApoB once LDL and non-HDL goals are met, with a goal below 55 mg/dL at very high risk [ACC/AHA 2026]. Brad uses ApoB first.
- **Calcium score.** ACC/AHA 2026 uses it when the decision to treat is uncertain, allows deferring treatment at a score of 0 in some people, and advises against repeat scans once treatment starts [ACC/AHA 2026]. Australia uses it to reclassify risk [Nelson 2024].

## Common questions

**What is a healthy ApoB?** In Brad's plan, below 0.5 g/L (50 mg/dL). Guidelines set higher goals for lower-risk people: ESC/EAS uses below 0.65, 0.8 and 1.0 g/L by risk [ESC/EAS 2019].

**My report says my LDL is normal, but the plan says "Above optimal". Who is right?** Both, by different rules. A European consensus advises labs to flag LDL from 3 mmol/L (115 mg/dL) [Nordestgaard 2016]. The plan's line is its treatment target of 1.4 mmol/L (55 mg/dL), set lower on purpose (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**What does a low non-HDL mean?** Fewer ApoB particles. Below 1.6 mmol/L (62 mg/dL) meets the plan's target.

**Which statin first, and when do I add ezetimibe?** A low-dose water-soluble statin, then ezetimibe before a higher dose (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Rosuvastatin or pitavastatin?** Both are on Brad's list of preferred low-dose starting statins (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). Rosuvastatin reaches a bigger drop: about 40% at 5 mg up to about 63% at 40 mg, against about 30% to 40% across pitavastatin's 1 to 4 mg (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Do I need to fast?** Usually not. Fasting changes LDL and triglycerides only slightly; a fasting test may help if non-fasting triglycerides are above 5 mmol/L [Nordestgaard 2016].

**Will a statin damage my memory?** The FDA's 2012 memory warning drew on adverse-event reports; a meta-analysis of 25 placebo-controlled trials then found no link to cognitive impairment [Ott 2015] ([statin panic post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/the-30-year-statin-panic-is-finally-over)).

**I started a weight-loss medicine and my LDL rose. What now?** Ask your doctor. The plan judges the new value against the same target, whatever caused the rise (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Is there a supplement that lowers ApoB?** Psyllium, by about 0.05 g/L in trials [Jovanovski 2018]; Brad's supplement entries cover the rest.

**How often should I test Lp(a)?** At least once in adulthood [Kronenberg 2022]. Brad's plan treats it as a one-time test (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
