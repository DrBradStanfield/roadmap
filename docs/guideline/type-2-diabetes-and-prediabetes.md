# Type 2 Diabetes and Prediabetes: Tests, Diet and Medicines

*Built from the ADA Standards of Care 2026, NICE, USPSTF and KDIGO guidelines, NHS pages, New Zealand and Australian guidance, US drug labels and trials. Brad's view comes from his algorithm and posts. Full list at the end.*

## In short

- HbA1c shows your average blood sugar over the past two to three months. An HbA1c of 5.7 to 6.4% is prediabetes in the ADA's criteria, and 6.5% or more, confirmed by a second test, is diabetes [1].
- Prediabetes often goes back to normal with weight loss and more activity. In the Diabetes Prevention Program, a lifestyle program cut new cases of diabetes by 58% and metformin by 31%, against placebo [25].
- Losing weight can put type 2 diabetes into remission: in the DiRECT trial, almost half of people on an intensive diet program were in remission at one year [27].
- Many people need medicine as well. SGLT2 inhibitors and GLP-1 medicines protect the heart and kidneys in people at risk, beyond lowering sugar [4, 13].
- Low blood sugar is a risk mainly with insulin and sulfonylureas. Everyone on them should know how to treat it [3, 14].
- **Brad's view:** his plan screens with HbA1c alone and, when the weight trigger is on, suggests a GLP-1 medicine first (tirzepatide preferred), then an SGLT2 inhibitor, then metformin (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

## Get help now

- If someone with diabetes is unconscious, having a seizure, or cannot swallow safely, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK). Do not give food or drink by mouth.
- Someone with very low blood sugar who is not responding normally, with no glucagon to hand or no recovery within 10 minutes of a glucagon injection, or who has been drinking alcohol: call your emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [14].
- On an SGLT2 inhibitor, blood ketones over 1.5 mmol/L, or confusion, marked drowsiness or struggling to breathe: stop the medicine and call your emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK). Ketoacidosis on these medicines can happen even when blood sugar is not very high [21].
- Diabetes with signs of ketoacidosis (vomiting or belly pain with deep or fast breathing, fruity breath, or sleepiness or confusion), and a blood ketone over 3 mmol/L or no way to test: call your emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [15].
- Extreme thirst and passing a lot of urine with vomiting, signs of dehydration or new confusion: call your emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK). These can be signs of a dangerous high-sugar crisis [3].
- On an SGLT2 inhibitor, pain, tenderness, redness or swelling of the genitals or the skin between the genitals and the back passage, with fever or feeling unwell: stop the medicine and get emergency care (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK). This can be a rare, serious infection [21].
- On a GLP-1 medicine or tirzepatide, belly pain that does not go away, sometimes spreading to your back: stop the medicine and get medical help the same day; if the pain is severe or you are vomiting, call your emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK). This can be pancreatitis [23, 24].

## What it is

Type 2 diabetes means blood sugar runs too high because the pancreas slowly loses its ability to make enough insulin, usually on a background of insulin resistance (the body responds poorly to insulin) [1]. Over years, high sugar can damage the eyes, kidneys and nerves [1, 5, 6].

Prediabetes means sugar is above normal but below the diabetes line. Risk rises steadily through the prediabetes range and is highest near the top of it [1]. The ADA links prediabetes with higher heart risk and advises checking and treating blood pressure, cholesterol and other heart risk factors [2].

**Symptoms.** Many people have none. The common ones are feeling very tired, passing urine more often, constant thirst and losing weight without trying. Blurred vision, slow-healing cuts and repeated thrush can also occur [16].

**Not every case is type 2.** Younger age, weight loss without trying, ketoacidosis, or needing insulin soon after diagnosis can point to type 1 diabetes in an adult; the ADA advises antibody tests in that case [1].

**Who should be tested.** The ADA advises testing every adult from age 35, and earlier for adults with overweight (a BMI of 25 or more, or 23 or more with Asian ancestry) plus at least one risk factor. Risk factors include a parent, brother or sister with diabetes, heart disease, high blood pressure, polycystic ovary syndrome and inactivity [1]. People with prediabetes should be tested every year; others with normal results at least every three years [1]. The USPSTF advises screening adults aged 35 to 70 with overweight or obesity [12]. Screening trials found no fall in deaths over about 10 years, which may have been too short [12]. Some medicines raise sugar, including statins, thiazide diuretics, some HIV medicines, antipsychotics and long-term steroids; the ADA advises considering screening for people who take them [1].

**Statins and diabetes.** Statins may slightly raise the risk of diabetes in people already at high risk. The ADA advises keeping the statin and checking sugar regularly [2].

## Your numbers

**What Brad's plan uses** (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). The plan reads HbA1c only:

| HbA1c | What the plan calls it | Plan card |
|---|---|---|
| Under 38.8 mmol/mol (under 5.7%) | Normal | "HbA1c in normal range" |
| 38.8 mmol/mol (5.7%) or more | Prediabetes | "HbA1c indicates prediabetes" |
| 47.5 mmol/mol (6.5%) or more | Diabetes range, urgent | "HbA1c in diabetic range" |

The plan sets these lines in % and converts them exactly, so 5.7% is 38.8 mmol/mol and 6.5% is 47.5 mmol/mol. Laboratories and guidelines round them to 39 and 48 mmol/mol [1]. A raised HbA1c in the plan also counts as a marker for its weight-medicine trigger from a BMI of 25 (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). The plan reminds you to repeat a test you have tracked once the last result is more than 12 months old (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Other tests guidelines use** (ADA criteria, not part of the plan) [1]:

| Test | Prediabetes | Diabetes |
|---|---|---|
| HbA1c | 5.7 to 6.4% (39 to 47 mmol/mol) | 6.5% (48 mmol/mol) or more |
| Fasting plasma glucose (no food for at least 8 hours) | 5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L (100 to 125 mg/dL) | 7.0 mmol/L (126 mg/dL) or more |
| 2-hour glucose after a 75 g oral glucose drink | 7.8 to 11.0 mmol/L (140 to 199 mg/dL) | 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) or more |
| Random glucose, with classic symptoms | not used | 11.1 mmol/L (200 mg/dL) or more |

Without clear symptoms, diabetes needs two abnormal results: two different tests, or the same test twice [1].

**When HbA1c misleads.** Some haemoglobin variants, pregnancy, G6PD deficiency, HIV, and conditions that change red cell turnover (anaemia, blood loss, transfusion, kidney failure) can make HbA1c read wrong. Glucose tests are used to diagnose instead [1].

**Targets once you have diabetes.** The ADA suggests an HbA1c under 7% (53 mmol/mol) for many adults, lower (under 6.5%) for some in good health with low risk of low sugar, and less strict goals for frailty or serious illness [3]. NICE suggests aiming for 48 mmol/mol (6.5%) when diet or medicines that do not cause low sugar are used, and 53 mmol/mol (7.0%) on medicines that can cause it, or once HbA1c reaches 58 mmol/mol (7.5%) on the first medicines [9]. HbA1c is checked at least twice a year, and about every three months when off target or after a change [3].

**Low blood sugar.** Below 3.9 mmol/L (70 mg/dL) is low; below 3.0 mmol/L (54 mg/dL) needs action now [3]. The NHS uses below 4 mmol/L [14].

**HOMA-IR.** This index is worked out from fasting glucose and fasting insulin to estimate insulin resistance. In the 1985 study that built it, it tracked a reference clamp test closely, but its estimate is imprecise for one person [32]. Brad's plan does not use it (see Brad's view).

## What helps

**Weight loss and activity.** The ADA advises adults with overweight at high risk to lose at least 5 to 7% of their weight, through a healthy reduced-calorie diet and at least 150 minutes of moderate activity a week [2]. Those were close to the goals in the Diabetes Prevention Program, where lifestyle change prevented more diabetes than metformin [25]. See the weight and exercise guidelines.

**Eating pattern.** For prediabetes, the ADA names Mediterranean and low-carbohydrate patterns among evidence-based choices [2]. NICE advises the same healthy eating as everyone else: high-fibre, low-glycaemic-index carbohydrates such as fruit, vegetables, wholegrains and pulses. It discourages foods sold specially for diabetes [10]. See the diet guideline.

**Remission.** In DiRECT, people diagnosed within six years and not on insulin stopped their diabetes medicines and used a formula diet of about 825 to 853 kcal a day for three to five months. At one year, 46% were in remission (HbA1c under 6.5% off all diabetes medicines for at least 2 months) against 4% with usual care. Remission tracked weight loss: 86% of those who lost 15 kg or more were in remission [27]. It often fades: of those in remission at two years, 26% were still in remission at five [35]. Do not try a very-low-calorie diet on insulin or a sulfonylurea without your doctor, because sugar can fall too low [3].

**Medicines in prediabetes with obesity.** In people with obesity and prediabetes, 1.3% of those on tirzepatide were diagnosed with type 2 diabetes over about three years, against 13.3% on placebo [36].

**Check your other risks.** Blood pressure, cholesterol and smoking matter as much as sugar for heart risk in prediabetes [2]. See the high blood pressure, high cholesterol and heart disease risk guidelines.

**Continuous glucose monitors.** The ADA finds too little evidence to use a monitor to screen for or diagnose prediabetes or diabetes [1]. For people with diabetes on insulin, or on other medicines that can cause low sugar, the ADA recommends one [4].

**Supplements.** In a 2021 review of 46 trials in type 2 diabetes, berberine lowered HbA1c by 0.38 percentage points on average when used alone, and by 0.73 alone or added to standard treatment [33]. Brad calls the effect of berberine alone borderline clinically meaningful ([berberine post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/is-the-berberine-hype-justified-should-you-take-it)). Its trials are smaller and shorter than metformin's; see Brad's view and the [berberine entry](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/berberine-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects).

## Medicines

Guidelines choose medicines by more than sugar: heart disease, heart failure, kidney disease, weight, risk of low sugar, and cost [4]. Doses below are from US prescribing information; your doctor sets yours.

**Metformin.** It lowers the sugar the liver makes. The ADA calls it effective, safe and cheap, and notes it has long been the first medicine for type 2 diabetes [4]. It rarely causes low sugar on its own [4].

- **Doses.** Metformin hydrochloride tablets: 500 mg twice a day or 850 mg once a day with meals, rising slowly to at most 2,550 mg a day in divided doses [19]. Metformin hydrochloride extended-release tablets: 500 mg once a day with the evening meal, rising by 500 mg a week to at most 2,000 mg once a day [20].
- **Side effects.** Bloating, belly discomfort and diarrhoea are common; a slow start or the extended-release form helps [4]. It can lower vitamin B12; the label advises checking B12 every 2 to 3 years [20].
- **Kidneys.** Not for use with an eGFR below 30; starting it is not recommended at 30 to 45 [19]. KDIGO advises adjusting the dose below 45 [13].
- **Lactic acidosis** is rare but serious. Risk rises with poor kidney function, heavy drinking, contrast scans and surgery. Stop it and see a doctor quickly if you get unexplained fast breathing, muscle aches or feel very unwell [19, 20].
- **In prediabetes,** the ADA advises considering metformin for people at high risk, especially those aged 25 to 59 with a BMI of 35 or more and higher fasting glucose and HbA1c (such as 6 mmol/L and 6.0%), or past gestational diabetes [2]. Over about 21 years in the Diabetes Prevention Program, neither metformin nor lifestyle change lowered deaths from any cause, cancer or heart disease, though both prevented diabetes [26].

**SGLT2 inhibitors (empagliflozin, dapagliflozin, canagliflozin).** They make the kidneys pass extra sugar in the urine.

- **Evidence.** In EMPA-REG OUTCOME, in people with type 2 diabetes and heart disease, empagliflozin lowered a combined count of heart deaths, heart attacks and strokes (10.5% against 12.1%) and heart failure admissions [28]. In DAPA-CKD and EMPA-KIDNEY, dapagliflozin and empagliflozin slowed kidney disease, with or without diabetes [29, 30].
- **Who.** The ADA recommends an SGLT2 inhibitor for people with type 2 diabetes and heart failure, and an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 medicine for heart or kidney disease, at any HbA1c [4]. KDIGO recommends one for type 2 diabetes with kidney disease and an eGFR of 20 or more, continued below 20 unless it is not tolerated or dialysis starts [13].
- **Doses.** Jardiance (empagliflozin) tablets: 10 mg once a day in the morning, which may rise to 25 mg for sugar control [21]. Farxiga (dapagliflozin) tablets: 5 mg once a day for sugar control, which may rise to 10 mg; 10 mg for other uses [22]. For sugar control they work less well as kidney function falls: Farxiga is not recommended for it below an eGFR of 45, Jardiance below 30 [21, 22].
- **Side effects.** Genital thrush and urine infections are the most common; dehydration and low blood pressure can occur [21]. Ketoacidosis is rare in type 2 diabetes (0.6 to 4.9 events per 1,000 patient-years) [3].
- **Before surgery or fasting.** Pause it 3 or more days before surgery or a procedure that needs a long fast, and restart when eating and drinking normally [21]. KDIGO also suggests pausing during severe illness [13].
- **Avoid** a ketogenic diet on these medicines [4].

**GLP-1 medicines and tirzepatide.** They raise insulin when sugar is high, slow stomach emptying and reduce appetite. Tirzepatide also acts on the GIP receptor [23, 24]. The ADA ranks tirzepatide and semaglutide highest for lowering both sugar and weight, and prefers GLP-1 based treatment to insulin when sugar is not severely high [4].

- **Evidence.** In SUSTAIN-6, semaglutide lowered a combined count of heart deaths, heart attacks and strokes (6.6% against 8.9%) but had more eye complications from retinopathy [31]. In SURPASS-CVOT, tirzepatide matched dulaglutide for heart events in people with heart disease; it was not shown to be better [24].
- **Doses.** Ozempic (semaglutide) injection: 0.25 mg once a week for 4 weeks, then 0.5 mg; it may rise to 1 mg and then 2 mg, each after at least 4 weeks [23]. Mounjaro (tirzepatide) injection: 2.5 mg once a week, rising in 2.5 mg steps at least 4 weeks apart, to at most 15 mg [24].
- **Side effects.** Nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, belly pain and constipation are the most common [23]. Rats given semaglutide developed thyroid C-cell tumours; whether this happens in people is unknown [23]. Low sugar is more likely when they are added to insulin or a sulfonylurea [23]. Tirzepatide can make birth control pills less reliable for 4 weeks after starting and after each dose rise [24].
**Others.** Sulfonylureas and insulin can cause low sugar and weight gain [3, 4]. Pioglitazone may help after a stroke in people with insulin resistance, at the cost of weight gain, swelling and fractures [2]. The ADA advises considering insulin when there are high-sugar symptoms or HbA1c is above 10% (86 mmol/mol) [4].

**Treating low blood sugar.** If you are awake and your sugar is 3.9 mmol/L or below, take about 15 g of fast carbohydrate, ideally glucose (not a fatty or protein food). Recheck after 15 minutes, repeat if still low, and get help if it stays low. People on an automated insulin pump usually need less; on acarbose, use pure glucose. Then eat a snack or meal, as low sugar can return [3, 14].

## Brad's view (reviewed 2 Nov 2026)

**HbA1c is the screening test.** His plan uses HbA1c rather than fasting glucose, because it reflects two to three months of sugar rather than a single snapshot, and it leaves out fasting insulin and HOMA-IR because the insulin test is not standardised and has no agreed cut-offs (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). He says insulin tests and glucose monitors for people without diabetes do not change what a family doctor does for prevention, though spending on them is your call ([five blood tests post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/5-livesaving-blog-tests)).

**Prediabetes is the window.** The plan's prediabetes card says lifestyle changes now can prevent progression to diabetes; the diabetes card says it needs medical management and lifestyle change (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). For prediabetes he advises a diet rich in non-starchy vegetables, legumes, nuts, whole fruit and whole grains, with fish and chicken, and cutting sugary, processed food ([five blood tests post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/5-livesaving-blog-tests)). He names metformin, psyllium husk and GLP-1 medicines as options to consider ([five blood tests post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/5-livesaving-blog-tests)).

**His medicine order runs through weight.** When the plan's weight trigger is on, it suggests discussing tirzepatide (preferred) or semaglutide, then a higher dose or a switch to tirzepatide, then an SGLT2 inhibitor, then metformin (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). The plan describes SGLT2 inhibitors as having proven heart and kidney protection in some populations (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). The cards need a known age of 18 or over, and the plan suggests no diabetes medicine when the weight trigger is off, as at a BMI under 25 (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Metformin for diabetes, not for longevity.** He calls metformin first-line for blood sugar: well established, effective and cheap. He expects GLP-1 medicines may replace it first-line as their cost falls ([berberine post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/is-the-berberine-hype-justified-should-you-take-it)). He calls it a wonderful medication for prediabetes and type 2 diabetes, but does not support it for people without them, because the 21-year Diabetes Prevention Program follow-up showed no benefit on deaths and it blunts gains from exercise (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). In one trial in older adults, metformin weakened the rise in fitness and insulin sensitivity from aerobic training [34].

**Berberine is not a metformin swap.** Its trials are small and short against metformin's large, long ones, so guidelines recommend metformin, not berberine, for prediabetes and type 2 diabetes ([berberine post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/is-the-berberine-hype-justified-should-you-take-it)). He considers it only as a last resort for cholesterol when other options fail or cost too much ([berberine post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/is-the-berberine-hype-justified-should-you-take-it)).

**Gentler targets when frail.** For older, frail patients he cites a target HbA1c under 8% rather than 7%, because chasing tight control raises the risk of low sugar and falls; in one case he halved the insulin, stopped a sulfonylurea and added an SGLT2 inhibitor ([deprescribing post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/when-your-meds-start-making-you-sick)).

**His own SGLT2 inhibitor.** Brad takes empagliflozin 6.25 mg a day, below the usual 10 mg dose, mainly to protect his kidneys. He says this is off-label, against current guidelines, not formally studied, and not a recommendation for anyone else (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Heart risk.** The plan treats each risk factor to its own target and uses no risk calculator. Aspirin is for people after a heart attack, stroke or stent, as their doctor directs, not for preventing a first event (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

## Who needs extra care

- **Pregnancy and planning pregnancy.** The ADA advises planning pregnancy with your diabetes team, including when to stop non-insulin medicines [4]. Stop Ozempic at least 2 months before a planned pregnancy [23]. Jardiance is not recommended in the second and third trimesters [21]. HbA1c reads less reliably in pregnancy [1].
- **Breastfeeding.** Farxiga is not recommended while breastfeeding [22].
- **Older adults.** The ADA suggests an HbA1c under 7.0 to 7.5% for healthy older adults, under 8.0% for those with several illnesses or some frailty, and avoiding low sugar above all in very poor health [7]. NICE advises offering an SGLT2 inhibitor to frail people only if they are not at risk of dehydration or low blood pressure from it [8].
- **Reduced kidney function.** Metformin doses change below an eGFR of 45 and it stops below 30 [13, 19]. SGLT2 inhibitors start at an eGFR of 20 or more for kidney protection [13]. The ADA advises a yearly urine albumin test and eGFR for everyone with type 2 diabetes [5]. For ACE inhibitor or ARB doses in reduced kidney function, check with your doctor or pharmacist.
- **On insulin or a sulfonylurea.** Adding an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 medicine may mean lower doses of these, to avoid low sugar [4].
- **Diabetic retinopathy.** Retinopathy complications were more common on semaglutide in SUSTAIN-6; have your eyes followed [23, 31].
- **Under 18.** Brad's plan gives no suggestions to anyone under 18 and tells them to talk with their doctor (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

## When to see your doctor

- You have tiredness, thirst, frequent urination or weight loss without trying [16].
- Your HbA1c is in the prediabetes or diabetes range, or you are 35 or older and have never been tested [1, 12].
- You have diabetes and have not had an eye exam, a foot check for nerve damage, or a kidney check this year [5, 6].
- You get low sugar often, at night, or without the usual warning signs [14].
- Blood ketones of 1.6 to 3 mmol/L, if not on an SGLT2 inhibitor: contact your diabetes team now [15].
- A new foot wound or ulcer, or a red, hot, swollen or discoloured foot: be seen the same day [37].
- You are on a GLP-1 medicine with vomiting or diarrhoea that does not settle, or vision changes [23].
- You plan surgery, a contrast scan or a procedure that needs a long fast while on metformin or an SGLT2 inhibitor [20, 21].
- You are planning a pregnancy [4].

## Common questions

**What HbA1c counts as prediabetes?** In Brad's plan and the ADA's criteria, 5.7% (about 39 mmol/mol) or more [1]. New Zealand and the UK start higher, at 42 mmol/mol [11, 17].

**Can I reverse prediabetes?** Often, yes. Losing 5 to 7% of your weight and doing 150 minutes of activity a week lowers the risk of diabetes [2, 25].

**Metformin or an SGLT2 inhibitor?** NICE now offers both together for most people [8]. The ADA prefers an SGLT2 inhibitor or GLP-1 medicine with heart failure, heart disease or kidney disease [4]. Brad's plan adds an SGLT2 inhibitor after a GLP-1 medicine, before metformin, when its weight trigger is on (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**What does HOMA-IR mean?** An estimate of insulin resistance from fasting glucose and insulin [32]. Brad's plan does not use it (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Is insulin resistance the same as prediabetes?** No. Prediabetes is defined by the sugar tests above [1]. Insulin resistance can show up before HbA1c rises, but has no agreed test cut-off (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
