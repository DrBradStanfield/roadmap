# Diet: Clinical Dietary Guidance

*Source: AHA 2026 Dietary Guidance to Improve Cardiovascular Health (DOI: 10.1161/CIR.0000000000001435)*

This guideline covers the 9 features of a heart-healthy dietary pattern as defined by the AHA 2026 Scientific Statement, cross-referenced with Dr. Stanfield's Health Roadmap algorithm.

*Note: Bracketed numbers like [3], [22-26] refer to the AHA 2026 paper's reference list (DOI: 10.1161/CIR.0000000000001435). Key studies are also cited with full DOIs in each section's References.*

---

## Dietary Principles

The AHA emphasises dietary *patterns* over single foods or nutrients. Heart-healthy dietary patterns contain primarily vegetables and fruits, whole grains, healthy sources of protein, liquid nontropical plant oils, and minimally processed foods. Dietary patterns higher in plant-based foods and lower in animal products are associated with lower coronary heart disease risk and more favourable metabolic profiles [3].

Heart-healthy dietary patterns should be established early in life (from age 1 year) and maintained across the life course. The guidance applies regardless of where food is procured, prepared, or consumed.

## Feature 1: Energy Balance & Body Weight

**Guideline position:** Adjusting energy intake and expenditure to achieve and maintain a healthy body weight is central to cardiovascular health. Obesity affects 21% of children/adolescents and 40% of US adults. Excess body fat contributes to type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular-kidney metabolic syndrome [17]. Adults should engage in at least 150 min/wk of moderate to vigorous physical activity [18]. A range of dietary patterns (DASH, Mediterranean, pescetarian, ovo/lacto vegetarian) support cardiovascular health and healthy weight when implemented as intended [20]. Some popular weight loss diets may result in short-term benefits but have uncertain long-term cardiovascular impact and can worsen cardiovascular risk factors [21].

**Brad's position:** Aligns with AHA. The algorithm uses BMI and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) as a composite assessment. A BMI 25-29 patient with WHtR < 0.5 is reclassified as normal weight (not overweight). For patients meeting obesity criteria (BMI ≥ 30, or BMI ≥ 25 with WHtR ≥ 0.5), the algorithm recommends lifestyle intervention first, then a GLP-1 medication cascade (semaglutide → dose increase → switch → SGLT2i → metformin). Brad recommends 150+ min/wk cardio plus 2-3 resistance training sessions.

**Algorithm links:** `weight-*` suggestions, BMI/WHtR thresholds, GLP-1 cascade (Section 6), `exercise` suggestion.

**References:**
- US Physical Activity Guidelines, 2nd ed., 2018 (DOI: odphp.health.gov)
- Paluch 2024, AHA scientific statement on resistance exercise (DOI: 10.1161/CIR.0000000000001189)

## Feature 2: Vegetables and Fruits

**Guideline position:** Whole or minimally processed vegetables and fruits are core components of heart-healthy dietary patterns. Consistently associated with cardiovascular health including blood lipids, blood pressure, and type 2 diabetes control [22-26]. Provide essential nutrients and dietary fibre. Fresh, frozen, and canned forms are all acceptable — if frozen or canned, preferably without added sugars or sodium.

**Brad's position:** Aligns with AHA. For specific targets, the DGA 2020-2025 recommends 2.5 cups vegetables and 2 cups fruit per day at 2,000 calories (see Daily Food Group Targets section below). The algorithm does not currently have a standalone vegetables/fruits suggestion, though the `lipid-diet` section recommends specific plant foods (edamame, beans, lentils) for lipid management, and the `high-potassium` suggestion encourages vegetable and fruit consumption for blood pressure.

**Algorithm gap:** No standalone "eat more vegetables and fruits" suggestion. This is a candidate for a new always-show lifestyle suggestion.

## Feature 3: Whole Grains

**Guideline position:** Whole grains (whole wheat, oats, brown rice, quinoa, barley, rye) contain all 3 components of the grain kernel — endosperm, germ, and bran — providing dietary fibre, vitamins, minerals, and bioactive compounds [27]. Regular consumption is associated with lower risks of CVD, coronary heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome [28]. RCTs show replacing refined grains with whole grains improves cardiovascular risk factors [29]. Diets high in fibre and whole grains are linked to favourable gut microbiota modulation and reduced inflammatory cytokines [30,31].

**Brad's position:** Aligns with AHA. The algorithm recommends 25-35g fibre daily (`fiber` suggestion) and oats specifically in the `lipid-diet` section (75g dry oats/day provides 3g beta-glucan for 5-10% LDL reduction). However, there is no standalone whole grains suggestion — the fibre recommendation is general, not grain-specific.

**Algorithm gap:** No standalone "choose whole grains over refined grains" suggestion. The `fiber` suggestion covers the fibre component but not the broader whole grain recommendation.

**References:**
- Hu 2023, whole grain consumption and CVD risk, dose-response meta-analysis (DOI: 10.1016/j.ajcnut.2022.10.010)
- Huang 2025, whole grain consumption and metabolic health, umbrella review (DOI: 10.1039/d5fo03240g)

## Feature 4: Healthy Protein Sources

**Guideline position:** The AHA 2026 recommends focusing on protein *sources* rather than protein *quantity* — the relationship between protein quantity and cardiovascular health is uncertain [32]. Recommendations: shift from meat to plant sources (legumes, nuts), regularly consume fish/seafood, choose low-fat dairy, and if red meat is desired, choose lean cuts, avoid processed forms, and limit portion size [33-52].

### Source matters more than quantity

This is one of the most important and nuanced areas of nutrition science. The evidence consistently shows that **the source of protein determines its health impact, not the total amount:**

- **Plant protein** (legumes, nuts, beans) is associated with *lower* all-cause mortality. An additional 3% energy from plant proteins per day was associated with a 5% lower risk of death from all causes (Naghshi 2020, BMJ, 32 cohort studies). A separate cohort study found high plant protein intake cut mortality risk by 36% (HR = 0.64) [DOI: 10.1038/s41598-023-30455-9].

- **Animal protein** (especially red meat and dairy, but not fish) is associated with *higher* mortality. High animal protein intake increased mortality risk by 52% (HR = 1.52) in one cohort [DOI: 10.1038/s41598-023-30455-9]. The Rotterdam Study (meta-analysis) found the association was "mainly explained by higher animal protein intake... contributed to by protein from meat and dairy" [DOI: 10.1007/s10654-020-00607-6].

- **Fish protein** is the exception among animal proteins — associated with 21% lower mortality risk (HR = 0.79) [DOI: 10.1038/s41598-023-30455-9].

- **Total protein** is associated with *lower* all-cause mortality and is NOT significantly associated with CVD or cancer mortality when analysed without regard to source (Naghshi 2020, BMJ) [DOI: 10.1136/bmj.m2412].

### The IGF-1/mTOR/cancer concern

Dr. Valter Longo's landmark 2014 study (6,381 adults, 18-year follow-up) found that in adults aged 50-65, the high-protein group had a 74% increase in all-cause mortality (HR: 1.74) and were 4x more likely to die of cancer (HR: 4.33) compared to the low-protein group. However, **when animal protein was controlled for, the association was eliminated or significantly reduced** — suggesting animal protein mediates these relationships [DOI: 10.1016/j.cmet.2014.02.006].

The mechanism: high protein (especially animal protein rich in leucine and methionine) activates the mTOR pathway, a master regulator of cell growth. When mTOR is chronically activated, it promotes growth over repair — potentially accelerating cancer. This is why protein *source* matters: plant proteins are lower in methionine and leucine than animal proteins.

**Critical age qualification:** The negative association with high protein only held until age ~65. After 65, the association reverses — lower protein intake is associated with *higher* cancer mortality. Higher protein after 65 may be necessary to prevent age-related muscle and weight loss [DOI: 10.1016/j.cmet.2014.02.006].

### How much protein: the dose-response

- **RDA:** 0.8 g/kg/day (minimum to prevent deficiency, not optimal)
- **PROT-AGE / ESPEN (over 65):** 1.0-1.2 g/kg/day for healthy older adults [DOI: 10.3390/nu10030360]
- **Morton 2018 meta-analysis:** muscle gains plateau at ~1.6 g/kg/day during resistance training [DOI: 10.1136/bjsports-2017-097608]
- **Newer meta-analysis:** the dose-response for lean body mass rapidly diminishes after **1.3 g/kg/day** (inflection point) — most muscle benefits are captured below 1.6 g/kg [DOI: 10.1093/nutrit/nuaa104]
- **Weight loss:** high-protein diets (1.07-1.6 g/kg/day) reduce body weight (-0.79 kg) and fat mass (-0.87 kg) while preserving lean mass compared to standard protein [DOI: 10.7570/jomes20028]

### Kidney considerations

- Current CKD guidelines: avoid >1.3 g/kg/day for mild CKD (stages 1-2); restrict to 0.6-0.8 g/kg/day for moderate-severe CKD (stages 3-5) [DOI: 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.26577]
- High-protein diets can cause intraglomerular hypertension, hyperfiltration, and proteinuria [DOI: 10.1681/ASN.2020010028]
- One cohort (1,797 adults, 6 years) found 50% higher CKD risk in the highest protein tertile [DOI: 10.1053/j.jrn.2018.10.007]
- **However:** a recent study found higher protein intake in older adults with mild-moderate CKD actually *reduced* mortality by 33% at 1.6 vs 0.8 g/kg/day — "benefits of proteins may outweigh the risks" [DOI: 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.26577]

### Ultra-processed "high protein" products

The protein trend has been hijacked by the food industry. High-protein products have quadrupled in the past decade [DOI: 10.52361/fsbh.2024.4.e9]. A study of the Spanish market found **91% of foods with protein claims were classified as "less healthy"** — more than 50% were high in fat or sodium, ~25% high in sugar or saturated fat [DOI: 10.3390/nu16244281].

Critically, protein-enriched ultra-processed foods **do not prevent overeating** — they lead to the same overconsumption as other UPFs [DOI: 10.1038/s42255-025-01247-4]. When protein is isolated from its whole-food context, the fibre, phytonutrients, and micronutrients that drive health benefits are lost.

### Brad's position

**Target: 1.2 g/kg of ideal body weight per day.** This is Brad's personal target and what he advises patients. The evidence doesn't show meaningful additional benefit above ~1.3 g/kg for most people — and 1.2 g/kg is already a challenging target to achieve from primarily plant-based sources, which is where the protein should come from. It's above the RDA (0.8 g/kg) to capture muscle maintenance and weight management benefits, but well below the 2.2 g/kg that some influencers promote without supporting evidence.

**Source priority:** Prioritise plant-based proteins — legumes (lentils, chickpeas, beans), nuts, and pea protein. Brad personally uses pea protein powder because it's relatively low in methionine (the amino acid flagged by Longo's research as potentially promoting cancer growth). Fish is the best animal protein source. If red meat is consumed, choose lean cuts and limit frequency.

**Avoid ultra-processed protein products.** A handful of almonds (30g) provides 6g protein, 3.5g fibre, potassium, calcium, and no artificial ingredients. A protein bar provides isolated protein stripped of these co-occurring nutrients.

**Use ideal body weight, not actual body weight,** to calculate the target — overweight individuals using actual body weight will overshoot.

**CKD adjustment:** The algorithm reduces to 1.0 g/kg for eGFR < 45 (CKD-3b boundary). This is conservative but aligns with current guidelines for moderate-severe CKD.

**Algorithm links:** `protein-target` suggestion (1.2 g/kg IBW, CKD-adjusted), `lipid-diet` (plant protein sources for lipid management).

**Algorithm gap:** The `protein-target` suggestion is quantity-focused. It should also emphasise protein source — prioritising plant sources and fish, limiting red/processed meat. This aligns with both the AHA 2026 position and the mortality data.

### Omega-3 supplementation divergence

The AHA states fish oil supplementation "has not been demonstrated to lower CVD risk in otherwise healthy adults" and may increase AF risk [42-44]. Brad recommends omega-3 supplementation for all users (`supplement-omega3`) based on two lines of evidence: (1) The VITAL trial (Manson 2019, NEJM) showed a 28% reduction in myocardial infarction, and the Bernasconi 2021 meta-analysis confirmed reduced cardiovascular events and cardiac death with benefits increasing at higher doses. (2) Cognitive protection — Jerneren 2015 and Oulhaj 2016 found that B-vitamin supplementation slowed brain atrophy by 40%, but only in participants with adequate omega-3 levels, suggesting omega-3 is necessary for B-vitamins to exert their neuroprotective effect.

## Feature 5: Unsaturated Fats Over Saturated Fats

**Guideline position:** Replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat and, to a lesser extent, monounsaturated fat reduces LDL cholesterol — a causal risk factor for CVD [53,54]. Modeling analyses show replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat is associated with reduced coronary heart disease risk [55,56].

Sources of saturated fat: animal fats (beef tallow, butter), tropical oils (coconut oil, cocoa butter, palm oil). Sources of unsaturated fat: nontropical plant oils (soybean, canola, olive oils). Strong evidence shows replacing butter with plant oils/spreads decreases LDL [22]. Replacement of tropical plant oils with nontropical plant oils also lowers LDL [22,62].

Target: dietary patterns adhering to all 9 features are unlikely to exceed 10% of energy from saturated fat [20,22].

**Brad's position:** Aligns. The `lipid-diet` section specifically recommends "extra-virgin olive oil — monounsaturated fat replacing butter/saturated fat" with a "modest direct LDL effect." The algorithm frames this as a specific dietary intervention for elevated lipids rather than a general population recommendation.

**References:**
- Sacks 2017, AHA presidential advisory on dietary fats and CVD (DOI: 10.1161/CIR.0000000000000510)
- Zhang 2025, butter vs plant-based oils and mortality (DOI: 10.1001/jamainternmed.2025.0205)

## Feature 6: Minimally Processed Foods Over Ultraprocessed Foods

**Guideline position:** Strong evidence links dietary patterns high in ultraprocessed foods to overweight/obesity, CVDs, type 2 diabetes, and all-cause mortality [67-70]. The AHA uses the term "ultraprocessed foods" (referencing Nova classification) and acknowledges limited evidence for specific mechanisms of harm, potentially due to the wide range of causal factors in the broad ultraprocessed category. Efforts should promote minimally processed foods and shift away from ultraprocessed foods in the marketplace [71].

**Brad's position:** Aligns in principle. The algorithm does not have a standalone ultraprocessed food suggestion, but the dietary pattern implied by the `lipid-diet` foods (oats, nuts, edamame, EVOO, flaxseed) is inherently whole-food and minimally processed.

**Algorithm gap:** No standalone "avoid ultraprocessed foods" suggestion. Candidate for a new always-show lifestyle suggestion.

**References:**

- AHA Science Advisory on ultraprocessed foods, 2025 (DOI: 10.1161/CIR.0000000000001365)

## Feature 7: Minimize Added Sugars

**Guideline position:** Strong evidence supports minimising added sugar intake across the life course [22,55]. Sugar-sweetened beverages are linked to higher risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, and cardiovascular mortality [73,74]. Adults consuming ≥25% of energy from added sugars have a nearly 3-fold higher risk of CVD mortality compared with those consuming <10% [75].

**Brad's position:** Aligns. The `trig-nutrition` suggestion recommends "reducing alcohol, sugar, and total calorie intake" for elevated triglycerides (≥1.69 mmol/L). However, sugar reduction is framed as a triglyceride intervention rather than a universal dietary recommendation.

**Algorithm gap:** No standalone "minimize added sugars" suggestion for the general population. Currently only triggered by elevated triglycerides. Candidate for a new always-show lifestyle suggestion.

## Feature 8: Reduce Sodium

**Guideline position:** Sodium intake raises blood pressure. Reducing sodium lowers BP in both nonhypertensive and hypertensive individuals [76]. Effects tend to be greater in Black individuals, middle/older-aged adults, and people with hypertension and diabetes. Potassium has an independent BP-lowering effect [79]. A combined approach of reducing sodium and increasing potassium is supported for hypertension prevention and control [80]. Potassium-enriched salt substitutes are an option but have questions about relevance when major sodium source is commercially prepared foods, and theoretical hyperkalemia risk in people with impaired renal function [82].

**Brad's position:** Aligns. The algorithm has two relevant suggestions:
- `low-salt`: triggered when systolic BP > 120 (age < 65) or > 130 (age ≥ 65). Target: <1,500 mg/day (ACC/AHA 2017). The AHA 2026 paper does not specify a single number but references the 2025 AHA/ACC BP guideline which recommends avoiding sodium for BP prevention/treatment [88].
- `high-potassium`: triggered when eGFR ≥ 45 (safe kidney function). The ISH recommends 3,500-5,000 mg/day from food sources. The algorithm correctly gates this behind adequate renal function.

Both the AHA and Brad's algorithm recognise the sodium-potassium interplay and the need for renal function assessment before potassium supplementation.

**References:**
- Ma 2022, 24-hour urinary sodium/potassium and CVD risk, NEJM (DOI: 10.1056/NEJMoa2109794)
- Neal 2021, salt substitution and CV events/death, NEJM (DOI: 10.1056/NEJMoa2105675)
- Sacks 2001, DASH-Sodium trial, NEJM (DOI: 10.1056/NEJM200101043440101)

## Feature 9: Alcohol

**Guideline position:** If alcohol is not consumed, do not start. If consumed, limit intake. The AHA notes that recent Mendelian randomization studies have called into question the protective association of moderate alcohol intake and coronary heart disease [84-86]. The relationship between alcohol and blood pressure is linear and progressive from the lowest intake levels [83,87]. The 2025 AHA/ACC BP guideline recommends avoiding alcohol for BP prevention/treatment [88]. The US DHHS and WHO advocate avoiding alcohol to reduce cancer risk [89,90]. Binge/heavy drinking should be strongly discouraged.

**Brad's position:** Aligns fully — the best amount of alcohol is none. Mendelian randomization data has dismantled the "moderate drinking is protective" hypothesis, and the linear relationship between alcohol and blood pressure starts at the lowest intake levels. The 2025 AHA/ACC BP guideline and the US Surgeon General's cancer advisory both support avoidance.

**Algorithm gap:** The `reduce-alcohol` suggestion is currently only triggered by obesity or elevated triglycerides. It should be promoted to an always-show lifestyle suggestion to align with Brad's position and the AHA 2026/2025 BP guideline consensus.

**References:**
- Piano 2025, AHA scientific statement on alcohol and CVD (DOI: 10.1161/CIR.0000000000001341)
- Biddinger 2022, habitual alcohol and CVD risk, Mendelian randomization (DOI: 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.3849)

## Additional Benefits of Heart-Healthy Dietary Patterns

The AHA notes that following the 9 features:
- **Fulfils essential nutrient requirements** for most individuals, obviating the need for dietary supplements (except for pregnant women, some older adults, and those following restricted diets)
- **Is rich in fibre** — supporting gastrointestinal function, gut microbiota, and blood glucose regulation
- **Limits foods high in cholesterol** — dietary cholesterol is no longer a primary CVD target, but heart-healthy patterns are naturally low in high-cholesterol foods. Moderate egg consumption is acceptable.
- **Facilitates saturated fat intake <10% of energy**
- **Is consistent with other chronic disease dietary recommendations** — type 2 diabetes, some cancers, kidney disease, cognitive health [100-108]

### Brad's position on supplements vs diet

The AHA notes that a healthy dietary pattern meets nutrient requirements for most individuals and that there is no evidence of reduced CVD risk from supplement use. Brad agrees — there is no strong evidence that multivitamin supplementation reduces cardiovascular events.

However, Brad's position is that while a healthy diet is foundational, specific supplementation provides additional evidence-based benefits for outcomes beyond CVD — particularly for nutrients that are difficult to obtain in optimal amounts from food alone:

- **Vitamin D** (1000 IU): most people are insufficient, especially at higher latitudes. Supplementation corrects deficiency safely.
- **Magnesium**: intakes are commonly below the RDA. Magnesium taurate in MicroVitamin supports cardiovascular and metabolic health.
- **Omega-3**: the VITAL trial showed a 28% reduction in myocardial infarction, and omega-3 is necessary for B-vitamins to exert their neuroprotective effect (Jerneren 2015, Oulhaj 2016). See Feature 4 divergence above.
- **Multivitamin/mineral**: the COSMOS trial (Baker 2023) demonstrated that daily multivitamin/mineral supplementation (Centrum Silver vs placebo) improved cognitive function in older adults over 2-3 years — providing justification for broad micronutrient support beyond cardiovascular endpoints.
- **Collagen**: Kim 2022 RCT showed reduced skin wrinkles after 12 weeks of supplementation.
- **Creatine**: evidence supports muscle preservation, cognitive function, and exercise performance — difficult to obtain in adequate amounts from diet alone without high red meat intake.

Brad's approach: diet first, supplements to fill specific evidence-based gaps. See the algorithm's supplement recommendations (`supplement-microvitamin`, `supplement-omega3`, `supplement-sleep`) and products.md for full clinical rationale and ingredient details.

## Priority Foods: Lean Protein Rich in Fibre and Potassium

### Why legumes are the cornerstone

Legumes (lentils, chickpeas, beans, edamame) are uniquely positioned in heart-healthy dietary patterns because they simultaneously address three AHA features:
- **Protein source shift** (Feature 4): plant protein replacing red/processed meat, associated with lower CVD risk [33-35]
- **Fibre** (Feature 3): 7-8g fibre per cup cooked, contributing to the 25-35g/day target
- **Potassium** (Feature 8): 350-700mg potassium per cup cooked, supporting the ISH target of 3,500-5,000mg/day

Legumes are also core components of all three named heart-healthy dietary patterns (DASH, Mediterranean, Portfolio — see below).

**Brad's position:** Aligns strongly. The algorithm's `lipid-diet` section includes edamame (soy protein for 3-5% LDL reduction) and recommends beans, lentils, and chickpeas as "additional options" for patients without IBS/IBD. The `protein-target` suggestion should emphasise legumes as the preferred protein source.

**Practical targets:** Aim for at least 3-4 servings per week (1 serving = ½ cup cooked). The DASH diet recommends 4-5 servings of nuts, seeds, and legumes per week.

### IBS/IBD caveat

Legumes are high-FODMAP (contain galacto-oligosaccharides) and may trigger bloating, gas, and abdominal pain in IBS patients. For patients with IBS or IBD:

- **IBS-safe alternatives from Brad's algorithm:** Oats (beta-glucan, 5-10% LDL reduction), walnuts (unsaturated fat + ALA, 3-7% LDL reduction), almonds (unsaturated fat + plant sterols, 3-5% LDL reduction), ground flaxseed (soluble fibre + ALA, 5-10% LDL reduction). All are low-FODMAP at recommended amounts.
- **IBS-safe protein sources:** Fish, eggs, tofu (firm, drained), tempeh, lean poultry.
- **Fibre:** Soluble fibre (psyllium husk) is ACG-recommended for IBS. Insoluble fibre may worsen symptoms.
- See "Dietary Management of IBS" section below for full guidance.

## Named Dietary Patterns

### DASH Diet (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension)

The DASH diet is one of the AHA's recommended heart-healthy dietary patterns [20]. It was specifically designed to lower blood pressure and has strong RCT evidence from the DASH-Sodium trial [80].

**Composition (2,000-calorie diet):**

| Food Group | Daily Servings |
|---|---|
| Grains (mostly whole grains) | 6-8 |
| Vegetables | 4-5 |
| Fruits | 4-5 |
| Low-fat or fat-free dairy | 2-3 |
| Lean meats, poultry, fish | ≤6 oz |
| Fats and oils | 2-3 |
| **Weekly:** Nuts, seeds, legumes | 4-5 |
| **Weekly:** Sweets | ≤5 |

**Sodium targets:** Standard 2,300 mg/day; enhanced 1,500 mg/day for greater BP reduction.

**Key nutrients emphasised:** Potassium, calcium, magnesium, fibre, protein — all from food sources.

**Brad's position:** Aligns. The algorithm's `low-salt` suggestion targets <1,500 mg/day sodium (the enhanced DASH target) for patients with elevated BP. The `high-potassium` suggestion (gated by eGFR ≥ 45) aligns with DASH's potassium emphasis.

**References:**
- Sacks 2001, DASH-Sodium trial, NEJM (DOI: 10.1056/NEJM200101043440101)
- NHLBI DASH Eating Plan (nhlbi.nih.gov/education/dash-eating-plan)

### Mediterranean Diet

The Mediterranean dietary pattern is recommended by the AHA as a heart-healthy pattern [9,20]. The PREDIMED trial demonstrated primary prevention of cardiovascular disease with a Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil or nuts [9].

**Key components:**
- High intake of vegetables, fruits, legumes, nuts, whole grains
- Extra-virgin olive oil as primary fat source
- Moderate intake of fish and seafood
- Low to moderate intake of dairy (mostly fermented: yoghurt, cheese)
- Low intake of red meat and processed meat
- Moderate wine consumption with meals (however, see Feature 9 — Brad's position is that the best amount of alcohol is none)

**Brad's position:** Aligns with the dietary pattern, diverges on alcohol. The algorithm's `lipid-diet` section recommends EVOO specifically ("monounsaturated fat replacing butter/saturated fat"). The Mediterranean pattern's emphasis on legumes, nuts, fish, and olive oil aligns closely with Brad's priority foods.

**References:**
- Estruch 2018, PREDIMED trial, NEJM (DOI: 10.1056/NEJMoa1800389)

### Portfolio Diet

The Portfolio Diet is a targeted LDL-lowering dietary pattern. Brad's algorithm uses Portfolio Diet foods directly in the `lipid-diet` section (Section 4b).

**Specific foods and expected LDL reductions:**

| Food | Mechanism | Daily Amount | Expected LDL Reduction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Oats | Beta-glucan binds bile acids | 75g dry (~3g beta-glucan) | 5-10% |
| Walnuts | Unsaturated fats, ALA omega-3 | ~40g | 3-7% |
| Almonds | Unsaturated fats, plant sterols | ~45g | 3-5% |
| Ground flaxseed | Soluble fibre + ALA omega-3 | ~30g | 5-10% |
| Edamame | Soy protein replacing animal protein | — | 3-5% |
| Extra-virgin olive oil | Monounsaturated fat replacing saturated fat | — | Modest |
| Psyllium husk | Soluble, gel-forming fibre | 7g/day | 5-10% |

**Combined effect:** These dietary changes can reduce LDL cholesterol by 15-25% [Jenkins 2011].

**Brad's position:** The algorithm implements this directly. The `lipid-diet` suggestion is triggered when any atherogenic marker is borderline or above (ApoB ≥ 0.5 g/L, Non-HDL ≥ 4.14 mmol/L, or LDL-C ≥ 3.36 mmol/L). Foods are categorised as "Universal (IBS/IBD-safe)" and "Additional options (no IBS/IBD)" — the universal foods are all low-FODMAP at recommended amounts.

## Dietary Management of IBS

Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS) affects 10-15% of adults globally and is one of the most common reasons people seek dietary guidance. Dietary management is first-line therapy.

### Low-FODMAP Diet

FODMAPs (fermentable oligosaccharides, disaccharides, monosaccharides, and polyols) are short-chain carbohydrates that are poorly absorbed and rapidly fermented in the colon, causing bloating, gas, abdominal pain, and altered bowel habits in susceptible individuals.

**ACG 2021 recommendation:** The American College of Gastroenterology conditionally recommends a limited trial of a low-FODMAP diet to improve global IBS symptoms (conditional recommendation, very low quality of evidence) [Lacy 2021].

**Three phases:**
1. **Elimination (2-6 weeks):** Remove high-FODMAP foods — legumes, wheat, onion, garlic, dairy (lactose), stone fruits, apples, honey, artificial sweeteners (sorbitol, mannitol)
2. **Reintroduction (6-8 weeks):** Systematically reintroduce one FODMAP group at a time to identify personal triggers
3. **Personalisation:** Long-term diet based on individual tolerance — most patients can tolerate some FODMAP groups

**Important:** The low-FODMAP diet is an elimination diet, not a permanent diet. Long-term restriction can reduce gut microbiota diversity and fibre intake. Patients should work with a dietitian for guided reintroduction.

### Fibre and Protein for IBS Patients

**Fibre:**
- **Soluble fibre (recommended):** Psyllium husk is ACG-recommended for IBS — it is soluble, gel-forming, and minimally fermented, reducing gas production. Dose: 5-7g/day, titrated slowly.
- **Insoluble fibre (caution):** Wheat bran and other insoluble fibres may worsen bloating and pain in IBS patients. Should not be the primary fibre source.
- **Brad's product connection:** MicroVitamin+ Powder includes Psyllium Husk 2.5g per serve — an ACG-recommended, IBS-safe fibre source.

**Protein sources for IBS patients:**
The AHA's priority foods (legumes) are high-FODMAP. IBS-safe protein alternatives that still support heart health:
- Fish and seafood (omega-3 fatty acids, AHA Feature 4b)
- Eggs (moderate consumption acceptable per AHA)
- Tofu (firm, drained — low FODMAP in standard servings)
- Tempeh (fermented, generally well-tolerated)
- Lean poultry (AHA Feature 4d)
- Canned/rinsed lentils (lower FODMAP than dried — some patients tolerate ½ cup)

**Brad's algorithm IBS-safe foods:** The `lipid-diet` section categorises foods as "Universal (IBS/IBD-safe, low-FODMAP at recommended amounts)" — oats, walnuts, almonds, ground flaxseed, edamame, EVOO — and "Additional options (no IBS/IBD)" — beans, lentils, chickpeas, mixed vegetables.

**References:**

## Dietary Considerations in IBD

Inflammatory bowel disease (Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis) involves chronic inflammation of the GI tract. Dietary management is more complex than IBS because:
- Nutritional deficiencies are common (iron, B12, vitamin D, zinc, folate)
- Stricturing disease may require low-fibre diets during flares
- Some dietary patterns may help maintain remission

### Key dietary considerations

**During active flares:**
- Low-residue/low-fibre diet may reduce symptoms (especially with strictures)
- Adequate protein intake is critical (increased requirements due to inflammation)
- Monitor for malabsorption — especially fat-soluble vitamins, B12 (ileal Crohn's), iron

**During remission:**
- Mediterranean diet pattern has emerging evidence for maintaining remission in Crohn's disease
- Specific carbohydrate diet (SCD) and Crohn's Disease Exclusion Diet (CDED) have limited but promising evidence
- Gradual fibre reintroduction is generally recommended when tolerated

**Brad's position:** IBD dietary management should be individualised with gastroenterology and dietetics input. The algorithm's IBS/IBD safety categorisation in `lipid-diet` provides safe baseline food choices. Patients with IBD should discuss dietary changes with their doctor.

**Algorithm link:** The `lipid-diet` section's IBS/IBD-safe food list provides the safe starting point. Supplements (MicroVitamin) may help address micronutrient deficiencies common in IBD — particularly B12, vitamin D, zinc, and folate.

**References:**

## Common Dietary Questions

### Eggs

**Guideline position:** The AHA 2026 states that dietary cholesterol is no longer a primary target for CVD risk reduction. Heart-healthy dietary patterns are naturally low in high-cholesterol foods, but moderate egg consumption can be included as part of a heart-healthy dietary pattern [99]. The AHA's 2020 science advisory on dietary cholesterol concluded that evidence does not support a specific cholesterol intake limit for most people [99].

**Brad's position:** Aligns. Eggs are a nutrient-dense, affordable protein source. Moderate consumption (up to ~1 egg per day for most people) is acceptable within a heart-healthy dietary pattern. The key consideration is what eggs replace — if they replace processed meat at breakfast (bacon, sausage), that's a net positive. If they're consumed with large amounts of processed meat, the overall pattern matters more than the eggs themselves.

**References:**
- Carson 2020, AHA science advisory on dietary cholesterol (DOI: 10.1161/CIR.0000000000000743)

### Coffee and Caffeine

**Guideline position:** The AHA 2026 dietary guidance does not specifically address coffee or caffeine. However, moderate coffee consumption (3-5 cups/day) has been consistently associated with neutral to reduced CVD risk in large prospective cohort studies. The 2025 AHA/ACC blood pressure guideline does not recommend caffeine restriction for blood pressure management in habitual coffee drinkers.

**Brad's position:** Moderate coffee consumption is acceptable and may be beneficial. Black coffee and unsweetened tea are consistent with heart-healthy dietary patterns. The concern is what people add to coffee — sugar, flavoured syrups, and full-fat cream can turn a neutral beverage into a significant source of added sugars and saturated fat. For detailed evidence, see Dr. Stanfield's blog articles on coffee and health.

### Intermittent Fasting

**Guideline position:** The AHA 2026 paper does not specifically address intermittent fasting (IF). The 2023 AHA scientific statement on meal timing and cardiovascular health noted that time-restricted eating patterns may have metabolic benefits, but long-term cardiovascular outcomes data are limited. Some popular weight loss diets (including IF patterns) "may result in short-term benefits but have uncertain long-term cardiovascular impact" [21].

**Brad's position:** Intermittent fasting can be a useful tool for energy balance (Feature 1) and metabolic health, but it's not inherently superior to other dietary patterns for cardiovascular outcomes. The quality of food consumed during eating windows matters more than the timing. Patients on insulin or sulfonylureas, those with eating disorders, or who are pregnant should not fast without medical supervision. For detailed evidence and practical guidance, see Dr. Stanfield's blog articles on intermittent fasting.
