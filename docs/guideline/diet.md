# Diet: Clinical Dietary Guidance

*Source: AHA 2026 Dietary Guidance to Improve Cardiovascular Health (DOI: 10.1161/CIR.0000000000001435)*

This guideline covers the 9 features of a heart-healthy dietary pattern as defined by the AHA 2026 Scientific Statement, cross-referenced with Dr. Stanfield's Health Roadmap algorithm.

---

## Dietary Principles

The AHA emphasises dietary *patterns* over single foods or nutrients. Heart-healthy dietary patterns contain primarily vegetables and fruits, whole grains, healthy sources of protein, liquid nontropical plant oils, and minimally processed foods. Dietary patterns higher in plant-based foods and lower in animal products are associated with lower coronary heart disease risk and more favourable metabolic profiles [3].

Heart-healthy dietary patterns should be established early in life (from age 1 year) and maintained across the life course. The guidance applies regardless of where food is procured, prepared, or consumed.

## Feature 1: Energy Balance & Body Weight

**Guideline position:** Adjusting energy intake and expenditure to achieve and maintain a healthy body weight is central to cardiovascular health. Obesity affects 21% of children/adolescents and 40% of US adults. Excess body fat contributes to type 2 diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular-kidney metabolic syndrome [17]. Adults should engage in at least 150 min/wk of moderate to vigorous physical activity [18]. A range of dietary patterns (DASH, Mediterranean, pescetarian, ovo/lacto vegetarian) support cardiovascular health and healthy weight when implemented as intended [20]. Some popular weight loss diets may result in short-term benefits but have uncertain long-term cardiovascular impact and can worsen cardiovascular risk factors [21].

**Brad's position:** Aligns with AHA. The algorithm uses BMI and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) as a composite assessment. A BMI 25-29 patient with WHtR < 0.5 is reclassified as normal weight (not overweight). For patients meeting obesity criteria (BMI ≥ 30, or BMI ≥ 25 with WHtR ≥ 0.5), the algorithm recommends lifestyle intervention first, then a GLP-1 medication cascade (semaglutide → dose increase → switch → SGLT2i → metformin). Brad recommends 150+ min/wk cardio plus 2-3 resistance training sessions.

**Algorithm links:** `weight-*` suggestions, BMI/WHtR thresholds, GLP-1 cascade (Section 6), `exercise` suggestion.

**References:**
- US Physical Activity Guidelines, 2nd ed., 2018 (DOI: odphp.health.gov)
- Paluch 2024, AHA scientific statement on resistance exercise (DOI: 10.1161/CIR.0000000000001189)

## Feature 2: Vegetables and Fruits

**Guideline position:** Whole or minimally processed vegetables and fruits are core components of heart-healthy dietary patterns. Consistently associated with cardiovascular health including blood lipids, blood pressure, and type 2 diabetes control [22-26]. Provide essential nutrients and dietary fibre. Fresh, frozen, and canned forms are all acceptable — if frozen or canned, preferably without added sugars or sodium.

**Brad's position:** Aligns with AHA. The algorithm does not currently have a standalone vegetables/fruits suggestion, though the `lipid-diet` section recommends specific plant foods (edamame, beans, lentils) for lipid management, and the `high-potassium` suggestion encourages vegetable and fruit consumption for blood pressure.

**Algorithm gap:** No standalone "eat more vegetables and fruits" suggestion. This is a candidate for a new always-show lifestyle suggestion.

**References:**
- Wang 2021, fruit/vegetable intake and mortality, 2 cohorts + 26-cohort meta-analysis (DOI: 10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.120.048996)
- Geravand 2025, fruit/vegetable and mortality in T2D, systematic review (DOI: 10.1093/nutrit/nuaf013)

## Feature 3: Whole Grains

**Guideline position:** Whole grains (whole wheat, oats, brown rice, quinoa, barley, rye) contain all 3 components of the grain kernel — endosperm, germ, and bran — providing dietary fibre, vitamins, minerals, and bioactive compounds [27]. Regular consumption is associated with lower risks of CVD, coronary heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes, and metabolic syndrome [28]. RCTs show replacing refined grains with whole grains improves cardiovascular risk factors [29]. Diets high in fibre and whole grains are linked to favourable gut microbiota modulation and reduced inflammatory cytokines [30,31].

**Brad's position:** Aligns with AHA. The algorithm recommends 25-35g fibre daily (`fiber` suggestion) and oats specifically in the `lipid-diet` section (75g dry oats/day provides 3g beta-glucan for 5-10% LDL reduction). However, there is no standalone whole grains suggestion — the fibre recommendation is general, not grain-specific.

**Algorithm gap:** No standalone "choose whole grains over refined grains" suggestion. The `fiber` suggestion covers the fibre component but not the broader whole grain recommendation.

**References:**
- Hu 2023, whole grain consumption and CVD risk, dose-response meta-analysis (DOI: 10.1016/j.ajcnut.2022.10.010)
- Huang 2025, whole grain consumption and metabolic health, umbrella review (DOI: 10.1039/d5fo03240g)

## Feature 4: Healthy Protein Sources

**Guideline position:** Recommendations centre on promoting heart-healthy protein *sources* rather than protein quantity. The relationship between protein quantity and cardiovascular health is uncertain [32].

**4a. Plant sources (legumes and nuts):** Dietary patterns higher in legumes and lower in red/processed meat are associated with lower CVD and coronary heart disease risk [33-35]. Nuts are associated with lower CVD and all-cause mortality risk [36,37]. Plant-based meat alternatives require caution — many are ultraprocessed with added sugars, sodium, and preservatives [38].

**4b. Fish and seafood:** Dietary patterns containing nonfried fish/seafood are associated with lower CVD events and MI risk [39-41]. Fish oil supplementation alone has not been demonstrated to lower CVD risk in otherwise healthy adults and may increase atrial fibrillation risk [42-44].

**4c. Dairy:** Low-fat dairy in dietary patterns is associated with lower CVD risk [23]. Evidence on full-fat vs low-fat dairy and CVD is limited and inconclusive [45]. Continue prior guidance: replace dairy fat with unsaturated fat sources, choose nonfat or low-fat dairy [1].

**4d. Red and processed meat:** Replacing red meat (including lean, unprocessed) with healthier protein sources, especially plant sources, improves CVD risk factors [22,49]. Associations strongest for processed meat. Processed meats include bacon, sausage, hot dogs, deli meats. Approaches: choose lean cuts, limit portion size and frequency [50-52].

**Brad's position:** Partially aligns. The algorithm recommends 1.2g/kg IBW daily protein (0.9g/kg if eGFR < 45) but focuses on *amount* rather than *source*. The `lipid-diet` section recommends specific plant protein sources (edamame, legumes) for lipid management.

**Divergence on omega-3 supplementation:** The AHA states fish oil supplementation "has not been demonstrated to lower CVD risk in otherwise healthy adults" and may increase AF risk. Brad recommends omega-3 supplementation for all users (`supplement-omega3`) based on two lines of evidence: (1) The VITAL trial (Manson 2019, NEJM) showed a 28% reduction in myocardial infarction with marine omega-3, and the Bernasconi 2021 meta-analysis confirmed reduced cardiovascular events and cardiac death with benefits increasing at higher doses. (2) Cognitive protection — Jerneren 2015 and Oulhaj 2016 found that B-vitamin supplementation slowed brain atrophy by 40%, but only in participants with adequate omega-3 levels, suggesting omega-3 is necessary for B-vitamins to exert their neuroprotective effect. Brad weighs the MI reduction data plus the B-vitamin cognitive synergy as supporting supplementation despite the AHA's cautious position on overall CVD risk.

**Algorithm gap:** No suggestion to shift from animal to plant protein sources. The protein-target suggestion is quantity-focused, not source-focused.

**References:**
- Naghshi 2020, protein intake and mortality, systematic review (DOI: 10.1136/bmj.m2412)
- Bernasconi 2021, omega-3 and CV events, meta-analysis (DOI: 10.1016/j.mayocp.2021.02.008)
- Manson 2019, VITAL trial, marine omega-3 and CVD/cancer (DOI: 10.1056/NEJMoa1811403)

## Feature 5: Unsaturated Fats Over Saturated Fats

**Guideline position:** Replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat and, to a lesser extent, monounsaturated fat reduces LDL cholesterol — a causal risk factor for CVD [53,54]. Modeling analyses show replacing saturated fat with polyunsaturated fat is associated with reduced coronary heart disease risk [55,56].

Sources of saturated fat: animal fats (beef tallow, butter), tropical oils (coconut oil, cocoa butter, palm oil). Sources of unsaturated fat: nontropical plant oils (soybean, canola, olive oils). Strong evidence shows replacing butter with plant oils/spreads decreases LDL [22]. Replacement of tropical plant oils with nontropical plant oils also lowers LDL [22,62].

Target: dietary patterns adhering to all 9 features are unlikely to exceed 10% of energy from saturated fat [20,22].

**Brad's position:** Aligns. The `lipid-diet` section specifically recommends "extra-virgin olive oil — monounsaturated fat replacing butter/saturated fat" with a "modest direct LDL effect." The algorithm frames this as a specific dietary intervention for elevated lipids rather than a general population recommendation.

**References:**
- Sacks 2017, AHA presidential advisory on dietary fats and CVD (DOI: 10.1161/CIR.0000000000000510)
- Zhang 2025, butter vs plant-based oils and mortality (DOI: 10.1001/jamainternmed.2025.0205)

## Feature 6: Minimally Processed Foods Over Ultraprocessed Foods

**Guideline position:** Strong evidence links dietary patterns high in ultraprocessed foods to overweight/obesity, CVDs, type 2 diabetes, and all-cause mortality [67-70]. The AHA uses the term "ultraprocessed foods" (referencing Nova classification) and acknowledges limited evidence for specific mechanisms of harm, potentially due to the wide range of causal factors in the broad ultraprocessed category. Efforts should promote minimally processed foods and shift away from ultraprocessed foods in the marketplace [71].

**Brad's position:** Aligns in principle. The algorithm does not have a standalone ultraprocessed food suggestion, but the dietary pattern implied by the `lipid-diet` foods (oats, nuts, edamame, EVOO, flaxseed) is inherently whole-food and minimally processed.

**Algorithm gap:** No standalone "avoid ultraprocessed foods" suggestion. Candidate for a new always-show lifestyle suggestion.

**References:**
- Monteiro 2025, ultra-processed foods and human health, Lancet (DOI: 10.1016/S0140-6736(25)01565-X)
- AHA Science Advisory on ultraprocessed foods, 2025 (DOI: 10.1161/CIR.0000000000001365)

## Feature 7: Minimize Added Sugars

**Guideline position:** Strong evidence supports minimising added sugar intake across the life course [22,55]. Sugar-sweetened beverages are linked to higher risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, coronary heart disease, and cardiovascular mortality [73,74]. Adults consuming ≥25% of energy from added sugars have a nearly 3-fold higher risk of CVD mortality compared with those consuming <10% [75].

**Brad's position:** Aligns. The `trig-nutrition` suggestion recommends "reducing alcohol, sugar, and total calorie intake" for elevated triglycerides (≥1.69 mmol/L). However, sugar reduction is framed as a triglyceride intervention rather than a universal dietary recommendation.

**Algorithm gap:** No standalone "minimize added sugars" suggestion for the general population. Currently only triggered by elevated triglycerides. Candidate for a new always-show lifestyle suggestion.

**References:**
- Yang 2014, added sugar intake and CVD mortality (DOI: 10.1001/jamainternmed.2013.13563)
- Malik 2019, sugar-sweetened beverages and mortality (DOI: 10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.118.037401)

## Feature 8: Reduce Sodium

**Guideline position:** Sodium intake raises blood pressure. Reducing sodium lowers BP in both nonhypertensive and hypertensive individuals [76]. Effects tend to be greater in Black individuals, middle/older-aged adults, and people with hypertension and diabetes. Potassium has an independent BP-lowering effect [79]. A combined approach of reducing sodium and increasing potassium is supported for hypertension prevention and control [80]. Potassium-enriched salt substitutes are an option but have questions about relevance when major sodium source is commercially prepared foods, and theoretical hyperkalemia risk in people with impaired renal function [82].

**Brad's position:** Aligns. The algorithm has two relevant suggestions:
- `low-salt`: triggered when systolic BP > 120 (age < 65) or > 130 (age ≥ 65). Target: <1,500 mg/day (ACC/AHA 2017). The AHA 2026 paper does not specify a single number but references the 2025 AHA/ACC BP guideline which recommends avoiding sodium for BP prevention/treatment [88].
- `high-potassium`: triggered when eGFR ≥ 45 (safe kidney function). The ISH recommends 3,500-5,000 mg/day from food sources. The algorithm correctly gates this behind adequate renal function.

Both the AHA and Brad's algorithm recognise the sodium-potassium interplay and the need for renal function assessment before potassium supplementation.

**References:**
- Ma 2022, 24-hour urinary sodium/potassium and CVD risk, NEJM (DOI: 10.1056/NEJMoa2109794)
- Neal 2021, salt substitution and CV events/death, NEJM (DOI: 10.1056/NEJMoa2105675)
- Sacks 2001, DASH-Sodium trial, NEJM (DOI: 10.1056/NEJM200101043440101)

## Feature 9: Alcohol

**Guideline position:** If alcohol is not consumed, do not start. If consumed, limit intake. The AHA notes that recent Mendelian randomization studies have called into question the protective association of moderate alcohol intake and coronary heart disease [84-86]. The relationship between alcohol and blood pressure is linear and progressive from the lowest intake levels [83,87]. The 2025 AHA/ACC BP guideline recommends avoiding alcohol for BP prevention/treatment [88]. The US DHHS and WHO advocate avoiding alcohol to reduce cancer risk [89,90]. Binge/heavy drinking should be strongly discouraged.

**Brad's position:** Aligns fully — the best amount of alcohol is none. Mendelian randomization data has dismantled the "moderate drinking is protective" hypothesis, and the linear relationship between alcohol and blood pressure starts at the lowest intake levels. The 2025 AHA/ACC BP guideline and the US Surgeon General's cancer advisory both support avoidance.

**Algorithm gap:** The `reduce-alcohol` suggestion is currently only triggered by obesity or elevated triglycerides. It should be promoted to an always-show lifestyle suggestion to align with Brad's position and the AHA 2026/2025 BP guideline consensus.

**References:**
- Piano 2025, AHA scientific statement on alcohol and CVD (DOI: 10.1161/CIR.0000000000001341)
- Biddinger 2022, habitual alcohol and CVD risk, Mendelian randomization (DOI: 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2022.3849)

## Additional Benefits of Heart-Healthy Dietary Patterns

The AHA notes that following the 9 features:
- **Fulfils essential nutrient requirements** for most individuals, obviating the need for dietary supplements (except for pregnant women, some older adults, and those following restricted diets)
- **Is rich in fibre** — supporting gastrointestinal function, gut microbiota, and blood glucose regulation
- **Limits foods high in cholesterol** — dietary cholesterol is no longer a primary CVD target, but heart-healthy patterns are naturally low in high-cholesterol foods. Moderate egg consumption is acceptable.
- **Facilitates saturated fat intake <10% of energy**
- **Is consistent with other chronic disease dietary recommendations** — type 2 diabetes, some cancers, kidney disease, cognitive health [100-108]
