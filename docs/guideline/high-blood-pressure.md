# High Blood Pressure: Targets, Home Readings and Treatment

*Written from the 2025 AHA/ACC, 2024 ESC, NICE, New Zealand and Australian hypertension guidelines, FDA drug labels, NIH pages and primary trials, plus Brad's posts and the Health Roadmap algorithm. Full list at the end.*

## In short

- Blood pressure is given as two numbers: systolic (the pressure as the heart beats) over diastolic (the pressure between beats), in mmHg.
- High blood pressure rarely causes symptoms, so the only way to know is to measure it [9].
- The lower your usual blood pressure, down to at least 115/75, the lower your risk of dying from stroke and heart disease, in large observational data [12].
- Salt reduction, potassium-rich food, weight loss, exercise and less alcohol all lower blood pressure in trials [17][18][21][22][23].
- When lifestyle is not enough, four classes of medicine are first choices: ACE inhibitors, ARBs, calcium channel blockers and thiazide-type diuretics [4][5].
- Guidelines disagree on the target. Brad's view: under 120/80 if you are under 65, under 130/80 from 65 (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

## Get help now

- Blood pressure of 180 systolic or more, or 120 diastolic or more (either number on its own), is dangerously high. With symptoms such as sudden severe chest, back or belly pain, shortness of breath, a severe headache or vision changes, seek immediate medical attention: call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [9][10].
- For chest pain or signs of a stroke (face drooping, arm or leg weakness or numbness, trouble speaking, vision loss, even if it passes, or a sudden severe headache), call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).
- On an ACE inhibitor or ARB: for trouble breathing or swelling of the face or throat, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [24].
- On blood pressure medicine: if you feel very unwell, confused or faint, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department [11].

## What it is

High blood pressure (hypertension, often written HTN) means the pressure in your arteries stays raised over time. It affects about 45% of US adults [7]. It is a major cause of heart attack, stroke, heart failure and chronic kidney disease [7], and the 2025 US guideline also lists atrial fibrillation and dementia [2].

The risk rises steadily with the numbers. A pooled analysis of 61 cohorts with one million adults found no threshold down to at least 115/75 mmHg. At ages 40 to 69, each 20 mmHg higher usual systolic pressure went with more than twice the stroke death rate [12].

Lowering blood pressure lowers that risk. A meta-analysis of 123 trials (613,815 people) found that each 10 mmHg fall in systolic pressure reduced major cardiovascular events (relative risk 0.80), stroke (0.73), heart failure (0.72) and death from any cause (0.87) [13].

## Your numbers

The Health Roadmap uses these blood pressure tiers, in mmHg (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). Blood pressure has the same units everywhere.

| Tier | Systolic | | Diastolic |
|---|---|---|---|
| Elevated | 120 or more | | |
| Stage 1 | 130 or more | or | above 80 |
| Stage 2 | 140 or more | or | 90 or more |
| Crisis | 180 or more | or | 120 or more |

- Either number on its own can put you in a tier. The plan counts a diastolic above 80 (81 or more) as raised.
- **Target under 65:** below 120/80. **Target at 65 or older:** below 130/80.
- **Crisis:** a reading of 180/120 or more on either number is dangerously high; with symptoms, get help now (above). Without symptoms it is still dangerously high and urgent (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). Wait 5 minutes and recheck; if it is still that high, contact your doctor the same day [9].
- The plan's potassium advice (3,500 to 5,000 mg a day from food) shows only when kidney function is good enough, an eGFR of 45 or more.

These tiers follow the US categories, which call 120 to 129 systolic elevated, 130 to 139 or 80 to 89 stage 1, and 140 or 90 and above stage 2 [2]. Other countries draw the lines differently (see "Where guidelines differ").

### Measuring at home

Clinic readings can mislead. Some people read high only in the clinic ("white coat" hypertension); others read normal in the clinic but high at home ("masked" hypertension), and that second group carries a substantially higher cardiovascular risk [8]. US and UK guidance both ask for readings outside the clinic to confirm the diagnosis before treatment starts [4][7].

- Use a validated, automated monitor with a cuff that goes around your upper arm. Upper-arm cuffs are more accurate than wrist or finger devices [11]. The cuff should fit snugly but not be too small [11].
- Sit and rest first. Caffeine, alcohol, tobacco and exercise all change the reading [11].
- To confirm a diagnosis, NICE asks for 2 readings at least 1 minute apart, seated, twice a day, ideally for 7 days. Drop the first day and average the rest [4].
- Home averages run lower than clinic readings. NICE treats a home or daytime ambulatory average of 135/85 or more as confirming hypertension after a clinic reading of 140/90 or more [4].

## What helps

Every guideline starts here, at every blood pressure level [1][2][5].

**Salt (sodium).** In 85 trials, blood pressure fell in a roughly straight line as sodium intake fell, with no floor at the lowest intakes; people with hypertension saw a steeper fall [17]. In the DASH-Sodium trial, a DASH diet with low sodium gave a systolic pressure 11.5 mmHg lower than a typical high-sodium diet in people with hypertension, and 7.1 mmHg lower in people without it [18]. US guidance recommends under 2,300 mg of sodium a day, moving toward an ideal limit of under 1,500 mg [2]. The diet guideline covers eating patterns in more detail.

**Potassium and salt substitutes.** The 2025 US guideline recommends moderate potassium supplementation, ideally from food, except in kidney disease or with medicines that reduce potassium excretion, where blood potassium should be checked [2]. It also says potassium-based salt substitutes (typically 25% to 30% potassium chloride) can be useful, with the same exceptions [2]. In a trial of 20,995 people in rural China, mostly with past stroke, a 75% sodium chloride and 25% potassium chloride salt cut stroke (rate ratio 0.86), major cardiovascular events (0.87) and death (0.88) over about 4.7 years [19]. A Cochrane review found these salts probably lower blood pressure slightly, and probably raise blood potassium slightly [20]. NICE says salt substitutes with potassium chloride should not be used by older people, people with diabetes, pregnant women, people with kidney disease, and people taking ACE inhibitors or ARBs [4]. ACE inhibitor and ARB labels say not to use potassium salt substitutes or supplements without asking your doctor [24][25]. See the [potassium page](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/potassium-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects) for foods and supplement safety.

**Weight.** In 25 trials, losing about 5 kg lowered blood pressure by 4.4/3.6 mmHg, about 1 mmHg per kilogram lost [22]. US guidance sets a target of at least 5% weight loss for people with overweight or obesity [2].

**Exercise.** A network meta-analysis of 270 trials (15,827 people) found every exercise type lowered resting blood pressure. Isometric training, in which muscles hold still under tension, such as a wall squat, lowered it most (8.2/4.0 mmHg), ahead of aerobic, resistance, combined and interval training (each about 4 to 6 mmHg systolic) [21]. The exercise guideline covers how to build a routine.

**Alcohol.** In 36 trials, cutting down lowered blood pressure in people who drank more than two drinks a day; the largest fall (5.5/4.0 mmHg) came when people who drank six or more a day roughly halved their intake. Cutting back from two or fewer drinks a day made no clear difference [23]. US guidance strongly recommends reducing or eliminating alcohol [2].

**Sleep and stress.** The Health Roadmap includes 7 to 9 hours of sleep among its blood pressure measures (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026); see the sleep guideline. US guidance rates meditation, breathing control and yoga as "may be reasonable" add-ons, a weak recommendation [2].

**Supplements.** Several supplements are studied for blood pressure, among them potassium, [beetroot and dietary nitrate](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/beetroot-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects), [garlic](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/garlic-benefits-best-forms-dosing-and-side-effects) and [cocoa flavanols](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/cocoa-flavanols-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects). Each has its own page with the evidence and safety notes. NICE advises against calcium, magnesium or potassium supplements as a way to lower blood pressure [4]. Some products raise it: large amounts of [licorice](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/licorice-and-dgl-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects) is one example. NSAID pain relievers such as ibuprofen carry a label warning to ask a doctor first if you have high blood pressure [28].

## Medicines

Medicine is added to lifestyle change, not used in its place. When guidelines recommend it differs by country (see "Where guidelines differ"). For stage 2 hypertension, the 2025 US guideline prefers starting two first-line medicines of different classes in one combination pill [2].

**The first-line classes** [4][5]:

- **ACE inhibitors** (for example lisinopril, perindopril, enalapril). Labelled side effects include cough, dizziness and headache [24]. They can cause angioedema, swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat, at any time during treatment; it can block the airway [24]. They can raise blood potassium, especially with kidney disease, diabetes, or potassium supplements or salt substitutes [24].
- **ARBs** (angiotensin II receptor blockers, for example losartan, candesartan, telmisartan). NICE uses an ARB when an ACE inhibitor causes cough [4]. ARB labels carry the same potassium cautions [25] and a warning about low blood pressure in people who are low on fluid or salt [25]. NICE advises never combining an ACE inhibitor with an ARB [4].
- **Calcium channel blockers** (for example amlodipine). Ankle swelling is the most common side effect, and it rises with the dose [26].
- **Thiazide-type diuretics** (for example chlorthalidone, indapamide, hydrochlorothiazide). Labelled effects include low potassium, low sodium and raised uric acid, which can trigger gout [27]. NICE prefers a thiazide-like diuretic such as indapamide [4].

Which class comes first depends on the guideline and the person. NICE, for example, starts an ACE inhibitor or ARB for people with type 2 diabetes, or aged under 55 and not of Black African or African-Caribbean family origin, and a calcium channel blocker for people 55 or older, or of Black African or African-Caribbean family origin, without diabetes [4]. Beta blockers are not among the four first-line classes in New Zealand guidance [5].

**Pregnancy.** ACE inhibitors and ARBs can harm an unborn baby. Their labels carry a boxed warning to stop them as soon as pregnancy is found [24][25].

**Kidney function.** How to start, dose and monitor ACE inhibitors and ARBs when kidney function is reduced is a decision for your doctor. This guide gives no doses for them: check with your doctor or pharmacist.

**When blood pressure stays high.** Resistant hypertension means blood pressure stays above target on an ACE inhibitor or ARB plus a calcium channel blocker and a thiazide-like diuretic at the best tolerated doses [4]. Before adding more, NICE confirms the readings at home or with ambulatory monitoring, checks for low blood pressure on standing, and asks about missed doses [4].

This guide gives no medicine doses. Your doctor or pharmacist sets them.

## Brad's view (reviewed 2 Nov 2026)

- **Targets.** The Health Roadmap aims for below 120/80 under 65 and below 130/80 from 65, based on the SPRINT and ESPRIT trials (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). The higher target from 65 balances the benefit of lower pressure against dizziness and falls from readings that are too low (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). In his [post on changing targets](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/blood-pressure-targets-just-changed), Brad writes that most of us should aim for a systolic pressure under 120, and that some older adults he sees in clinic need a slightly higher pressure to balance risks and benefits [29].
- **The evidence he leans on.** SPRINT (9,361 people at high risk without diabetes) stopped early: aiming for under 120 rather than under 140 lowered the main cardiovascular outcome (1.65% vs 2.19% a year; hazard ratio 0.75) and death from any cause (hazard ratio 0.73), with more fainting, low blood pressure, electrolyte problems and acute kidney injury [14]. ESPRIT (11,255 people, including people with diabetes or past stroke) found the same direction (hazard ratio 0.88), with more fainting [15].
- **Lifestyle first, medicine as an addition.** The stage 1 card asks for less sodium (under 1,500 mg a day), exercise (150 minutes a week or more) and 7 to 9 hours of sleep; when an adult age is known, the stage 2 card adds that medicine is typically recommended at that level (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). In his [brain health post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/a-healthy-blood-pressure-is-wrecking-your-brain-new-study), Brad says that when lifestyle and weight are in place and systolic pressure is still above 120, he discusses blood pressure medicine with his patients, as an addition to the other steps, never a replacement [30].
- **Weight.** When the plan's weight-medicine trigger is on, the stage cards add that weight loss is one of the most effective ways to lower blood pressure and that even a 5% loss can help; with a known adult age, they add that GLP-1 medicines (tirzepatide, semaglutide) may help both weight and blood pressure (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). Brad takes blood pressure medicines himself, for a pressure that runs high for genetic reasons; since starting low-dose tirzepatide, off-label, he has cut them to about a third of their original doses (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). This is off-label, not formally studied, and not a recommendation for anyone else (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).
- **Salt and potassium.** In his [salt post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/completely-wrong-about-salt-new-study), Brad concludes that for most people, the lower the sodium intake, the better, and calls a potassium salt substitute a useful swap because it cuts sodium and adds potassium at once [31]. The roadmap's potassium card (3,500 to 5,000 mg a day from fruit, vegetables and legumes) shows only with an eGFR of 45 or more (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). In his [salt substitute post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/salt-substitutes-can-slash-your-death-risk-by-12), he recommends a broader approach: more potassium-rich food, more fibre and more lean protein [32].
- **Exercise.** In his [exercise post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/study-exercise-blood-pressure), Brad highlights isometric exercise, especially wall squats, as the most effective type in one large meta-analysis, and uses short "exercise snacks" himself [33].
- **Home monitoring.** Brad recommends an automated upper-arm home monitor and a reading every 2 to 4 weeks to follow the trend (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026). In his [home monitor post](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/spend-40-dollars-avoid-dying-early), he advises choosing a device independently tested for accuracy, and describes how anxiety in the clinic can push readings up [34].

## Who needs extra care

- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding.** Raised blood pressure during pregnancy needs its own plan from your midwife or doctor. Neither ACE inhibitors nor ARBs are used in pregnancy [24][25]. The lisinopril label advises against breastfeeding while taking it [24]. NICE lists pregnant women among those who should not use potassium salt substitutes [4].
- **Older adults.** In SPRINT participants aged 75 or older, aiming for under 120 lowered cardiovascular events (hazard ratio 0.66) and death (0.67), and injurious falls were not more common [16]. Frailty changes the balance: NICE sets a clinic target below 150/90 from age 80 and asks doctors to use judgement in frailty [4]. If you feel dizzy on standing, tell your doctor; NICE bases targets on standing pressure when it drops a lot on standing [4].
- **Reduced kidney function.** Potassium advice and potassium salt substitutes need care, because damaged kidneys may not clear extra potassium [2][4]. ACE inhibitors and ARBs need monitoring of potassium and kidney tests [24]; check with your doctor or pharmacist about doses.
- **Diabetes.** People with diabetes are among those NICE advises against potassium salt substitutes [4], and ESPRIT found the benefit of lower targets held with or without diabetes [15].
- **Under 18.** Blood pressure in children and teenagers is judged differently; talk to their doctor.

## When to see your doctor

- Home readings that average 135/85 or more, or a clinic reading of 140/90 or more [4].
- New ankle swelling, a persistent cough, dizziness on standing or fainting after starting a medicine [24][25][26].
- Blood pressure that stays above your target despite treatment [4].
- If you take an ACE inhibitor, ARB or diuretic, for the blood tests your doctor asks for (potassium and kidney function) [24][27].
- Before starting potassium supplements or a potassium salt substitute if you have kidney disease or take a medicine that raises potassium [2][24][25].

## Common questions

**What does HTN mean?** It is shorthand for hypertension, high blood pressure.

**My home readings are lower than the clinic's. Which counts?** Both matter. Many people read higher in the clinic, and guidelines use home or ambulatory averages to confirm a diagnosis [4][7][8]. Home thresholds are lower: NICE treats 135/85 at home like 140/90 in the clinic [4].

**Are wrist monitors accurate?** Upper-arm cuffs are more accurate than wrist or finger devices [11]. Choose a validated model with the right cuff size.

**Can lifestyle alone fix it?** Sometimes. Each measure lowers blood pressure by a few mmHg, and they add up: DASH plus low sodium lowered systolic pressure by 11.5 mmHg in people with hypertension [18]. If your pressure stays above target, medicine is added, not swapped in [2][5].

**Will potassium lower my blood pressure?** Potassium from food is recommended by the 2025 US guideline, except in kidney disease or with medicines that reduce potassium excretion, where blood potassium should be checked [2]. NICE does not use potassium supplements to lower blood pressure [4]. See the [potassium page](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/potassium-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects).

**Should I use a salt substitute?** It may help if you salt food at home [2][19], but not with kidney disease or on an ACE inhibitor, ARB or potassium-sparing diuretic without asking your doctor [2][4][24][25].

**Can I take an ACE inhibitor or ARB with reduced kidney function?** Often yes, but the choice, dose and blood tests are for your doctor. Check with your doctor or pharmacist.

**I feel dizzy when I stand up since starting a pill.** Tell your doctor. Blood pressure medicines can drop pressure too far, especially if you are low on fluid or salt [25]. If you faint or feel very unwell, see "Get help now".

**Does coffee raise blood pressure?** NICE advises against excessive coffee and other caffeine-rich products [4], and caffeine shortly before a reading can raise it [11].
