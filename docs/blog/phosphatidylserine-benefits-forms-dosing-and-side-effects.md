## Current evidence position

Brad has not made a video on this topic; this is a summary of the evidence, not his view. The clearest memory results came from phosphatidylserine (PS) made from cow brain: 100 mg three times a day for 12 weeks helped learning and memory tests in 149 people with age-associated memory impairment, and 300 mg a day for 6 months helped behaviour and memory scores in a trial that recruited 494 older adults with moderate to severe cognitive decline [7][8]. PS made from soy has done less well: 300 mg a day or 600 mg a day for 12 weeks made no difference in 120 older adults with memory complaints, and in another trial memory gains were seen only in people with low scores at the start [10][12]. Two small trials at 600 and 800 mg a day blunted the cortisol rise after exercise [18][26]; two at 750 mg did not [24][25]; performance results were mixed. Trials lasting 6 months reported PS was well tolerated [8][10].

## Key safety points

- PS takes part in blood clotting, and very high injected doses (30 and 60 mg/kg, species not reported) slowed clotting in one old study; any effect of oral PS on blood thinners is unknown. If you take a blood thinner, ask your doctor first [4][5].
- Trials in children with ADHD gave PS for 2 months to 30 weeks and reported it was well tolerated [27][28]. Ask a doctor before giving it to a child, and do not use it in place of ADHD treatment.
- In a small study without a placebo group, soy PS unexpectedly lowered blood pressure [17]. If you take blood pressure medicines, tell your doctor.
- Trials in older adults lasting 6 months reported PS was well tolerated; one saw no side effects and no change in blood tests [8][10]. Safety beyond 6 months is not known.

## 1. Overview

Phosphatidylserine (PS) is a phospholipid, a fat that forms part of cell membranes. It is the main negatively charged phospholipid in the inner layer of nerve cell membranes [3]. A 2015 narrative review describes it as needed for healthy nerve cell membranes and myelin [30].

PS normally sits on the inner side of the cell membrane. When it moves to the outer side of a dying cell, immune cells recognise and clear that cell; on the surface of activated platelets it binds clotting factors and speeds up thrombin production, which helps blood clot [4].

Nerve tissue is rich in PS, and brain PS carries a lot of docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), an omega-3 fat [3]. PS in nerve cell membranes affects the release of neurotransmitters and the activity of several synaptic receptors and proteins [3].

The body makes PS from phosphatidylcholine or phosphatidylethanolamine by swapping their head group for serine. Two enzymes in the endoplasmic reticulum, phosphatidylserine synthase 1 and 2, carry out this reaction [3]. DHA promotes PS production in nerve cells [3].

Research on PS supplements began with PS taken from cow brain cortex, tested in older adults with memory problems in the late 1980s and early 1990s [7][8][16]. Concern about bovine spongiform encephalopathy (BSE, or "mad cow disease") led the FDA in 2003 to say that bovine sources, if used, should not come from cattle in countries where BSE exists [9]. Later studies tested soy-derived PS and PS with omega-3 fats attached, which their authors described as possible safe alternatives to PS from bovine cortex [13][17].

In 2003, the FDA said it intended to exercise enforcement discretion for two qualified health claims on PS supplements: "Consumption of phosphatidylserine may reduce the risk of dementia in the elderly," and the same wording for "cognitive dysfunction in the elderly" [9]. Each claim carries its own disclaimer; the dementia one reads: "Very limited and preliminary scientific research suggests that phosphatidylserine may reduce the risk of dementia in the elderly. FDA concludes that there is little scientific evidence supporting this claim" [9]. The FDA also said cow-brain and soy PS may be different substances, the only soy trial it weighed showed no effect, and soy PS sold with the claims should be of very high purity [9]. In 2004 it re-reviewed the evidence and kept the same wording and conditions [11].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Bovine-Derived vs. Plant-Derived Phosphatidylserine

PS supplements come from two kinds of source, and the trial results differ between them.

**Bovine cortex-derived PS** came from cow brain, and brain PS is rich in DHA [3]. The early trials that found benefits in older adults with memory problems used this form [7][8].

**Soy lecithin-derived PS** is made from soybean lecithin by an enzyme reaction with the amino acid L-serine [10]. Trials of soy PS for memory have had mixed or null results [10][12][17].

**Sunflower lecithin-derived PS** is also sold. Whether it works like soy PS is unknown.

### DHA-Conjugated Phosphatidylserine (PS-DHA)

PS-DHA is PS with omega-3 long-chain fatty acids attached to its backbone [14]. It was developed as a safe-sourced alternative to PS from bovine cortex [13].

Trials of PS-DHA have been small and limited to older adults with memory complaints who did not have dementia [13][14][15]. The largest found better verbal recall than placebo, but its authors called the study exploratory [14].

### Absorption and Bioavailability

A 2015 review concluded that PS taken by mouth at 300 to 800 mg a day is absorbed efficiently and crosses the blood-brain barrier [30]. That review was a narrative review, not a systematic one.

### Source Material: Lecithin

Soy PS is made from soybean lecithin, which is reacted with L-serine using an enzyme [10]. In the body, the same swap of a head group for serine turns phosphatidylcholine into PS [3].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Memory and Cognitive Function

#### Early Bovine-Derived PS Studies (1980s-1990s)

The earliest evidence came from PS taken from cow brain cortex, studied from the late 1980s to the early 1990s.

**Amaducci (1988)** reported a multicentre study of PS in Alzheimer's disease for the SMID group [16].

**Crook et al. (1991)** treated 149 people with age-associated memory impairment with 100 mg of cow-brain PS three times a day or placebo for 12 weeks. The PS group improved more than the placebo group on tests of learning and memory tasks from daily life. People who scored relatively low before treatment were the most likely to respond [7].

**Cenacchi et al. (1993)** ran a double-blind trial in 23 units in northeastern Italy. It recruited 494 older adults aged between 65 and 93 years with moderate to severe cognitive decline, who took 300 mg a day of cow-brain PS or placebo for 6 months; 69 dropped out. The PS group improved more than placebo on behaviour and memory scores, and PS was well tolerated [8].

These trials used PS from cow brain. Their results may not apply to the soy and sunflower PS sold today [7][8][10][12].

#### Plant-Derived PS Studies

Trials of soy-derived PS for memory have been small, and their results are mixed.

**Jorissen et al. (2001)** randomised 120 older adults with age-associated memory impairment to placebo, 300 mg a day or 600 mg a day of soy PS for 12 weeks. Neither dose changed memory or any other cognitive test compared with placebo [12].

**Kato-Kataoka et al. (2010)** randomised Japanese adults aged 50 to 69 with mild cognitive impairment to 100 mg or 300 mg a day of soy PS, or placebo, for 6 months. Test scores rose in all groups, including placebo. In people with relatively low scores at the start, memory scores rose from baseline with PS and stayed flat with placebo, mostly through better delayed verbal recall [10].

**Richter et al. (2013)** gave 300 mg a day of soy PS for 12 weeks to 30 older adults with memory complaints, with no placebo group. Memory, executive function and learning scores improved, and blood pressure fell; the authors called the study exploratory [17].

The authors of the soy PS pilot study described soy PS as a possible safe alternative to PS from bovine cortex and called for larger studies [17].

#### DHA-Conjugated PS Studies

Researchers then tested PS-DHA, which has omega-3 fatty acids attached to its backbone [14].

**Richter et al. (2010)** gave PS-DHA for 6 weeks to 8 older volunteers with memory complaints, with no placebo group. Delayed word recall rose by 42% [13].

**Vakhapova et al. (2010)** randomised 157 older adults with memory complaints but no dementia to PS-DHA or placebo for 15 weeks. Verbal immediate recall improved more with PS-DHA than with placebo. In a post-hoc analysis, people with relatively good scores at the start improved most. The authors called the study exploratory and asked for confirming trials [14].

**Vakhapova et al. (2014)** followed 122 of these participants in a 15-week open-label extension on 100 mg a day of PS-DHA. People new to PS-DHA improved on sustained attention and on memory recognition, and those who continued it held their scores [15].

These trials were small, limited to older adults with memory complaints, and run largely by the same research group [13][14][15].

#### Summary of Cognitive Evidence

| PS Form | Evidence Level | Key Findings |
|---------|---------------|--------------|
| Bovine-derived (300 mg/day) | Two placebo-controlled trials | Benefit in age-associated memory impairment and in moderate to severe cognitive decline; low scorers responded most [7][8] |
| Plant-derived soy | Mixed | No benefit in one trial; gains only in low scorers in another [10][12] |
| PS-DHA | Limited (small or exploratory trials) | Better verbal recall in one trial; best response in those with higher baseline scores [14][15] |
| FDA position | Qualified health claim with mandatory disclaimer | "Very limited and preliminary scientific research" and "little scientific evidence supporting this claim" [9] |

### Cortisol and Stress Response

PS has been studied for effects on the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis, the body's main stress hormone system. Several trials measured ACTH and cortisol, the hormones this system releases under stress [18][20][29].

**Monteleone et al. (1990, 1992)** ran the first human studies. In 1990, 8 healthy men received 50 or 75 mg of cow-brain PS into a vein before exercise; both doses blunted the ACTH and cortisol responses [29]. In 1992, 9 healthy men took 800 mg a day of cow-brain PS by mouth for 10 days, which blunted the ACTH and cortisol responses to exercise compared with placebo [18].

**Benton et al. (2001)** gave young adults 300 mg of PS a day for a month before a stressful mental arithmetic task. In those with above-median neuroticism scores, PS was linked to feeling less stressed and a better mood [22].

**Hellhammer et al. (2004)** gave four groups of 20 people 400 mg, 600 mg or 800 mg of a soy phosphatidic acid and PS complex (PAS) a day, or placebo, for 3 weeks before the Trier Social Stress Test. The 400 mg dose blunted ACTH and cortisol responses and lowered distress after the test; larger doses did not [20].

**Baumeister et al. (2008)** gave 16 healthy people PS or placebo for 42 days. PS lowered Beta-1 brain-wave power over the right frontal region before and after induced stress, which the authors linked to a more relaxed state [21].

Results have been inconsistent. The cortisol effect in the PAS trial was seen at 400 mg but not at higher doses [20], and two exercise trials found no effect on cortisol [24][25]. Cortisol is a normal stress hormone, and lowering it is not always helpful.

### Athletic Performance and Exercise Recovery

PS has been tested in athletes mainly because of its effect on cortisol after exercise.

**Monteleone et al. (1992)** found that 800 mg a day of cow-brain PS for 10 days blunted the cortisol response to exercise [18].

**Kingsley et al. (2005, 2006)** ran two double-blind studies of 750 mg of soybean-derived PS, taken daily for 10 days:
- In active men cycling to exhaustion, time to exhaustion at 85% VO2max rose with PS, a significant effect (P = 0.005); cortisol did not change [24].
- In 16 male soccer players doing an intermittent running test, PS did not change cortisol, soreness, muscle damage or oxidative stress markers; running time to exhaustion tended to rise (P = 0.084) [25].

**Jager et al. (2007)** gave 10 young golfers 200 mg per day of PS in a nutrition bar and 10 a placebo, for 42 days. PS raised the number of good ball flights; perceived stress during tee-off showed a non-significant trend toward improvement (p = 0.07), and heart rate did not change [19].

**Starks et al. (2008)** gave 10 healthy men 600 mg per day of PS or placebo for 10 days in a crossover trial. With PS, peak cortisol and total cortisol after moderate cycling were lower than with placebo, and the testosterone to cortisol ratio rose; lactate and growth hormone did not change [26].

**Summary of exercise evidence:** Small trials of 600 to 800 mg a day for 10 days blunted cortisol after exercise [18][26], but two others found no cortisol effect [24][25]. Performance results are mixed and come from small trials [19][24][25].

### ADHD and Attention in Children

Two trials have tested PS in children with attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD).

**Hirayama et al. (2014)** randomised 36 children aged 4 to 14 years who had never had ADHD drug treatment to placebo or 200 mg of soy PS each day for 2 months. PS improved ADHD symptoms, short-term auditory memory and inattention, and showed no adverse effects [27].

**Manor et al. (2012)** randomised 200 children with ADHD to PS with omega-3 fats attached (PS-Omega3) or placebo for 15 weeks, followed by a 15-week open-label extension. PS-Omega3 reduced restless and impulsive behaviour on a parent rating scale. An exploratory subgroup with marked hyperactivity and mood dysregulation improved more. The treatment was well tolerated [28].

The evidence is preliminary. The trials are few, the first was small, and the second's strongest findings came from a subgroup. PS should not replace established ADHD treatment.

### Depression and Mood

Evidence in depression is thin. **Maggioni et al. (1990)** gave 10 older women with depressive disorders a placebo for 15 days, then 300 mg a day of cow-brain PS for 30 days. Depression scores, memory and behaviour improved [31]. The study was tiny and had no parallel control group: everyone took the placebo first, then PS. It also used cow-brain PS, not the soy form tested in later trials. Most other mood findings come from stress studies in healthy people.

**Benton et al. (2001)** found that 300 mg of PS a day for a month was linked to better mood in young adults with above-median neuroticism scores [22].

**Baumeister et al. (2008)** reported brain-wave changes that the authors linked to a more relaxed state [21].

**Hellhammer et al. (2004)** found less distress after a stress test with the 400 mg PAS dose, but not with larger doses [20].

PS should not replace established treatments for depression or anxiety.

### Mechanism of Action: HPA Axis Modulation

In human studies, PS blunted the ACTH and cortisol responses to physical or mental stress [18][20][29]. The 1992 authors concluded that PS taken by mouth may counter stress-induced activation of the HPA axis [18].

In nerve cells, PS in the membrane affects neurotransmitter release and several synaptic receptors and proteins [3]. Memory gains have been seen in some cognitively impaired people taking PS, but the mechanism is unclear [3].

### Phosphatidylcholine and Choline Connection

In the body, PS is made from phosphatidylcholine or phosphatidylethanolamine [3]. The choline reference covers choline forms such as citicoline and alpha-GPC.

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### General Guidelines

Trials have used a wide range of doses. Memory trials gave 100 mg of cow-brain PS three times a day [7], 300 mg a day of cow-brain PS [8], 100 mg or 300 mg a day of soy PS [10], and 300 mg a day or 600 mg a day of soy PS [12]. A PS-DHA extension used 100 mg a day [15]. Stress and exercise trials used 200 mg per day [19], 300 mg a day [22], 400 to 800 mg a day of a soy PS complex (PAS) [20], 600 mg per day [26], 750 mg a day [24][25] and 800 mg a day [18].

### Dosing by Indication

| Indication | Dose | Duration | Evidence Level | Notes |
|-----------|------|----------|---------------|-------|
| Age-related memory decline | Bovine: 300 mg/day [8]. Soy: 300 mg/day or 600 mg/day [12] | 12 weeks [12] to 6 months [8] | Mixed (soy); stronger (bovine) | Only the bovine trials found clear benefits [7][8][12] |
| Cortisol response to stress | 400 mg PAS | 3 weeks | Limited | Larger PAS doses did not work [20] |
| Cortisol response to exercise | 600-800 mg/day | 10 days | Limited (cortisol); mixed (performance) | Small trials [18][26] |
| Memory support with PS-DHA | 100 mg/day PS-DHA | 15 weeks | Limited | Open-label extension [15] |
| ADHD (children) | 200 mg soy PS | 2 months | Preliminary | One small trial; not a replacement for ADHD treatment [27] |
| General brain health maintenance | Not established | Not established | None | Not studied |

### Practical Considerations

- **Split doses.** Crook's trial gave 100 mg three times a day [7].
- **Soy vs. sunflower source.** The plant PS trials above used soy PS [10][12][17]. Whether sunflower PS works the same way is unknown.
- **PS-DHA forms.** PS-DHA trials found some memory gains in older adults, but they were small and exploratory [14][15].
- **Duration of use.** The longest adult trials lasted 6 months [8][10].
- **Combining with omega-3s.** Taking soy PS with fish oil is not the same as PS-DHA, where omega-3 fats are attached to the PS molecule [14]. The combination has not been tested.

### Important Context on Expectations

1. The clearer memory results came from PS made from cow brain [7][8].
2. Soy PS has had mixed or null results for memory [10][12].
3. PS-DHA trials were small and exploratory [14][15].
4. Cortisol effects after stress or exercise appeared in some small trials but not others [18][20][24][25][26].
5. The FDA's disclaimer says there is "little scientific evidence" for its memory claims [9].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Safety Profile

Trials of PS lasting 6 months in older adults reported it was well tolerated [8][10]. In the 6-month soy PS trial, blood markers and vital signs did not change and no side effects were seen [10]. In a 12-week study without a placebo group, soy PS was well tolerated with no serious adverse events, but it unexpectedly lowered systolic and diastolic blood pressure [17].

### Common Side Effects

Common side effects are not well documented; trials reported it was well tolerated [8][10][17].

Trials in children reported PS was well tolerated, with no adverse effects in the smaller trial [27][28].

### Long-Term Safety

The longest trials lasted 6 months in older adults and 30 weeks in children [8][10][28]. Safety over years of use is not known.

### Special Populations

- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** The safety of PS supplements in pregnancy and breastfeeding is not established. Ask your doctor before using them.
- **Children:** A trial in children aged 4 to 14 years used 200 mg of soy PS each day for 2 months with no adverse effects [27], and a 30-week trial of PS-Omega3 in children with ADHD found it well tolerated [28]. These trials are too few to settle safety in children.
- **Soy allergy:** Soy PS is made from soybean lecithin [10]. With a soy allergy, check with your doctor or pharmacist first.
- **Older adults:** Most PS trials were in older adults, and those lasting 6 months reported it was well tolerated [8][10].

### Blood Clotting Considerations

PS normally sits on the inner side of the cell membrane. On activated platelets it moves to the outer side, where it binds clotting factors and speeds up thrombin production [4]. When this exposure fails, blood disorders such as Scott syndrome can occur [4].

In a 1981 study, PS given into a vein at high doses (30 and 60 mg/kg), species not reported, lowered blood clotting activity in proportion to the dose, and in the lab it slowed thrombin formation [5]. Whether PS supplements taken by mouth affect clotting is unknown.

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Anticoagulants and Antiplatelet Agents

PS takes part in blood clotting, and very high injected doses slowed clotting in one old study; any effect of oral PS on blood thinners is unknown. If you take a blood thinner, ask your doctor first [4][5].

### Anticholinergic Medications

PS affects neurotransmitter release in nerve cells [3], so it could in theory work against anticholinergic medicines such as diphenhydramine, amitriptyline, oxybutynin or tolterodine. This interaction is theoretical. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

### Cholinesterase Inhibitors

PS could in theory add to the effects of cholinesterase inhibitors used for Alzheimer's disease, such as donepezil, rivastigmine and galantamine. This interaction is theoretical. If you take one of these, tell your doctor you are taking PS.

### Cortisol-Modulating Substances

PS blunted cortisol responses to stress in some trials [18][20], so it could in theory add to other products or medicines that affect cortisol. This interaction is theoretical. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

## 7. Dietary Sources

PS is a normal part of cell membranes, and nerve tissue is rich in it [3].

### Foods Containing Phosphatidylserine

Brain PS carries a lot of DHA [3].

### Practical Notes on Dietary PS

- **Cow brain.** Cow brain was the original source of PS supplements. The FDA's BSE condition on bovine PS applies to that source [9].
- **Soy.** Soy PS supplements are made from soybean lecithin by an enzyme reaction with L-serine [10].
- **Fatty acid context.** Brain PS is rich in DHA [3], while PS-DHA supplements have omega-3 fats attached in manufacture [14].
