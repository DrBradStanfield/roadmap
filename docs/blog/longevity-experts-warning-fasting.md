Intermittent fasting has surged in popularity, touted as a powerful tool for weight loss, metabolic health, and longevity. Yet, despite its initial hype, some of the most respected voices in the field of longevity, including Dr. Peter Attia and Dr. Rhonda Patrick, have shifted their stance on fasting. This blog post delves into the reasons behind this change and offers evidence-based insights on how to approach fasting to optimize health.

## The Early Excitement: Rodent Studies

The initial enthusiasm for intermittent fasting stemmed from studies on rodents that showed remarkable benefits. These studies reported that time-restricted feeding (TRF) - where food intake is limited to a specific window during the day - resulted in reduced body weight, improved blood sugar control, lower insulin levels, and even increased lifespan in rodents ( [Mattson et al., 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6627766/)).

Even more intriguingly, these benefits were observed even when the food intake was matched between the TRF group and the control group, suggesting that the timing of food intake played a crucial role in these health improvements ( [Mattson et al., 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6627766/)).

Rodent studies have long been a cornerstone of medical research due to their biological similarities to humans. However, the excitement generated by these findings led to the assumption that similar benefits could be translated to humans.

## Human Studies: The Initial Promise

When human studies on intermittent fasting began to emerge, they appeared promising at first. A 2020 meta-analysis suggested that time-restricted feeding offered greater weight loss and reductions in blood sugar levels compared to unrestricted eating patterns ( [Sutton et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29754952/)).

The initial evidence seemed to validate the rodent studies. For example, the meta-analysis found that TRF regimens achieved superior results in promoting weight loss and reducing fasting glucose levels compared to diets where people could eat whenever they wanted ( [Sutton et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29754952/)).

This led to widespread adoption of intermittent fasting among health enthusiasts and longevity experts. The idea was simple and appealing: restrict the hours in which you eat, and enjoy numerous health benefits without having to drastically change what you eat.

## The Cracks Begin to Show: Calorie Matching in Studies

As more research was conducted, however, the initial excitement began to wane. A deeper look into the human studies revealed a significant flaw: most of these studies did not properly match calorie intake between the fasting and control groups ( [Sutton et al., 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29754952/)).

In many of these studies, the fasting groups consumed fewer calories than the control groups, which likely accounted for the observed weight loss and metabolic improvements. When the Cochrane organization performed a meta-analysis in 2021, they found that when calorie intake was matched between groups, there were no significant differences in weight loss or blood sugar levels ( [AlHerbish et al., 2021](https://www.cochrane.org/CD013496/VASC_does-limiting-times-you-eat-intermittent-fasting-prevent-cardiovascular-disease)).

This finding was critical. It suggested that the benefits of intermittent fasting might not be due to the fasting itself but rather the result of reduced calorie intake. Essentially, it was not the timing of the meals but the overall caloric deficit that led to weight loss and metabolic improvements.

Further studies supported this conclusion. For instance, a 2019 study on alternate day fasting found that although the fasting group showed greater reductions in fasting insulin and insulin resistance, they also consumed fewer calories compared to the calorie restriction group ( [Mir et al., 2019](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7138754/)).

The same pattern was observed in studies on fatty liver disease. A 2023 study concluded that time-restricted feeding did not produce additional benefits for reducing liver fat content or metabolic risk factors compared to daily calorie restriction ( [Wang et al., 2023](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2802553)).

## The Protein Dilemma: Fasting and Muscle Preservation

One of the less discussed but critical issues with intermittent fasting is its potential impact on protein intake and muscle preservation. To maximize the benefits of resistance exercise and support muscle building, it is recommended to consume around 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight per day ( [Cope et al., 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5355425/)).

For individuals who practice intermittent fasting by skipping meals, particularly breakfast, reaching these protein targets can be challenging. This is especially concerning because muscle preservation becomes increasingly important as we age. If someone is not consuming enough protein due to the restricted eating window, they risk losing muscle mass, which can have significant long-term health implications.

To address this, I often recommend that my patients who choose to practice intermittent fasting supplement their diet with a low-calorie protein shake during their fasting period. This allows them to restrict calories while still meeting their protein needs to preserve muscle mass.

Additionally, ensuring sufficient protein intake is crucial for those engaging in regular resistance training. The synergy between adequate protein intake and exercise is essential for maintaining and building muscle mass, particularly as we age. This combination not only supports muscle health but also contributes to overall metabolic health, making it an essential consideration for anyone adopting intermittent fasting ( [Cope et al., 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5355425/)).

## The Truth About Multi-Day Fasts

Some proponents of fasting take it a step further and engage in multi-day fasts, believing that prolonged fasting will significantly activate autophagy - a process where the body clears out damaged cells and regenerates new ones. While this process is indeed beneficial and has been observed in rodent studies, the translation to human health benefits is not as straightforward ( [Zeng et al., 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8151159/)).

In rodents, a 16-hour fast might be roughly equivalent to a 4-day fast in humans due to differences in metabolic rate ( [Zeng et al., 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8151159/)). However, this analogy is quite crude and does not take into account the complexities of human physiology. Moreover, humans have energy reserves in the form of glycogen stored in the liver, which can take up to 48 hours to deplete before autophagy is significantly activated ( [Zeng et al., 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8151159/)).

The theory behind multi-day fasts is intriguing, but it is largely speculative. Currently, there is no robust human evidence supporting the long-term benefits of such prolonged fasting. More concerning is the potential for muscle loss during these extended periods of fasting. As we fast, our bodies begin to break down muscle tissue for energy, and if this muscle is not rebuilt, we experience a net loss in muscle mass ( [Zeng et al., 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8151159/)).

Moreover, muscle loss during prolonged fasts can have significant implications for overall health and well-being. Muscle tissue plays a critical role in maintaining metabolic health, physical strength, and mobility, especially as we age. The loss of muscle mass, known as sarcopenia, is associated with increased risks of frailty, falls, and metabolic disorders. Therefore, the potential risks of multi-day fasting, particularly in terms of muscle preservation, must be carefully weighed against any speculative benefits ( [Zeng et al., 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8151159/)).

## My Personal Experience: Why I Stopped Prolonged Fasting

Several years ago, I experimented with multi-day fasts, fasting for 7 days every 3 months. The idea was to activate autophagy and reap the benefits observed in rodent studies. However, the reality was far from ideal.

During these prolonged fasts, I noticed significant muscle loss. After each fast, I had to spend weeks trying to regain the lost muscle mass, and over time, I began to feel weaker and weaker. My experience mirrored that of Dr. Peter Attia, who also stopped his fasting protocol due to the difficulty of regaining lost lean muscle.

Ultimately, the potential benefits of prolonged fasting were vastly outweighed by the negative impact on my muscle stores. This experience, combined with the lack of strong evidence supporting the benefits of prolonged fasting in humans, led me to abandon the practice.

The loss of muscle mass was not just a physical challenge but also a psychological one. The effort required to regain strength and muscle after each prolonged fast was immense, and the cumulative impact over time was detrimental to my overall sense of well-being. This experience underscores the importance of listening to one's body and being cautious about extreme dietary practices that may not be backed by solid scientific evidence.

## Practical Recommendations: How to Approach Fasting

Given the current evidence, how should we approach fasting?

Here are some practical recommendations:

1.  ****Focus on Calorie Restriction, Not Meal Timing:**** The benefits of intermittent fasting appear to be more related to calorie restriction than the timing of meals. If you find that fasting helps you control your calorie intake, it can be a useful tool for weight management and metabolic health ([AlHerbish et al., 2021](https://www.cochrane.org/CD013496/VASC_does-limiting-times-you-eat-intermittent-fasting-prevent-cardiovascular-disease)).
2.  ****Prioritize Protein Intake:**** If you choose to fast, be mindful of your protein intake. Consider incorporating a low-calorie protein shake during your fasting period to ensure you meet your daily protein needs and protect your muscle mass ([Cope et al., 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5355425/)).
3.  ****Be Cautious with Multi-Day Fasts:**** While multi-day fasts might seem like a good way to activate autophagy, the evidence supporting their benefits in humans is weak. Additionally, the risk of muscle loss is significant. If you are considering a prolonged fast, it is essential to consult with a healthcare professional.
4.  ****Tailor Fasting to Your Lifestyle:**** Fasting should fit into your life in a way that supports your overall health and well-being. For some, this might mean skipping breakfast and having their main meals later in the day. For others, it might involve eating a large breakfast and having a light dinner. Find what works best for you ([Cope et al., 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5355425/)).
5.  ****Monitor Your Health:**** If you are practicing intermittent fasting, it is important to monitor your health. Pay attention to how your body responds, and adjust your approach as needed. Using tools like a smartwatch to track your overnight heart rate can provide insights into how meal timing affects your sleep and energy levels.

## Conclusion: The Balanced Approach to Fasting

Intermittent fasting has its place in a balanced approach to health, particularly for weight management and blood sugar control. However, the benefits of fasting are likely more related to calorie restriction than the fasting itself. For most people, focusing on a balanced diet, adequate protein intake, and regular exercise will yield better long-term results than strict fasting protocols.

As always, it is essential to tailor your approach to your individual needs and consult with a healthcare professional before making significant changes to your diet or lifestyle.
