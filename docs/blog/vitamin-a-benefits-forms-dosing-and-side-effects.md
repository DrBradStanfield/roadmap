## Current view (reviewed 22 October 2026)

Brad does not see a role for vitamin A in a multivitamin, given the safety signals in the trials so far ([Brad, 19 Aug 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/multivitamins-are-no-longer-expensive-urine)). The key trials gave high-dose beta-carotene to smokers: the American CARET trial, which combined 30 mg of beta-carotene a day with retinol, found a lung cancer relative risk of 1.28 and was stopped early ([Brad, 19 Aug 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/multivitamins-are-no-longer-expensive-urine)). In the AREDS2 eye trial, which replaced beta-carotene with lutein and zeaxanthin, lung cancer occurred in 2.0 per cent of people on the older beta-carotene formula and 0.9 per cent on the newer one, mostly in former smokers ([Brad, 19 Aug 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/multivitamins-are-no-longer-expensive-urine)). For most people, a healthy diet provides the vitamin A they need without supplements, and deficiency is rare ([Brad, 15 May 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-incredibly-popular-supplement-damages-your-blood-vessels)). Too much vitamin A can weaken bones, damage the liver and cause birth defects ([Brad, 30 Jul 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/the-only-three-supplements-doctor-brad-takes)). On the skin, Brad's plan suggests a topical retinoid, adapalene 0.3% or tretinoin 0.05% on 2 to 3 nights a week, which must not be used during pregnancy (Brad's current guidance, 23 Feb 2026).

## Key safety points

- Do not take vitamin A supplements with retinoid medicines such as isotretinoin, acitretin or bexarotene, because together they raise the risk of vitamin A toxicity [109][111].
- Preformed vitamin A above the upper limit can cause birth defects. In pregnancy, keep preformed vitamin A from food and supplements within it: 3,000 mcg RAE (10,000 IU) a day from age 19, or 2,800 mcg at 14 to 18 years [109].
- In current and former smokers and asbestos workers, high-dose beta-carotene supplements raised the risk of lung cancer [109]. The USPSTF recommends against beta-carotene supplements to prevent heart disease or cancer [60].
- Vitamin A in high doses can injure the liver, causing jaundice, an enlarged liver and spleen, and cirrhosis [14].
- High retinol intakes have been linked to lower bone density and more hip fractures in observational studies [83][110].
- Children are more sensitive to vitamin A than adults, and swallowing products such as retinol skin cream can poison them. If you think a child has taken too much, contact your doctor [112].
- Long-term excess beta-carotene can turn the skin yellow-orange, which is harmless and reverses when you stop [109].

## Overview

Vitamin A is the name of a group of fat-soluble retinoids, primarily retinol and retinyl esters, that are essential for vision, immune function, cellular communication, growth, development, and reproduction [1][2]. Vitamin A supports cell growth and differentiation, playing a critical role in the normal formation and maintenance of the heart, lungs, eyes, and other organs [1][2]. It is also critical for vision as an essential component of rhodopsin, the light-sensitive protein in retinal rod cells that responds to light entering the eye, and because it supports the normal differentiation and functioning of the conjunctival membranes and cornea [2][3].

The human diet provides two sources of vitamin A: preformed vitamin A (retinol and retinyl esters) from animal sources (primarily liver, dairy products, eggs, and fish) and provitamin A carotenoids from plant sources, including beta-carotene, alpha-carotene, and beta-cryptoxanthin [1][4]. The body converts provitamin A carotenoids into vitamin A in the intestine via the beta-carotene monooxygenase type 1 (BCMO1) enzyme, although conversion rates vary genetically: certain polymorphisms in the BCMO1 gene can reduce enzyme activity in humans [5][6][7]. No upper limit has been set for beta-carotene, and unlike preformed vitamin A it is not known to cause birth defects [109].

Other carotenoids found in food, such as lycopene, lutein, and zeaxanthin, are not converted into vitamin A and are classified as non-provitamin A carotenoids, though they may have other important biological activities [1].

Vitamin A deficiency is uncommon in the United States: NHANES data show less than 1% of the U.S. population with a serum retinol level of less than 20 mcg/dL [8]. Average daily intakes from foods and beverages were 682 mcg RAE for men aged 20 and older and 616 mcg RAE for women in 2017–2018 [9]. About 65–80% of vitamin A consumed in the US and other high-income countries comes from preformed vitamin A, while provitamin A is the main form consumed in low-income countries [2][10]. About 12–40% of the US population, depending on age, uses supplements containing vitamin A [11].

Globally, however, vitamin A deficiency remains a major public health problem. A pooled analysis of population-based surveys from 138 low-income and middle-income countries found that 29% of children aged 6 months to 5 years had vitamin A deficiency in 2013, with the highest rates in sub-Saharan Africa (48%) and South Asia (44%) [12]. Approximately 10–20% of pregnant women in low-income countries also have vitamin A deficiency [10]. In 2013, an estimated 94,500 children in low-income and middle-income countries died of diarrhea and 11,200 died of measles as a result of vitamin A deficiency; more than 95% of these deaths occurred in sub-Saharan Africa and Asia [12].

Conversely, excessive intake of preformed vitamin A poses real risks, including liver damage, birth defects, osteoporosis, hypercalcemia, and potentially increased mortality. This makes it a nutrient where more is decidedly not better [1][14].

## Forms and Bioavailability

### Preformed Vitamin A (Retinol)

Supplements and animal foods supply preformed vitamin A as retinol or as its esters [109]:

- **Retinyl palmitate**: an ester of retinol, widely used in supplements and multivitamins [109].
- **Retinyl acetate**: another retinol ester common in supplements and multivitamins [109].
- **Retinol**: the alcohol form; the body converts retinyl esters to retinol after uptake [109].

Absorption of preformed vitamin A esters from dietary supplements is 70–90% [109][118]. The body can absorb up to 75–100% of retinol from foods [16]. Various forms of vitamin A are solubilized into micelles in the intestinal lumen and absorbed by duodenal mucosal cells. Retinyl esters are converted to retinol after uptake, which is then oxidized to retinal and retinoic acid, the two main active metabolites [1]. Most of the body's vitamin A is stored in the liver in the form of retinyl esters [1].

Retinol and carotenoid levels are typically measured in plasma or serum, but these are not always reliable indicators of vitamin A status because they do not decline until liver and tissue stores are almost depleted [1]. Acute and chronic infections can also decrease serum retinol concentrations. In clinical practice, a serum or plasma retinol concentration of 20 mcg/dL (0.70 micromoles/L) or less reflects moderate vitamin A deficiency, and a level of 10 mcg/dL (0.35 micromoles/L) or less indicates severe deficiency [1].

### Provitamin A Carotenoids

Beta-carotene is one of three provitamin A carotenoids, with alpha-carotene and beta-cryptoxanthin [109].

- **Absorption from supplements:** 8.7–65% [109].
- **Absorption from foods:** In most cases, 10–30% of beta-carotene from foods is absorbed [16]. Cooking and heat treatment can increase bioavailability from foods [118].

Genetic variability significantly affects conversion of beta-carotene to vitamin A. Certain polymorphisms in the BCMO1 gene reduce the activity of the BCMO1 enzyme. A study in the Philippines among 693 children and adolescents found an inverse association between vitamin A status and the A379V TT variant in the BCMO1 gene [5]. Two common single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs) in the BCMO1 gene altered beta-carotene metabolism in female volunteers [6][7]. Individuals who are poor converters due to these genetic variants may have a harder time meeting vitamin A needs from plant sources alone.

### Unit Conversions

Supplement labels are transitioning from International Units (IU) to micrograms of retinol activity equivalents (mcg RAE), which more accurately reflect the different bioactivities of retinol and provitamin A carotenoids [4]:

- 1 mcg RAE = 1 mcg retinol = 2 mcg supplemental beta-carotene = 12 mcg dietary beta-carotene = 24 mcg dietary alpha-carotene or beta-cryptoxanthin
- 1 IU retinol = 0.3 mcg RAE
- 1 IU supplemental beta-carotene = 0.3 mcg RAE
- 1 IU dietary beta-carotene = 0.05 mcg RAE
- 900 mcg RAE = 3,000 IU as retinol or supplemental beta-carotene = 18,000 IU as dietary beta-carotene = 36,000 IU as dietary alpha-carotene or beta-cryptoxanthin

A mixed diet containing 900 mcg RAE provides between 3,000 and 36,000 IU of vitamin A, depending on the proportion of preformed vitamin A versus carotenoids in foods consumed [4].

Multivitamins commonly contain 750–1,050 mcg RAE (83–117% of the Daily Value), often as retinyl acetate, retinyl palmitate, provitamin A beta-carotene, or a combination. In stand-alone supplements, 3,000 mcg RAE (333% DV) is common [19].

### Topical Retinoids

Retinoids are forms of vitamin A applied to the skin. Tretinoin is the oldest and most studied; adapalene is less irritating and acts more selectively on skin receptors [114].

A large review of 180 studies found that topical tretinoin improved wrinkling, uneven colour and age spots from photoaging in as little as one month [113]. In a head-to-head trial in 2018, adapalene and tretinoin were equally good at reversing signs of skin aging [114].

Retinoid creams can make the skin more sensitive and inflamed at first, so they are usually used at night with sunscreen during the day, and pregnant women should not use them [114].

### Cod Liver Oil and Fermented Cod Liver Oil

Cod liver oil is high in preformed vitamin A [116]. See Birth Defects below before using it in pregnancy.

## Evidence for Benefits

### Vision and Eye Health

Vitamin A is essential for vision as a component of rhodopsin, the light-sensitive protein in retinal rod cells. Deficiency causes night blindness (the inability to see in low light), the most common clinical sign of vitamin A deficiency, which develops after plasma retinol has been low and the eye's vitamin A reserves become depleted [1][10][12]. Xerophthalmia (chronic vitamin A deficiency affecting the eye) can also damage the cornea and eventually lead to permanent blindness. Vitamin A deficiency is one of the top causes of preventable blindness in children [10].

### Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD)

AMD is the leading cause of significant vision loss in older people. Its etiology involves complex interactions among genetic susceptibility, environmental factors including oxidative stress, and normal aging [30].

**AREDS (Age-Related Eye Disease Study):** This trial randomized 3,640 people aged 55 to 80 years to daily antioxidants (vitamin C 500 mg, vitamin E 400 IU and beta-carotene 15 mg), zinc 80 mg with copper 2 mg, both, or placebo, with an average follow-up of 6.3 years. Antioxidants plus zinc reduced the odds of developing advanced AMD (OR 0.72, 99% CI 0.52 to 0.98), and the reduction was larger once people at lowest risk were excluded [31].

**AREDS2:** This 5-year follow-up randomized clinical trial of 4,203 participants aged 50–85 confirmed the value of the AREDS supplement in reducing AMD progression [32]. Critically, the study showed that:

- Beta-carotene was not a required ingredient: the formula without it protected against advanced AMD just as well.
- Substituting lutein (10 mg) and zeaxanthin (2 mg) for beta-carotene reduced disease risk.
- Adding omega-3 fatty acids produced no additional benefit.
- Participants with the lowest dietary intakes of lutein and zeaxanthin had a 26% lower risk of advanced AMD when taking a supplement containing these two carotenoids.
- The risk of advanced AMD was 18% lower in participants taking the modified AREDS formula with lutein and zeaxanthin but not beta-carotene, compared to those taking the formula with beta-carotene but not lutein or zeaxanthin.

**10-year AREDS2 follow-up:** After the 5-year trial, participants were all offered the final AREDS2 formulation with lutein and zeaxanthin replacing beta-carotene. In a follow-up analysis of 3,882 participants 5 years after the trial ended, those who had taken the supplement with lutein and zeaxanthin had an additional 20% reduced risk of progression to late AMD compared with those who had taken the supplement containing beta-carotene [33]. This confirmed the benefit of replacing beta-carotene with lutein and zeaxanthin.

**Long-term dietary analysis:** A study monitoring dietary intakes in 4,504 AREDS and 3,738 AREDS2 participants (mean age 71) for a median of 10.2 years found that those in the two highest quintiles of intake for vitamin A, beta-carotene, or lutein and zeaxanthin had a lower risk of progression to late AMD. The risk of late AMD was 18% lower among those in the fifth quintile for vitamin A intake and 20% lower among those in the fourth quintile compared to those in the first quintile [34].

The current evidence supports lutein and zeaxanthin rather than beta-carotene as the preferred carotenoids in AMD supplement formulations.

### Cancer

Because of vitamin A's role in regulating cell growth and differentiation, numerous studies have examined its relationship with various cancers. The evidence is mixed and highly dependent on the form and dose of vitamin A, smoking status, and cancer type [1].

**Observational evidence for reduced risk:** Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses of observational studies have shown that higher dietary intakes of retinol, carotenoids, fruits and vegetables, or a combination are associated with lower risks of non-Hodgkin lymphoma [36], pancreatic cancer [37], oral cavity and laryngeal cancer [38], ovarian cancer [40][41], glioma [42], and bladder cancer [43]. However, other observational studies have found no association between intakes of different forms of vitamin A and risk of liver cancer [44], non-Hodgkin lymphoma [45], colorectal cancer [46], or all cancers combined [47].

#### Beta-Carotene Supplementation and Lung Cancer Risk in Smokers

The most important finding in vitamin A and cancer research is the consistently harmful effect of high-dose beta-carotene supplementation in smokers and asbestos-exposed workers:

**CARET (Carotene and Retinol Efficacy Trial):** This trial enrolled 18,314 male and female current and former smokers (with at least a 20 pack-year history) and some men occupationally exposed to asbestos, all aged 45–74. Participants were randomized to take 30 mg beta-carotene plus 25,000 IU (7,500 mcg RAE) retinyl palmitate or placebo daily. The trial was ended prematurely after a mean of 4 years because the supplements unexpectedly increased lung cancer risk by 28%, death from lung cancer by 46%, and all-cause mortality by 17% [51]. In a subsequent 6-year follow-up after stopping supplements, differences in lung cancer risk were no longer statistically significant (RR 1.12, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.31). Women had a larger relative risk of lung cancer death than men (1.33 vs 1.14), though the difference was not significant, and subgroup analyses suggested the excess risk was mainly in women [52]. In a separate analysis, men who took the two supplements had a 35% lower risk of nonaggressive prostate cancer during the active trial, but men who additionally took self-prescribed supplements (typically a multivitamin) had a 52% higher risk of aggressive prostate cancer during the active trial [53].

**ATBC (Alpha-Tocopherol, Beta-Carotene Cancer Prevention Study):** This study enrolled 29,133 male smokers aged 50–69 who smoked an average of 20.4 cigarettes per day for an average of 35.9 years. Participants were randomized to take 50 mg/day alpha-tocopherol, 20 mg/day beta-carotene, both, or placebo for 5–8 years. Beta-carotene supplementation increased lung cancer risk by 18% and the overall rate of death (primarily from lung cancer and ischemic heart disease) by 8% [54]. In an 18-year post-trial follow-up of 25,563 participants, the increased lung cancer risk was no longer present, but participants who had taken beta-carotene during the trial had a 20% higher risk of death due to prostate cancer [55].

**AREDS2 lung cancer finding:** Among the 4,203 AREDS2 participants, no current smokers received beta-carotene supplements. At the end of the 5-year trial, more lung cancers were found in the beta-carotene group than in the no-beta-carotene group (23 vs. 11 cases), and 31 of the 34 affected were former smokers. In a follow-up analysis 5 years later, the increased lung cancer risk persisted, with an 82% higher risk among participants who had taken beta-carotene during the trial [33].

**Negative results in non-smokers:** Three other clinical trials found no relationship between vitamin A or beta-carotene supplementation and lung cancer incidence or mortality in populations with smaller proportions of smokers [56]:

- The Physicians' Health Study randomized 22,071 male physicians aged 40–84 to take 50 mg beta-carotene on alternate days or placebo for 12 years (11% current smokers, 38% former smokers). No differences were found between groups in cases of lung cancer, any malignant neoplasm, or cancer deaths [57].
- A trial randomized 7,627 women (mean age 60.4) to take 50 mg beta-carotene on alternate days, 600 IU vitamin E on alternate days, 500 mg vitamin C daily, or placebo for a mean of 9.4 years (15% current smokers, 41% former smokers). None of the supplements had any significant effect on total cancer incidence or mortality, including lung cancer [58].
- The Linxian, China trial enrolled 29,584 healthy men and women aged 40–69 (in an area where micronutrient deficiencies are common) and randomized them to a placebo or one of four vitamin/mineral combinations for 5.25 years with 10 years of additional follow-up. Lung cancer death rates did not differ among groups [59].

**Overall assessment:** The CARET and ATBC results indicate that large supplemental doses of beta-carotene, with or without retinyl palmitate, have detrimental effects in current or former smokers and workers exposed to asbestos. The US Preventive Services Task Force concludes with moderate certainty that the harms of beta-carotene supplements outweigh the benefits, and recommends against them for preventing cardiovascular disease or cancer [60]. Among nonsmokers, beta-carotene and vitamin A supplements do not appear to affect cancer risk.

### Measles

In 2023, measles was responsible for about 107,500 deaths worldwide, mostly in young children in low-income countries [62][63]. Vitamin A deficiency is a risk factor for severe measles [12].

Research suggests that vitamin A supplements reduce the risk of measles in children at high risk of deficiency, but they do not appear to reduce the risk of death from measles [109]. A 2022 Cochrane Review of six clinical trials enrolling a total of 19,566 children aged 6 months to 5 years in low- and middle-income countries found that vitamin A supplementation reduced the risk of new measles cases by 50%. Vitamin A doses ranged from 15,000 mcg RAE (50,000 IU) to 60,000 mcg RAE (200,000 IU) depending on age, administered as a single dose or every 4–6 months. However, the same review found that vitamin A supplementation did not affect the risk of death due to measles, based on six trials in a total of 1,088,261 children [65]. This review tested routine supplements to prevent measles, not treatment of children who already have it, which doctors manage. A separate 2005 Cochrane review of children who already had measles found no significant overall reduction in deaths with vitamin A (RR 0.70, 95% CI 0.42 to 1.15), but two doses of 200,000 IU on consecutive days were associated with lower mortality in children under 2 years (RR 0.18, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.61) [119].

### Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD)

A small pilot study in China gave vitamin A to children with autism spectrum disorder, who had lower serum retinol than other children, and reported improved symptoms and lower serum serotonin (5-HT) afterwards [66]. It compared the children before and after supplementation, with no placebo group, so the change cannot be credited to vitamin A alone.

### Aging Skin

For skin ageing, the trials Brad cites test retinoids applied to the skin, covered under Topical Retinoids above [113][114].

### Acne

A 2022 review found 9 studies of oral vitamin A for acne, 8 of them clinical trials; acne improved in 8, at doses ranging from 36,000 IU daily to 500,000 IU, but the studies had limited controls [69]. At these doses vitamin A is a teratogen, as isotretinoin is [69].

### Erythropoietic Porphyria and Sun Sensitivity

A systematic review of treatments for the light sensitivity of erythropoietic protoporphyria, a rare inherited condition, found 16 studies of beta-carotene, mostly from the 1970s [117]. Their results were strongly contradictory, four of the five well-designed studies suggested a lack of efficacy for beta-carotene, N-acetylcysteine and vitamin C, and the reviewers judged the data insufficient to prove that any treatment works [117].

### Other Potential Benefits of Beta-Carotene

The USPSTF recommends against beta-carotene supplements for preventing cardiovascular disease or cancer [60].

|-----------|------|--------|-----------|-----------|
| Birth to 6 months* | 400 mcg RAE | 400 mcg RAE | — | — |
| 7–12 months* | 500 mcg RAE | 500 mcg RAE | — | — |
| 1–3 years | 300 mcg RAE | 300 mcg RAE | — | — |
| 4–8 years | 400 mcg RAE | 400 mcg RAE | — | — |
| 9–13 years | 600 mcg RAE | 600 mcg RAE | — | — |

### Tolerable Upper Intake Levels (UL) for Preformed Vitamin A

The ULs cover preformed vitamin A (retinol and its esters) from food and supplements combined; they do not apply to beta-carotene or other provitamin A carotenoids [109]:

| Age Group | UL (mcg RAE) | UL (IU as retinol) |
|-----------|-------------|---------------------|

Many supplements provide vitamin A partly or entirely from beta-carotene. In such cases, only the retinol/retinyl ester portion should be considered when assessing whether intake exceeds the UL. For example, a supplement with 3,000 mcg RAE of vitamin A where 60% comes from beta-carotene provides 1,200 mcg RAE of preformed vitamin A. That is below the UL for older children and adults but above the UL for children from birth to 8 years [4].

No UL has been established for beta-carotene, but the Food and Nutrition Board advises against beta-carotene supplementation for the general population, except as a provitamin A source to prevent deficiency [71].

### Specific Therapeutic Doses

- **Cystic fibrosis:** Lifelong supplementation at 750–3,000 mcg RAE per day depending on age, alongside other fat-soluble vitamins and pancreatic enzymes [72][73].

### Beta-Carotene from Diet

Nutritional needs should be met mainly through diet, and the Food and Nutrition Board advises against beta-carotene supplements for the general population except as a source of vitamin A to prevent deficiency [109].

## Safety and Side Effects

### Preformed Vitamin A Toxicity

Because vitamin A is fat-soluble, excess amounts accumulate primarily in the liver.

**Acute toxicity (hypervitaminosis A):** This follows one or a few very high doses, typically more than 100 times the RDA, within days to weeks. Typical symptoms are severe headache, blurred vision, nausea, dizziness, aching muscles and poor coordination; in severe cases, raised pressure in the cerebrospinal fluid can lead to drowsiness, coma and death [14][109]. Stop vitamin A supplements and contact your doctor if you have these symptoms [112]. If someone has severe symptoms such as extreme drowsiness or loss of consciousness after a large dose, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department [109].

**Chronic toxicity:** Regular high doses can cause dry skin, painful muscles and joints, fatigue, depression and abnormal liver tests [14][109]. In adults it may occur with regular intakes of more than 50,000 IU a day [112].

**Liver damage:** Vitamin A in high doses can injure the liver, causing jaundice, an enlarged liver and spleen, portal hypertension and cirrhosis [14]. If you have liver disease, check with your doctor before taking vitamin A supplements.

**Hypercalcemia:** Too much vitamin A can raise blood calcium to very high levels, which can damage the kidneys [112].

**Hair loss:** Hair changes, including hair loss and oily hair, are among the symptoms of too much vitamin A [112].

**Bone health:** In a Swedish study, women with retinol intakes greater than 1.5 mg a day had bone mineral density 10% lower at the femoral neck and 14% lower at the lumbar spine than women with intakes less than 0.5 mg a day, and their risk of hip fracture was doubled (odds ratio 2.1, 95% CI 1.1 to 4.0) [83]. In the Nurses' Health Study, postmenopausal women with the highest retinol intakes had a higher risk of hip fracture than those with the lowest (RR 1.89, 95% CI 1.33 to 2.68), while beta-carotene did not add significantly to the risk [110]. Both are observational studies, so they show an association, not proof that retinol causes fractures.

**Mortality:** A 2012 Cochrane review of 78 trials found that, in trials at low risk of bias, vitamin A did not significantly change mortality (RR 1.07, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.18), but higher doses of vitamin A may be associated with higher mortality [84]. An earlier version of the review, which Brad cites, found vitamin A increased mortality by 16% (RR 1.16, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.24) [115].

### Birth Defects (Teratogenicity)

Total intakes of preformed vitamin A above the UL can cause birth defects, including malformations of the eye, skull, lungs and heart [109]. Women who are or might be pregnant, and those who are breastfeeding, are advised not to take high doses (more than 3,000 mcg RAE, or 10,000 IU, a day) of vitamin A supplements [109]. The UL covers preformed vitamin A from food and supplements together: 3,000 mcg RAE in pregnancy from age 19, and 2,800 mcg RAE at 14 to 18 years [109].

Liver is very high in preformed vitamin A, and beef liver tops the food table below [109]. If you are pregnant, check with your doctor or midwife before eating liver or taking any supplement that contains retinol, cod liver oil included.

Unlike preformed vitamin A, beta-carotene is not known to be teratogenic or cause reproductive toxicity [1][71].

Some retinoid medicines also cause birth defects, such as isotretinoin for severe acne and etretinate for severe psoriasis [109]. Retinoid creams must not be used during pregnancy [114].

### Beta-Carotene Risks

**Lung cancer in smokers:** In male smokers, 20 mg a day of beta-carotene, with or without vitamin E, for 5 to 8 years raised the risk of lung cancer and of death, mainly from lung cancer and ischemic heart disease [109]. In current and former smokers and some men exposed to asbestos, 30 mg a day of beta-carotene plus 7,500 mcg RAE (25,000 IU) of retinyl palmitate raised the risk of lung cancer and of death from lung cancer [109].

**Carotenodermia:** The most common effect of long-term excess beta-carotene is skin that turns yellow-orange. It is harmless and reverses when beta-carotene intake stops [71][109]. Yellow skin has other causes too, so see your doctor if you are not sure why it has happened.

**Mortality:** In the 2012 Cochrane review, beta-carotene increased mortality in trials at low risk of bias (RR 1.05, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.09) [84].

### Children

Babies and children are more sensitive to vitamin A than adults and can become ill at smaller doses, around 20,000 IU a day for children [112]. Swallowing products that contain vitamin A, such as skin cream with retinol, can also poison them [112]. Store vitamin A supplements and retinol creams out of children's reach, and contact your doctor if you think a child has taken too much vitamin A [112].

## Drug Interactions

### Retinoid Medications

Retinoid medicines are made from vitamin A, and taking them with vitamin A supplements can raise the risk of vitamin A toxicity [89][109]. The isotretinoin label says to avoid supplements containing vitamin A during treatment [111]. Examples include:

- Isotretinoin (Accutane): severe acne treatment
- Acitretin (Soriatane): psoriasis treatment
- Bexarotene (Targretin): T-cell lymphoma treatment
- Etretinate

### Orlistat (Alli, Xenical)

Orlistat, a weight-loss treatment, can decrease the absorption of vitamin A, other fat-soluble vitamins, and beta-carotene, resulting in low plasma levels in some patients [89][90]. The manufacturers of Alli and Xenical recommend that patients on orlistat take a multivitamin supplement containing vitamin A and beta-carotene as well as other fat-soluble vitamins [92][93].

### Hepatotoxic Medications

Because large doses of vitamin A can injure the liver [14], check with your doctor or pharmacist before taking vitamin A supplements if you take medicines that can also affect the liver.

## Dietary Sources

Preformed vitamin A concentrations are highest in liver, fish, eggs, and dairy products. Most dietary provitamin A comes from leafy green vegetables, orange and yellow vegetables, tomato products, fruits, and some vegetable oils [1][4]. Vitamin A is routinely added to some foods including milk and margarine, and some ready-to-eat cereals are fortified [1][2]. Among US children and adolescents, enriched and fortified foods account for 34–40% of vitamin A intakes from food [94].

### Who Tends to Run Low on Vitamin A

**Premature infants:** Preterm infants have low liver stores of vitamin A at birth, and plasma concentrations often remain low throughout the first year of life. Those with vitamin A deficiency have a higher risk of eye and chronic lung diseases [95][96][97].

**Infants and children in low-income countries:** Approximately 190 million preschool-age children (one-third of all children in this age group), mostly in Africa and Southeast Asia, have vitamin A deficiency, with higher risk of visual impairment and illness/death from childhood infections [1][12][98].

**Pregnant and lactating women in low-income countries:** The World Health Organization estimates that 9.8 million pregnant women (15% worldwide) have xerophthalmia from vitamin A deficiency [99]. Breast milk from mothers with adequate vitamin A contains sufficient amounts for the first 6 months of life, but in vitamin A-deficient mothers, breast milk content is insufficient [100].

**People with cystic fibrosis:** Up to 90% of people with cystic fibrosis have pancreatic insufficiency, increasing their risk of vitamin A deficiency. Studies indicate that 2–13% of children and adolescents with cystic fibrosis have vitamin A deficiency [72][73]. Standard care includes lifelong vitamin A supplementation at 750–3,000 mcg RAE per day depending on age.

**People with Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis:** Approximately one-quarter of children with these conditions have vitamin A deficiency, and adults who have had them for several years are also at higher risk [103][104]. Some evidence supports vitamin A supplements in these conditions [102], but other research has found no benefit [109].

**People with celiac disease:** Some children and adults with newly diagnosed celiac disease have vitamin A deficiency. A gluten-free diet can eliminate the deficiency, but does not always [105][106][107][108].
