## Current view (reviewed 20 October 2026)

Brad's view is that most adults gain little from vitamin D supplements: in the VITAL trial, 2,000 IU a day for a median of 5.3 years in 25,871 adults did not significantly reduce cancer or major cardiovascular events ([Brad, 3 Dec 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-vitamin-d-study-forced-me-to-change-my-view)). He reads the trials as showing that people who are low in vitamin D benefit from supplementing while people with adequate levels do not, which suggests most people already have adequate levels ([Brad, 18 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-study-proved-we-were-wrong-about-vitamin-d)). For most adults he follows the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline: the recommended daily intake of 600 IU for younger adults, rising to 800 IU at 70 and above, with supplementation singled out for children up to 18, pregnant women, people with prediabetes and people over 75 ([Brad, 16 Oct 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/new-vitamin-d-study-on-telomeres-and-aging)). He takes 1,000 IU of vitamin D3 himself, and says that does not mean you need to ([Brad, 16 Oct 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/new-vitamin-d-study-on-telomeres-and-aging)). More is not better: in a 3-year Canadian trial, 4,000 and 10,000 IU a day made wrist bone density worse, falling by about 2.4% and 3.5% ([Brad, 16 Oct 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/new-vitamin-d-study-on-telomeres-and-aging)). He does not order vitamin D tests for otherwise healthy people, but since the TARGET-D trial (52% fewer repeat heart attacks with treat-to-target dosing, in an unblinded trial whose main outcome was not significant) he offers testing after a heart attack as an option, not a must, though the stroke and heart-failure results leaned the wrong way ([Brad, 3 Dec 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-vitamin-d-study-forced-me-to-change-my-view)).

## Key safety points

- Thiazide diuretics plus vitamin D can raise the risk of high blood calcium. Calcium should be monitored, especially in older adults and people with kidney problems [1].
- High vitamin D intakes might reduce the potency of atorvastatin, lovastatin and simvastatin [1]. If you take a statin or other prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting high-dose vitamin D.
- People with kidney disease may need prescription calcitriol under medical supervision rather than standard vitamin D [1]. In pregnancy, discuss higher doses with your doctor [1].
- For adults the tolerable upper intake level is 4,000 IU a day [1]. In one trial, high daily doses for 3 years lowered bone density compared with a low dose [29].
- Large infrequent doses may be harmful: a yearly 500,000 IU dose increased falls in older women [154].
- Too much vitamin D raises blood calcium. Symptoms range from nausea and weakness to dehydration and kidney stones, and in extreme cases kidney failure, heart rhythm problems and death [1].
- In the WHI trial, high calcium intake with moderate vitamin D raised kidney stone risk [1].

## 1. Overview

Vitamin D, a fat-soluble vitamin, functions as a prohormone, regulating calcium and phosphorus homeostasis, bone mineralization, immune function, cell differentiation, and neuromuscular health [1][2][3]. It is unique among vitamins because it can be synthesized endogenously when ultraviolet B (UVB) radiation from sunlight strikes the skin and triggers the conversion of 7-dehydrocholesterol to previtamin D3, which becomes vitamin D3 (cholecalciferol) [1][2].

Vitamin D obtained from sun exposure, food, and supplements is biologically inert and must undergo two hydroxylations for activation. The first occurs in the liver, converting vitamin D to 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D, also called calcidiol or calcifediol), the primary circulating form and the standard clinical marker of vitamin D status. The second hydroxylation occurs primarily in the kidney, producing 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (1,25(OH)2D, also called calcitriol), the physiologically active hormone form [1][2][3].

In foods and supplements, vitamin D exists in two main forms: D2 (ergocalciferol), made by UV irradiation of ergosterol in yeast, and D3 (cholecalciferol), made by irradiation of 7-dehydrocholesterol from lanolin (sheep's wool), with an animal-free D3 from lichen also available [1][2]. Both forms are well absorbed in the small intestine via passive diffusion and intestinal membrane carrier proteins, with fat in the gut enhancing absorption. Neither aging nor obesity alters vitamin D absorption from the gut [2].

### Prevalence of Deficiency

Brad's view is that the evidence does not support the idea that a large part of the population is low in vitamin D ([Brad, 18 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-study-proved-we-were-wrong-about-vitamin-d)). According to NHANES 2011-2014 data, 5% of the US population is at risk of deficiency (25(OH)D below 30 nmol/L or 12 ng/mL), and an additional 18% is at risk of inadequacy (30-49 nmol/L or 12-19.6 ng/mL) [1]. Most people in the United States consume less than recommended amounts: 92% of men, more than 97% of women, and 94% of people aged 1 year and older ingest less than the Estimated Average Requirement (400 IU) of vitamin D from food and beverages alone [1]. Sun exposure partially compensates, which is why serum levels are generally higher than dietary intake alone would predict.

Rates of deficiency vary significantly by race and ethnicity: 17.5% of non-Hispanic Black Americans are at risk of deficiency, compared to 7.6% of non-Hispanic Asians, 5.9% of Hispanics, and 2.1% of non-Hispanic White people [1].

### Assessing Vitamin D Status

Serum 25(OH)D concentration is the standard indicator of vitamin D status, with a circulating half-life of approximately 15 days [1][2]. The Food and Nutrition Board (FNB) at the National Academies has established the following thresholds [1]:

| 25(OH)D Level | Health Status |
|---|---|
| < 30 nmol/L (< 12 ng/mL) | Deficient — risk of rickets (children) and osteomalacia (adults) |
| 30 to < 50 nmol/L (12 to < 20 ng/mL) | Generally inadequate for bone and overall health |
| >= 50 nmol/L (>= 20 ng/mL) | Generally adequate for bone and overall health |
| > 125 nmol/L (> 50 ng/mL) | Potential adverse effects, particularly > 150 nmol/L (> 60 ng/mL) |

It is important to note that assay variability across laboratories can produce falsely low or high results. The Vitamin D Standardization Program has developed procedures to improve measurement consistency [1][5]. The Endocrine Society does not recommend routine 25(OH)D testing in healthy individuals [6][7].

### Signs and Symptoms of Deficiency

In children, vitamin D deficiency manifests as rickets — failure of bone tissue to properly mineralize, resulting in soft bones, skeletal deformities, developmental delay, hypocalcemic seizures, and dental abnormalities [1][2][8]. In adults and adolescents, deficiency can cause osteomalacia, in which bone is poorly mineralized and weak, with bone pain [1][8]. Too much is also a concern: the FNB notes that serum levels above 125 nmol/L (50 ng/mL) can be associated with adverse effects [1].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Vitamin D2 vs. Vitamin D3

Both forms raise serum 25(OH)D levels, but most evidence indicates that D3 raises levels more and keeps them higher for longer [1][9]. A trial in Dunedin, New Zealand found that 1,000 IU/day of D3 kept total serum 25(OH)D unchanged over the winter from a baseline of about 80 nmol/L (32 ng/mL), while levels on D2 at the same dose ended 21 nmol/L (about 8 ng/mL) lower than on D3 (Logan, Br J Nutr 2013) [40]. A 12-week trial in South Asian and white European women in the UK found that 600 IU/day of D3, given in juice or biscuits, raised 25(OH)D more than the same dose of D2 (Tripkovic, Am J Clin Nutr 2017) [41][1]. Vitamin D3 may therefore be the better form for most people (Tripkovic, Am J Clin Nutr 2017) [41].

### Calcifediol (25-hydroxyvitamin D)

Calcifediol is the form of vitamin D that the liver makes and that circulates in the blood. Per equivalent microgram dose, it is three to five times as potent as vitamin D3 at raising blood levels [1][10]. However, no calcifediol dietary supplements appear to be available to consumers in the United States [1]. Small amounts of calcifediol occur naturally in some animal foods, such as beef liver, egg yolks, and cheese [1].

### Tablets, Gummies, Liquids, and Sprays

Because vitamin D is fat-soluble, taking it with a meal containing fats significantly enhances absorption. A study found 32% higher peak blood levels when 50,000 IU of D3 was taken with a fat-containing breakfast versus a fat-free breakfast (Dawson-Hughes, J Acad Nutr Diet 2014) [42]. In a small study of Cleveland Clinic patients who had not responded to vitamin D treatment, taking their usual dose with the largest meal of the day raised serum 25(OH)D by 56.7% on average (Mulligan, J Bone Miner Res 2010) [43].

In a crossover study in healthy adults, a single 20,000 IU dose of D3 taken as gummies gave roughly twice the blood vitamin D3 levels of the same dose taken as tablets (average peak 47.3 ng/mL vs 23.4 ng/mL), which suggests gummies may be better absorbed (Wagner, Nutrients 2019) [44].

In a crossover trial in India, adults took 1,000 IU of D3 daily for 30 days as a buccal (cheek) spray or as a softgel. In patients with intestinal malabsorption, the spray raised 25(OH)D by 10.46 versus 3.96 ng/mL with the softgel; in healthy adults the rise was 8.0 versus 4.06 ng/mL (Satia, Nutr J 2015) [45].

### Key Principles for Form Selection

**General supplementation:** Vitamin D3 in any oral form (tablet, softgel, gummy, liquid), taken with your largest meal.

**Vegan option:** Vitamin D3 sourced from lichen is available and animal-free. Vitamin D2 from yeast is also vegan but less effective at maintaining levels [1].

**Malabsorption conditions:** In one small trial in people with intestinal malabsorption, an oral spray raised 25(OH)D more than a softgel (Satia, Nutr J 2015) [45]. Taking D3 with a meal that contains fat also improves absorption (Dawson-Hughes, J Acad Nutr Diet 2014) [42]. If you have a malabsorption condition, ask your doctor about testing and the right dose.

**Dose-response:** People with obesity might need more vitamin D to reach the same 25(OH)D level as people of normal weight [1]. In a trial of older adults with type 2 diabetes, 2,000 IU/day of D3 for 2 years raised 25(OH)D more in those who started below 50 nmol/L (20 ng/mL) than in those who started at or above it (47.9 vs 30.7 nmol/L) (Best, J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2022) [46].

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Bone Health and Fractures

Vitamin D regulates calcium and phosphorus absorption, maintains serum mineral concentrations for bone mineralization, and prevents hypocalcemic tetany. Without sufficient vitamin D, bones become thin, brittle, or misshapen [1][2].

**Overall evidence:** A review of 81 trials concluded that its findings "suggest that vitamin D supplementation does not prevent fractures or falls, or have clinically meaningful effects on bone mineral density" (Bolland, Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2018) [14].

**VITAL trial (bone substudy):** Among 25,871 generally healthy adults (mean age 67.1), 2,000 IU/day D3 for a median of 5.3 years did not lower risk of total, hip, or nonvertebral fractures versus placebo. Most participants were vitamin D sufficient at baseline (only 2.4% below 12 ng/mL, 12.9% below 20 ng/mL). Fracture risk did not differ by baseline 25(OH)D level (LeBoff, NEJM 2022) [12][1].

**Possible benefit at higher doses in older adults:** A pooled analysis of 11 trials in people aged 65 and older concluded that 800 IU a day or more was "somewhat favorable" for preventing hip and nonvertebral fractures. Only the highest intake level (792 to 2,000 IU/day) lowered hip fracture risk versus control groups (HR 0.70, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.86) (Bischoff-Ferrari, NEJM 2012) [13]. In the Women's Health Initiative, 1,000 mg calcium plus 400 IU D3 daily lowered hip fracture risk among women also taking hormone therapy (HR 0.59, 95% CI 0.38 to 0.93) but not among women on placebo hormone therapy (HR 1.20, 95% CI 0.85 to 1.69) (Robbins, Menopause 2014) [47]. A review of up to 20 years of WHI follow-up (68,132 women in the trials) concluded that the evidence does not support routine calcium plus vitamin D for fracture prevention in all postmenopausal women, though it is appropriate for women who do not get recommended amounts from their diet (Manson, JAMA 2024) [15].

**Women's Health Initiative — long-term follow-up:** Daily 400 IU D3 plus 1,000 mg calcium for 7 years did not reduce fracture incidence during the trial, but over a median 22.3-year follow-up, the supplements were associated with a 6% reduction in any fracture and 16% reduction in hip fracture (Yao, JAMA Netw Open 2019) [1].

**U-shaped relationship:** A cohort study of 1,662 men aged 70 to 97 found a U-shaped link between vitamin D levels and fracture risk. Compared with men at 60 to 72 nmol/L (about 24 to 29 ng/mL), men at or below 36 nmol/L (14.4 ng/mL) had a higher fracture risk (HR 3.5, 95% CI 1.7 to 7.0), as did men above 72 nmol/L (about 29 ng/mL) (HR 2.7, 95% CI 1.4 to 5.4) (Bleicher, J Bone Miner Res 2014) [48].

**Bone mineral density in generally healthy adults:** In a VITAL substudy of 771 adults not selected for low vitamin D, 2,000 IU/day D3 for 2 years did not improve bone mineral density or bone structure versus placebo (LeBoff, J Bone Miner Res 2020) [49]. In older women with low starting levels (at or below 50 nmol/L, about 20 ng/mL), doses from 400 to 4,800 IU/day with calcium had no significant effect on bone mineral density over 12 months (Smith, J Intern Med 2018) [50].

**High-dose harm to bone:** In a 3-year Canadian trial in healthy adults, 4,000 IU and 10,000 IU daily lowered bone density at the forearm (radius) compared with 400 IU/day; only 10,000 IU also lowered it at the lower leg (tibia) (Burt, JAMA 2019) [29]. The losses were greater in women than in men (Burt, J Bone Miner Res 2020) [51]. In Brad's account the participants were healthy adults, and wrist bone density fell by about 2.4% at 4,000 IU and 3.5% at 10,000 IU, compared with 1.2% at 400 IU ([Brad, 14 Jun 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/when-dosed-correctly-vitamin-d-cuts-sick-days-by-17)).

**Fracture healing:** Vitamin D deficiency may impede fracture healing. A case report described a 44-year-old man whose thighbone fracture had not healed 4 years after the injury despite three operations; once his low vitamin D was treated, the fracture fully healed (Moonen, Nutrition 2021) [52]. However, in a small trial in postmenopausal women with a wrist fracture, a 6-week bolus equal to 700 IU/day of D3 did not improve healing, and a dose equal to 1,800 IU/day may have hindered the return of bone stiffness in the first 12 weeks (Heyer, J Bone Miner Res 2021) [53].

**Vegan women:** In a North American cohort of adults aged 45 and older, female vegans had a higher risk of hip fracture than non-vegetarians (HR 1.55, 95% CI 1.06 to 2.26). Among women who took both vitamin D and calcium supplements, vegans were at no greater risk (Thorpe, Am J Clin Nutr 2021) [54].

**USPSTF position:** The USPSTF recommends against supplementation with 400 IU or less of vitamin D and 1,000 mg or less of calcium to prevent fractures in community-dwelling adults aged 50+ without osteoporosis or vitamin D deficiency. Evidence is insufficient for higher doses [1].

**Synthesis:** Vitamin D supplementation with calcium appears beneficial for fracture prevention primarily in people who are deficient (below 20 ng/mL), particularly older adults. There is no clear bone benefit from supplementation in people with adequate levels, and high doses (4,000+ IU/day) may actually reduce bone density.

### Muscle Strength and Falls

**Benefit in deficient older adults:** In a 9-month trial in Brazilian postmenopausal women with low vitamin D (mean 15 ng/mL), 1,000 IU/day raised lower-limb muscle strength on a chair-rising test by 25.3%, while women on placebo lost 6.8% of their lean mass (Cangussu, Osteoporos Int 2015) [55]. In postmenopausal women with levels averaging 10 ng/mL, 800 IU/day significantly increased quadriceps and hamstring strength (Apaydin, BMC Endocr Disord 2018) [56].

**No benefit at moderate levels:** Several trials found extra vitamin D did not improve muscle strength or function in people with moderate vitamin D levels. In postmenopausal women with osteopenia or osteoporosis (mean 23.4 ng/mL), 3,200 IU/day did no better than placebo on lower-limb function (Bischoff-Ferrari, Osteoporos Int 2025) [57]. In low-functioning older adults with levels of 18 to under 30 ng/mL, 2,000 IU/day for a year did not improve leg power, strength or physical performance (Houston, Am J Clin Nutr 2023) [58]. In postmenopausal women with osteopenia or osteoporosis, a year of high-dose vitamin D had no effect on muscle strength compared with a standard dose (Grimnes, Clin Endocrinol 2017) [59].

**High doses may harm muscles:** In postmenopausal women with low vitamin D and high parathyroid hormone levels, 2,800 IU/day for 3 months reduced handgrip strength by 9% and knee flexion strength by 13% compared with placebo (Bislev, Calcif Tissue Int 2018) [60]. Among overweight or obese postmenopausal women with insufficient vitamin D (10 to 32 ng/mL) in a weight-loss program, 2,000 IU/day for 12 months decreased leg strength compared with placebo (Mason, JAGS 2016) [61]. Among generally healthy, active older adults (mean age 75), most with adequate vitamin D, 2,000 IU/day for 3 years modestly reduced self-reported physical activity (Hussein, J Nutr Health Aging 2025) [62].

**In younger adults:** In 39 young men with low vitamin D on a 12-week resistance training program, 8,000 IU/day raised levels to 57 ng/mL but did not add to the strength or lean-mass gains seen with placebo. Strength gains in two exercises (chest press and seated row) were larger with placebo (Savolainen, Eur J Appl Physiol 2021) [63].

**Falls — U-shaped dose response:** In a 12-month trial in older women with levels below 20 ng/mL, falls decreased on 1,600-3,200 IU/day but not on lower or higher doses, compared with placebo. The share of women who fell was 21% among those whose levels reached 32-38 ng/mL and 72% among those at 38-46 ng/mL (Smith, J Steroid Biochem Mol Biol 2017) [27]. In adults aged 70 and older with a prior fall, 60,000 IU/month led to more falls than 24,000 IU/month (Bischoff-Ferrari, JAMA Intern Med 2016) [64]. Among adults aged 70 and older with elevated fall risk and low vitamin D levels, 1,000 IU/day or more did not prevent falls compared with 200 IU/day, and serious falls were more common on 1,000 IU/day (HR 1.87, 95% CI 1.03 to 3.41) (Appel, Ann Intern Med 2020) [28].

**USPSTF position:** The USPSTF concluded with moderate certainty that vitamin D supplementation does not reduce falls or fall-related injuries in community-dwelling adults aged 65+ without osteoporosis or deficiency [1].

### Cancer

**Incidence:** Clinical trials have generally not shown that vitamin D supplementation reduces overall cancer incidence [1]. The VITAL trial (25,871 participants, 2,000 IU/day, median 5.3 years) found no reduction in breast, prostate, or colorectal cancer rates (Manson, NEJM 2018) [17][1]. However, normal-weight participants had greater reductions in cancer incidence than those with overweight or obesity [1].

**Mortality:** Three meta-analyses of clinical trials found vitamin D supplementation significantly reduces total cancer mortality by 12-13%, with most benefit from daily dosing rather than bolus doses [1]. A secondary analysis of VITAL found fewer advanced (metastatic or fatal) cancers with vitamin D overall (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.69 to 0.99), with the strongest reduction in people with normal BMI (HR 0.62, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.86) (Chandler, JAMA Netw Open 2020) [18]. In VITAL's main analysis, the reduction in cancer deaths did not reach statistical significance ([Brad, 16 Oct 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/new-vitamin-d-study-on-telomeres-and-aging)). In an exploratory analysis of a European trial of 2,157 adults aged 70+, 2,000 IU/day D3 alone did not significantly lower invasive cancer risk (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.49 to 1.18), but combined with omega-3s and a simple home strength exercise program, risk was lower (HR 0.39, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.85) (Bischoff-Ferrari, Front Aging 2022) [19].

**WHI long-term follow-up:** 400 IU D3 plus 1,000 mg calcium daily did not affect cancer incidence during the 7-year trial, but reduced cancer mortality by 7% over a 22.3-year median follow-up (WHI, 2024) [1].

**Colorectal cancer:** In a pooled analysis of 17 cohorts, levels of 30-40 ng/mL went with 19-27% lower colorectal cancer risk than levels of 50 to under 62.5 nmol/L (20 to 25 ng/mL) (McCullough, J Natl Cancer Inst 2018) [34][1]. In a nested case-control study, people in the highest third of plasma 25(OH)D had a lower risk of colorectal tumors with a strong immune reaction than those in the lowest third (OR 0.10, 95% CI 0.03 to 0.35), but not of tumors with a weaker reaction (Song, Gut 2015) [65]. Among patients with advanced or metastatic colorectal cancer, levels at or above 24.1 ng/mL before first-line chemotherapy, compared with the lowest fifth, were associated with longer overall survival (HR 0.66, 95% CI 0.53 to 0.83) and progression-free survival (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.66 to 1.00) (Yuan, Clin Cancer Res 2019) [66]. However, in a trial of people who had colorectal adenomas removed, daily vitamin D3 (1,000 IU), calcium (1,200 mg) or both did not reduce recurrent adenomas (Baron, NEJM 2015) [67].

**Breast cancer:** In a meta-analysis, women with the highest vitamin D levels at diagnosis had approximately half the breast cancer death rate of those with the lowest levels (Mohr, Anticancer Research 2014) [68]. In women with low vitamin D and breast cancer, 2,000 IU/day during chemotherapy before surgery raised pathological complete response rates to 43% vs. 24% for placebo (Omodei, Nutr Cancer 2025) [69]. However, in women newly diagnosed with breast cancer, very high-dose D3 (40,000 IU/day) for a few weeks before surgery did not slow tumor growth, and side effects were reported as minimal (Arnaout, Breast Cancer Res Treat 2019) [70].

**Prostate cancer:** In a large study, both low and high vitamin D levels were associated with increased prostate cancer risk (Kristal, Cancer Epi Biomark Prev 2014) [35]. In that study, men with levels of 45 to 70 nmol/L (18-28 ng/mL) had a lower risk than men with lower or higher levels [1]. More recent studies and meta-analyses have generally found no relationship between 25(OH)D levels and prostate cancer risk, although higher levels may be associated with lower mortality from the disease [1].

**Pancreatic cancer:** In a pooled analysis of five large cohorts, levels of 20-29 ng/mL (OR 0.75, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.98) and 30 ng/mL or above (OR 0.71, 95% CI 0.52 to 0.97) were associated with lower pancreatic cancer risk compared with levels below 20 ng/mL (Wolpin, Cancer Epidemiol Biomarkers Prev 2011) [71]. However, a pooled analysis described by the NIH found a higher pancreatic cancer risk at levels of 100 nmol/L (40 ng/mL) or above [1].

**Overall mortality from all causes:** In a cohort of over 300,000 UK adults, the fall in mortality risk with higher vitamin D levels appeared to level off at 24 ng/mL (Fan, J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2020) [31]. A meta-analysis of trials found D3 supplementation lowered overall mortality (RR 0.89, 95% CI 0.80 to 0.99), while D2 showed no significant effect (Chowdhury, BMJ 2014) [20]. A 2020 meta-analysis covering 21 trials and more than 83,000 participants found no mortality benefit ([Brad, 14 Jun 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/when-dosed-correctly-vitamin-d-cuts-sick-days-by-17)). A Danish population study found 20-24 ng/mL was associated with the lowest mortality risk, and levels of 56 ng/mL with higher mortality than 20 ng/mL (HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.31 to 1.53) (Durup, J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2012) [30].

**Cancer treatment adjunct:** In an observational study of people with advanced cancer receiving immune checkpoint inhibitors, most of whom had low vitamin D, systematic vitamin D repletion was associated with a lower risk of death (HR 0.55, 95% CI 0.34 to 0.90) and of treatment discontinuation (HR 0.61, 95% CI 0.40 to 0.91) than in an unsupplemented control group (Bersanelli, Cancer Immunol Immunother 2023) [72]. In a post hoc subgroup of people with digestive tract cancers that were p53 immunoreactive (a marker of p53 mutations), 2,000 IU/day raised 5-year relapse-free survival to 80.9% versus 30.6% with placebo (HR 0.27, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.61) (Kanno, JAMA Netw Open 2023) [73]. In a Swedish trial in 244 people with advanced cancer in palliative care and levels below 50 nmol/L, 4,000 IU/day for 12 weeks led to a smaller rise in fentanyl doses and less fatigue than placebo (Frankling, Cancers 2021) [74].

**Synthesis:** In people with enough vitamin D, trials have not shown that supplements prevent cancer. Meta-analyses suggest they may lower cancer deaths, mainly with daily dosing [1], and in VITAL the clearest drop in advanced cancers was in people of normal weight [18]. The 2024 guideline found no proven target level and does not recommend routine testing [1].

### Cardiovascular Disease

**Observational data:** A meta-analysis of 34 observational studies (180,667 participants) found CVD event risk was 10% lower for each 10 ng/mL increase in 25(OH)D [1]. However, a Danish study (247,574 adults) found both low (~5 ng/mL) and high (~50 ng/mL) levels associated with greater CVD mortality risk — a U-shaped relationship [1].

**VITAL trial:** 2,000 IU/day D3 for a median of 5.3 years produced no significant reduction in heart attacks, strokes, coronary revascularization, or cardiovascular deaths in 25,871 adults (Manson, NEJM 2018) [1][17].

**Other trials:** A New Zealand trial (5,110 adults, 100,000 IU monthly, median 3.3 years) found no effect on any cardiovascular endpoint [1]. In heart failure patients with low vitamin D given 4,000 IU/day for 3 years, there was no mortality reduction and increased need for mechanical circulatory support implants (Zittermann, Eur Heart J 2017) [37]. In another heart failure trial (229 patients with levels below 20 ng/mL), 4,000 IU/day for a year did not improve walking distance but did improve the heart's pumping function (Witte, J Am Coll Cardiol 2016) [75].

**Blood pressure:** A meta-analysis of 46 trials found no effect of vitamin D on systolic or diastolic blood pressure, and no baseline factor predicted a better response; the authors concluded it should not be used to treat high blood pressure (Beveridge, JAMA Intern Med 2015) [21]. However, in a Chinese trial in people with high blood pressure already taking nifedipine, 2,000 IU/day D3 for 6 months raised mean levels from 19.4 to 34.1 ng/mL and lowered systolic blood pressure by 6.2 mmHg and diastolic by 4.2 mmHg more than placebo (Chen, Atherosclerosis 2014) [76].

**Cholesterol:** In statin-treated patients with high cholesterol, 2,000 IU/day D3 for 6 months significantly improved cholesterol: total cholesterol fell by 22.1 mg/dL and triglycerides by 28.2 mg/dL versus placebo (Qin, Clin Nutr 2015) [77]. In contrast, a small study of obese adolescents found that monthly high-dose vitamin D3 for 3 months did not significantly change fasting lipids (Javed, Pediatric Obesity 2015) [78].

**Arterial stiffness:** In overweight African-Americans with vitamin D deficiency, monthly vitamin D3 for 16 weeks improved arterial stiffness in a dose-dependent way: the clearest benefit came with 120,000 IU monthly, while 18,000 IU monthly did not help (Raed, PLOSOne 2017) [79].

**TARGET-D trial:** In 630 people after a heart attack, treat-to-target vitamin D3 (doses adjusted to keep 25(OH)D above 40 ng/mL; 52.4% started at 5,000 IU a day) over an average of 4.2 years did not change deaths (8.9% vs 9.2%), but there were fewer repeat heart attacks (3.8% vs 7.9%; HR 0.48), while heart failure hospitalizations (HR 1.21) and strokes (HR 1.69) leaned higher; only the heart attack result was statistically significant (May, Eur Heart J 2026) [80]. The trial was not blinded, and its main combined outcome of death, heart attack, heart failure hospitalization or stroke did not differ significantly between groups ([Brad, 3 Dec 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-vitamin-d-study-forced-me-to-change-my-view)).

**Synthesis:** Despite strong observational associations between low vitamin D and cardiovascular risk, clinical trials do not support supplementation for CVD prevention, even in deficient populations. The exception is a potential synergistic benefit with statin therapy, where maintaining sufficient vitamin D levels may improve lipid profiles. Brad reads TARGET-D as a hint of heart benefit from treat-to-target dosing in some groups that needs better-designed follow-up studies ([Brad, 3 Dec 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-vitamin-d-study-forced-me-to-change-my-view)).

### Diabetes and Insulin Resistance

**Type 1 diabetes risk:** In a nested case-control study of military service members, people with lower vitamin D levels were more likely to develop insulin-requiring diabetes later. Compared with the highest fifth of levels, the odds were higher in the lowest fifth (OR 3.5, 95% CI 2.0 to 6.0) and the second-lowest fifth (OR 2.5, 95% CI 1.5 to 4.2), and the authors linked levels of 60 nmol/L or more with lower risk (Gorham, Diabetologia 2012) [81].

**Prediabetes with vitamin D deficiency:** In a large 2.5-year trial, 4,000 IU/day D3 in people with prediabetes and adequate vitamin D (mean 27.7 ng/mL) did not reduce overall type 2 diabetes risk (Pittas, NEJM 2019) [1][22]. However, a post hoc analysis found 62% fewer new diabetes cases with vitamin D among those with levels below 12 ng/mL [1]. When only protocol-adherent participants were considered, supplementation lowered diabetes risk (HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.71 to 0.99) and made a return to normal blood sugar regulation more likely (rate ratio 1.31, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.70, or 1.45, 95% CI 1.05 to 2.00, depending on the definition) (Hsia, Diabetes Res Clin Pract 2023) [82].

**Insulin resistance:** In older adults in Lebanon with vitamin D deficiency, 30,000 IU D3 weekly for 6 months modestly reduced insulin resistance and fasting blood sugar compared with placebo (El Hajj, J Nutr Health Aging 2018) [83]. In healthy Japanese adults, 420 IU/day for 1 year lowered fasting glucose from 88.3 to 85.3 mg/dL and reduced an index of insulin resistance (Sun, Nutr Res 2016) [84].

**Established type 2 diabetes:** In a US trial in people with well-controlled type 2 diabetes who had not been chosen for low vitamin D (mean 26.6 ng/mL), 4,000 IU/day for 48 weeks did not change insulin secretion or HbA1c (Angellotti, J Endocrine Society 2018) [85]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 35 trials (43,407 adults) found vitamin D had no significant effects on glucose homeostasis, insulin secretion or resistance, or HbA1c, irrespective of population or dose [1].

**VITAL trial (diabetes substudy):** 2,000 IU/day D3 for a median of 5.3 years did not reduce type 2 diabetes risk in over 22,000 generally healthy older adults, and results did not differ by baseline vitamin D level (Tobias, Nat Commun 2025) [86].

**Gestational diabetes:** In a 6-week trial in 56 Iranian women with gestational diabetes, 1,000 mg calcium per day and 50,000 IU D3 at weeks 0 and 3 significantly lowered fasting glucose and improved insulin sensitivity compared with placebo (Asemi, Diabetologia 2014) [87]. The journal has since issued an expression of concern about this paper.

**Diabetic neuropathy:** In an open-label trial of 68 people with diabetic peripheral neuropathy, 5,000 IU/day for 8 weeks in addition to standard treatment modestly reduced overall pain scores compared to standard treatment alone (Pinzon, J Pain Res 2021) [88].

**Synthesis:** Vitamin D supplementation helps prevent type 2 diabetes primarily in people who are severely deficient (below 12 ng/mL). It does not improve glycemic control in people with established diabetes or adequate vitamin D levels.

### Depression and Mood

**Observational data:** A meta-analysis of 14 observational studies (31,424 adults) found an association between deficient or low 25(OH)D levels and depression [1]. In older Italian adults, levels below 20 ng/mL went with a rise in depressive symptoms (Milaneschi, J Clin Endocrinol Metab 2010) [89], and older US women with a total vitamin D intake of less than 100 IU had more depressive symptoms than those with higher intakes (Bertone-Johnson, Am J Clin Nutr 2011) [90].

**Clinical trials — mostly negative:** A meta-analysis of 9 trials (4,923 adults) found no significant reduction in depressive symptoms after vitamin D supplementation, using doses from 400 IU/day to 40,000 IU/week [1]. The VITAL trial found 2,000 IU/day for a median of 5.3 years did not affect depression incidence or mood scores in 16,657 adults (Okereke, JAMA 2020) [1][91]. A year-long Dutch trial in older adults with relatively low vitamin D found 1,200 IU/day did not improve depression or anxiety (de Koning, Am J Clin Nutr 2019) [92]. A trial in people with diagnosed depression found 2,800 IU/day on top of standard treatment did not reduce depression scores compared with placebo (Hansen, BMC Res Notes 2019) [93].

**Exception — diabetes and depression:** Among 68 people with type 2 diabetes, mild-moderate depressive symptoms and low vitamin D, 4,000 IU D3 daily for 3 months decreased depression scores by 27.6% versus 10.8% with placebo, and appeared to improve HbA1c (Omidian, Diabetes Metab Syndr 2019) [94].

**High-dose risk:** A large Australian trial in older adults (D-Health) found monthly high-dose vitamin D3 did not improve depression or change antidepressant use overall. In a subgroup analysis, people whose predicted level was 50 nmol/L or more were more likely to start antidepressants on vitamin D (HR 1.10, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.20) (Rahman, Int J Geriatr Psychiatry 2022) [95].

Brad notes a meta-analysis in which supplements improved symptoms in people with clinical signs of depression, but not mood in people without clinically significant symptoms ([Brad, 18 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-study-proved-we-were-wrong-about-vitamin-d)).

**Synthesis:** Despite plausible mechanisms and epidemiological associations, vitamin D supplementation does not prevent or treat depression in most populations. The sole exception may be in people with comorbid diabetes and vitamin D deficiency.

### Cognitive Function and Dementia

**Dementia risk:** A study of 1,658 older US adults found those with 25(OH)D below 10 ng/mL had a higher risk of dementia (HR 2.25, 95% CI 1.23 to 4.13) and of Alzheimer's disease (HR 2.22, 95% CI 1.02 to 4.83) over 5.6 years compared with people at 20 ng/mL or above (Littlejohns, Neurology 2014) [33]. A French study of 916 older adults found vitamin D deficiency was associated with nearly triple the risk of Alzheimer's (HR 2.85, 95% CI 1.37 to 5.97) over up to 12 years of follow-up (Feart, Alzheimers Dement 2017) [96]. Higher midlife vitamin D levels were associated with lower tau protein deposits on PET scans 16 years later, a major Alzheimer's marker (Mulligan, Neurol Open Access 2026) [97]. A French study found older women with the highest dietary vitamin D intake had lower odds of developing Alzheimer's over 7 years (OR 0.23, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.67) (Annweiler, J Gerontol A Biol Sci Med 2012) [98].

**Cognitive improvement in MCI:** A 12-month Chinese trial in 183 older adults with mild cognitive impairment (MCI) found 800 IU/day improved overall cognitive function scores and increased telomere length (Yang, J Alzheimers Dis 2020) [99].

**No cognitive benefit in other trials:** A 2-year trial in 620 older adults with mild to moderate vitamin D deficiency and early cognitive impairment found daily vitamin D did not improve cognitive function (Corbett, J Am Med Dir Assoc 2025) [100]. High-dose vitamin D (10,000 IU three times weekly) for 20 weeks did not improve cognition in people with MCI (Montero-Odasso, JAMA Netw Open 2023) [101]. An Australian trial in older adults who were largely vitamin D replete found monthly 60,000 IU of vitamin D for 5 years did not improve cognition (Pham, J Am Geriatr Soc 2023) [102].

**Possible concern with long-term use:** A population-based cohort study found dementia-free older adults taking vitamin D3 supplements for more than 146 days a year were 1.8 times more likely to develop dementia than those not taking them (Lai, Aging Cell 2022) [103]. This is an observational finding and needs cautious interpretation.

**Synthesis:** Low vitamin D levels are linked with a higher risk of dementia in observational studies, but most trials of supplements have not improved cognition. One trial in people with MCI found better cognitive scores with low-dose vitamin D [99]. High doses have shown no cognitive benefit, and long-term use may carry risks [103].

### Respiratory Infections and COVID-19

**Respiratory infections:** A meta-analysis of 25 trials found vitamin D supplementation protected against acute respiratory tract infection overall (adjusted OR 0.88, 95% CI 0.81 to 0.96), with benefit seen with daily or weekly dosing (adjusted OR 0.81, 95% CI 0.72 to 0.91) and the most benefit in people who were very deficient (below 10 ng/mL) and dosed daily or weekly (adjusted OR 0.30, 95% CI 0.17 to 0.53) (Martineau, BMJ 2017) [23]. Large monthly doses (60,000-100,000 IU) did not reduce infections (Murdoch, JAMA 2012; Pham, Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2021) [104][105]. In a winter trial in Danish children (mean starting level 79.8 nmol/L), 800 IU/day for 24 weeks led to 17% fewer sick days from respiratory infections and 43% fewer days with fever from a respiratory infection than placebo (Clerico, Eur J Nutr 2025) [106]. Brad's account of the same trial gives the dose as 25 µg (1,000 IU) daily for six months ([Brad, 14 Jun 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/when-dosed-correctly-vitamin-d-cuts-sick-days-by-17)).

**COVID-19 prevention:** A Mexican trial in 321 frontline healthcare workers found 4,000 IU/day of vitamin D for 30 days lowered the risk of COVID-19 infection (RR 0.23, 95% CI 0.09 to 0.55) (Villasis-Keever, Arch Med Res 2022) [107].

**COVID-19 severity:** In a pilot trial in 50 patients hospitalized with COVID-19 and vitamin D deficiency, supplementation shortened hospital stay (4 days vs 8 days) and time on supplemental oxygen (4 days vs 7 days) (De Niet, Nutrients 2022) [108]. In a UK study of 472 hospitalized patients, deficiency as commonly defined (below 50 nmol/L) was not linked to higher 28-day mortality. Compared with 50-74 nmol/L, the odds of death were higher both below 25 nmol/L (OR 2.37, 95% CI 1.17 to 4.78) and at 100 nmol/L or more (OR 4.65, 95% CI 1.51 to 14.34) (Subramanian, Am J Clin Nutr 2022) [109]. Single high doses (100,000-200,000 IU) given in hospital did not improve outcomes (Murai, JAMA 2021; Cannata-Andia, BMC Med 2022) [110][111].

### Autoimmune Disease

**Rheumatoid arthritis:** The VITAL trial found 2,000 IU/day of vitamin D reduced confirmed autoimmune disease by 22% overall (HR 0.78, 95% CI 0.61 to 0.99) (Hahn, BMJ 2022) [25]. Lower vitamin D levels have been associated with greater disease activity and severity in people with RA (Hong, Rheumatology 2014; Mouterde, J Rheumatol 2020) [112][113].

**Multiple sclerosis:** Epidemiological studies consistently show lower 25(OH)D levels are associated with higher MS risk. A Finnish study found women with 25(OH)D below 12 ng/mL had 43% higher MS risk than those at or above 20 ng/mL, and among women with two or more samples, levels below 30 nmol/L were associated with double the risk [1][114]. However, clinical trials (12 trials, 933 participants) found no effect of vitamin D supplementation on MS relapse rates or disability progression, though the evidence was of very low quality (Jagannath, Cochrane Review 2018) [1][115]. A study of 204 adults with clinically isolated syndrome found that vitamin D supplementation (1,000-10,000 IU/day) did not decrease conversion to MS diagnosis (Butzkueven, Brain 2023) [116].

**Hashimoto's thyroiditis:** Among 179 female patients with newly diagnosed Hashimoto's thyroiditis, vitamin D for 6 months significantly decreased TSH and thyroid peroxidase antibodies compared with controls (Jiang, Am J Transl Res 2023) [117]. Whether vitamin D changes the risk of developing thyroid disease is uncertain, so if you have a thyroid condition, discuss vitamin D with your doctor rather than relying on it as treatment.

**Graves' disease:** 2,800 IU/day, given alongside anti-thyroid medication (for up to 24 months) and for 12 months after stopping it, did not improve remission rates in people with normal or insufficient vitamin D levels (Grove-Laugesen, Thyroid 2023) [118].

### Asthma and COPD

**Asthma:** A Cochrane review of trials in children and adults with mostly mild to moderate asthma found vitamin D likely reduces asthma attacks requiring systemic steroids (rate ratio 0.63, 95% CI 0.45 to 0.88) and the risk of an attack needing an emergency visit or hospital admission (OR 0.39, 95% CI 0.19 to 0.78) (Martineau, Cochrane Database Syst Rev 2016) [24]. A later analysis of individual participant data confirmed an overall reduction in attacks requiring systemic steroids; protective effects were seen in people with very low vitamin D (below 10 ng/mL) but not in those with higher levels, although the authors found no definitive evidence that the effect differed between these groups (Jolliffe, Lancet Respir Med 2017) [119]. A study in Japanese schoolchildren found 800 IU/day for 2 months significantly improved asthma control, with asthma control test scores still better at 6 months (Tachimoto, Allergy 2016) [120]. In contrast, 4,000 IU/day for 48 weeks did not improve time to a severe asthma attack in US children with asthma whose levels were less than 30 ng/mL (Forno, JAMA 2020) [121]. In a secondary analysis of a prenatal trial of 4,400 IU/day, the greatest reduction in children's asthma or recurrent wheezing was seen in mothers with severe deficiency (below 12 ng/mL; aOR 0.48, CI 0.17 to 1.34), though this estimate was not statistically significant, and starting earlier in pregnancy appeared to help more (Shadid, Am J Clin Nutr 2023) [122].

**COPD:** In a UK trial of people with COPD, 3 mg (120,000 IU) of vitamin D3 every 2 months for a year did not reduce moderate or severe exacerbations overall, but protected against them in people with baseline levels below 20 ng/mL and not in those with higher levels (Martineau, Lancet Respir Med 2014) [123]. In a Belgian trial, 100,000 IU every 4 weeks for a year did not reduce exacerbations overall; a post hoc analysis suggested fewer exacerbations in people with severe deficiency at baseline (less than 10 ng/mL; rate ratio 0.57, CI 0.33 to 0.98) (Lehouck, Ann Intern Med 2012) [124].

### Vertigo (BPPV)

Multiple studies found vitamin D supplementation reduces recurrence of benign paroxysmal positional vertigo (BPPV) in people with low levels. In a multicentre trial, people with BPPV and levels below 20 ng/mL who took 400 IU vitamin D plus 500 mg calcium twice daily for 1 year had fewer recurrences (incidence rate ratio 0.76, 95% CI 0.66 to 0.87) (Jeong, Neurology 2020) [125]. Another Korean trial of 38 patients with low vitamin D found 7,000 IU weekly for a year lowered recurrence compared with placebo (Kong, Laryngoscope Investig Otolaryngol 2024) [126]. A post hoc analysis of a small trial in older adults with BPPV found vitamin D replacement was associated with fewer recurrences and better performance on a chair-stand test (Huang, Aging Clin Exp Res 2025) [127].

### Skin Conditions

**Eczema:** In a trial of 65 people with eczema (atopic dermatitis) on standard treatment, those whose levels were 20 ng/mL or more had lower severity scores than those below 20 ng/mL, whether or not they had taken the supplement; scores did not differ between 20 ng/mL or more and 30 ng/mL or more (Sanchez-Armendariz, Int J Dermatol 2018) [128]. In children with severe eczema, 1,600 IU/day for 12 weeks lowered severity scores more than placebo (Mansour, Pharmacol Res Perspect 2020) [129].

**Psoriasis:** A Norwegian study of 122 adults with mild psoriasis found high-dose D3 did not reduce severity despite raising levels from 15 to 30 ng/mL (Jenssen, JAMA Dermatol 2023) [130].

**Acne:** After 2 months of 1,000 IU/day, inflammatory lesions improved in 39 acne patients with vitamin D deficiency (Lim, PLoS One 2016) [131].

**Chronic hives (urticaria):** In 120 deficient people with chronic urticaria, 60,000 IU every 2 weeks for 12 weeks significantly reduced disease severity and inflammatory cytokines (Mony, Clinica Chimica Acta 2020) [132].

### NAFLD (Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease)

In 40 adults with significant liver fat and low vitamin D (mean 11.8 ng/mL), 20,000 IU vitamin D weekly reduced liver fat by approximately 5% after 4 weeks, and the improvement held over 6 months of supplementation (Papapostoli, J Gastrointestin Liver Dis 2016) [133]. A 6-month study found 2,000 IU/day raised levels to 30 ng/mL or above in 75% of NAFLD patients with simple fatty liver but only 15% of those with steatohepatitis; only those who reached adequate levels showed improvements in ALT (a liver enzyme) and insulin resistance (Dasarathy, J Nutr 2017) [134].

### Pregnancy

In an Indian trial, supplementing pregnant women with vitamin D (dose based on their starting level) raised average levels to about 32 ng/mL and lowered the combined rate of preterm labor, preeclampsia, and gestational diabetes (44% without vitamin D vs. 20.3% with). Babies had higher average birth weight (Sablok, Clin Endocrinol 2015) [135].

Prenatal vitamin D may reduce childhood asthma risk, though the evidence is uncertain. In a secondary analysis of a trial of 4,400 IU/day started at 10-18 weeks of pregnancy, the largest drop in asthma or recurrent wheeze by age 6 was in children of the most deficient mothers (below 12 ng/mL; aOR 0.48, 95% CI 0.17 to 1.34), an interval that includes no effect; starting earlier in pregnancy appeared to help more (Shadid, Am J Clin Nutr 2023) [122]. High-dose prenatal vitamin D (2,800 IU/day in total from week 24) was linked with fewer enamel defects in children's permanent teeth at age 6 (OR 0.47, 95% CI 0.27 to 0.81) (Norrisgaard, JAMA Pediatr 2019) [136]. In a UK trial in women without severe deficiency, daily vitamin D3 from early pregnancy led to greater bone mineral density in their children at age 4 (Curtis, JBMR Plus 2022) [137]. The effect was still seen at ages 6-7 (Moon, Am J Clin Nutr 2024) [138]. High-dose vitamin D during pregnancy did not improve children's motor, language, or cognitive development by age 6 (Sass, JAMA Netw Open 2020) [139].

### Weight and Body Composition

Clinical trials do not support vitamin D supplementation for weight loss. A meta-analysis of 12 vitamin D supplementation trials found no effect on body weight or fat mass without caloric restriction [1]. In postmenopausal women with overweight or obesity on a reduced-calorie diet and exercise program, vitamin D3 did not add to weight loss compared with placebo (Mason, Am J Clin Nutr 2014) [140]. However, people who lose weight tend to have larger rises in vitamin D levels than people whose weight stays the same [1].

### Allergies

In a US national survey, low vitamin D levels went with more allergic sensitization in children and teenagers but not in adults. Among US children and adolescents with serum vitamin D below 15 ng/mL, allergic sensitization was more common for 11 of 17 allergens tested; compared with levels above 30 ng/mL, the strongest associations were for oak (OR 4.75), peanut (OR 2.39), and ragweed (OR 1.83) (Sharief, J Allergy Clin Immunol 2011) [141]. In people with allergic rhinitis and low vitamin D (mean about 14 ng/mL), adding vitamin D to an antihistamine (cetirizine) may have modestly improved symptoms (Bakhshaee, Eur Arch Otorhinolaryngol 2019) [142]. In a larger Indian trial in persistent allergic rhinitis treated with nasal steroid sprays, continued vitamin D3 improved nasal symptoms and IgE levels, and symptoms came back when it was stopped (Rana, Indian J Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg 2025) [143].

### Erectile Dysfunction

In a retrospective study of men with erectile dysfunction and vitamin D deficiency, adding 4,000 IU D3 to daily tadalafil was linked with greater gains in erectile function and sexual desire than tadalafil alone (Demirci, Urol Int 2021) [144]. However, in a large Australian trial of older men, 60,000 IU vitamin D monthly for 3 years did not lower the rate of erectile dysfunction (prevalence ratio 1.00, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.03) (Romero, Clin Nutr ESPEN 2024) [145].

### Crohn's Disease and IBS

In a small pilot study of mild-moderate Crohn's disease with low vitamin D (mean 16 ng/mL), raising levels to a mean of 45 ng/mL over 24 weeks decreased Crohn's Disease Activity Index scores by 112 points (Yang, Clin Transl Gastroenterol 2013) [146]. The American Gastroenterological Association advises monitoring for vitamin D deficiency in all individuals with inflammatory bowel disease (Hashash, Gastroenterology 2024) [147]. A UK trial in 135 adults with IBS found 3,000 IU/day did not reduce symptom severity, although vitamin D levels rose (Williams, Eur J Nutr 2021) [148].

### Fibromyalgia

In 30 women with fibromyalgia and vitamin D below 32 ng/mL, raising levels to a target of 32 to 48 ng/mL for 20 weeks reduced pain compared with placebo (Wepner, Pain 2013) [149]. A trial in 80 women with fibromyalgia found 50,000 IU weekly for 12 weeks did not improve symptoms or pain (Lozano-Plata, Clin Rheumatol 2021) [150].

### Statin Interactions and Benefits

Vitamin D adequacy may blunt statin-related decline in exercise performance. In a small trial in vitamin D-deficient adults with type 2 diabetes doing aerobic exercise, simvastatin alone lowered cardiorespiratory fitness, while adding vitamin D (60,000 IU/week) blunted that decline; a similar pattern for muscle mitochondrial content did not reach statistical significance (Singla, J Diabetes 2017) [151]. In a pooled analysis of older adults given vitamin D, the rise in vitamin D levels was 21.4% smaller in statin users than in non-users (Bischoff-Ferrari, JAGS 2017) [152]. However, vitamin D supplementation (2,000 IU/day) did not prevent statin-associated muscle symptoms or reduce statin discontinuation in a large trial (OR 0.97, 95% CI 0.80 to 1.18) (Hlatky, JAMA Cardiol 2022) [153].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Recommended Dietary Allowances (RDAs)

From the Food and Nutrition Board at the National Academies [1]:

| Age Group | RDA |
|---|---|
| 0-12 months | 10 mcg (400 IU)* |
| 1-70 years | 15 mcg (600 IU) |
| > 70 years | 20 mcg (800 IU) |
| Pregnancy/Lactation | 15 mcg (600 IU) |

*Adequate Intake (AI), not RDA

The Endocrine Society (2024) recommends routine vitamin D supplementation for children and teens (1-18 years), pregnant women, adults with prediabetes, and adults aged 75+, but not for healthy adults aged 19-74 [6][7].

### Tolerable Upper Intake Levels (ULs)

| Age | UL |
|---|---|
| 0-6 months | 25 mcg (1,000 IU) |
| 7-12 months | 38 mcg (1,500 IU) |
| 1-3 years | 63 mcg (2,500 IU) |
| 4-8 years | 75 mcg (3,000 IU) |
| 9+ years, including pregnancy | 100 mcg (4,000 IU) |

While signs of toxicity are unlikely below 10,000 IU/day, the FNB noted that even intakes below the UL may have adverse effects over time, and recommended avoiding serum 25(OH)D levels above 50-60 ng/mL [1].

### Practical Dosing by Indication

Brad follows the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline. It recommends routine vitamin D supplementation for four groups (children and teens age 1 to 18 years, pregnant women, adults with pre-diabetes, and adults age 75 years and older), but not for healthy adults age 19 to 74. It does not recommend specific doses but notes that all individuals should adhere to the RDA [1][6].

**Healthy adults (19 to 74):** Follow the recommended daily intake: 600 IU for younger adults, increasing to 800 IU at 70 and above ([Brad, 16 Oct 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/new-vitamin-d-study-on-telomeres-and-aging)). Taking vitamin D with the largest meal of the day may improve absorption [43].

**No routine testing, no target level:** The guideline acknowledges we don't know what optimal levels of vitamin D are, so it abandoned its earlier thresholds for insufficiency and deficiency and advises against routine screening ([Brad, 18 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-study-proved-we-were-wrong-about-vitamin-d)). A test makes sense when risk factors point toward checking ([Brad, 16 Oct 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/new-vitamin-d-study-on-telomeres-and-aging)). Brad does not order vitamin D tests for otherwise healthy patients; since the TARGET-D trial he offers testing after a heart attack as an option, not a must (see Cardiovascular Disease) ([Brad, 3 Dec 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-vitamin-d-study-forced-me-to-change-my-view)).

**Children and teens (1 to 18 years):** Empiric vitamin D helps prevent nutritional rickets and may lower respiratory-infection risk ([Brad, 14 Jun 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/when-dosed-correctly-vitamin-d-cuts-sick-days-by-17)). In Brad's summary of the guideline, kids up to 18 should get about 1,500 IU daily ([Brad, 18 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-study-proved-we-were-wrong-about-vitamin-d)).

**Pregnancy:** Supplementation during pregnancy can lower the risk of preeclampsia and preterm birth; in Brad's summary of the guideline, the benefits came with doses around 3,000 IU daily ([Brad, 18 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-study-proved-we-were-wrong-about-vitamin-d)). Talk to your doctor before taking more than the RDA in pregnancy.

**Prediabetes:** For people at high risk of progressing to diabetes, vitamin D could play a preventive role; studies suggest an average dose of about 3,500 IU daily ([Brad, 18 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-study-proved-we-were-wrong-about-vitamin-d)).

**Older adults (75+):** The Endocrine Society recommends routine supplementation at this age but no specific dose, beyond following the RDA of 800 IU over age 70 [1]. Taking with calcium (800-1,200 mg/day from food and supplements combined) improves bone outcomes [1][6].

**People at high risk of deficiency:** If someone is older, housebound, or clearly at high risk of deficiency, Brad recommends a moderate daily dose rather than a blood test first ([Brad, 3 Dec 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-vitamin-d-study-forced-me-to-change-my-view)). Where a doctor has diagnosed a deficiency, the doctor sets the dose.

**Breastfed infants:** 400 IU D3 daily starting shortly after birth, per the American Academy of Pediatrics [1].

**Statin users:** Statin users had a 21.4% smaller rise in vitamin D levels than non-users (Bischoff-Ferrari, JAGS 2017) [152].

**Avoid bolus dosing:** Large infrequent doses (monthly or annual) may be harmful. A yearly 500,000 IU dose increased falls and fractures in older women compared with placebo (Sanders, JAMA 2010) [154]. Monthly 60,000 IU was linked to higher cancer mortality in an exploratory analysis of the D-Health trial (Neale, Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2022) [36]. Lower doses taken more often may be a better approach (Owens, Med Sci Sports Exerc 2017) [155].

### How to Read a Supplement Label

Vitamin D is measured in International Units (IU) or micrograms (mcg). The conversion: **1 mcg = 40 IU**, so 1,000 IU = 25 mcg. The FDA Daily Value (DV) for vitamin D is 20 mcg (800 IU) for adults and children aged 4+. Products listing 100% DV contain 800 IU [1].

### Target Blood Levels

There is no target level to dose toward: the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline has not identified 25(OH)D concentrations associated with sufficiency and does not recommend routine testing in healthy people [1] ([Brad, 16 Oct 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/new-vitamin-d-study-on-telomeres-and-aging)). The Food and Nutrition Board considers levels of 20 ng/mL or more sufficient for most people [1]. It advised avoiding levels above about 50 to 60 ng/mL, and found that even levels of about 30 to 48 ng/mL are associated with higher all-cause mortality, cancer at some sites, cardiovascular events, and falls and fractures in older adults [1].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Common Side Effects

For adults the tolerable upper intake level is 4,000 IU/day [1]. Taking too much can raise blood calcium, which may cause effects such as [1]:
- Nausea, vomiting, loss of appetite
- Constipation
- Muscle weakness
- Headache, confusion, irritability
- Metallic taste
- Fatigue

### Toxicity (Hypercalcemia)

Vitamin D toxicity almost always results from excessive supplement intake; experts do not believe sun exposure causes it [1]. Toxicity causes hypercalcemia (serum calcium > 11.1 mg/dL) with serum 25(OH)D typically above 150 ng/mL [1]. Symptoms range from nausea and weakness to dehydration and kidney stones, and in extreme cases kidney failure, cardiac arrhythmias, calcification of soft tissues, and death [1].

### Adverse Effects at Levels of 30-50 ng/mL

Even below classic toxicity thresholds, some studies have linked higher vitamin D levels or doses with harm:

- **Bone density loss:** 4,000 and 10,000 IU/day for 3 years lowered radial bone density versus 400 IU/day, with no difference in bone strength (Burt, JAMA 2019) [29]
- **Falls:** In one trial, levels of 38-46 ng/mL had the highest fall rates (Smith 2017) [27]. In another, higher daily doses did not prevent falls compared with a low dose, and several analyses raised safety concerns, including more serious falls (Appel 2020) [28]
- **All-cause mortality:** A Danish study found higher mortality at 56 ng/mL versus 20 ng/mL (HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.31 to 1.53) (Durup 2012) [30]; a multinational trial, mostly in South Asia, South East Asia, and South America, found 60,000 IU/month was associated with higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.29, 95% CI 1.03 to 1.61) (Joseph 2022) [156]
- **Cancer-related mortality:** In an exploratory analysis of a 5-year trial, monthly 60,000 IU D3 was linked to higher cancer mortality after the first 2 years of follow-up (HR 1.24, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.54), though the main cancer mortality result was not statistically significant (Neale, Lancet Diabetes Endocrinol 2022) [36]
- **Sleep disruption:** In a small trial in people with multiple sclerosis, high-dose D3 lowered nighttime melatonin (Golan, Brain Behav Immun 2013) [157]. In postmenopausal women losing weight, vitamin D supplements did not change overall sleep quality versus placebo, but women who reached 32 ng/mL or more reported worse sleep (Mason, Prev Med 2016) [158]

### Harm from Single Large Doses

Very high single doses may cause harm. Giving 540,000 IU once to critically ill, deficient patients did not lower mortality compared with placebo (Nat Heart Lung Blood Inst, NEJM 2019) [38]. A single yearly 500,000 IU dose increased falls in older women (incidence rate ratio 1.15, 95% CI 1.02 to 1.30) (Sanders, JAMA 2010) [154]. In elite athletes, very high weekly doses raised 24,25(OH)2D, an inactive breakdown product, and the authors suggest such doses may work against their intended purpose (Owens, Med Sci Sports Exerc 2017) [155]. In a small pilot trial in older adults, adding a one-time 50,000 IU bolus to 1,000 IU daily made no reported difference to vitamin D levels or other secondary outcomes compared with 1,000 IU daily alone (Feehan, Exp Gerontol 2021) [159].

### Kidney Stones

The combination of high calcium intake (~2,100 mg/day) with moderate vitamin D (~765 IU/day) increased kidney stone risk by 17% over 7 years in the WHI trial. However, shorter trials of vitamin D alone or with calcium found increased hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria but not kidney stones [1].

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy:** 600-1,000 IU/day appears safe. Higher doses should be discussed with a healthcare provider [1].

**Infants:** 400 IU/day is adequate and safe [1]. 1,000 IU/day is the upper limit for infants up to 6 months [1]. In a trial in breastfed infants, a 1,600 IU/day dose was stopped early because it raised vitamin D levels too high (Gallo, JAMA 2013) [160].

**Obesity:** Subcutaneous fat sequesters vitamin D, so people with obesity might need higher doses to reach the same serum levels [1].

**Kidney disease:** Impaired conversion of 25(OH)D to active 1,25(OH)2D. Supplementation with calcitriol (prescription) rather than standard vitamin D may be necessary under medical supervision [1].

**Bariatric surgery:** Reduced absorption of fat-soluble vitamins including vitamin D. Screening, monitoring, and replacement are recommended before and after surgery [1].

**Black Americans:** Common genetic variants likely explain why many Black Americans have lower levels of vitamin D binding protein, so bioavailable vitamin D can be similar despite lower total 25(OH)D levels. A US study found that while total levels were 15.6 ng/mL in Black vs. 25.8 ng/mL in White participants, bioavailable vitamin D was similar, and bone mineral density was actually higher in the Black group (Powe, NEJM 2013) [161]. Standard total 25(OH)D measurements may overestimate deficiency in this population.

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Vitamin D Affects Drug Metabolism

| Drug | Interaction | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Thiazide diuretics | Vitamin D increases calcium absorption; combined with thiazide-reduced calcium excretion, risk of hypercalcemia increases | Monitor calcium levels, especially in older adults and those with kidney impairment [1] |

### Drugs That Affect Vitamin D Levels

| Drug Class | Effect on Vitamin D | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Orlistat (weight loss) | Reduces vitamin D absorption from food and supplements | Supplement vitamin D and separate from orlistat doses [1] |
| Statins | Complex bidirectional effects. Rosuvastatin may increase levels [162]; atorvastatin may decrease levels [163]. Statins may reduce the body's own vitamin D synthesis [1] | Statin users had a 21.4% smaller rise in vitamin D levels than non-users [152]. High vitamin D intakes might reduce the potency of atorvastatin, lovastatin, and simvastatin, which appear to compete with vitamin D for the same metabolizing enzyme [1]. If you take a statin, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting high-dose vitamin D |
| Corticosteroids (prednisone) | Reduce calcium absorption and impair vitamin D metabolism. Deficiency was more than twice as common in oral steroid users (11%) vs. nonusers (5%) [1] | If you take steroids long term, ask your doctor whether you need vitamin D testing or supplements |
| Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) | Long-term use associated with lower vitamin D levels. In one study, 100% of pantoprazole users were deficient vs. 30% of non-users (Losurdo, J Clin Med 2023) [164] | If you take a PPI long term, ask your doctor whether vitamin D testing makes sense |
| Anticonvulsants | Phenobarbital, phenytoin increase vitamin D catabolism | May require higher supplement doses [1] |
| Cholestyramine | Reduces vitamin D absorption | Separate doses by several hours [1] |
| Combined oral contraceptives | Some formulations may raise vitamin D binding protein and lower bioavailable vitamin D, while total levels change little | Effects differ by formulation; levonorgestrel alone showed no significant change (Stanczyk, J Steroid Biochem Mol Biol 2021) [165] |

### Practical Notes on Food Sources

- **Cooking method matters:** Cooking and storage may lower the vitamin D in UV-exposed mushrooms, though levels are likely to stay higher than in most foods if eaten before the best-before date (Cardwell, Nutrients 2018) [166]
- **Fat may help absorption:** Fat in the gut enhances vitamin D absorption, though some is absorbed without it [1]. In an 8-week trial in young women in autumn, eating salmon sausage slowed the seasonal fall in 25(OH)D compared with control, while smoked salmon providing similar vitamin D did not (Utri-Khodadady, Nutrients 2024) [167]
- **Animal foods contain calcifediol:** The 25(OH)D form in animal tissues is approximately 5 times more potent than parent vitamin D, meaning the total vitamin D activity of beef, pork, chicken, and eggs may be 2-18 times higher than the standard vitamin D content listed [1]
- **Fortification:** Almost all US milk is voluntarily fortified with ~120 IU/cup [1]. Ready-to-eat breakfast cereals often contain added vitamin D [1]

### Sun Exposure

Some expert bodies suggest that approximately 5-30 minutes of sun exposure between 10 AM and 4 PM, daily or at least twice weekly, to the face, arms, hands, and legs without sunscreen usually leads to sufficient vitamin D synthesis [1]. However, many factors reduce production: higher latitude, winter season, dark skin, older age, cloud cover, smog, and sunscreen use. UVB does not penetrate glass [1].

Experts do not believe that excess sun exposure causes vitamin D toxicity, because heat in the skin turns previtamin D3 into inactive forms [1]. However, frequent tanning bed use can raise levels very high [1]. Importantly, a large US cross-sectional study found that routine sunscreen use and other sun protection were not associated with lower bone density or more osteoporotic fractures, and its authors note emerging evidence that sun protection does not cause vitamin D deficiency (Afarideh, JAMA Dermatol 2021) [168].

In Korean adults with low vitamin D, 30-60 minutes of summer sunlight 3 times per week raised levels by only 0.9 ng/mL over one month, suggesting standard sun exposure recommendations may not be enough to correct low levels (Lee, J Korean Med Sci 2020) [169].

### Magnesium and Vitamin D

Adequate magnesium intake may help maintain optimal vitamin D levels. In a trial in adults with overweight or obesity, 360 mg magnesium glycinate plus 1,000 IU D3 daily raised 25(OH)D by about 6 ng/mL, the largest rise of the three groups (Cheung, Nutrition 2022) [170]. Among 180 US adults, magnesium raised D2 levels more as baseline levels rose, while for D3, magnesium raised levels when they started near 30 ng/mL and lowered them when they started higher, which the authors suggest may help optimize vitamin D status (Dai, Am J Clin Nutr 2018) [39]. Most US adults do not meet the recommended intake of magnesium [39].

### Exercise and Vitamin D Levels

A single bout of exercise may briefly raise vitamin D levels. In healthy adults, 60 minutes of moderate-intensity treadmill exercise raised 25(OH)D and the biologically active 1,25(OH)2D3 immediately afterward compared with rest, and 1,25(OH)2D3 was still raised 1 hour later (Davies, J Physiol 2024) [171]. A winter trial in adults with overweight or obesity found regular exercise may prevent the winter decline in the active form of vitamin D, even without weight loss (Perkin, Adv Sci 2025) [172].
