## Key safety points

- Thiazide diuretics taken with vitamin D might lead to high blood calcium, especially in older adults and people with reduced kidney function or hyperparathyroidism [1]. High calcium can bring on heart rhythm problems in people taking digoxin [2].
- High intakes might reduce the potency of atorvastatin, lovastatin and simvastatin [1], one more reason to avoid high-dose vitamin D. Steroids and orlistat can lower vitamin D [1]. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before taking more than the RDA.
- If you are pregnant or have kidney disease, sarcoidosis or another granulomatous disease, ask your doctor before taking more than the RDA [1]. In sarcoidosis, supplements raised urine calcium in some patients [3]. Rare CYP24A1 gene faults make people very sensitive to vitamin D [4].
- For adults the tolerable upper intake level is 4,000 IU a day [1]. In a 3-year trial, 4,000 and 10,000 IU a day lowered forearm bone density compared with 400 IU [5].
- In the WHI trial, high calcium intake with moderate vitamin D raised kidney stone risk [1], and the USPSTF found that vitamin D with calcium raises kidney stone rates [36].
- Large infrequent doses may be harmful: a yearly 500,000 IU dose raised falls and fractures in women aged 70 and over [6].
- Too much vitamin D raises blood calcium. Symptoms range from nausea, weakness and thirst to kidney stones, and in extreme cases kidney failure, heart rhythm problems and death [1].

## 1. Overview

Vitamin D is a fat-soluble vitamin. The skin makes it when UVB light converts 7-dehydrocholesterol to previtamin D3, which becomes vitamin D3 [1]. Vitamin D from sun, food and supplements is inactive until the body changes it twice: the liver makes 25-hydroxyvitamin D (25(OH)D, also called calcidiol), the main form in blood, and the kidney makes 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D (calcitriol), the active hormone [1]. Its main job is to help the gut absorb calcium and to keep blood calcium and phosphate high enough for bone to mineralise [1].

Supplements contain D2 (ergocalciferol), made by UV irradiation of ergosterol in yeast, or D3 (cholecalciferol), usually made from lanolin in sheep's wool; an animal-free D3 from lichen is also available [1]. Both are well absorbed in the small intestine. Fat in the gut helps, but some vitamin D is absorbed without it, and neither ageing nor obesity changes absorption from the gut [1].

### Prevalence of Deficiency

Brad's view is that the evidence does not support the idea that a large part of the population is low in vitamin D ([Brad, 18 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-study-proved-we-were-wrong-about-vitamin-d)). The expert committee that set US intakes in 2011 concluded that vitamin D inadequacy in North America had been overestimated [7]. In US survey data from 2011 to 2014, 5% of people were at risk of deficiency (below 30 nmol/L, or 12 ng/mL) and 18% at risk of inadequacy (30 to 49 nmol/L, or 12 to 19.6 ng/mL); 4% had levels above 125 nmol/L (50 ng/mL) [1].

Rates of deficiency vary by race and ethnicity: 17.5% of non-Hispanic Black Americans are at risk of deficiency, compared to 7.6% of non-Hispanic Asians, 5.9% of Hispanics, and 2.1% of non-Hispanic White people [1]. People who are housebound, cover most of their skin for religious reasons or work out of the sun are less likely to get enough from sunlight [1]. Conditions that impair fat absorption, such as some liver diseases, cystic fibrosis, coeliac disease, Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, also raise the risk of deficiency [1].

### Assessing Vitamin D Status

Blood 25(OH)D is the usual marker of vitamin D status, with a half-life of about 15 days [1]. One ng/mL equals 2.5 nmol/L [1]. The Food and Nutrition Board (FNB) uses these levels [1]:

| 25(OH)D Level | Health Status |
|---|---|
| < 30 nmol/L (< 12 ng/mL) | Linked to deficiency, which can lead to rickets in children and osteomalacia in adults |
| 30 to < 50 nmol/L (12 to < 20 ng/mL) | Generally inadequate for bone and overall health |
| >= 50 nmol/L (>= 20 ng/mL) | Generally adequate for bone and overall health |
| > 125 nmol/L (> 50 ng/mL) | Potential adverse effects, particularly > 150 nmol/L (> 60 ng/mL) |

An international conference on vitamin D tests judged levels of 20 to 50 ng/mL safe and sufficient for bone health in the general population, and below 12 ng/mL a risk for rickets and osteomalacia [8]. It also warned that results from tests that are not standardised are inconsistent [8]. If you do get tested, note that in a crossover study an hour of moderate exercise briefly raised 25(OH)D compared with rest, so a workout just before the blood draw may nudge the result up [103].

The 2024 Endocrine Society guideline advises against routine 25(OH)D testing in all the groups it considered, including people with obesity or dark skin [9]. Its review found no trials of screening [10], and the US Preventive Services Task Force found too little evidence to judge screening adults without symptoms [1].

### Signs and Symptoms of Deficiency

In children, deficiency causes rickets, in which bones fail to mineralise and become soft and misshapen [1]. In adults it causes osteomalacia, in which bone is poorly mineralised and weak [1]. Too much is also a problem: the FNB notes that levels above 125 nmol/L (50 ng/mL) can be associated with adverse effects [1].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Vitamin D2 vs. Vitamin D3

A meta-analysis of randomised trials found that D3 raised 25(OH)D more than D2, although in that analysis the gap was lost with daily dosing [11]. Two daily trials still favoured D3. In Dunedin, New Zealand, 1,000 IU a day of D3 held 25(OH)D steady over winter from a starting level of 80 nmol/L, while levels on D2 ended 21 nmol/L lower [12]. In UK women, 15 µg (600 IU) a day of D3 in juice or biscuits raised winter levels more than the same dose of D2 [13]. D3 is likely the better choice for most people.

### Calcifediol (25-hydroxyvitamin D)

Calcifediol is 25(OH)D itself. Per microgram it is three to five times as potent as D3, but no calcifediol supplement appears to be sold to consumers in the US [1]. In a trial in older adults it was about 3 times as effective as D3 at raising 25(OH)D [14]. Meat and eggs carry small amounts too (see Dietary Sources).

### Tablets, Gummies, Liquids, and Sprays

Taking D3 with a meal that contained fat gave a 32% higher peak blood level than a fat-free meal [15]. In 17 clinic patients who had not responded to treatment, taking the usual dose with the largest meal of the day raised 25(OH)D by 56.7% on average after 2 to 3 months; the study had no control group [16].

In a crossover study, a single 20,000 IU dose taken as gummies gave a higher peak blood level of vitamin D3 than the same dose as tablets (47.3 vs 23.4 ng/mL) [17]. In a crossover trial in India, adults took 1,000 IU of D3 a day for 30 days as a cheek spray or a softgel. The spray raised 25(OH)D by 10.46 vs 3.96 ng/mL in people with intestinal malabsorption [18].

A skin patch containing cholecalciferol phosphate did not meaningfully raise 25(OH)D3 over 8 weeks in a placebo-controlled trial: the difference was +0.52 ng/mL [19].

### Key Principles for Form Selection

**General supplementation:** Vitamin D3 in any oral form, taken with a meal that contains some fat [15].

**Vegan option:** Vitamin D3 sourced from lichen is available and animal-free [1]. Vitamin D2 from yeast is also vegan, but raised 25(OH)D less than D3 in trials [11].

**Malabsorption conditions:** In one small trial in people with intestinal malabsorption, a cheek spray raised 25(OH)D more than a softgel [18]. If you have a malabsorption condition, ask your doctor about testing and the right dose.

**Dose-response:** People with obesity might need more vitamin D to reach the same 25(OH)D level as people of normal weight [1]. In a trial of older adults with type 2 diabetes, 2,000 IU a day of D3 raised 25(OH)D more in those who started below 50 nmol/L than in those at or above it (47.9 vs 30.7 nmol/L) [20].

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Bone Health and Fractures

Vitamin D helps the gut absorb calcium and keeps blood calcium and phosphate high enough for bone to mineralise. Without enough of it, bones can become thin, brittle or misshapen [1].

**Overall evidence:** An 81-trial meta-analysis found no effect on total fracture, hip fracture or falls, and no clinically meaningful effect on bone mineral density [21]. A 2026 meta-analysis of 69 trials and 153,902 people, most of them living in the community and not at high risk, found that vitamin D alone did not change fracture risk (risk ratio 1.00, high certainty) and calcium plus vitamin D gave a risk ratio of 0.91 [22]. The authors judged the benefit for fractures and falls to be little to none, and noted limited evidence for people at high risk or in residential care [22].

**VITAL trial (bone substudy):** In 25,871 generally healthy adults, 2,000 IU a day of D3 for a median of 5.3 years did not reduce total fractures (hazard ratio 0.98), and the effect did not differ by starting 25(OH)D level [23].

**Finnish trial (2026):** In 2,495 men aged 60 and over and women aged 65 and over, most of whom had enough vitamin D, neither 1,600 nor 3,200 IU a day for 5 years reduced fractures [24]. The 1,600 IU group had more fractures than placebo during treatment (hazard ratio 1.52), but the 3,200 IU group did not (hazard ratio 1.07) [24]. In the 7.3-year follow-up the pattern held: hazard ratio 1.40 for 1,600 IU and 1.08 for 3,200 IU [24]. Because the higher dose showed no excess, the signal may be chance; the authors concluded that supplements did not reduce the risk of fractures [24]. Falls and fall injuries were similar in all three groups [25].

**Possible benefit at higher doses in older adults:** A pooled analysis of 11 trials in people aged 65 and older found a 10% lower risk of hip fracture by intention to treat, which was not statistically significant (hazard ratio 0.90) [26]. Only at the highest actual intake (792 to 2,000 IU a day) was hip fracture risk lower (hazard ratio 0.70), and this analysis by intake was not a randomised comparison [26].

**Women's Health Initiative:** In 36,282 postmenopausal women taking 1,000 mg of calcium plus 400 IU of D3 a day for 7 years, hip fractures were not significantly reduced, and kidney stones were more common [27]. A review of up to 20 years of follow-up concluded that the trial does not support routine calcium plus vitamin D to prevent fractures in menopausal women, though both are appropriate for women who do not get recommended amounts from their diet [28].

**Women's Health Initiative, long-term follow-up:** After a median of 22.3 years, women assigned to calcium plus vitamin D had 7% fewer cancer deaths and 6% more cardiovascular deaths, with no effect on all-cause mortality [29].

**U-shaped relationship:** In 1,662 men aged 70 to 97, fracture risk was higher both at 36 nmol/L or less (hazard ratio 3.5) and above 72 nmol/L (hazard ratio 2.7) than at 60 to 72 nmol/L [30]. This was an observational study.

**Bone mineral density in generally healthy adults:** In a VITAL substudy of 771 adults not selected for low vitamin D, 2,000 IU a day of D3 for 2 years did not improve bone density or structure [31]. In older women with low starting levels, doses from 400 to 4,800 IU a day with calcium did not change bone density over 12 months [32].

**High-dose harm to bone:** In a 3-year Canadian trial in 311 healthy adults, forearm bone density fell by 1.2% on 400 IU a day, 2.4% on 4,000 IU and 3.5% on 10,000 IU; only 10,000 IU also lowered it at the shin [5]. Women lost more bone than men [33]. In Brad's account the participants were healthy adults, and wrist bone density fell by about 2.4% at 4,000 IU and 3.5% at 10,000 IU, compared with 1.2% at 400 IU ([Brad, 14 Jun 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/when-dosed-correctly-vitamin-d-cuts-sick-days-by-17)).

**Fracture healing:** In a small trial in postmenopausal women with a wrist fracture, a lower bolus dose did not improve healing, and a bolus equal to 1,800 IU a day may have hindered the return of bone stiffness in the first 12 weeks [113].

**Vegan women:** In a North American cohort, female vegans had a higher risk of hip fracture than non-vegetarians (hazard ratio 1.55). Among those who took both vitamin D and calcium supplements, the extra risk disappeared [34].

**Vitamin K:** In a post hoc analysis of a trial of 2,800 IU a day of vitamin D for 8 weeks, vitamin K status did not change the effect of vitamin D on bone or heart markers [35].

**USPSTF position:** The US Preventive Services Task Force found that 400 IU or less of vitamin D with 1,000 mg or less of calcium a day does not prevent fractures in postmenopausal women living in the community, and that vitamin D with calcium raises the rate of kidney stones [36].

**Synthesis:** In people with enough vitamin D, large trials show no fracture benefit, and in one trial daily doses of 4,000 IU or more lowered bone density [22][5]. Evidence for people at high risk or in residential care is limited [22].

### Muscle Strength and Falls

**Benefit in deficient older adults:** In a 9-month trial in Brazilian postmenopausal women with low vitamin D, 1,000 IU a day raised lower-limb strength on a chair-rising test by 25.3% [37].

**No benefit at moderate levels:** In low-functioning older adults with levels of 18 to under 30 ng/mL, 2,000 IU a day for a year did not improve leg power, strength or physical performance [38]. In postmenopausal women with osteopenia or osteoporosis (mean level 23.4 ng/mL), 3,200 IU a day of D3 or 20 µg a day of calcifediol did no better than placebo [39].

**High doses may harm muscles:** In women with low vitamin D and raised parathyroid hormone, 2,800 IU a day for 3 months reduced handgrip strength by 9% and knee flexion strength by 13% compared with placebo [40]. In postmenopausal women with overweight or obesity and low vitamin D on a weight-loss programme, 2,000 IU a day for 12 months decreased leg strength compared with placebo [111].

**Falls, U-shaped dose response:** In a 12-month trial in older women with low vitamin D, falls decreased on 1,600 to 3,200 IU a day but not on lower or higher doses [41]. The share who fell was 21% among women whose levels reached 32 to 38 ng/mL and 72% among those at 38 to 46 ng/mL [41]. In adults aged 70 and older with a prior fall, 60,000 IU a month led to more falls than 24,000 IU a month (66.9% vs 47.9%) [42]. In adults aged 70 and older at raised risk of falls and with low vitamin D, 1,000 IU a day or more did not prevent falls compared with 200 IU a day, and serious falls (hazard ratio 1.87) and falls needing hospital care (hazard ratio 2.48) were more common [43]. In the same trial, doses of 1,000 IU a day or more went with more fall-related fractures (hazard ratio 2.66) [44]. A single yearly dose of 500,000 IU raised falls and fractures in older women (see Safety) [6].

**USPSTF position:** The US Preventive Services Task Force found that vitamin D supplements do not prevent falls in older adults living in the community [45].

### Cancer

**Incidence:** In VITAL, 2,000 IU a day for a median of 5.3 years did not lower invasive cancer (hazard ratio 0.96) or breast, prostate or colorectal cancer [46]. A meta-analysis of 12 trials found no effect on total cancer incidence (summary relative risk 0.99) [47].

**Mortality:** NIH reports that three meta-analyses found 12% to 13% lower cancer mortality with vitamin D, mostly with daily dosing [1]. A 2022 meta-analysis found an overall result that was not statistically significant (summary relative risk 0.92), while daily dosing gave 0.87 [47]. In VITAL, cancer deaths were lower (hazard ratio 0.83) but not significantly so [46]. A secondary analysis found fewer advanced (metastatic or fatal) cancers with vitamin D (hazard ratio 0.83), strongest in people of normal weight (hazard ratio 0.62) [48]. In the Australian D-Health trial of 21,315 adults aged 60 years or older, 60,000 IU a month for 5 years did not change all-cause mortality, and an exploratory analysis found higher cancer mortality after the first 2 years (hazard ratio 1.24) [49].

**WHI long-term follow-up:** Over a median of 22.3 years, cancer deaths were lower in women assigned to 400 IU of D3 plus 1,000 mg of calcium (hazard ratio 0.93) [29].

**Colorectal cancer:** In a pooled analysis of 17 cohorts, levels below 30 nmol/L went with 31% higher colorectal cancer risk than 50 to 62.5 nmol/L, and levels of 75 to 100 nmol/L with 19% to 27% lower risk [50]. In people who had colorectal adenomas removed, 1,000 IU of D3, 1,200 mg of calcium or both did not reduce new adenomas [51]. In the 2026 SOLARIS trial of 455 people with untreated metastatic colorectal cancer, high-dose D3 (8,000 IU a day for 14 days, then 4,000 IU a day) added to chemotherapy did not delay progression compared with 400 IU a day [52].

**Pancreatic cancer:** NIH describes a pooled analysis that found an increased risk of pancreatic cancer with 25(OH)D levels of 100 nmol/L (40 ng/mL) or above [1].

**Overall mortality from all causes:** In a meta-analysis of trials, D3 lowered overall mortality (relative risk 0.89) and D2 did not (1.04) [53]. The guideline review found a very small reduction in mortality in adults over 75 [10]. Giving 540,000 IU once to critically ill, deficient patients did not lower 90-day mortality (23.5% vs 20.6% on placebo) [54]. In a trial of 5,670 people at raised heart risk, 60,000 IU a month went with more deaths than placebo (hazard ratio 1.29) [55]. In observational data, Copenhagen adults had the lowest mortality at 50 to 60 nmol/L and higher mortality at 140 nmol/L than at 50 nmol/L (hazard ratio 1.42) [56], and in UK Biobank the fall in risk levelled off at 60 nmol/L [57]. A 2020 meta-analysis covering 21 trials and more than 83,000 participants found no mortality benefit ([Brad, 14 Jun 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/when-dosed-correctly-vitamin-d-cuts-sick-days-by-17)).

**Cancer treatment adjunct:** In a post hoc subgroup of a Japanese trial in digestive tract cancer, people whose tumours were p53 immunoreactive had higher 5-year relapse-free survival on 2,000 IU a day (hazard ratio 0.27) [58]. A post hoc subgroup finding like this needs confirming in its own trial.

**Synthesis:** In people with enough vitamin D, trials have not shown that supplements prevent cancer. Meta-analyses suggest they may lower cancer deaths with daily dosing [47], while monthly doses in D-Health leaned the other way [49].

### Cardiovascular Disease

**Observational data:** A meta-analysis of 34 observational studies with 180,667 people linked higher 25(OH)D with lower risk of cardiovascular events [1].

**VITAL trial:** 2,000 IU a day of D3 for a median of 5.3 years did not reduce major cardiovascular events (hazard ratio 0.97) [46].

**Other trials:** A New Zealand trial in 5,110 adults found that 100,000 IU a month for a median of 3.3 years had no effect on heart attack, heart failure, stroke or death from cardiovascular disease [1]. In 400 people with heart failure, 4,000 IU a day for 3 years did not reduce deaths (hazard ratio 1.09), and more needed a mechanical circulatory support implant (15.4% vs 9.0%) [59]. In long-term WHI follow-up, cardiovascular deaths were 6% higher in women assigned to calcium plus vitamin D (hazard ratio 1.06) [29].

**Blood pressure:** A meta-analysis of 46 trials with 4,541 people found no effect on blood pressure; the authors concluded vitamin D should not be used to treat high blood pressure [60].

**TARGET-D trial:** In 630 people after a heart attack, doses were adjusted to keep 25(OH)D within a target of above 40 to 80 ng/mL (52.4% started at 5,000 IU a day). Over an average of 4.2 years, major cardiovascular events were not significantly reduced [61]. Repeat heart attacks were fewer (3.8% vs 7.9%; hazard ratio 0.48), while heart failure admissions (hazard ratio 1.21) and strokes (hazard ratio 1.69) leaned higher without reaching significance [61]. The trial was not blinded, and its main combined outcome of death, heart attack, heart failure hospitalization or stroke did not differ significantly between groups ([Brad, 3 Dec 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-vitamin-d-study-forced-me-to-change-my-view)).

**Synthesis:** Despite observational links between low vitamin D and heart disease, trials do not support supplements to prevent it. Brad reads TARGET-D as a hint of heart benefit from treat-to-target dosing in some groups that needs better-designed follow-up studies ([Brad, 3 Dec 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-vitamin-d-study-forced-me-to-change-my-view)).

### Diabetes and Insulin Resistance

**Prediabetes with vitamin D deficiency:** In the D2d trial of 2,423 people with prediabetes, not selected for low vitamin D, 4,000 IU a day for about 2.5 years did not significantly reduce diabetes (hazard ratio 0.88) [62]. A post hoc analysis found 62% fewer new cases in people who started below 30 nmol/L (12 ng/mL) [1]. Among people who followed the protocol, vitamin D lowered diabetes risk (hazard ratio 0.84) and made a return to normal blood sugar more likely [63]. A 2026 genetic analysis found a 19% lower risk (hazard ratio 0.81) in people with ApaI AC or CC variants of the vitamin D receptor gene, and no response in the 618 people with the AA variant [64]. These are secondary analyses.

**Established type 2 diabetes:** In people with well-controlled type 2 diabetes not selected for low vitamin D, 4,000 IU a day for 48 weeks changed neither insulin secretion nor HbA1c [65]. A meta-analysis of 35 trials with 43,407 adults found no significant effect on blood sugar control [1].

**VITAL trial (diabetes substudy):** 2,000 IU a day for a median of 5.3 years did not reduce type 2 diabetes (hazard ratio 0.91); pooled with three earlier trials, the hazard ratio was 0.89 [66].

**Synthesis:** Vitamin D does not clearly prevent type 2 diabetes in people with prediabetes, though secondary analyses hint at benefit in people who are very low or who take it as directed. It does not improve blood sugar control in established diabetes.

### Depression and Mood

**Observational data:** A meta-analysis of 14 observational studies with 31,424 adults linked low 25(OH)D with depression [1].

**Clinical trials, mostly negative:** A meta-analysis of nine trials with 4,923 adults found no significant reduction in depressive symptoms [1]. In VITAL, 2,000 IU a day for a median of 5.3 years did not change depression or mood scores in 18,353 adults (hazard ratio 0.97) [67]. In the 2026 DepFuD trial of 281 adults with a major depressive episode, 4,000 IU a day added to treatment for 6 months did no better than 400 IU [68]. In D-Health, monthly doses did not change depression scores, and antidepressant use rose in people whose predicted level was 50 nmol/L or more (hazard ratio 1.10) [69].

Brad notes a meta-analysis in which supplements improved symptoms in people with clinical signs of depression, but not mood in people without clinically significant symptoms ([Brad, 18 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-study-proved-we-were-wrong-about-vitamin-d)).

**Synthesis:** Vitamin D does not prevent depression in the general population, and higher doses added to treatment have not helped.

### Cognitive Function and Dementia

**Dementia risk:** In older US adults, levels below 25 nmol/L went with a higher risk of dementia (hazard ratio 2.25) and Alzheimer's disease (hazard ratio 2.22) over 5.6 years than levels of 50 nmol/L or more [70]. This was an observational study.

**Cognitive improvement in MCI:** In a 12-month Chinese trial in 183 older adults with mild cognitive impairment, 800 IU a day improved cognitive scores [71].

**No cognitive benefit in other trials:** A 2-year trial in older adults with mild to moderate deficiency found no improvement in cognition [72]. In D-Health, 60,000 IU a month for 5 years neither helped nor harmed cognition [73].

**Long-term use and dementia:** In Taiwanese insurance records, older adults without dementia who used prescription calcitriol, the active hormone form of vitamin D, at a high cumulative dose (more than 146 capsules of 0.25 mcg a year) were 1.8 times as likely to develop dementia as non-users [74]. The abstract calls this vitamin D3 supplements, but the methods describe calcitriol prescriptions, so the finding cannot be applied to ordinary D3 supplements [74]. This is an observational finding, and the authors caution against prolonged vitamin D use in people with Alzheimer's disease [74].

**Synthesis:** Low vitamin D is linked with dementia in observational studies, but most trials of supplements have not improved cognition.

### Respiratory Infections and COVID-19

**Respiratory infections:** A meta-analysis of individual data from 25 trials found that vitamin D lowered the risk of acute respiratory infection (adjusted odds ratio 0.88) [75]. The benefit was seen with daily or weekly dosing (0.81) and was largest in people below 25 nmol/L taking it daily or weekly (0.30) [75]. Large monthly doses did not reduce infections in New Zealand adults (100,000 IU) [76] or in D-Health (60,000 IU) [77]. In Danish children aged 6 to 8, 20 µg (800 IU) a day of D3 over winter gave 17% fewer sick days from respiratory infection and 43% fewer days with fever than placebo [78].

**COVID-19 severity:** In people in hospital with moderate to severe COVID-19, a single 200,000 IU dose did not shorten hospital stay [79]. In a UK observational study of 472 patients in hospital with COVID-19, the odds of death within 28 days were higher both below 25 nmol/L and at 100 nmol/L or more, compared with 50 to 74 nmol/L [112].

### Autoimmune Disease

**Autoimmune disease overall:** In VITAL, 2,000 IU a day for a median of 5.3 years reduced confirmed autoimmune disease by 22% (hazard ratio 0.78, P=0.05) [80].

**Multiple sclerosis:** In observational studies, low 25(OH)D goes with higher MS risk; Finnish women below 30 nmol/L had a 43% higher risk than those at 50 nmol/L or more [1]. A Cochrane review of 12 trials with 933 people found no effect on relapses or disability, based on very low-quality evidence [81]. In 204 people with clinically isolated syndrome, 1,000 to 10,000 IU a day did not delay conversion to MS [82].

### Asthma and COPD

**Asthma:** An analysis of individual data from 7 trials (955 people) found fewer asthma attacks needing steroid tablets (adjusted rate ratio 0.74) [83]. The protection was seen in people starting below 25 nmol/L, but the authors did not show that the effect differed by starting level [83].

**COPD:** In a UK trial of 240 people with COPD, vitamin D3 did not reduce moderate or severe flare-ups overall, but protected people who started below 50 nmol/L (hazard ratio 0.57) [84].

### Vertigo (BPPV)

In people with benign paroxysmal positional vertigo and levels below 20 ng/mL, vitamin D with calcium reduced repeat attacks (incidence rate ratio 0.76) compared with observation [85]. In a 2026 placebo-controlled trial in 160 adults with low vitamin D, 10,000 IU a week for 24 months halved recurrences (27.5% vs 55.0%) [86].

### Pregnancy

The Endocrine Society suggests vitamin D supplements in pregnancy [9]. The RDA is 600 IU a day and the upper limit is 4,000 IU a day [1]. In an Indian trial, dosing by starting level lowered the combined rate of preterm labour, pre-eclampsia and gestational diabetes (20.3% vs 44%) [87]. In a Danish trial, 2,400 IU a day from week 24 of pregnancy went with fewer enamel defects in the children's permanent teeth (odds ratio 0.47) [88].

### Weight and Body Composition

In women on a diet and exercise programme, 2,000 IU a day for a year did not add to weight loss compared with placebo [89].

### Statin Interactions and Benefits

In VITAL, 2,000 IU a day did not prevent muscle symptoms in new statin users (31% vs 31%; odds ratio 0.97), even in people below 20 ng/mL [90]. High vitamin D intakes might reduce the potency of some statins (see Drug Interactions) [1].

### Skin Conditions

Only small trials have tested vitamin D in eczema, psoriasis, acne and chronic hives, with mixed results.

### NAFLD (Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease)

Only small studies have tested vitamin D in fatty liver disease, and none settles whether it helps.

### Allergies

Only small or observational studies have looked at vitamin D for allergies, and none settles whether it helps.

### Erectile Dysfunction

Trials have not shown that vitamin D helps erectile dysfunction.

### Crohn's Disease and IBS

Only small trials have tested vitamin D in Crohn's disease and irritable bowel syndrome, with mixed results.

### Fibromyalgia

Two small trials of vitamin D in fibromyalgia gave conflicting results.

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Recommended Dietary Allowances (RDAs)

From the Food and Nutrition Board at the National Academies [1]:

| Age Group | RDA |
|---|---|
| 0-12 months | 10 mcg (400 IU)* |
| 1-70 years | 15 mcg (600 IU) |
| > 70 years | 20 mcg (800 IU) |
| Pregnancy/Lactation | 15 mcg (600 IU) |

*Adequate Intake (AI), not RDA

The RDAs assume minimal sun exposure [1]. The Endocrine Society (2024) suggests vitamin D supplements for children and teens (1 to 18 years), pregnant women, people with high-risk prediabetes and adults aged 75 and older, and suggests against doses above the dietary reference intake for healthy adults younger than 75 years [9].

### Tolerable Upper Intake Levels (ULs)

| Age | UL |
|---|---|
| 0-6 months | 25 mcg (1,000 IU) |
| 7-12 months | 38 mcg (1,500 IU) |
| 1-3 years | 63 mcg (2,500 IU) |
| 4-8 years | 75 mcg (3,000 IU) |
| 9+ years, including pregnancy | 100 mcg (4,000 IU) |

While signs of toxicity are unlikely below 10,000 IU a day, the FNB noted that even intakes below the UL might have adverse effects over time, and advised avoiding 25(OH)D levels above about 50 to 60 ng/mL [1].

### Practical Dosing by Indication

Brad follows the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline ([Brad, 16 Oct 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/new-vitamin-d-study-on-telomeres-and-aging)). It names four groups for supplements and says the best doses for them remain unclear [9].

**Healthy adults (19 to 74):** Follow the recommended dietary allowance: 600 IU a day up to age 70 and 800 IU from 71 ([Brad, 7 Aug 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/nasa-found-how-to-stop-osteoporosis)). Taking vitamin D with a meal that contains fat may improve absorption [15].

**No routine testing, no target level:** The guideline acknowledges we don't know what optimal levels of vitamin D are, so it abandoned its earlier thresholds for insufficiency and deficiency and advises against routine screening ([Brad, 18 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-study-proved-we-were-wrong-about-vitamin-d)). A test makes sense when risk factors point toward checking ([Brad, 16 Oct 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/new-vitamin-d-study-on-telomeres-and-aging)). Brad does not order vitamin D tests for otherwise healthy patients; since the TARGET-D trial he offers testing after a heart attack as an option, not a must (see Cardiovascular Disease) ([Brad, 3 Dec 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-vitamin-d-study-forced-me-to-change-my-view)).

**Children and teens (1 to 18 years):** The guideline suggests vitamin D for this age group [9]. Brad notes it may help prevent rickets and respiratory infections ([Brad, 18 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-study-proved-we-were-wrong-about-vitamin-d)). In Brad's summary of the guideline, children up to 18 should get about 1,500 IU a day ([Brad, 18 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-study-proved-we-were-wrong-about-vitamin-d)).

**Pregnancy:** The guideline suggests vitamin D in pregnancy [9]. The RDA is 600 IU a day and the upper limit 4,000 IU a day [1]. In Brad's summary of the guideline, the benefits came with doses around 3,000 IU a day ([Brad, 18 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-study-proved-we-were-wrong-about-vitamin-d)); the guideline itself says the best dose remains unclear [9]. Talk to your doctor before taking more than the RDA in pregnancy.

**Prediabetes:** The guideline suggests vitamin D for people with high-risk prediabetes [9]. In the main trial, 4,000 IU a day did not significantly reduce diabetes [62]. In Brad's summary, the trials averaged about 3,500 IU a day ([Brad, 18 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-study-proved-we-were-wrong-about-vitamin-d)); the guideline says the optimal dose remains unclear [9].

**Older adults (75+):** The guideline suggests vitamin D after age 75 [9]. The RDA is 800 IU a day over 70 [1]. Its review found a very small reduction in mortality in this age group [10]. The calcium RDA for adults ranges from 1,000 to 1,200 mg, depending on age [114].

**People at high risk of deficiency:** If someone is older, housebound, or clearly at high risk of deficiency, Brad recommends a moderate daily dose rather than a blood test first ([Brad, 3 Dec 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-vitamin-d-study-forced-me-to-change-my-view)). Where a doctor has diagnosed a deficiency, the doctor sets the dose.

**Breastfed infants:** The American Academy of Pediatrics advises 400 IU a day for breastfed infants, starting shortly after birth [1].

**Avoid bolus dosing:** For nonpregnant people older than 50 years who need vitamin D, the guideline suggests daily doses rather than large intermittent ones [9]. A yearly 500,000 IU dose increased falls and fractures in older women compared with placebo [6]. In older adults in sheltered housing and their carers, adding a large dose every 2 months to a low daily dose did not prevent respiratory infections overall but raised the risk of upper respiratory infections (hazard ratio 1.48) [115]. Monthly 60,000 IU was linked to higher cancer mortality in an exploratory analysis of D-Health [49]. In athletes, large weekly doses raised an inactive breakdown product, and the authors favour lower doses taken more often [91].

### How to Read a Supplement Label

Labels give vitamin D in International Units (IU) or micrograms (mcg). The conversion: **1 mcg = 40 IU**, so 1,000 IU = 25 mcg. The FDA Daily Value (DV) for vitamin D is 20 mcg (800 IU) for adults and children aged 4+. Products listing 100% DV contain 800 IU [1]. Choose a product that an independent lab has tested.

### Target Blood Levels

There is no target level to dose toward: the 2024 Endocrine Society guideline found no clear optimal level and advises against routine testing [9] ([Brad, 16 Oct 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/new-vitamin-d-study-on-telomeres-and-aging)). The FNB considers levels of 20 ng/mL or more sufficient for most people [1]. It advised avoiding levels above about 50 to 60 ng/mL, and found that even levels of about 30 to 48 ng/mL are associated with higher all-cause mortality, cancer at some sites, cardiovascular events, and falls and fractures in older adults [1]. A 2026 narrative review proposed a target range of 20 to 30 ng/mL; that is an opinion, not a trial result [92].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Signs of too much vitamin D

For adults the tolerable upper intake level is 4,000 IU a day [1]. Too much vitamin D raises blood calcium, which can cause [1]:
- Nausea, vomiting and loss of appetite
- Constipation [104]
- Muscle weakness
- Headache and a metallic taste [105]
- Confusion and other changes in mood or thinking
- Feeling tired [104]
- Pain
- Dehydration, passing a lot of urine and excessive thirst
- Kidney stones

### Toxicity (Hypercalcemia)

Vitamin D toxicity almost always results from excessive supplement intake; experts do not believe sun exposure causes it [1]. Toxicity causes hypercalcemia (serum calcium > 11.1 mg/dL) with serum 25(OH)D typically above 150 ng/mL [1]. Symptoms range from nausea and weakness to dehydration and kidney stones, and in extreme cases kidney failure, cardiac arrhythmias, calcification of soft tissues, and death [1]. Cases have come from supplements with manufacturing errors, supplements taken wrongly or in excess, and incorrect prescriptions [1].

### Harm signals from high doses or high levels

Even below classic toxicity thresholds, some studies have linked higher vitamin D levels or doses with harm:

- **Bone density loss:** 4,000 and 10,000 IU a day for 3 years lowered forearm bone density compared with 400 IU a day [5]
- **Falls:** In one trial, women whose levels reached 38 to 46 ng/mL fell most often [41]. In another, higher daily doses did not prevent falls compared with a low dose, and serious falls were more common [43]
- **All-cause mortality:** In Copenhagen, mortality was higher at 140 nmol/L than at 50 nmol/L (hazard ratio 1.42) [56]; in a multinational trial, 60,000 IU a month went with more deaths (hazard ratio 1.29) [55]
- **Cancer-related mortality:** In an exploratory analysis of D-Health, monthly 60,000 IU went with higher cancer mortality after the first 2 years (hazard ratio 1.24), though the main cancer mortality result was not statistically significant [49]
- **Sleep:** In a small trial in people with multiple sclerosis, high-dose D3 lowered nighttime melatonin [110]. In postmenopausal women losing weight, vitamin D did not change overall sleep quality compared with placebo, but women who reached 32 ng/mL or more reported worse sleep [93]

### Harm from Single Large Doses

A single yearly 500,000 IU dose in women aged 70 and over raised falls (rate ratio 1.15) and fractures (rate ratio 1.26) compared with placebo [6]. In elite athletes, large weekly doses (70,000 IU) raised 24,25(OH)2D, an inactive breakdown product, and it stayed raised after stopping; the authors favour lower, more frequent doses and a gradual stop [91].

### Kidney Stones

The combination of high calcium intake (about 2,100 mg a day) with moderate vitamin D (about 765 IU a day) raised kidney stone risk by 17% over 7 years in the WHI trial. Shorter trials of vitamin D alone or with calcium found more hypercalcemia and hypercalciuria but not more kidney stones [1]. In a 1-year trial, 10,000 IU a day with 1,200 mg of calcium raised the odds of high urine calcium 3.6 times compared with 600 IU [94].

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy:** The RDA in pregnancy is 600 IU a day and the upper limit is 4,000 IU a day [1]. Higher doses should be discussed with a healthcare provider.

**Infants:** The American Academy of Pediatrics advises 400 IU a day for breastfed infants [1], and 1,000 IU a day is the upper limit up to 6 months [1]. In a trial in breastfed infants, 1,600 IU a day was stopped early because levels rose too high [95]. In 2010 the FDA warned that some liquid vitamin D droppers hold more than an infant should get: use only the dropper that comes with the product, give no more than 400 IU a day, and if you cannot tell the dose the dropper gives, ask a health professional first [116].

**Obesity:** Body fat stores more vitamin D, so people with obesity might need higher doses to reach the same blood levels [1].

**Kidney disease:** The kidneys make the active form of vitamin D, and people whose kidneys cannot do this can become deficient [1]. People with kidney disease may need a different form or dose, so they should take vitamin D only as their doctor advises.

**Sarcoidosis and other granulomatous diseases:** Granulomas can make active vitamin D outside the kidney. In two small studies of supplements in sarcoidosis, average blood calcium did not change, but a few patients developed high urine calcium [3]. Take vitamin D only as your doctor advises.

**Primary hyperparathyroidism:** In a meta-analysis of 11 studies (388 patients with primary hyperparathyroidism and low vitamin D), vitamin D lowered parathyroid hormone without causing high blood or urine calcium [96]. Thiazides with vitamin D might still lead to high blood calcium in people with hyperparathyroidism [1], so supplements belong under a doctor's care.

**Rare CYP24A1 gene faults:** Faults in CYP24A1, the gene for the enzyme that breaks down active vitamin D, cause severe high blood calcium in some infants given vitamin D, and make people unusually sensitive to it [4]. People with this condition should take vitamin D only as their doctor advises.

**Bariatric surgery:** Expert groups have guidelines on vitamin D screening, monitoring and replacement before and after bariatric surgery [1].

**Black Americans:** In a US study, total 25(OH)D was 15.6 ng/mL in Black and 25.8 ng/mL in White participants, yet bone density was higher in the Black group (1.05 vs 0.94 g per square centimetre), and bioavailable vitamin D was similar in people with the same gene type [97]. Standard total 25(OH)D measurements may overestimate deficiency in this population.

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Vitamin D Affects Drug Metabolism

| Drug | Interaction | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Thiazide diuretics | Thiazides lower urinary calcium excretion and vitamin D raises calcium absorption, so together they might lead to hypercalcemia, especially among older adults and individuals with compromised renal function or hyperparathyroidism [1] | Ask your doctor whether your calcium should be checked |
| Digoxin | Too much vitamin D raises blood calcium [1]; high calcium can add to digoxin's effects on heart rhythm [2] | If you take digoxin, check with your doctor before taking more than the RDA |
| Statins | High intakes of vitamin D, especially from supplements, might reduce the potency of atorvastatin, lovastatin, and simvastatin, which appear to compete with vitamin D for the same metabolizing enzyme [1] | This is one more reason to avoid high-dose vitamin D. If you take a statin, check with your doctor before taking more than the RDA |

### Drugs That Affect Vitamin D Levels

| Drug Class | Effect on Vitamin D | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Orlistat (weight loss) | Reduces vitamin D absorption from food and supplements [1] | Ask your doctor whether you need vitamin D and how to time it around orlistat |
| Statins | Effects differ by statin. Rosuvastatin raised vitamin D levels in one trial [106]; atorvastatin lowered them in another [107]. Statins may reduce the body's own vitamin D synthesis [1] | Statin users had a 21.4% smaller rise in vitamin D levels than non-users [108]. See the statin row in the table above |
| Corticosteroids (prednisone) | Reduce calcium absorption and impair vitamin D metabolism. Deficiency was more than twice as common in oral steroid users (11%) vs. nonusers (5%) [1] | If you take steroids long term, ask your doctor whether you need vitamin D testing or supplements |
| Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) | In one small study, all 30 long-term pantoprazole users had low vitamin D, against 30% of non-users [98] | If you take a PPI long term, ask your doctor whether vitamin D testing makes sense |
| Anticonvulsants and rifampicin | Long-term phenobarbital or rifampicin can cause vitamin D deficiency or osteomalacia, likely through faster breakdown of vitamin D [99]; carbamazepine users had lower vitamin D levels than non-users (21.8 vs 28.0 ng/mL) [100] | If you take one of these long term, ask your doctor whether you need vitamin D testing or supplements |
| Combined oral contraceptives | Some formulations may raise vitamin D binding protein and lower bioavailable vitamin D, while total levels change little [109] | Effects differ by formulation; levonorgestrel alone made no significant difference [109] |

### Practical Notes on Food Sources

- **Fat may help absorption:** Fat in the gut enhances vitamin D absorption, though some is absorbed without it [1]
- **Animal foods contain calcifediol:** The 25(OH)D form in animal foods is about 5 times as potent as the parent vitamin, so the total vitamin D in beef, pork, chicken, turkey and eggs may be 2 to 18 times higher than the parent vitamin alone [1]
- **Fortification:** Almost all US milk is voluntarily fortified with about 120 IU a cup [1]. Ready-to-eat breakfast cereals often contain added vitamin D [1]

### Sun Exposure

Some expert bodies suggest that about 5 to 30 minutes of sun between 10 a.m. and 4 p.m., daily or at least twice a week, on the face, arms, hands and legs without sunscreen usually makes enough vitamin D [1]. Season, time of day, cloud, smog, skin colour, age and sunscreen all change how much the skin makes, and UVB does not pass through glass [1]. UV light also causes skin cancer, so health agencies advise sun protection [1].

Experts do not believe that excess sun exposure causes vitamin D toxicity, because heat in the skin turns previtamin D3 into inactive forms [1]. However, frequent tanning bed use can raise levels very high [1]. In a large US survey, sunscreen, shade and long sleeves were not linked to lower bone density [101].

### Magnesium and Vitamin D

In a trial, magnesium supplements raised 25(OH)D3 in people who started near 30 ng/mL and lowered it in people who started between about 30 and 50 ng/mL; the authors suggest adequate magnesium may help keep vitamin D status in a good range [102].

### Exercise and Vitamin D Levels

In a randomised crossover study in healthy adults, a single 60-minute bout of moderate exercise briefly raised 25(OH)D and the active 1,25(OH)2D3 compared with rest [103].
