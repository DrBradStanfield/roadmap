## 1. Overview

Garcinia cambogia (also known as Garcinia gummi-gutta, Malabar tamarind, or brindle berry) is a tree native to India and Southeast Asia, commonly found in tropical evergreen forests [1][2]. The fruit has a long history in traditional medicine — used as a tea for inflammation and stomach complaints — and its rind has been used as a food preservative and culinary ingredient throughout the region [2].

The compound of primary interest in Garcinia supplements is hydroxycitric acid (HCA), which occurs naturally in the dried fruit rind at concentrations up to 30% by weight [1]. Commercial Garcinia extracts are typically standardized to contain 50-60% HCA [1]. HCA has been studied primarily for its potential effects on appetite suppression and body weight, based on a proposed biochemical mechanism involving inhibition of ATP-citrate lyase, a cellular enzyme involved in fatty acid synthesis [1][3].

Interest in Garcinia cambogia as a weight loss supplement surged after it was popularized on television in the early 2010s, and it remains one of the most commonly purchased weight loss supplements worldwide. However, the clinical evidence for its efficacy is mixed at best, and serious safety concerns — particularly liver toxicity — have emerged from case reports and regulatory investigations [1][2][4][5].

Laboratory studies in animals have demonstrated that HCA can suppress food intake and decrease body weight gain [1]. However, translating these findings to humans has proven inconsistent. Several systematic reviews and meta-analyses have concluded that any weight loss effect of Garcinia cambogia is modest and clinically uncertain [2][4][5]. The U.S. National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) states plainly: "It's unclear whether garcinia cambogia products help with weight loss" [2].

Adding to the complexity, the French Agency for Food, Environmental and Occupational Health & Safety (ANSES) has advised against the consumption of any products containing Garcinia, citing the potential for liver injury and other adverse effects [1][6]. This regulatory stance reflects accumulating case reports of hepatotoxicity associated with Garcinia-containing supplements [1][2][7][8][9].

This article reviews all available clinical evidence for Garcinia cambogia, including weight loss trials, kidney stone research, safety data, drug interactions, and dosing considerations.

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### HCA Chemistry and Stability

Hydroxycitric acid (HCA) in its free molecular form is chemically unstable [1]. To address this, ingredient manufacturers commonly bind HCA to minerals — most often calcium, potassium, or magnesium — to create stable salt forms suitable for supplement encapsulation [1].

Two branded versions of Garcinia extract dominate the clinical literature:

- **Citrimax** — standardized to 50% HCA, with HCA bound to calcium and potassium [1]
- **SuperCitrimax** (also referred to in studies as "Citrimax HCA 600 SGX") — standardized to 60% HCA, also with HCA bound to calcium and potassium [1]

Both are manufactured by InterHealth (now Lonza). The company claims that calcium/potassium-bound HCA is more soluble and better absorbed than unbound HCA preparations [1].

### Absorption Characteristics

Despite the claims of enhanced absorption, research indicates that even calcium/potassium-bound HCA is only partially absorbed:

- **Time to peak blood levels:** A study of Citrimax taken as a liquid showed HCA reaching maximum blood concentrations 60 to 90 minutes after ingestion, falling rapidly thereafter [1][10]. A separate study of SuperCitrimax showed peak levels at 2 hours post-ingestion [1][11].
- **Partial absorption:** Research by Downs et al. (2005) confirmed that even in the calcium/potassium-bound form, HCA absorption is incomplete [1][12]. Earlier work by Loe et al. (2001) reached similar conclusions [1][11].
- **Food effect:** Taking Garcinia with a full meal results in significantly lower blood levels of HCA compared to taking it on an empty stomach, underscoring the importance of pre-meal dosing [1][12].
- **Fiber interference:** It has been suggested that high-fiber diets may impair HCA absorption, though this remains speculative [1][13].

### Mineral Content of Garcinia Supplements

Because HCA is typically bound to calcium and potassium salts, Garcinia supplements at typical daily doses can deliver meaningful amounts of these minerals. For example, a daily dose of 4,500 mg of SuperCitrimax would be expected to provide approximately 720 mg of potassium and 495 mg of calcium [1]. This is a clinically relevant amount — 720 mg potassium represents about 15% of the adequate intake, and 495 mg calcium is roughly 38-50% of the RDA depending on age and sex. Consumers taking other mineral supplements or medications affected by calcium/potassium intake should be aware of this contribution.

### Extract Standardization

Garcinia cambogia extracts are typically standardized to contain 50-60% HCA [1]. The remainder of the extract (40-50% of total weight) is composed of carbohydrates and fat from the fruit rind, though these contribute negligible calories — approximately 3-4 calories per capsule [1].

### Comparison Table: Garcinia Extract Forms

| Form | HCA Content | Mineral Binding | Peak Absorption | Notes |
|------|:-:|:-:|:-:|------|
| Citrimax | 50% | Calcium + Potassium | 60-90 min (liquid) | Widely used in clinical trials [1][10] |
| SuperCitrimax (HCA 600 SGX) | 60% | Calcium + Potassium | ~2 hours | Higher HCA concentration; used in several clinical trials [1][11] |
| Unbranded extracts | 50-60% | Varies (often calcium) | Unknown | Quality and standardization vary; no consistent clinical data [1] |
| Free HCA | Variable | None | Unstable | Not used in supplements due to chemical instability [1] |

### Multi-Ingredient Products: A Caution

Garcinia is frequently included as an ingredient in weight loss "formulas" or "blends" containing multiple ingredients without listing the amount of each [1]. These products should be avoided for several reasons: they are unlikely to have been clinically tested as a formula; single-ingredient supplements are preferable for determining whether a specific ingredient works for a given individual; and multi-ingredient weight loss products are more likely than single-ingredient products to contain dangerous, undeclared drug-like substances or to have other quality problems [1].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Weight Loss

The evidence that Garcinia cambogia aids weight loss is mixed and overall disappointing. While laboratory and animal studies have consistently shown appetite suppression and reduced body weight gain with HCA administration, human clinical trials have produced conflicting results [1][2].

#### Early Clinical Trials

**Heymsfield et al. (1998) — The first major controlled trial (negative)**

The first well-controlled clinical trial of Garcinia cambogia for weight loss was published in JAMA and found no benefit [13]. This landmark study involved 135 overweight men and women (many of whom were markedly obese) randomized to receive either G. cambogia extract (standardized to 50% HCA) or placebo. Participants took 1,000 mg of extract 30 minutes before each meal — a total daily dose of 3,000 mg of extract containing 1,500 mg of HCA. Over the 12-week study period, all participants followed a high-fiber, low-calorie diet.

Results: The Garcinia group did NOT lose significantly more weight or fat mass than the placebo group. It was subsequently suggested that the high-fiber diet may have impaired HCA absorption, though this explanation remains speculative [1][13][14].

**Mattes et al. (2000) — Positive in women, not men**

A 12-week study in overweight women found that those given G. cambogia extract lost more weight than those on placebo: 8.2 lbs versus 5.3 lbs (a difference of 2.9 lbs over 12 weeks) [1][15]. Importantly, there was no decrease in appetite despite the weight difference. Participants followed a low-calorie diet with exercise encouragement.

- **Dosing:** 800 mg of G. cambogia extract (50% HCA) 30-60 minutes before each meal, for a daily total of 2,400 mg extract containing 1,200 mg HCA.
- **Sex difference:** A small number of men were initially included in the study but showed no significant difference from placebo. Their results were excluded from the overall findings, meaning the positive result applies only to women [1][15].
- **Magnitude of effect:** The 2.9 lb difference over 12 weeks, while statistically significant, is a modest clinical effect — roughly 0.24 lbs per week more than diet and exercise alone.

**Kovacs et al. (2001) — No effect in men**

A smaller 2-week study in men found no effect on appetite or weight loss when participants took 500 mg of HCA three times daily (1,500 mg/day HCA) [1][16]. This study adds to evidence suggesting HCA may be less effective — or entirely ineffective — in men.

#### Later Clinical Trials

**Westerterp-Plantenga et al. (2002) — Appetite effect without weight loss**

This 2-week study found that G. cambogia extract reduced appetite and food intake but did NOT produce a significant reduction in body weight or BMI [1][10]. The dosing protocol was notable:

- Participants took G. cambogia extract (60% HCA) at three specific times: 1 hour before lunch, 1 hour before dinner, and 2 hours after dinner (to prevent evening snacking).
- Each dose was 500 mg of extract for a daily total of 1,500 mg extract containing 900 mg HCA.
- The appetite reduction was statistically significant, but the study was too short (2 weeks) to demonstrate meaningful weight loss [1][10].

**Vasques et al. (2014) — Triglyceride reduction without weight loss**

A study of obese women examined 2 months of Garcinia supplementation with a reduced-calorie diet [1][17]:

- **Dosing:** 800 mg G. cambogia extract (50% HCA) 30 minutes before breakfast, lunch, and dinner — a total daily dose of 2,400 mg extract containing 1,200 mg HCA.
- **Triglycerides:** The Garcinia group showed a significant reduction in triglyceride levels compared to placebo (average reduction of ~23 mg/dL vs. an increase of 4.5 mg/dL in the placebo group) [1][17].
- **No weight effect:** There was no significant reduction in body weight or BMI [1][17].
- **No lipid panel improvement:** No significant effects on total cholesterol, HDL, LDL, or insulin levels [1][17].
- **No hormonal effect:** No effect on leptin levels (a hormone that regulates fat storage) [1][17].

#### Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses

**Golzarand et al. (2020) — Dose-response meta-analysis**

A systematic review and dose-response meta-analysis evaluated the effect of Garcinia cambogia supplements on obesity indices [4]. The analysis found that any weight loss effect was modest and that more research was needed to confirm the findings.

**Maunder et al. (2020) — Herbal medicines for weight loss**

A broader systematic review of herbal medicines for weight loss, including Garcinia cambogia, found that the evidence for effectiveness was limited and inconsistent [5]. The review highlighted the poor quality of many existing trials and the heterogeneity of study designs.

#### Summary of Weight Loss Evidence

| Study | Year | Duration | Population | Daily HCA Dose | Key Finding |
|-------|:---:|:---:|------|:---:|------|
| Heymsfield et al. | 1998 | 12 weeks | 135 overweight M+F | 1,500 mg | No benefit over placebo [13] |
| Mattes et al. | 2000 | 12 weeks | Overweight women | 1,200 mg | 2.9 lbs more than placebo (women only) [15] |
| Kovacs et al. | 2001 | 2 weeks | Men | 1,500 mg | No effect on appetite or weight [16] |
| Westerterp-Plantenga et al. | 2002 | 2 weeks | Mixed | 900 mg | Reduced appetite, no weight change [10] |
| Vasques et al. | 2014 | 8 weeks | Obese women | 1,200 mg | Reduced triglycerides, no weight change [17] |

#### Interpretation

The evidence suggests that HCA may, in some situations, modestly help with weight loss, but it is far from reliable [1]. Several patterns emerge from the clinical data:

1. **HCA may be more effective in women than men** — the only positive weight loss trial excluded men's data, and a dedicated men's trial found no effect [1][15][16].
2. **Effects on appetite vs. body weight diverge** — some studies show reduced appetite or food intake without corresponding weight loss, suggesting that appetite suppression alone may be insufficient for clinically meaningful weight management [1][10].
3. **The magnitude of any benefit is small** — even in the most positive trial, the additional weight loss was approximately 2.9 lbs over 12 weeks beyond diet and exercise [1][15].
4. **People who are markedly obese may not benefit** — the largest negative trial (Heymsfield) included many markedly obese participants [1][13].

### Kidney Stones

Laboratory and animal studies have suggested that HCA may prevent the formation of, and help break down, calcium oxalate kidney stones — the most common type of kidney stone [1][18][19][20].

**Mechanism:** HCA appears to interfere with calcium oxalate crystallization through a direct molecular interaction. A study published in Nature demonstrated that HCA binds to calcium oxalate crystal surfaces and inhibits crystal growth in vitro [18].

**Preclinical evidence:**

- **Chung et al. (2016)** — Published in *Nature*, this study demonstrated that HCA effectively inhibited calcium oxalate crystal growth in laboratory settings [18].
- **Fan et al. (2020)** — A study in the *European Review of Medical and Pharmacological Sciences* showed HCA reduced calcium oxalate formation in animal models [19].
- **Yang et al. (2022)** — Published in *Urolithiasis*, this study provided additional evidence for HCA's effects on calcium oxalate stone prevention in preclinical models [20].

**Human evidence:**

The only human study available is a very small clinical trial that produced negative results:

- **Adiga et al. (2019)** — Presented as a poster at Kidney Week 2019, this study examined the effect of 300 mg HCA taken three times daily for one week in both kidney stone formers and non-stone formers. The results were disappointing: HCA supplementation did NOT affect 24-hour urinary excretion of calcium or oxalate, nor did it reduce the supersaturation of calcium oxalate (a key risk factor for stone formation) [1][21].

**Assessment:** While the preclinical data on HCA and kidney stones is intriguing, the mechanism has not been validated in human subjects. The single human study was very small, very short (1 week), and entirely negative. There is currently no evidence that Garcinia cambogia supplements prevent or treat kidney stones in humans [1][21].

### Serotonin Modulation

Research in rats has demonstrated that Garcinia extract (SuperCitrimax) is capable of inhibiting the reuptake of serotonin in brain cells [1]. A study in human subjects showed a 39.8% increase in serum serotonin concentrations after 8 weeks of Garcinia supplementation [1]. This serotonergic activity has implications for both potential mood effects and for serious safety concerns regarding drug interactions with SSRIs (see Drug Interactions section) [1][22].

The serotonin effect appears to build up gradually over several weeks rather than occurring immediately, which is consistent with the time course observed in case reports of serotonin-related adverse events [1][22].

### Lipid Effects

Beyond the triglyceride reduction found by Vasques et al. (2014), the effects of Garcinia on blood lipids have been inconsistent. The same study found no significant effects on total cholesterol, HDL, or LDL [1][17]. No other well-controlled trial has demonstrated reproducible lipid benefits.

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Doses Used in Clinical Trials

Clinical trials of Garcinia cambogia have used a range of doses, typically expressed as total extract weight and corresponding HCA content:

| Trial | Total Daily Extract | HCA Content | Timing | Outcome |
|-------|:---:|:---:|------|------|
| Heymsfield (1998) | 3,000 mg | 1,500 mg | 30 min before meals | No benefit [13] |
| Mattes (2000) | 2,400 mg | 1,200 mg | 30-60 min before meals | Modest weight loss in women [15] |
| Kovacs (2001) | — | 1,500 mg | Before meals | No benefit [16] |
| Westerterp-Plantenga (2002) | 1,500 mg | 900 mg | 1h before meals + 2h after dinner | Appetite reduction only [10] |
| Vasques (2014) | 2,400 mg | 1,200 mg | 30 min before meals | Triglyceride reduction only [17] |

### Timing and Administration

Timing appears to be a critical factor in Garcinia cambogia's potential efficacy:

- **Take on an empty stomach, well before meals.** Research indicates that taking Garcinia with a full meal significantly reduces HCA blood levels compared to fasted administration [1][12].
- **Take 1-2 hours before eating.** Given that HCA reaches peak blood levels 60-90 minutes after ingestion (liquid form) or approximately 2 hours after ingestion (tablet/capsule form), taking the supplement 1-2 hours before a meal — rather than the 30-60 minutes commonly suggested on product labels — may optimize efficacy [1][10][11].
- **Divide doses across meals.** Most clinical trials administered Garcinia in divided doses before each main meal (typically breakfast, lunch, and dinner) [1].

### Typical Supplement Doses

Most Garcinia cambogia supplements provide 500-1,000 mg of extract per serving (containing 250-600 mg HCA), with label directions typically recommending 2-3 servings per day for total daily intakes of 1,500-3,000 mg of extract (750-1,800 mg HCA) [1].

### Important Dosing Considerations

1. **Elemental HCA vs. extract weight:** Supplement labels may list either the weight of the total Garcinia extract or the weight of HCA specifically. A product listing "1,000 mg Garcinia cambogia (50% HCA)" provides 500 mg of HCA per serving.
2. **High-fiber diets may reduce absorption:** The possibility that dietary fiber impairs HCA absorption has been raised but not conclusively demonstrated [1][13][14].
3. **No established effective dose:** Given the inconsistent clinical evidence, there is no well-established "effective dose" for any specific health outcome [2][4].
4. **No long-term safety data at any dose:** Most clinical trials lasted 2-12 weeks. Safety beyond this timeframe is poorly characterized [1][2].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Common Side Effects

Side effects reported in clinical trials and post-marketing surveillance include [1][2]:

- Nausea
- Gastrointestinal discomfort and diarrhea
- Headache

These are generally mild and similar in frequency to placebo in controlled trials.

### Liver Toxicity — The Primary Safety Concern

The most serious safety concern with Garcinia cambogia is hepatotoxicity (liver damage). Multiple case reports have documented liver injury, including cases requiring liver transplantation and one case resulting in death [1][2][7][8][9][23].

#### Case Reports

**Case 1 — Liver transplant (Lunsford et al., 2016)**

A previously healthy 34-year-old man with no known risk factors for liver disease developed liver failure after consuming Garcinia cambogia supplements for five months [1][7]. He had been taking two 80-mg capsules of a Garcinia cambogia 5:1 extract three times daily before meals. He subsequently required a liver transplant [7].

**Case 2 — Acute hepatitis resolved (Sharma et al., 2018)**

A woman developed acute hepatitis (liver inflammation) and liver failure after consuming 1,400 mg of Garcinia cambogia extract once to twice daily for one month [1][8]. Her condition resolved within one month after stopping supplementation [8].

**Case 3 — Acute hepatitis resolved (Yousaf et al., 2019)**

Another woman developed acute hepatitis after consuming 1,400 mg of Garcinia cambogia extract daily for one month [1][9]. Similar to Case 2, the condition resolved after discontinuation [9].

**Case 4 — Death (ANSES, 2020)**

A 71-year-old woman in France died from liver failure three months after beginning a supplement containing 400 mg of Garcinia cambogia once daily [1][6]. The product (Slim Metabol by Zuccari, Italy) contained several other ingredients linked to liver injury, including red yeast rice, rhubarb, and marine collagen. A review of published reports concluded that Garcinia and/or red yeast rice were the most likely causes [1][6].

#### Systematic Reviews of Liver Injury

**Crescioli et al. (2018)** — Published in *Internal and Emergency Medicine*, this case series and literature review documented multiple cases of acute liver injury following Garcinia cambogia supplementation, establishing a pattern of hepatotoxicity associated with these products [23].

**Ballotin et al. (2021)** — A systematic review and meta-analysis of herb-induced liver injury published in *World Journal of Clinical Cases* identified Garcinia cambogia among the herbal products associated with hepatotoxicity [24].

**Ferreira et al. (2020)** — An ACG Case Reports Journal publication documented severe liver injury related to long-term Garcinia cambogia intake [25].

#### Regulatory Response

The accumulation of hepatotoxicity reports has prompted regulatory action:

- **France (ANSES, 2025):** The French food safety agency now advises against the consumption of ANY products containing Garcinia, citing the potential for liver injury and other adverse effects [1][6].
- **United States (FDA):** The FDA has not banned Garcinia supplements but has issued warnings about certain products. The LiverTox database maintained by the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases catalogues the hepatotoxicity evidence [26].
- **NCCIH assessment:** The NIH's National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health states: "It may be unsafe to consume garcinia cambogia products, including multi-ingredient products containing garcinia cambogia extract. Several cases of liver damage have been reported. Some of these cases were severe, but this appears to be uncommon" [2].

### Psychiatric Effects — Mania in Bipolar Disorder

Episodes of mania have been reported in three adults with bipolar disorder (or a possible predisposition to bipolar disorder) who took Garcinia supplements [1][27]:

**Case 1:** A 50-year-old man with bipolar I disorder who had been stable without medication for six years was hospitalized for mania after taking 1-2 pills of Garcinia daily for two months [27].

**Case 2:** A 34-year-old woman with bipolar II disorder who was taking several medications (aripiprazole, bupropion, topiramate) developed irritability, pressured speech, decreased need for sleep, and agitation after taking Garcinia for 4-6 weeks [27].

**Case 3:** A 25-year-old man without a psychiatric history was hospitalized for severe mania and diagnosed with bipolar I disorder after taking Garcinia daily for 2 months [27].

In each case, the manic episode resolved after the patients stopped taking Garcinia and received standard treatment [1][27]. The mechanism may relate to Garcinia's serotonergic activity.

### Testicular Toxicity (Animal Data)

A three-month study in genetically obese male rats found that Garcinia cambogia extract caused testicular toxicity and atrophy (shrinkage) at high doses, but not at lower doses [1][28]. The study also showed fat accumulation suppression, but this beneficial effect occurred only at the doses that also caused toxicity [28].

In terms of body weight, the high doses given to rats were approximately 20-30 times the dose normally taken by humans [1][28]. A similar risk of testicular toxicity may not exist at human doses, and testicular toxicity has not been reported in humans. However, this finding contributes to the overall concern about Garcinia's safety profile.

### Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

Little is known about whether it is safe to use Garcinia cambogia during pregnancy or while breastfeeding [2]. Given the hepatotoxicity concerns and the lack of safety data, Garcinia supplements should be avoided during pregnancy and breastfeeding.

## 6. Drug Interactions

### SSRI Antidepressants — Serotonin Toxicity Risk

The most clinically significant drug interaction involves Garcinia cambogia and SSRI (selective serotonin reuptake inhibitor) antidepressants. There is concern that combining the two may cause serotonin toxicity (serotonin syndrome) [1][2][22].

**Case report (Lopez et al., 2014):** A 35-year-old woman in Oregon had been taking 20 mg daily of the SSRI escitalopram (Lexapro) for over one year without incident [22]. Approximately 1-2 months after starting Garcinia supplements, she developed symptoms of serotonin toxicity:

- Tremor
- Flushing and sweating
- Stuttering speech
- Rapid heartbeat

The Garcinia product label listed: G. cambogia (fruit rind) extract (60% HCA) 1,000 mg, chromium 200 mcg, and small amounts of potassium and calcium per 2-capsule serving [1][22]. She was taking two capsules three times daily for a total daily intake of 3,000 mg of extract [22].

**Mechanism:** Research in rats has demonstrated that Garcinia extract (SuperCitrimax) inhibits serotonin reuptake in brain cells, and a human study showed a 39.8% increase in serum serotonin concentrations after 8 weeks [1]. Because both SSRIs and Garcinia increase serotonin levels — through different mechanisms — the combination may push serotonin to toxic levels.

**Time course:** There appears to be a delay in serotonin buildup, so toxicity would not be expected immediately but rather after several weeks of concurrent use — consistent with the 1-2 month timeframe in the case report [1][22].

**Clinical implication:** Individuals taking SSRI antidepressants (escitalopram, sertraline, fluoxetine, paroxetine, citalopram, fluvoxamine) or SNRI antidepressants (venlafaxine, duloxetine, desvenlafaxine) should avoid Garcinia cambogia supplements due to the risk of serotonin toxicity [1][2][22].

### Drugs Affecting the Liver

The NCCIH warns that interactions between Garcinia cambogia and drugs affecting the liver have been reported [2]. Given the hepatotoxicity signal from case reports, concomitant use of Garcinia with other hepatotoxic drugs (e.g., acetaminophen at high doses, statins, certain antibiotics, methotrexate) may compound liver injury risk. Patients taking any medications metabolized by or potentially toxic to the liver should consult their healthcare provider before using Garcinia [2].

### Theoretical Interactions

Based on Garcinia's known pharmacological properties, theoretical interactions exist with [2]:

- **Diabetes medications** (insulin, sulfonylureas, metformin) — HCA may have modest effects on blood sugar, potentially altering medication requirements.
- **Cholesterol-lowering medications** (statins) — potential additive hepatotoxicity risk, and Garcinia's HCA inhibits an enzyme in the same metabolic pathway (fatty acid synthesis) that statins affect upstream.
- **Blood thinners** — theoretical concern based on limited preclinical data; no clinical cases reported.
- **Psychiatric medications** beyond SSRIs — the mania cases in bipolar patients suggest Garcinia can affect mood regulation, potentially interacting with mood stabilizers, antipsychotics, or other psychotropic medications [27].

### Summary of Key Drug Interactions

| Drug Class | Interaction Type | Risk Level | Recommendation |
|-----------|------|:---:|------|
| SSRIs/SNRIs | Additive serotonergic effect → serotonin toxicity | High | Avoid combination [1][22] |
| Hepatotoxic drugs | Additive liver injury risk | Moderate-High | Avoid or use with extreme caution [2][6] |
| Diabetes medications | Potential blood sugar alteration | Low-Moderate | Monitor blood sugar if combined [2] |
| Mood stabilizers / antipsychotics | Potential mood destabilization | Moderate | Avoid in bipolar disorder [27] |
| Statins | Additive hepatotoxicity + metabolic pathway overlap | Moderate | Caution advised [2] |

## 7. Dietary Sources

Garcinia cambogia is not a nutrient found in typical Western diets. The fruit grows natively in India, Southeast Asia, and Indonesia, where it has traditional culinary uses [1][2].

### Traditional Uses

- **Culinary:** The fruit rind is used as a souring agent in South Asian and Southeast Asian cooking — similar in function to tamarind or lemon juice. It is a traditional ingredient in fish curries, particularly in the coastal regions of India (Kerala, Karnataka, Goa) [2].
- **Food preservation:** The rind has been used historically as a food preservative [2].
- **Tea:** The fruit has been used in folk medicine as a tea for inflammation and stomach complaints [2].

### Obtaining HCA from Food vs. Supplements

The dried rind of the Garcinia cambogia fruit naturally contains up to 30% HCA by weight [1]. However, achieving the doses used in clinical trials (900-1,500 mg HCA per day) through dietary consumption of the fruit alone is impractical for most Western consumers. The fruit is not widely available outside of South and Southeast Asia.

For context, to obtain 1,200 mg of HCA (the dose used in the Mattes and Vasques trials) from dried rind alone, one would need to consume approximately 4,000 mg (4 grams) of dried rind daily — assuming 30% HCA content. While this is theoretically possible for those with access to the fruit, it is not a practical dietary strategy for most supplement consumers.

### Related Garcinia Species

Other Garcinia species also contain HCA, though G. cambogia (G. gummi-gutta) is the primary commercial source:

- **Garcinia indica** (Kokum) — used in Indian cuisine, contains lower HCA levels
- **Garcinia atroviridis** — used in Malaysian cooking
- **Garcinia mangostana** (Mangosteen) — a different species, primarily valued for xanthone antioxidants rather than HCA

These related species are NOT interchangeable with G. cambogia supplements and have not been studied in the same clinical contexts.
