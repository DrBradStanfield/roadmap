## Current view (reviewed 22 October 2026)

Brad sees benefits from berberine for people with prediabetes or type 2 diabetes, but for otherwise healthy people he does not want to risk lowering exercise performance or testosterone levels ([Brad, 28 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/is-the-berberine-hype-justified-should-you-take-it)). A 2021 meta-analysis found berberine lowered HbA1c by 0.38% in type 2 diabetes, an effect Brad calls borderline clinically meaningful ([Brad, 28 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/is-the-berberine-hype-justified-should-you-take-it)). Metformin stays his first-line medicine for blood sugar, because it is well established, it works and it is very cheap, and its trials are much larger and longer: the head-to-head pilot that favoured berberine enrolled 36 people and lasted 3 months ([Brad, 28 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/is-the-berberine-hype-justified-should-you-take-it)). For high cholesterol he considers berberine only as a last resort, when statins are not tolerated, ezetimibe is not enough and PCSK9 inhibitors or bempedoic acid are not affordable, because its effect is much smaller and there are no heart-outcome data ([Brad, 28 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/is-the-berberine-hype-justified-should-you-take-it)). It is not "nature's Ozempic": the larger meta-analysis found an average weight loss of 0.84 kg, against 15.3 kg over 68 weeks with semaglutide ([Brad, 28 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/is-the-berberine-hype-justified-should-you-take-it)). Berberine appears safe so far, but there is not the same level of long-term data as for metformin ([Brad, 28 Apr 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/is-the-berberine-hype-justified-should-you-take-it)).

## Key safety points

- Case reports link berberine to QT prolongation and torsades de pointes, a life-threatening heart rhythm, so talk to your doctor first if you have a heart rhythm condition or take QT-prolonging medicines. If you faint or have a racing or irregular heartbeat, stop berberine and call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department [58][59][60][61].
- Berberine raised cyclosporine levels in kidney transplant patients and slowed three liver enzymes that clear many medicines (CYP3A4, CYP2D6 and CYP2C9). If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting [36][37].
- If you take insulin or other diabetes medicines, talk to your doctor first: berberine lowers blood sugar, and goldenseal (which contains berberine) lowered metformin levels, which may weaken blood sugar control [3][6][35][65].
- Do not take berberine or goldenseal while pregnant or breastfeeding, and do not give it to babies. Berberine may raise the risk of jaundice-related brain damage (kernicterus) in newborns and may cause severe hemolysis in babies with G6PD deficiency [3][28][62].
- Stomach and bowel side effects are the most common. In one pilot study, 34.5% of people had brief gastrointestinal upset, and constipation was the most common side effect in the colorectal adenoma trials [7][14].
- Goldenseal or berberine used on the skin may cause a reaction to sunlight [34].

## 1. Overview

Berberine is a plant alkaloid. It is the main active compound of Chinese goldthread (*Coptis chinensis*, huanglian) and is also found in goldenseal (*Hydrastis canadensis*) [3][28][51]. *Coptis chinensis* has been used in Chinese medicine for more than 2500 years [20].

Berberine is usually used as an antibiotic drug, and its glucose-lowering effect was noticed when people with diabetes took it for diarrhea [6]. It is now widely used to treat gastrointestinal infections, diabetes, high blood pressure and high cholesterol [36].

Research interest has centred on blood sugar, cholesterol and metabolic syndrome. Berberine activates AMPK, an enzyme that acts as a cellular energy sensor and that is also one of the ways metformin works [57]. It also prompts the liver to make more LDL receptors while reducing PCSK9, a protein that breaks those receptors down [57].

Berberine is poorly absorbed when taken by mouth, and higher doses cause more gastrointestinal upset [38]. This has driven the development of forms designed to raise absorption, such as berberine Phytosome and dihydroberberine [4][38]. Dihydroberberine has been tested in a short absorption study rather than an outcome trial [38].

Some influencers call berberine "nature's Ozempic," but the comparison is misleading [57]. In a 2020 meta-analysis berberine lowered average body weight by 2.07 kg, while semaglutide lowered it by 15.3 kg in 68 weeks in a large trial [55][56].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

Berberine supplements come as salts (mainly berberine hydrochloride and berberine sulfate) and as formulations designed to raise absorption. The basic problem across all forms is poor absorption from the gut [38].

### Standard Forms

| Form | Berberine Content | Notes |
|------|:-:|------|
| Berberine Hydrochloride (HCl) | Less than the salt weight | The most common supplement form. Trial abstracts usually give the dose simply as "berberine." |
| Berberine Sulfate | Less than the salt weight | An alternative salt form. |

Because these are salts, part of the stated weight is the chloride or sulfate, so a dose of either holds somewhat less berberine than its stated weight.

### Enhanced-Absorption Formulations

**Berberine Phytosome:** A food-grade berberine formulation. In healthy volunteers, berberine absorption (AUC) was around 10 times higher than with unformulated berberine, with no side effects observed [4]. In a placebo-controlled trial, two tablets a day (550 mg/tablet) improved blood sugar and some blood fats in people with impaired fasting glucose [40].

**Dihydroberberine:** In a crossover study in five men, 100 mg and 200 mg doses of dihydroberberine produced higher plasma berberine levels over a two-hour window than 500 mg of berberine. Glucose and insulin did not change, which the authors put down to the short study and insulin-responsive participants [38].

### Key Principles for Form Selection

**General supplementation:** Plain berberine is the best-studied form. The largest trials, including the type 2 diabetes, colorectal adenoma and MASLD trials, used plain berberine, not enhanced formulations [5][6][13].

**Absorption does not guarantee efficacy:** Dihydroberberine raised blood levels of berberine but did not change glucose or insulin in the available study [38]. Higher blood levels do not automatically mean better clinical results.

### Synergistic Combinations

In the PREMOTE trial, berberine plus probiotics lowered HbA1c by 1.04%, about the same as berberine alone (0.99%) [32]. In a 2012 meta-analysis, adding berberine to oral diabetes drugs controlled blood sugar better than the same drugs alone [45].

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Blood Sugar and Type 2 Diabetes

Blood sugar is one of berberine's most studied uses. Brad notes that berberine activates AMPK, the same energy-sensing pathway through which metformin works [57].

**Berberine vs. placebo in type 2 diabetes:** In a trial of 116 adults with type 2 diabetes plus abnormal blood fats, berberine 1.0 g daily for 3 months lowered HbA1c from 7.5% to 6.6% and fasting glucose from 7.0 to 5.6 mmol/L, with all measures differing significantly from placebo. Triglycerides, total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol also fell. Five people on berberine had mild to moderate constipation [6]. In the PREMOTE trial of 409 people with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes, 12 weeks of berberine lowered HbA1c by 0.99%, against 0.59% with placebo, but caused more gastrointestinal side effects [32].

**Berberine vs. metformin:** In a 3-month pilot study of 36 adults with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes, berberine and metformin (each 0.5 g three times a day) lowered blood sugar to a similar degree. In the berberine group, HbA1c fell from 9.5% to 7.5% [7]. Brad points out that this trial was small and short, and that larger meta-analyses find a more modest effect [57]. A 2012 meta-analysis of 14 trials (1068 participants) found that berberine did not control blood sugar better than metformin, glipizide or rosiglitazone, but that adding berberine to oral diabetes drugs worked better than the same drugs alone. Trial quality was generally low [45].

**Meta-analyses for glycemic control:** A 2021 meta-analysis of 46 trials found berberine, alone or with standard diabetes treatment, lowered HbA1c (mean difference -0.73) and fasting glucose [51]. Used alone, berberine lowered HbA1c less (mean difference -0.38) [51]. A 2022 meta-analysis of 37 studies with 3,048 patients found reductions in fasting glucose (-0.82 mmol/L) and HbA1c (-0.63%), and the effect was related to people's starting glucose and HbA1c [52]. A 2024 meta-analysis of 50 studies with 4,150 participants found that berberine combined with diabetes drugs lowered HbA1c by 0.69%; the most common dose was 0.9 to 1.5 g a day [53].

**Important limitations:** Berberine is not a replacement for established diabetes medicines. Brad treats metformin as the first-line drug for blood sugar, because it is well established, effective and cheap, with much larger and longer trials behind it [57]. Many berberine trials are small and of low methodological quality [45].

### Prediabetes and Insulin Resistance

**Prediabetes with Berberine Phytosome:** In a trial of 49 overweight adults with impaired fasting glucose, two tablets a day of Berberine Phytosome (550 mg/tablet) for two months improved blood glucose, total cholesterol, triglycerides, insulin, the ApoB to ApoA ratio (by 0.08), visceral fat and fat mass compared with placebo. No adverse events were reported [40].

### Cholesterol and Lipids

Research suggests berberine lowers cholesterol by several routes. It reduces how much cholesterol from food is absorbed in the gut, prompts the liver to make more LDL receptors and reduces PCSK9, the protein that breaks those receptors down [57].

**Meta-analysis of lipid effects:** A 2018 meta-analysis of 16 trials with 2147 participants found berberine lowered total cholesterol (-0.47 mmol/L), LDL cholesterol (-0.38 mmol/L) and triglycerides (-0.28 mmol/L), and raised HDL cholesterol (0.08 mmol/L) when used alone. The authors urged caution because the trials varied widely and most were of low methodological quality [54]. A 2024 meta-analysis found berberine alone lowered LDL cholesterol by 0.30 mmol/L [53].

**Large MASLD trial (Lei 2026):** In a double-blind trial of 337 adults without diabetes who had obesity and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), six months of berberine 1 g a day did not reduce visceral fat or liver fat compared with placebo. Berberine did lower LDL cholesterol (-7.72 mg/dL), apolipoprotein B (-3.42 mg/dL) and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein more than placebo [5].

**Cardiovascular risk meta-analysis:** A 2022 dose-response meta-analysis found berberine lowered triglycerides (-23.70 mg/dl), total cholesterol (-20.64 mg/dl) and LDL cholesterol (-9.63 mg/dl). The best dose for triglycerides, total cholesterol and weight was 1 g/day [12].

**Zhang 2008 lipid data:** In the 116-patient diabetes trial, LDL cholesterol fell from 3.23 to 2.55 mmol/L on berberine, a significant difference from placebo [6].

**Yin 2008 lipid data:** In a second group of 48 adults with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes, berberine also brought down total and LDL cholesterol significantly [7].

**Synthesis:** Lowering LDL cholesterol is berberine's most consistent effect, including in the 2026 Lei trial that found no benefit for visceral fat or liver fat [5]. Brad notes that the effect is much smaller than that of cholesterol medicines and that berberine has no heart-outcome data, which is why he keeps it as a last resort [57].

### Weight Loss and Obesity

Influencers have called it "nature's Ozempic," yet the clinical evidence for meaningful weight loss is weak.

**Meta-analysis results:** A 2020 meta-analysis of 12 trials found berberine lowered body weight by 2.07 kg and BMI by 0.47 kg/m2 [55]. A 2022 meta-analysis found smaller effects on weight (-0.84) and BMI (-0.25 kg/m2) [12]. Brad reports the weight figure as 0.84 kg [57].

**Large MASLD trial, no fat loss:** The largest trial (Lei 2026, 337 participants) found that six months of berberine 1 g a day had no effect on visceral fat or liver fat, compared with placebo, in adults with obesity and no diabetes [5].

**Berberine vs. semaglutide:** The comparison is not close. Semaglutide lowered body weight by 16.0% versus 5.7% for placebo during 68 weeks, in a trial that added intensive behavioural therapy plus a low-calorie diet at the start [42]. In another large trial, people on semaglutide lost 15.3 kg in 68 weeks [56].

**Synthesis:** Claims that berberine causes large weight loss are overstated. The best trial evidence shows no meaningful fat loss, and the average weight change in meta-analyses is small. Brad prescribes GLP-1 medicines, alongside diet and exercise, for people who want to lose weight [57].

### Non-Alcoholic Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD/MASLD)

**Open-label study:** In an open-label trial of 184 people with NAFLD, berberine 0.5 g three times a day plus lifestyle change for 16 weeks reduced liver fat more than lifestyle change alone (52.7% vs 36.4%), with better weight, insulin resistance and blood fats. There was no placebo [44].

**Large MASLD RCT, negative result:** The larger, double-blind Lei 2026 trial (337 participants) found that six months of berberine 1 g a day had no effect on liver fat content compared with placebo [5]. A 2020 meta-analysis also found no effect on the liver enzymes ALT and AST [55].

**Synthesis:** The evidence for berberine in NAFLD/MASLD is mixed. The largest placebo-controlled trial showed no benefit for liver fat. The earlier positive trial had no placebo [44]. Berberine should not be relied on to treat NAFLD/MASLD.

### Colorectal Adenoma Prevention

This is one of berberine's better-studied uses, though the main trial and its follow-up were done in China [13][15].

**Two-year trial:** In a double-blind trial across seven hospitals in China, people aged 18 to 75 years who had had colorectal adenomas removed took berberine 0.3 g twice daily or placebo. Recurrent adenomas were found in 36% of the berberine group and 47% of the placebo group (relative risk 0.77, 95% CI 0.66 to 0.91). No serious adverse events were reported [13].

**Pooled analysis:** A meta-analysis of three trials with 1076 patients found that berberine was linked to fewer adenoma recurrences at 1 year (RR 0.69, 95% CI 0.57 to 0.84) and 2 years (RR 0.75, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.88). Berberine had more adverse events than placebo (RR 2.91, 95% CI 1.24 to 6.85), mainly constipation, which went away once the drug was stopped [14].

**Long-term follow-up:** In a follow-up of 648 patients from the two-year trial, the protection lasted at least 6 years after berberine was stopped, with lower adenoma recurrence (34.7% vs 52.1%) and lower neoplasm occurrence (63.4% vs 71.0%) [15].

**Limitations:** The main trial and its follow-up were done in China, and whether the results apply elsewhere is unknown. Berberine should not replace standard colorectal cancer screening (colonoscopy) or established prevention strategies.

### H. pylori Infection

Berberine is usually given as an antibiotic [6], and some evidence supports it as part of H. pylori treatment.

**Berberine vs. bismuth:** In a trial of 612 previously untreated patients, a 14-day berberine-containing quadruple therapy cleared H. pylori in 90.1% of patients, against 86.4% with bismuth-containing quadruple therapy, and was judged not inferior [17].

### Polycystic Ovary Syndrome (PCOS)

A 2019 review of 12 randomised trials found no solid evidence that berberine improves live birth or other clinical outcomes in women with PCOS. Live birth rates were lower than with letrozole (RR 0.61, 95% CI 0.44 to 0.82). Compared with placebo, berberine lowered total testosterone, and compared with metformin it improved some cholesterol and waist measures [18].

### Cardiovascular Effects and Arrhythmias

A 2015 review reported research suggesting berberine may have effects against heart failure, hypertension and arrhythmias [20]. A 2026 systematic review found antiarrhythmic effects but also QT prolongation, bradycardia and low blood pressure [61], and case reports describe life-threatening torsades de pointes in people taking berberine [58][59][60]. Anyone with a heart rhythm disorder should talk to their doctor before taking it (see Section 5).

### Male Reproductive Health

Brad does not advise berberine for otherwise healthy people partly because he does not want to risk lowering testosterone, a concern that rests on metformin data; the evidence for berberine itself is severely limited [57].

### Antimicrobial Activity

Berberine is usually used as an antibiotic drug [6], and it is widely used to treat gastrointestinal infections [36]. Its use as part of H. pylori treatment is covered above [17].

### Anticancer and Antiviral Activity

Beyond colorectal adenoma prevention, the anticancer research is early. Berberine has fought several types of cancer cells in a petri dish, but results like these often do not carry over to people [57].

### Goldenseal

There is not enough evidence to say whether goldenseal is useful for any health condition, and no rigorous studies have tested it in people. Very little berberine may be absorbed from goldenseal taken by mouth, so berberine study results may not apply to it [3].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Typical Clinical Trial Doses

Clinical trials have used the following doses of berberine:

| Indication | Dose | Frequency | Duration | Notes |
|-----------|------|-----------|----------|-------|
| Type 2 diabetes | 1.0 g daily | Daily | 3 months | Placebo-controlled trial [6] |
| Type 2 diabetes | 0.5 g | 3 times a day | 3 months | Compared with metformin [7] |
| Type 2 diabetes (meta-analysis) | 0.9 to 1.5 g/d | Divided | 1 to 3 months | Most common dose across trials [53] |
| Prediabetes (impaired fasting glucose) | Berberine Phytosome, 550 mg tablet | 2 tablets a day | 60 days | Enhanced absorption formulation [40] |
| Obesity with MASLD | 1 g/d | Daily | 6 months | No effect on liver or visceral fat [5] |
| NAFLD | 0.5 g | 3 times a day | 16 weeks | With lifestyle change, open-label [44] |
| Colorectal adenoma prevention | 0.3 g | Twice daily | 2 years | Lower dose than metabolic studies [13] |

### Practical Dosing Guidance

**Optimal dose range:** The most common dose in diabetes trials was 0.9 to 1.5 g a day [53]. A dose-response meta-analysis found 1 g/day worked best for triglycerides, total cholesterol and weight [12].

**Side effects rise with dose:** Higher doses of berberine cause more gastrointestinal distress [38].

**Duration:** Diabetes trials typically lasted 1 to 3 months [53]. The colorectal adenoma trial lasted 2 years [13].

**Goldenseal dosing:** Small studies have given goldenseal at about 3 g per day for short periods without serious harm; the safety of longer use is uncertain [3].

### How to Read a Supplement Label

Labels usually give the weight of the salt (berberine HCl or berberine sulfate), which includes the chloride or sulfate part, so the amount of berberine itself is a little lower.

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Common Side Effects

The most common side effects of berberine affect the stomach and bowel. In a 3-month pilot study, 20 patients (34.5%) had brief gastrointestinal side effects [7]. Constipation was the most common side effect in the colorectal adenoma trials and went away once berberine was stopped [13][14]. In the PREMOTE trial, berberine caused more gastrointestinal side effects than placebo [32].

Higher doses cause more gastrointestinal distress [38].

In meta-analyses, total adverse events were not higher with berberine than with control treatments (RR 0.64, 95% CI 0.31 to 1.30 in lipid trials; RR 0.73, 95% CI 0.55 to 0.97 in diabetes trials) [52][54].

### Serious Adverse Effects

**Heart rhythm problems:** Case reports describe torsades de pointes, a form of polymorphic ventricular tachycardia, in people taking berberine. A man aged 92 had fainting spells and pulseless episodes about two weeks after starting berberine, with a QTc of 616 ms that improved once berberine was withheld [58]. A man aged 61 who took berberine, dihydroberberine and other supplements, with mildly low magnesium, had recurrent torsades with a QTc of 669 ms [59]. A woman aged 63 had a cardiac arrest from torsades after several months of berberine, and her ECG returned to normal after she stopped it [60]. A 2026 systematic review of 22 studies found that berberine blocks several heart potassium channels, including hERG, and can cause QT prolongation, bradycardia and low blood pressure [61]. If you have a heart rhythm condition or take medicines that affect heart rhythm, talk to your doctor before taking berberine. If you faint or have a racing or irregular heartbeat, stop berberine and call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department.

**Hyperbilirubinemia and jaundice:** Berberine pushes bilirubin off albumin, the blood protein that carries it, about ten times more strongly than phenylbutazone in laboratory tests, and it raised unbound bilirubin in rats. Herbs rich in berberine have been reported to pose some risk of kernicterus (bilirubin-related brain damage) in jaundiced newborns and are best avoided in jaundiced newborns and pregnant women [28].

**Skin reactions:** A 2018 case report described symmetrical drug-related intertriginous and flexural exanthema (SDRIFE), a rash in skin folds, caused by berberine [29].

**Hypoglycemia risk:** Berberine lowers blood sugar [6][7]. In a 2022 meta-analysis, berberine alone or with oral diabetes drugs did not significantly increase hypoglycemia (RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.21 to 1.08), but if you take insulin or other diabetes medicines, talk to your doctor before adding it [52].

**Blood pressure:** Berberine lowered systolic blood pressure (-5.46 mmHg) in a 2022 meta-analysis [12]. If your blood pressure is low or you take blood pressure medicines, check with your doctor first.

### Potential DNA Damage

In human liver cancer cells, berberine was the most potent DNA-damaging compound among goldenseal's alkaloids, an effect linked to blocking topoisomerase II, which untangles DNA. Goldenseal increased liver tumours in rats and mice in a 2-year National Toxicology Program study, and the authors suggest this enzyme effect may contribute [30]. This has not been shown in people at supplement doses.

### Effects on Gut Microbiome

Berberine acts on gut bacteria. In the PREMOTE trial in newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes, berberine's blood sugar effect appeared to work partly through gut bacteria (inhibition of DCA biotransformation by *Ruminococcus bromii*), and berberine caused more gastrointestinal side effects than placebo [32].

### Hemolytic Anemia

A 1996 review of Chinese herbal treatments for newborn jaundice states that berberine can cause severe acute hemolysis in babies with G6PD deficiency [62]. A 2012 study found the evidence on newborn jaundice conflicting and saw no organ toxicity in 20 patients with chronic blood disorders who took berberine-containing herbs [63]. People with G6PD deficiency or a history of hemolytic anemia should talk to their doctor before taking berberine, and it should not be given to babies (see Pregnancy and Breastfeeding).

### Photosensitivity

A laboratory study found that berberine plus UVA light sharply reduced the survival of skin cells and increased DNA damage. The authors advise avoiding sunlight or UVA light sources when using topical products containing goldenseal or berberine [34].

### Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

Do not use goldenseal during pregnancy or breastfeeding, and do not give it to infants, because its berberine can harm newborns [3]. The main concern is berberine's effect on bilirubin, which may raise the risk of kernicterus in jaundiced newborns; herbs rich in berberine are best avoided in jaundiced newborns and pregnant women [28].

### Long-Term Safety

Long-term safety data are limited. In the 2026 Lei trial (6 months), adverse events were similar with berberine 1 g a day and placebo [5]. In the 2-year colorectal adenoma trial, no serious adverse events were reported [13]. Brad notes there is not the same level of long-term data as for metformin [57].

### Goldenseal Safety

Goldenseal has been given at about 3 g per day for short periods in small studies without serious harm, but the safety of longer use is uncertain. Some goldenseal products have contained other herbs or ingredients not on the label. It should not be used during pregnancy or breastfeeding or given to infants [3].

Goldenseal may change the way the body processes many medicines [3]. In 16 healthy volunteers, goldenseal raised midazolam exposure (geometric mean ratio 1.43) and lowered metformin exposure (geometric mean ratio 0.77), which may weaken blood sugar control [65]. NCCIH describes this as a fall in metformin levels of about 25 percent [3]. In adults with type 2 diabetes, the overall interaction was not clinically meaningful, but metformin exposure fell by about 20% at low metformin doses (500 to 750 mg), and the authors suggest doctors may caution patients about combining them [35].

## 6. Drug Interactions

Berberine slows some of the liver enzymes that break down many medicines, which can raise their blood levels. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

### Cytochrome P450 Enzyme Inhibition

In healthy men, berberine 300 mg three times a day for 2 weeks reduced the activity of CYP2D6, CYP2C9 and CYP3A4 [36]. Midazolam exposure (AUC) rose 40%, the dextromethorphan ratio used to measure CYP2D6 rose ninefold, and the losartan ratio used to measure CYP2C9 doubled [36].

**CYP3A4 substrates:** In kidney transplant patients, berberine 0.2 g three times a day for 12 days raised cyclosporine exposure (AUC) by 34.5%, most likely by blocking CYP3A4 [37]. Many other medicines are broken down by CYP3A4, CYP2D6 or CYP2C9, including some statins, the blood thinner warfarin and some antidepressants, so berberine could in principle raise their levels too.

### P-glycoprotein Inhibition

Berberine may also affect P-glycoprotein, a pump in the gut wall that limits how much of some drugs, such as digoxin, is absorbed. In rats, berberine raised blood levels of digoxin and cyclosporine taken by mouth, with larger doses having a larger effect, by blocking this pump in the gut [64]. This was a rat study. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

### Blood Sugar-Lowering Medications

Berberine lowers blood sugar, so taking it with insulin or other diabetes medicines could add to their effect [6][7]. A 2022 meta-analysis did not find a significant rise in hypoglycemia when berberine was combined with oral diabetes drugs (RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.21 to 1.08) [52]. Talk to your doctor before combining them.

### Blood Pressure-Lowering Medications

Berberine lowered systolic blood pressure in a 2022 meta-analysis [12]. If you take blood pressure medicines or have low blood pressure, check with your doctor first.

### Bilirubin-Related Interactions

Berberine displaces bilirubin from albumin, its carrier protein in the blood, more strongly than phenylbutazone, a known displacer [28]. Herbs rich in berberine are best avoided in jaundiced newborns [28].

### Omeprazole and PPIs

In the same enzyme study, berberine did not significantly change the handling of omeprazole, the test drug for CYP2C19 [36].

### Goldenseal and Metformin

Goldenseal lowered metformin levels about 25 percent in healthy adults, which may weaken blood sugar control [3]. In adults with type 2 diabetes, the effect was about 20% at low metformin doses and absent at high doses [35].

### Summary of Key Interactions

| Interaction Type | Mechanism | Medications Affected | Clinical Significance |
|-----------------|-----------|---------------------|----------------------|
| CYP3A4 inhibition | Reduced drug metabolism | Cyclosporine, midazolam [36][37] | Cyclosporine exposure rose 34.5% in transplant patients |
| CYP2D6 and CYP2C9 inhibition | Reduced drug metabolism | Dextromethorphan, losartan (test drugs) [36] | Other drugs cleared by these enzymes may be affected |
| Additive blood sugar lowering | Both lower blood sugar | Insulin and other diabetes medicines [6][52] | Talk to your doctor first |
| Goldenseal and metformin | Altered metformin absorption | Metformin [3][35] | Larger effect at low metformin doses |
| Bilirubin displacement | Competes for albumin binding | Relevant in newborns [28] | Avoid in jaundiced newborns |

## 7. Dietary Sources

Berberine is not found in ordinary foods. It comes from medicinal plants.

### Plant Sources of Berberine

Berberine is extracted from the rhizomes of Chinese goldthread (*Coptis chinensis*, huanglian) [14][28]. It is also found in goldenseal (*Hydrastis canadensis*), whose rhizome is bright yellow [3].

### Why Diet Cannot Provide Therapeutic Doses

The plants that contain berberine are not eaten as food. Trials used concentrated berberine at 0.9 to 1.5 g a day [53], and very little berberine may be absorbed from goldenseal taken by mouth [3].
