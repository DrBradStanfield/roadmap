## Key safety points

- Magnesium can reduce the absorption of some medicines, such as bisphosphonates, tetracycline and quinolone antibiotics, levothyroxine, rosuvastatin, sotalol, gabapentin, levodopa and some HIV medicines [1][48][49][50][55][58][60]. Take tetracycline and quinolone antibiotics at least 2 hours before or 4 to 6 hours after magnesium, and bisphosphonates at least 2 hours apart from it [1]; ask your doctor or pharmacist how far apart to take the others.
- Long-term proton pump inhibitors and loop or thiazide diuretics can lower magnesium [1]. Potassium-sparing diuretics reduce its loss, so check with your doctor first, as magnesium could build up [1].
- If you take an SGLT2 inhibitor or digoxin, ask your doctor before taking magnesium [52][53].
- The risk of magnesium toxicity rises when kidney function is impaired [1]. People with kidney disease should check with their doctor before taking magnesium supplements.
- The daily upper limit for supplemental magnesium is 350 mg for adults [1]. Higher doses, such as those used for migraine, belong under a doctor's supervision [1].
- Very large doses can cause magnesium toxicity, with muscle weakness, difficulty breathing or an irregular heartbeat [1]. If these appear, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department.
- The most common side effect is diarrhea, which can come with nausea and abdominal cramping, mainly at high doses [1].

## 1. Overview

Magnesium is an essential mineral involved in over 300 enzymatic reactions in the human body [1]. It is needed for energy production, the making of DNA, RNA and protein, muscle and nerve function, blood glucose control and blood pressure regulation [1]. Approximately 50-60% of the body's magnesium resides in bone, where it contributes to bone structure, with most of the rest in soft tissues [1].

Low magnesium intake is widespread [4]. Almost half (48%) of the US population ate less magnesium than they needed from food in 2005-2006 [4]. That is low intake, not measured deficiency: there are no current national data on magnesium status in the US [1]. Men aged 71 and over and teenagers are the most likely to have low intakes, and older adults and people with gastrointestinal disease, type 2 diabetes or alcohol dependence are more likely to have low magnesium [1]. Serum magnesium holds less than 1% of total body magnesium and is a poor marker of whole-body status [1]. Subclinical deficiency can exist despite a normal serum level [5]. Judging magnesium status well may need both laboratory tests and a clinical assessment [1].

Symptoms of deficiency progress from mild to severe [1]:
- **Early:** Loss of appetite, nausea, vomiting, fatigue, weakness
- **Moderate:** Numbness, muscle contractions and cramps, seizures, abnormal heart rhythms, coronary spasms
- **Severe:** Low blood calcium or potassium (hypocalcemia, hypokalemia)

Symptomatic deficiency is uncommon in healthy people; certain health conditions and medicines are the usual causes [1].

Absorption efficiency is typically 30-40% of dietary magnesium [1].

### Key Principles for Form Selection

**For correcting deficiency or raising levels:** In small studies, the aspartate, citrate, lactate and chloride forms were absorbed better than oxide [1][6]. A review found that the type of salt appears less relevant than is often thought; the dose and a person's magnesium status matter more [7].

**For cardiovascular support:** Taurate is promoted for heart health, but the main evidence is a rat study in which magnesium taurate restored blood pressure and reduced heart damage [8]. Brad, who calls both glycinate and taurate well absorbed [45], uses taurate to also get the potential benefits of taurine itself [46].

**For sleep and relaxation:** The most recent sleep trial used bisglycinate [17]. Two L-threonate (Magtein) trials measured sleep: a 21-day trial reported better ring-measured sleep scores [12], and a 6-week trial found no difference in ring-measured sleep [81].

**For cognitive function:** L-threonate (Magtein) is marketed for brain health, but human results are mixed (see Section 3). Brad is not convinced it offers extra benefit, given its high cost [45].

**For constipation relief:** Magnesium is a main ingredient in some laxatives, such as milk of magnesia (magnesium hydroxide) [1]. In two Japanese placebo-controlled trials of chronic constipation, magnesium oxide taken for 28 days improved overall symptoms: 0.5 g three times a day helped 70.6% vs 25.0% of patients [75], and 1.5 g helped 68.3% vs 11.7% [76]. These are doses of the whole compound (see How to Read a Supplement Label), but 1.5 g of magnesium oxide still holds far more magnesium than the 350 mg upper limit for supplements [1][45]. In one study of older people, high blood magnesium was more common at magnesium oxide doses of 900 mg a day or more [67], which is less than the 1.5 g in that trial. See Toxicity (Hypermagnesemia) below before using a magnesium laxative regularly.

### Magnesium Orotate: A Safety Note

Magnesium orotate is sometimes marketed as a "highly bioavailable" form. The European Food Safety Authority has raised concerns about the orotic acid it contains, based on animal studies in which orotic acid promoted tumours, and concluded that the safety of magnesium orotate as a supplement could not be established at the dose proposed to it [54]. If you are thinking of taking it, check with your doctor or pharmacist first.

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Blood Pressure

**Overall effect:** A meta-analysis of 34 RCTs (n=2,028) found that magnesium supplementation at a median dose of 368 mg/day reduced systolic blood pressure (SBP) by 2.00 mmHg (95% CI: 0.43-3.58) and diastolic blood pressure (DBP) by 1.78 mmHg (95% CI: 0.73-2.82) (Zhang et al., *Hypertension*, 2016) [13].

**In those already on BP medications:** A subsequent meta-analysis of 38 trials found substantially larger effects in hypertensive patients already taking antihypertensive drugs: SBP reduction of 7.68 mmHg and DBP reduction of 2.96 mmHg. In normotensive groups, the effect did not reach statistical significance (Argeros et al., *Hypertension*, 2025) [14]. People with low blood magnesium also had a larger fall in systolic pressure, of 5.97 mm Hg [14]. The authors concluded that their findings support a blood pressure-lowering effect of magnesium in people with hypertension or low magnesium, to be interpreted with caution because the studies varied widely [14].

**FDA position:** In 2022, the FDA said it would allow a qualified health claim that diets with enough magnesium may reduce the risk of high blood pressure, while concluding that the evidence for it is inconsistent and inconclusive [92].

**Other reviews:** A Cochrane review of 12 trials in 545 people with high blood pressure found no significant fall in systolic pressure (a mean difference of 1.3 mmHg) and a small fall in diastolic pressure (2.2 mmHg), from trials of poor quality [77]. NIH concludes that clinical trials suggest magnesium supplements only marginally lower blood pressure [1].

### Sleep

**Older adults with insomnia:** An RCT in 46 elderly subjects found that a daily dose of 500 mg of magnesium for 8 weeks significantly decreased sleep onset latency (P=0.02) and increased serum melatonin and renin levels compared to placebo (Abbasi et al., *J Res Med Sci*, 2012) [15]. It also reported more sleep time and better sleep efficiency, but no significant difference between the groups in total sleep time (P=0.37) [15].

**Meta-analysis in older adults:** A meta-analysis of 3 RCTs (n=151 older adults) found that magnesium reduced sleep onset latency by 17.36 minutes (95% CI: -27.27 to -7.44, P=0.0006) (Mah & Pitre, *BMC Complement Med Ther*, 2021) [16]. Total sleep time rose by 16.06 min, which was not statistically significant [16]. Brad notes that the review's authors judged the trials to be at moderate to high risk of bias, with small benefits not well supported by quality evidence [47]. His current guidance adds that the figure of about 17 minutes sooner pooled 2 small trials that both gave 500 to 729 mg a day of elemental magnesium as magnesium oxide (Brad's current guidance, 25 Oct 2026).

**Magnesium bisglycinate:** An RCT of 155 adults found that 250 mg elemental magnesium as bisglycinate for 4 weeks modestly reduced insomnia severity (ISI score), with a small effect size (Cohen's d=0.2). Exploratory analyses suggested greater benefit in those with lower baseline magnesium intake (Schuster et al., *Nat Sci Sleep*, 2025) [17].

The ISI score fell by 3.9 points on magnesium and 2.3 on placebo (p = 0.049), and no other psychological outcome differed significantly [17].

**Magnesium L-threonate (Magtein) for sleep:** An RCT of 1 g/day Magtein for 21 days in 80 adults reported improved deep sleep and REM sleep scores via Oura ring and improved daytime functioning (Hausenblas et al., *Sleep Med X*, 2024) [12]. The trial was short and industry-funded (by AIDP Inc., which also employed three of its six authors), and on the Insomnia Severity Index and most other sleep questionnaire measures the magnesium group did not improve significantly more than placebo [12]. A later 6-week trial of Magtein found no difference in ring-measured sleep [81]. Brad is not convinced that L-threonate offers extra benefit, given its high cost [45].

**Reviews:** A 2023 systematic review of 9 studies in 7,582 people found that observational studies link magnesium status with sleep quality, while the randomised trials gave contradictory results [78]. A 2024 review found that 5 of 8 trials measuring sleep reported some improvement, but the trials were small and differed in dose, form and length [79].

**Restless legs:** A systematic review found too little evidence to say whether magnesium helps restless legs syndrome [80].

### Cognitive Function

**Calcium-to-magnesium ratio:** A randomized trial that enrolled 250 participants at Vanderbilt University Medical Center found that lowering a high dietary calcium-to-magnesium ratio to around 2.3 with personalized magnesium supplements improved cognitive function by 9.1% in those aged over 65 years [18]. The trial linked this effect partly to changes at the APOE gene (Zhu et al., *J Alzheimers Dis*, 2020) [18].

**Long-term observational data:** A follow-up of more than 20 years of 6,473 women aged 65-79 years found that those in the middle fifth of magnesium intake had a lower risk of mild cognitive impairment than those in the lowest fifth (hazard ratio 0.63) (Lo et al., *BMJ Open*, 2019) [19]. The authors concluded that total magnesium intake between the estimated average requirement and the recommended dietary allowance may be associated with a lower risk of mild cognitive impairment [19].

**Magnesium L-threonate (Magtein):** The original animal study demonstrated enhanced synaptic plasticity and memory in rats (Slutsky et al., *Neuron*, 2010) [9]. Human results are mixed: one small, manufacturer-funded study showed a small improvement in one cognitive task but no other benefits, and a study in people with dementia showed no meaningful improvement [45]. Brad is not convinced it offers extra benefit, given its high cost [45]. A newer trial in 100 adults with poor sleep found that 2 g a day of Magtein for 6 weeks improved a total cognition score slightly more than placebo (p = 0.043), with no difference on a reasoning test or in ring-measured sleep [81].

### Diabetes and Insulin Resistance

Diabetes increases urinary magnesium losses, and the resulting magnesium inadequacy might impair insulin secretion and action [1].

**Prediabetes with low magnesium:** An RCT of hypomagnesemic adults with prediabetes found that a daily dose of 382 mg of magnesium (as magnesium chloride) for four months improved glucose status in 50.8% of the magnesium group versus 7.0% of the placebo group. At the end of the trial, fasting glucose was 86.9 vs 98.3 mg/dL (Guerrero-Romero et al., *Diabetes & Metabolism*, 2015) [20].

**Metabolic syndrome with low magnesium:** The same research group found that after 16 weeks of the same dose and form (magnesium chloride), only 48% of the magnesium group still met criteria for metabolic syndrome versus 77.5% of placebo (Rodriguez-Moran et al., *Adv Chronic Kidney Dis*, 2018) [21].

**Established type 2 diabetes:** An RCT in 14 people with type 2 diabetes who used insulin and had low serum magnesium found that 6 weeks of oral magnesium did NOT improve insulin sensitivity (Drenthen et al., *Diabetologia*, 2024) [22]. The dose was 15 mmol of magnesium a day [22]. In an older trial in 50 people with insulin-treated type 2 diabetes, 15 mmol a day for 3 months raised plasma magnesium but did not improve glucose control [82].

**Long-term studies:** Across 13 cohort studies of 536,318 people, each extra 100 mg a day of magnesium intake went with a lower risk of type 2 diabetes (relative risk 0.86) [83]. This shows association, not cause, and the American Diabetes Association finds too little evidence to recommend magnesium for glucose control in people with diabetes [1].

### Bone Health

Approximately 50-60% of body magnesium resides in bone, where it contributes to bone structure [1].

**Bone mineral density:** An analysis of approximately 2,000 adults aged 70-79 found that higher magnesium intake was associated with higher whole-body bone mineral density (BMD) in white women and men, but not in Black participants (Ryder et al., *J Am Geriatr Soc*, 2005) [23].

**Fracture risk:** A US study of 3,765 adults followed for 8 years found that those in the highest fifth of magnesium intake had a lower fracture risk than those in the lowest fifth (hazard ratio 0.47 in men and 0.38 in women) [24]. Women meeting the RDA had 27% lower fracture risk (Veronese et al., *Br J Nutr*, 2017) [24].

**Mechanism and gaps:** Severe magnesium deficiency can cause low blood calcium [1]. Some studies link higher intake with higher bone density, but more research is needed to know whether magnesium supplements can help prevent or manage osteoporosis [1].

### Exercise and Muscle Recovery

Magnesium is involved in the active transport of calcium and potassium ions across cell membranes, a process important to nerve impulse conduction, muscle contraction, and normal heart rhythm [1].

**Muscle soreness in low-Mg individuals:** In a crossover trial of nine male runners on low-magnesium diets, 500 mg/day of magnesium for 7 days reduced muscle soreness after a downhill run but did not improve performance (Steward et al., *Eur J Appl Physiol*, 2019) [25].

**Delayed-onset muscle soreness:** A double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in 9 men and 13 women of college age found that 350 mg/day of magnesium for 10 days modestly reduced delayed-onset muscle soreness (Reno et al., *J Strength Cond Res*, 2022) [26].

**Performance in adequate-Mg adults:** An RCT of 15 adults with adequate magnesium levels found that 300 mg magnesium chloride twice a day for 9 days modestly WORSENED cycling performance (Bomar et al., *Nutrients*, 2025) [27].

**Elderly women:** An RCT enrolled 139 healthy older women, with a mean age of 71.5, who attended a mild fitness program. Those given 300 mg magnesium oxide for 12 weeks improved walking speed and chair-rise performance compared with an untreated control group (Veronese et al., *AJCN*, 2014) [28].

**Practical implication:** Athletes should ensure adequate dietary magnesium but should not megadose; one small 9-day trial found that supplementation in people with adequate magnesium modestly worsened performance [27].

### Leg Cramps

Despite being one of the most commonly cited reasons for taking magnesium, the evidence is disappointing.

A Cochrane systematic review concluded that magnesium supplementation is unlikely to provide clinically meaningful benefit for skeletal muscle cramps regardless of form or dose (Garrison et al., *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*, 2020) [29].

A subsequent RCT in Finland (n=109) confirmed that magnesium hydrochloride taken daily for 4 weeks did NOT reduce cramp frequency compared with placebo (Kuusipalo et al., *Trials*, 2026) [30]. The adjusted difference from placebo was 0.20 cramps a week, while compression stockings cut cramps by 1.43 a week [30].

**Pregnancy cramps:** Results are mixed, but the overall meta-analysis indicates magnesium is NOT effective for pregnancy-related leg cramps (Liu et al., *Taiwan J Obstet Gynecol*, 2021) [31]. One Thai trial in 86 pregnant women did find that 300 mg a day of magnesium bisglycinate for 4 weeks halved cramp frequency in more women than placebo (86.0% vs 60.5%) [90].

**Synthesis:** Trials in older adults with leg cramps have not found a meaningful benefit from magnesium [29][30].

### Migraine Prevention

**Positive evidence:** An RCT of 600 mg magnesium citrate daily reduced migraine frequency (Koseoglu et al., *Magnes Res*, 2008) [32]. Another trial using 600 mg trimagnesium dicitrate found a 41.6% reduction in migraine frequency versus 15.8% for placebo (Peikert et al., *Cephalalgia*, 1996) [33]. In 20 women with menstrual migraine, 360 mg a day from day 15 of the cycle for 2 months reduced headache days and pain scores more than placebo [89].

**Negative evidence:** A trial of 300 mg magnesium with riboflavin 400 mg and feverfew did no better than its control, riboflavin 25 mg (Maizels et al., *Headache*, 2004) [34]. A trial of 10 mmol magnesium twice daily for 12 weeks was stopped early when 28.6% responded on magnesium and 29.4% on placebo; mild side effects, mainly soft stools and diarrhea, affected 45.7% on magnesium vs 23.5% on placebo [86].

**Guidelines and reviews:** The American Academy of Neurology and the American Headache Society rate magnesium as probably effective for migraine prevention (Level B) [88]. A 2018 systematic review of 5 trials rated it possibly effective (Grade C) [87].

**Practical note:** Migraine trials have used up to 600 mg/day, well above the 350 mg supplement UL. GI side effects (diarrhea) are common at these doses. This indication should be discussed with a healthcare provider [1].

### Depression

**Epidemiological data:** In a cross-sectional study of 8,894 US adults, very low magnesium intake was associated with depression (risk ratio 1.16), mainly in adults younger than 65; in older adults low intake appeared protective [36].

**Interventional evidence:** The best evidence comes from a small RCT (n=60) in depressed people with low magnesium, finding that 250 mg magnesium oxide twice daily produced modest reductions in depression scores compared with placebo (Rajizadeh et al., 2017) [37]. An open-label trial in 126 adults with mild to moderate depression found that 248 mg a day of magnesium for 6 weeks improved depression scores by 6.0 points, but it had no placebo [91].

**Synthesis:** In depressed people with low magnesium, correcting the deficiency may help [37]. Magnesium is not a substitute for established depression treatment.

### Cardiovascular Disease (Beyond Blood Pressure)

**Arrhythmia:** In 3,530 Framingham Offspring participants followed for up to 20 years, those in the lowest quartile of serum magnesium were about 50% more likely to develop atrial fibrillation (Khan et al., *Circulation*, 2013) [38].

**Heart failure:** In 1,569 patients with chronic heart failure, serum magnesium of 2 mEq/L or less was associated with higher cardiovascular mortality (hazard ratio 1.38) but not with more hospitalizations (Adamopoulos et al., *Int J Cardiol*, 2009) [39].

**Stroke and heart failure in cohorts:** Each extra 100 mg a day of magnesium intake went with an 8% lower risk of stroke in 7 cohort studies of 241,378 people [85]. In 40 cohort studies, the same increase went with a 22% lower risk of heart failure, but not with lower risk of coronary heart disease [84]. These are associations, not proof that supplements help.

### Preeclampsia and Pregnancy

In the Magpie Trial of 10,141 women with pre-eclampsia, magnesium sulphate cut the risk of eclampsia by 58% compared with placebo and probably reduced maternal deaths [40]. Side effects were reported by 24% of women given magnesium sulphate versus 5% given placebo (Magpie Trial, *Lancet*, 2002) [40]. Magnesium needs increase during pregnancy to 350-360 mg/day [1].

### Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL)

The UL for supplemental magnesium (not including food sources) is **350 mg/day** for adults [1]. Magnesium from food does not count toward this limit, because in healthy people the kidneys eliminate excess magnesium from food in the urine [1]. High doses from supplements or medicines, by contrast, often cause diarrhea [1].

For children the limit is 65 mg at 1 to 3 years and 110 mg at 4 to 8 years, and from 9 years it is 350 mg [1].

In Europe the limit is lower: the European Food Safety Authority's upper limit for supplemental magnesium is 250 mg/day [54].

### Practical Dosing by Indication

**General supplementation (to fill dietary gaps):** Brad uses a low-dose supplement as a top-up to a healthy diet, not a replacement for it [45]. In July 2025 he gave that top-up as 126 mg, about 30% of the RDA [45]. His current guidance gives these amounts: his multivitamin capsules have 150 mg in the US (126 mg in the UK and EU) and his multivitamin powder 224 mg, as magnesium taurate, and his sleep supplement has 126 mg as magnesium bisglycinate (Brad's current guidance, 1 Nov 2026). His Australian multivitamin capsules have 260 mg, labelled magnesium amino acid chelate (Brad's current guidance, 1 Nov 2026). The powder with the sleep supplement gives 350 mg a day, at the upper limit, so anyone also taking another magnesium supplement, antacid or laxative would go over it (Brad's current guidance, 1 Nov 2026). The Australian capsules with the sleep supplement give 386 mg a day, above the 350 mg daily upper limit for magnesium from supplements, so check with your doctor or pharmacist before combining them (Brad's current guidance, 1 Nov 2026).

**Blood pressure support:** The two main meta-analyses used median doses of 368 mg/day [13] and 365 mg/day [14]. Both are just above the 350 mg/day upper limit for supplemental magnesium [1]. Talk to your doctor before taking more than that limit.

**Sleep:** The trials used 250 mg a day as bisglycinate [17] and 500 mg a day [15].

The 500 mg dose is above the 350 mg upper limit for supplemental magnesium [1]; talk to your doctor before taking more than that limit.

**Migraine prevention:** Trials used up to 600 mg/day [32][33], which exceeds the UL [1]; discuss it with your doctor.

**Prediabetes/insulin resistance with low magnesium:** The main trial used a daily dose of 382 mg as magnesium chloride [20].

**Muscle recovery (athletes with low Mg):** The main soreness trial used 500 mg/day for 7 days, which is above the UL [1][25].

### How to Read a Supplement Label

The weight of the magnesium compound and the weight of elemental magnesium are different.

- 400 mg magnesium oxide = about 240 mg elemental magnesium, because oxide is 60% elemental magnesium [45]
- 1,000 mg magnesium citrate = about 110 mg elemental magnesium, because citrate is 11% elemental magnesium [45]

The Supplement Facts panel on a US supplement label declares the amount of elemental magnesium, not the weight of the whole compound [1].

### Practical Notes on Dietary Magnesium

- **Refining grains removes magnesium.** Refining that removes the germ and bran substantially lowers the magnesium content of grains [1]. White rice has about a quarter of the magnesium of brown rice [1].
- **Water can be a significant source.** Tap water magnesium content varies widely (1 mg/L to more than 120 mg/L) depending on the source. Hard water areas can contribute meaningfully to daily intake [1].
- **Food-first approach:** Brad advises reaching the RDA from food first (leafy greens, legumes, nuts, seeds and whole grains) and using a supplement only to fill the gap [45].

## 6. Safety and Side Effects

### Common Side Effects

The primary side effect of oral magnesium supplementation is **osmotic diarrhea**, caused by unabsorbed magnesium drawing water into the intestinal lumen [1]. Risk varies by form: the forms most commonly reported to cause diarrhea are magnesium carbonate, chloride, gluconate and oxide [1]. Brad notes that citrate has a laxative effect [45].

Other reported side effects include nausea and abdominal cramping at high doses [1].

### Toxicity (Hypermagnesemia)

Magnesium toxicity from oral supplements is rare in individuals with normal kidney function because the kidneys efficiently excrete excess magnesium [1]. However, hypermagnesemia can occur with:
- **Renal impairment:** Reduced magnesium clearance makes supplementation potentially dangerous [1]. People with kidney disease should check with their doctor before taking magnesium supplements. If you have severe kidney disease or are on dialysis, do not take magnesium supplements, laxatives or antacids unless your kidney doctor prescribes them.
- **Massive oral doses** from magnesium-containing laxatives or antacids used in excess, typically more than 5,000 mg a day; such doses have killed a young child and an older man [1].
- **Daily magnesium oxide laxatives in older adults:** In Japanese studies, high levels were most common with poor kidney function and higher oxide doses [67][68][69]. In one study of older patients, high levels developed in 11.6% of those taking 900 mg a day or more, 27.0% of those with low kidney function, and 53.1% of those with both [67]. Four older patients with kidney problems became seriously ill, and one died [70].
- **Constipation or a slow bowel:** A magnesium laxative held in the gut keeps being absorbed, and fatal cases have occurred even with normal kidney function [71].

Symptoms of hypermagnesemia usually develop once serum magnesium exceeds 1.74 to 2.61 mmol/L and progress with severity [1]:
- **Early:** Hypotension, nausea, vomiting, facial flushing, urine retention, ileus, depression, lethargy
- **Later:** Muscle weakness, difficulty breathing, extreme hypotension, irregular heartbeat, cardiac arrest

If these later symptoms appear, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department. A few cases of fatal hypermagnesemia have been reported [1].

### Swallowing and Tablet Problems

In laboratory tests, magnesium oxide tablets did not break up in xanthan-gum swallowing thickeners, and in one patient with a narrowed esophagus such a tablet caused a blockage [73]. A magnesium oxide bezoar, a hard mass in the stomach, has also been reported [74].

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy:** Magnesium needs increase to 350-360 mg/day [1]. In the Magpie Trial, magnesium sulphate for pre-eclampsia halved the risk of eclampsia, but 24% of women reported side effects versus 5% on placebo [40].

**Elderly:** Older adults are more likely to have low magnesium [1], and medicines that lower it, such as PPIs and diuretics, are a common cause [1]. They are also the group in which high levels from daily magnesium oxide laxatives are most often reported [67][70].

**Kidney disease:** The risk of magnesium toxicity increases with impaired kidney function, because the ability to remove excess magnesium is reduced or lost [1]. Check with your doctor before taking magnesium supplements. If you have severe kidney disease or are on dialysis, do not take magnesium supplements, laxatives or antacids unless your kidney doctor prescribes them. In a trial in 148 people with advanced kidney disease, magnesium hydroxide 15 mmol twice daily for a year did not slow artery calcification; there were 5 deaths and 6 cardiovascular events on magnesium vs 2 deaths and none on placebo, and gut side effects were more common (35 vs 9) [72].

## 7. Drug Interactions

Magnesium supplements can interact with numerous medications. The interactions fall into two categories: magnesium reducing drug absorption (chelation), and drugs affecting magnesium levels.

### Magnesium Reduces Drug Absorption

These medications should be **separated from magnesium supplements** [1]:

| Drug Class | Examples | Separation Time | Notes |
|-----------|---------|:-:|----------|
| Bisphosphonates | Alendronate, risedronate | 2+ hours | Magnesium-rich supplements can decrease bisphosphonate absorption [1] |
| Tetracycline antibiotics | Doxycycline, demeclocycline | 2h before or 4-6h after | Forms insoluble complexes [1] |
| Fluoroquinolone antibiotics | Ciprofloxacin, levofloxacin | 2h before or 4-6h after | Forms insoluble complexes [1] |
| Levothyroxine | Synthroid, Levoxyl | Ask your pharmacist | Taken together with levothyroxine, magnesium aspartate reduced its absorption by 12% in a trial of 15 healthy adults; the authors advise taking levothyroxine separately from magnesium [55] |
| Statins (rosuvastatin) | Crestor | Separate as much as you can; even a 2-hour gap still cut exposure by 22% [48]; ask your pharmacist | An aluminium and magnesium hydroxide antacid taken at the same time reduced rosuvastatin exposure by 54%; taken 2 hours after rosuvastatin, by 22% [48] |
| Sotalol | Betapace | 2+ hours | An antacid of magnesium hydroxide and aluminium oxide lowered sotalol blood levels; a two-hour interval avoided the interaction [49] |
| Gabapentin | Neurontin | Ask your pharmacist | Magnesium oxide lowered gabapentin bioavailability by 32% in one study [50] |
| Levodopa | Levodopa with carbidopa | Ask your doctor | In healthy volunteers, magnesium oxide cut levodopa exposure by 35.3% and carbidopa exposure by 80.9% [58]; in 35 people with Parkinson's disease it lowered levodopa levels [59] |
| HIV integrase inhibitors | Dolutegravir, raltegravir | Ask your pharmacist | A magnesium and aluminium antacid cut dolutegravir exposure by 74% when taken together and by 26% when dolutegravir came 2 hours earlier [60]; an antacid also lowered raltegravir levels [61]. In 20 people taking once-daily raltegravir, a magnesium and aluminium hydroxide antacid taken 12 hours later still cut the trough level, and the authors advise against metal-cation antacids with that dose [93] |

Two hours is not enough for every medicine: take tetracycline and quinolone antibiotics, such as ciprofloxacin, at least 2 hours before or 4 to 6 hours after a magnesium-containing supplement [1]. Ask your pharmacist about the gap for your medicine. In one study, an antacid taken 2 hours before ciprofloxacin left 23.2% of the dose absorbed and one taken 4 hours before left 70%; taken 6 hours before or 2 hours after, it had no effect [57]. For tetracyclines, a 3-hour gap prevented the interaction [56].

Magnesium can also speed up the absorption of a medicine: magnesium hydroxide taken with glipizide, a sulfonylurea for diabetes, raised early insulin and lowered blood glucose more than glipizide alone [62]. If you take a sulfonylurea, ask your doctor before starting magnesium.

### Drugs That Affect Magnesium Levels

| Drug Class | Effect on Magnesium | Clinical Implication |
|-----------|:---:|----------|
| Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) | Deplete | Use for more than about a year can cause low magnesium; in a quarter of the cases the FDA reviewed, supplements did not correct it and the PPI had to stop [1]. The FDA issued a safety communication on this in 2011 [1], and it advises doctors to consider checking magnesium before long-term PPI treatment and from time to time during it [1]. In observational studies, PPI users had a higher risk of low magnesium (risk ratio 1.43) [63]. |
| Loop diuretics | Deplete | Furosemide, bumetanide increase renal magnesium excretion [1]. |
| Thiazide diuretics | Can deplete | Long-term use can raise urinary magnesium loss and lead to depletion [1]. In a 1-week trial in 12 men, hydrochlorothiazide did not change plasma magnesium, though red-cell magnesium fell [64]. |
| Potassium-sparing diuretics | May increase | Amiloride and spironolactone reduce magnesium excretion [1]. If you take one, check with your doctor before taking magnesium supplements. |
| SGLT2 inhibitors | May increase | Empagliflozin, dapagliflozin and other SGLT2 inhibitors marginally increased serum magnesium in a meta-analysis of trials [52]. Across 10 trials, dapagliflozin raised serum magnesium by 0.06 mmol/L more than placebo [65]. Ask your doctor before supplementing. |
| Digoxin | May increase loss | After an acute intravenous dose in 9 patients with atrial fibrillation, digoxin increased urinary magnesium loss, and low magnesium is a known risk factor for digoxin toxicity [53]. Ask your doctor before taking magnesium; they may want to check your levels. |
| High-dose zinc | May impair Mg | Zinc supplements of 142 mg a day lowered magnesium absorption and balance [66]. |

People who take any medicine regularly should discuss their magnesium intake with their doctor [1].

## 8. Frequently Asked Questions

**Which form of magnesium is best?**
There is no single best form; it remains unclear which form is absorbed best [7]. Brad favours glycinate and taurate, which he calls well absorbed, over poorly absorbed oxide [45]. Migraine trials have mostly used citrate [32][33].

**Can I take too much magnesium?**
The upper limit for supplemental magnesium is 350 mg/day for adults. The main risk of excess is diarrhea, not systemic toxicity, in individuals with normal kidney function. The risk of toxicity rises with impaired kidney function, so people with kidney disease should check with their doctor first [1].

**How long does magnesium take to work?**
Sleep trials that found benefits lasted 3 to 8 weeks [12][15][17]. In the main blood pressure meta-analysis, people took magnesium for about three months [13].

**Is magnesium L-threonate (Magtein) worth the premium price?**
Brad does not think so. Human results for memory and thinking are mixed, and he is not convinced it offers extra benefit, given its high cost [45]. The animal data are promising [9]. Two trials measured sleep: a 21-day trial reported better ring-measured sleep scores [12], and a 6-week trial found no difference in ring-measured sleep [81].

**Does magnesium help with leg cramps?**
Probably not. A Cochrane systematic review concluded that magnesium supplementation is unlikely to provide meaningful benefit for muscle cramps regardless of form or dose [29][30].

**Do Epsom salt baths or topical magnesium creams work?**
Evidence for transdermal magnesium absorption is weak. A small placebo-controlled pilot study found no statistically significant overall increase in blood magnesium from applying a magnesium cream to the skin daily for two weeks; the rise was significant only in a subgroup of non-athletes [51].

**Can I combine different forms of magnesium?**
Yes, provided total elemental magnesium from supplements stays within the 350 mg/day UL [1].
