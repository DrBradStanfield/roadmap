There has been a battle raging for decades about what oils we should cook our food in, to reduce heart disease. And despite mistakes along the way, to me, I find this story redeeming because eventually good science shone through.

Right now you can find experts on both sides. In January 2026, the new US Dietary Guidelines said this about cooking fats: "Other options can include butter or beef tallow." [1] Then in November 2026, a cardiologist from Tufts University told Dr Rhonda Patrick that seed oils are "really good for us". [2]

So who's right?

## How the Battle Started

The battle started in the 1960s, when heart disease was behind 1 in 3 deaths in America. [3]

At the time, a scientist called Ancel Keys had been running feeding experiments on men in a Minnesota hospital. When they ate more saturated fat, the kind that butter and beef fat are rich in, their blood cholesterol went up. When they ate more of the fats in vegetable oils, it came down. [4] He also set up a study of nearly 13,000 men in seven countries, and its first results found that the groups eating more saturated fat had higher cholesterol in their blood and more deaths from heart disease. [5] And that idea set off a fight that's still going on today.

Based on Ancel Keys' feeding experiments, heart doctors advised their patients to cut back on animal fats and use vegetable oils instead. That meant oils pressed from seeds, like corn and soybean oil. Olive oil is a plant oil too, but it's pressed from the fruit. [6]

So people switched over to seed oils and margarine. But the margarine back then was full of trans fats. Trans fats are created when a liquid oil is hardened into a solid spread. And in 1993, a study that followed 85,000 American nurses found that the women eating the most trans fat had a 50% higher risk of heart disease than the women eating the least. [7]

People had been told to switch from butter to something that was even worse for their hearts. And that's why people, rightfully so, distrusted industry oils, because they felt lied to.

So should we use beef tallow and saturated fats instead, since those fats don't have the factory-made trans fats? We have a study that was designed to directly test that idea, but there was a problem that a Mayo Clinic doctor called Robert Frantz found.

## The Boxes in the Basement

Robert's father was Ivan Frantz, a scientist at the University of Minnesota. And together with Ancel Keys, he ran that study. It was called the Minnesota Coronary Experiment. From 1968 to 1973, more than 9,000 people living in six state mental hospitals and a nursing home were served one of two diets. One was high in saturated fat. In the other, corn oil and corn oil margarine took its place. People were put on one diet or the other by chance, and weren't told which.

But here was the problem. The trial ended in 1973, and the full results were never published. A short paper came out sixteen years later, with much of the data still missing. [8] Nobody knows for certain why.

Then Christopher Ramsden, a scientist at the US National Institutes of Health, came looking for that missing data. By then Ivan Frantz had died, so his son Robert Frantz drove 90 minutes to his childhood home, to search his late father's basement. On his third trip, in the far corner, he found unlabeled boxes. Inside were old computer tapes and piles of yellowed papers. [9]

Ramsden's team published what was on them in 2016. Blood cholesterol fell by about 14% on the corn oil diet. But deaths did not fall. [10]

That seems to be a big problem for the pro seed oil argument.

And it wasn't the only one. Ramsden's team had already recovered another trial, from Sydney. This one was 458 men who'd recently had a heart attack or serious chest pain. One group was given safflower oil and safflower margarine to replace animal fats. And about 18% of them died, against about 12% of the animal fat group. [11]

Seed oils lowered cholesterol, and no lives were saved, and in the Sydney trial, deaths were lower in the animal fat group. So is that the end of it for seed oils? Not so fast.

## Why Those Trials Don't End It

The Minnesota trial is deeply flawed. The average person was only on the diet for about a year, and about three in ten of them were under 35. Patients came and went, and only about a quarter stayed for a year or longer. And heart disease builds over decades, so being on a diet intermittently for a year isn't expected to have much if any impact. And nobody knows how much trans fat was in either diet, so we can't tell what it did to the results [8] [10]. And because of these flaws, I don't think we can draw conclusions from this trial.

The Sydney trial has its own problems. The trial was small, and about 70% of those men smoked, so that population doesn't represent today's population. And because the trial is small, the results can easily swing one way or the other due to chance. [11]

So instead of relying on one or two studies, what happens when the dietary fat trials are combined?

Well, it depends which trials you count. Ramsden's team combined five trials, including Minnesota and Sydney, and found that the seed oil diets didn't lower deaths from heart disease. But more than eight in ten of the people in that analysis came from the Minnesota trial, with all the flaws we just went through. [10]

Instead, a Cochrane review in 2020 only counted trials that ran for at least two years, because remember, heart disease takes years to show up. So Minnesota was left out, and Sydney stayed in. And cutting saturated fat led to 17% fewer cardiovascular events, like heart attacks and strokes. That's a modest benefit, and death rates didn't clearly change, which may be because even these trials were short. [12]

But the anti seed oil side has an answer to that. They agree that a few years is too short, but for a different reason. Seed oils may cause damage slowly, and here's the rationale.

## The Slow Build-Up

Linoleic acid, that's the main fat in most seed oils, builds up in our body fat, and it builds slowly. In 1959, linoleic acid made up about 9% of the fat Americans carried. By 2008 that was about 21%. [13]

And the worry is inflammation. Linoleic acid is an omega-6 fat. The claim is that we evolved eating about equal amounts of omega-6 and omega-3, that we now eat around 15 times more omega-6, and that this ratio pushes the body toward inflammation. [14] But when the randomized trials were pooled, eating more linoleic acid didn't raise markers of inflammation overall. [15]

But a blood marker isn't a heart attack. So what happens to people who carry more linoleic acid, year after year?

The best answer we have comes from an analysis of 30 studies in 13 countries. They measured how much linoleic acid nearly 69,000 people were carrying, mostly from a blood sample, and then followed them for up to 32 years. And the people with more linoleic acid had less cardiovascular disease overall, fewer strokes, and a 22% lower risk of dying from cardiovascular disease. [16]

Now, these studies only watched people. Nobody was put on a diet, so they can't prove cause. But if omega-6 fats such as linoleic acid were toxic, we would expect to see more strokes and more heart disease. Instead we see the opposite. And to be upfront, that analysis was part-funded by Unilever, a company that sold margarine. [16]

But those studies can't tell us how the oil was cooked. So what about heating? Because that's where the anti seed oil side says the real damage is done.

I think that's the main argument.

## What Heat Does to the Oil

And on the chemistry, they're right. When fat meets oxygen it breaks down, and that's called oxidation. The weak spots where it starts are called double bonds. A saturated fat has none. That's what saturated means. Oleic acid, the main fat in olive oil, has one. Linoleic acid has two. So in the heat, linoleic acid breaks down the fastest. [17]

And when it breaks down, it produces compounds called aldehydes, and those are the worry. In one lab study, cooking fats were held at frying temperature for up to 90 minutes. The seed oils produced high levels of aldehydes. The animal fats, lard and dripping, produced only low levels. And used seed oils collected from restaurants were high in them too. [18]

But do those aldehydes in our food go on to cause disease in people? Nobody has shown that over the long term. In a Spanish study that followed about 40,000 adults for 11 years, there was no clear link between eating fried food and heart disease, whether people fried in olive oil or sunflower oil, although the researchers didn't know how often the oil was reused. [19] And in one small experiment, sunflower oil that had been reheated 20 times raised inflammation markers in the blood after a meal, and olive oil didn't. The oils for that one were supplied by an olive oil company. [20]

So what should we use to cook our food at home? And in a perfect world, people shouldn't be eating French fries. But while people are still eating French fries, what can we do to make them less unhealthy?

Well, in frying tests, regular seed oil breaks down the fastest, and tallow and olive oil both hold up well, although one of the main tests was part-funded by an olive industry group. [21] So the decision is tallow versus olive oil. How do we choose between those two?

## What Tallow Does to LDL

Well, beef tallow is about half saturated fat, the same as butter, and saturated fat raises LDL cholesterol. There's no modern trial of tallow itself, so butter is the closest test we have. In a British trial, people ate about three and a half tablespoons of butter a day, or the same amount of olive oil. After four weeks, LDL cholesterol was clearly higher on the butter. [22]

And LDL cholesterol is causal for heart disease. That comes from more than 200 studies and over 2 million people, in a review by European heart specialists. These studies were observational studies, Mendelian randomization studies, and trials where LDL cholesterol was actively lowered and heart disease rates were recorded. [23]

But does a higher LDL from butter actually show up as people dying earlier? Trials of a few years can't tell us. For that we need a lot of people, followed for decades.

## The Biggest Study

And the biggest study like that is from a group at Harvard in 2025, funded by the US National Institutes of Health. They followed 221,000 adults for up to 33 years. The people who ate the most butter had a 15% higher risk of dying over that time than the people who ate the least. And swapping less than a tablespoon of butter a day for plant oils, so that's olive oil and the seed oils together, was linked with a 17% lower risk of dying. Now, this study only watched people, so it can't prove cause, and the butter link was clearest for cancer deaths, not heart deaths. [24]

So between those two, I'd choose olive oil.

## Where Good Science Shone Through

So to me, this is where good science shone through, twice. Trans fats were a mistake, and it was researchers who caught it, and since 2018 American manufacturers can't add them to most foods. [25] And the missing Minnesota and Sydney data was found and published, by a government scientist, in a leading medical journal. So now it can be weighed with everything else.

And when I weigh it all up, if a person has a diet high in seed oils, that's probably a marker that their diet isn't great, rather than seed oils themselves being the issue. And we probably do want to be reducing our saturated fat intake. So keep butter and tallow for now and then, and cut back further if your LDL cholesterol is high.

And even those new US guidelines still cap saturated fat at 10% of daily calories, and the first cooking fat they name is olive oil. [1]

And in my own kitchen, I use extra-virgin olive oil.

**Other studies**

These studies didn't change the answer, so they weren't covered above, but they're worth knowing about.

- In the 2020 Cochrane review, the trials that replaced saturated fat with polyunsaturated fats, the kind in seed oils, saw 21% fewer cardiovascular events (95% confidence interval 0 to 38%). That subgroup wasn't statistically different from the trials that replaced saturated fat with carbohydrate [12].

- A 2017 review that kept only the trials it judged to be adequately controlled found no effect on major coronary events (risk ratio 1.06, 0.86 to 1.31) [26].

- In the Harvard study, the people who ate the most plant oils had a 16% lower risk of dying than the people who ate the least [24].

- In 2026, the American Heart Association [27] and the American College of Cardiology [28] both advised choosing unsaturated fats in place of saturated fat.

- The meetings and travel of the European panel that wrote the LDL review were paid for by unrestricted educational grants from two drug companies, MSD and Amgen [23].
