## Zinc Lozenges: The Accidental Discovery

In 1979, a 3-year-old girl named Karen was undergoing chemotherapy for leukemia in Austin, Texas. The treatment was saving her life — but it had destroyed her immune system. Every cold hit her hard, and her doctor warned that once a cold took hold, it could last for months [1].

Her father, George Eby, was giving her zinc tablets to bring up her levels and improve her immune system's function [2]. One afternoon, as yet another cold was flaring up, Karen refused to swallow the zinc tablet. She complained that her throat was too sore and she just wanted to sleep. So Eby told her to crush it with her teeth and let it dissolve in her mouth while she took a nap.

After several hours, she woke from her nap playing and smiling. The cold was gone. Eby, skeptical, told her to go back to bed. But she knew what she felt. She said, "No, Daddy, zinc cured my cold" [1].

It was an accident. A tired child, a tablet she wouldn't swallow, and a father who noticed what happened next. But that accident would launch decades of research into something millions of people experience every year — and most of us still handle the wrong way.

Most of what we reach for when a cold strikes doesn't actually work. High-dose vitamin C, echinacea, garlic, megadoses of vitamin D — all tested in human clinical trials, none of them effective. Yet companies keep selling them to people who are desperate to feel better. That's a billion-dollar industry built on remedies that fail when you test them properly.

Eby set out to find answers. He dove into the literature and found that zinc ions interfere with a virus's ability to replicate and spread in the laboratory — including rhinoviruses that cause the common cold [2]. He conducted the first randomized controlled trial of zinc lozenges. Symptoms resolved much more quickly in those taking zinc, with more than 3 times as many in the placebo group still reporting symptoms at treatment end [2].

In the first 24 hours, 11% of zinc-treated subjects were asymptomatic within 12 hours and 22% within 24 hours. None in the placebo group recovered this quickly [2].

The next two decades saw contradictory trials — about half showing benefit, half showing nothing [3]. The variation came down to chemistry: manufacturers added citric acid and sweeteners that bind to zinc ions and neutralize them. Ionized zinc ranged from 100% in zinc acetate lozenges to 0% in some formulations [3]. One of the most-cited negative trials used lozenges with ingredients Eby had warned would neutralize the zinc [4].

With this issue sorted out, a meta-analysis of zinc acetate trials found it shortened colds by 2.73 to 2.94 days [5]. Researcher Harri Hemilä concluded: "The evidence is thus very strong that zinc lozenges may shorten the duration of colds by approximately 33%" [4].

## Saline Nasal Irrigation

For thousands of years, people across South Asia have rinsed their noses with salt water to treat colds — described in ancient Indian yogic texts as "Jalaneti" [6]. Western medicine largely ignored this practice.

The first crack came from Australian woodworkers suffering chronic nasal symptoms from wood dust. Saline rinsing improved symptoms significantly [7], and buried in the data was an incidental finding: fewer colds and sore throats [6]. A 1961 lab finding had shown sodium chloride inactivated viruses [8].

Dr. Sandeep Ramalingam, a virologist in Edinburgh, connected these threads [9]. Around 2018, his team explained the mechanism: nasal cells produce tiny amounts of hypochlorous acid (a natural bleach) to kill viruses, but need chloride ions as raw material. Saline solution floods those cells with chloride [10]. You're not only washing the virus away — you're resupplying your cells with the ammunition they need.

In Ramalingam's RCT, participants starting within 48 hours of symptoms recovered nearly two days faster, used 36% less over-the-counter medication, and were 35% less likely to transmit to household contacts [6]. The pilot study had 66 participants and was open-label [6].

A larger 2024 trial with over 400 children confirmed the findings: 6 days of symptoms vs 8 in the control group, with fewer household transmissions [9].

## Honey: The Oldest Remedy

An Ancient Egyptian papyrus with 900 remedies mentions honey 500 times [11]. Hippocrates prescribed it for coughs and sore throats [12].

In 2020, an Oxford team pooled data from 14 RCTs. Honey improved combined symptom scores, reduced cough frequency, and reduced cough severity compared to usual care [13]. As researcher Joseph Lee noted: "Honey is cheap and widely available — many people will probably have some sitting in the cupboard, so it's worth giving it a try before visiting your GP" [14].

We don't know the optimal dose or whether raw honey differs from processed. But across 14 trials, honey reliably outperformed usual care.

## Practical Steps for the First 24 Hours

**Zinc lozenges**: Start within 24 hours of symptoms [15]. Dose above 75mg zinc per day [16]. Use zinc acetate or zinc gluconate without citric acid [3], sorbitol, or mannitol [4]. Dissolve slowly in mouth — don't chew. One lozenge (23mg zinc) every 2 hours while awake [2]. Take after eating to avoid nausea. Zinc gluconate lozenges "may be as effective as zinc acetate lozenges" [4].

**Saline nasal irrigation and gargling**: Start immediately, several times daily. Use hypertonic saline (~1 teaspoon non-iodized salt in a cup of boiled, cooled water). Rinse each nostril, then gargle.

**Honey**: Around a teaspoon before bedtime. Won't shorten the infection like zinc, but may improve comfort and sleep.

## The Frontier

**Nitric oxide nasal spray**: SaNOtize's spray inactivated influenza A/B within 15 seconds, SARS-CoV-2 within 2 minutes, and reduced rhinovirus by >99.98% in lab studies [18]. COVID clinical trials showed 95% viral load reduction within 24 hours [19]. A large European trial is underway [20]. Available in limited countries.

**Povidone-iodine (Betadine) nasal spray**: Phase III trial (260 adults) missed its primary endpoint, but the subgroup starting within 24 hours showed 40% improvement — only 29 people though [21]. PVP-I gargle is available OTC and included in Japan's national respiratory guidelines.

Karen Eby survived her leukemia. Her father spent the rest of his career studying zinc and the common cold [1]. The next time you feel that tell-tale scratch in the back of your throat, consider having these tools ready. The evidence says the first 24 hours matter most.
