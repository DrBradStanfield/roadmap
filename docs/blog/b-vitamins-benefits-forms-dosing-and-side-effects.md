## Current view (reviewed 10 November 2026)

Brad's view is that the dose decides: reaching the recommended intake of a vitamin is one thing, megadosing is an entirely different proposition, and plenty of multivitamins and B-complex products hold riboflavin doses orders of magnitude above the recommended 1.3 mg a day, which the bright yellow urine proves ([Brad, 19 Aug 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/multivitamins-are-no-longer-expensive-urine)). For vitamin B6, he reports that Australia has required a warning label on products supplying more than 10 mg a day since 2022 after reports of nerve damage, and will make oral products with more than 50 mg and up to 200 mg per daily dose Pharmacist Only Medicines from 1 June 2027; he puts the recommended intake at 1.7 mg, notes that the symptoms of too much B6 look like those of too little, and would not rely on switching to the P5P form to avoid the problem ([Brad, 19 Aug 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/multivitamins-are-no-longer-expensive-urine)). For the brain, he points to the VITACOG trial: in older people with mild memory problems, two years of high-dose B vitamins slowed brain shrinkage by 40% compared with placebo in those with high omega-3 levels and gave no benefit in those with low omega-3, which he calls a lead worth following rather than a finished answer ([Brad, 19 Aug 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/multivitamins-are-no-longer-expensive-urine)). In July 2026 the WHO dementia guideline said it does not recommend B vitamins for reducing dementia risk in the absence of a diagnosed deficiency, which Brad describes as recommending against them; he notes that the guideline never mentions the COSMOS trial and did not reassess supplements, which does not make WHO wrong but means it answers an older question ([Brad, 19 Aug 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/multivitamins-are-no-longer-expensive-urine)). On vitamin B3, he is now concerned about megadosing: high-dose niacin added to statins did not reduce heart attacks or strokes and, given with a flushing blocker, caused more serious side effects, and a 2024 study linking a breakdown product called 4PY to about twice the risk of heart attack, stroke or death is an association, not proof, but one more reason for caution ([Brad, 29 Oct 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/nr-in-parkinsons-what-the-nopark-trial-found)). ## Key safety points

- Extended-release niacin may raise the INR in people taking warfarin: one patient's INR rose above 12.3 a week after her dose was doubled, a probable interaction [1].
- Added to statins, high-dose niacin did not cut cardiovascular events and caused more serious side effects, including disturbed diabetes control, infections and bleeding [2][3].
- Too much vitamin B6 can damage nerves, reported even with a 6 mg multivitamin. Health Canada found a possible link to nerve damage at 10 mg a day or more, and Europe's upper limit is 12 mg a day [4][5][6].
- Biotin can make blood tests read falsely high or low, including thyroid tests (even at over-the-counter doses of 5 to 20 mg a day) and the troponin test for heart attack; an FDA alert warned this can lead to wrong diagnoses and even death. Stop biotin before blood tests and tell your doctor and the lab [7][8][9][124][125].
- Large amounts of folic acid can correct the anemia of B12 deficiency but not its nerve damage, which may hide the deficiency [10].
- In diabetic kidney disease, high-dose folic acid, B6 and B12 made kidney function fall faster than placebo and were linked to more heart attacks, strokes and deaths [11].
- High-dose nicotinic acid can injure the liver, more often in extended-release form [12][13].
- Long-term metformin is linked to low B12, so ask your doctor about B12 testing [14].

## 1. Overview

The B vitamins are a family of eight water-soluble nutrients that act as coenzymes in energy metabolism, the making of DNA and neurotransmitters, and red blood cell formation [15]. The body absorbs large doses of vitamin B6 well but quickly removes most of it in the urine [16], so regular intake from food or supplements is needed.

The eight B vitamins are:

- **Thiamine (B1)**: energy metabolism and nerve function
- **Riboflavin (B2)**: energy production
- **Niacin (B3)**: the source of NAD, needed by more than 400 enzymes
- **Pantothenic acid (B5)**: coenzyme A production
- **Pyridoxine (B6)**: amino acid metabolism and neurotransmitter synthesis
- **Biotin (B7)**: fatty acid synthesis and glucose production
- **Folate (B9)**: cell division and DNA synthesis
- **Cobalamin (B12)**: myelin, red blood cells and DNA synthesis

These vitamins work together. B6, B12 and folate all take part in homocysteine metabolism, and the enzymes that turn tryptophan into niacin depend on riboflavin, B6 and iron [16][12]. Deficiency of one B vitamin often comes with deficiency of others, particularly with poor diet, alcohol use disorder, malabsorption (celiac disease, Crohn's disease, ulcerative colitis) or long-term use of some medicines, such as proton pump inhibitors, metformin and antiepileptic drugs [16][17][10].

A B complex can fill a gap in the diet; taking far more than you need brings risk without proven benefit. The strongest warning comes from a large US cohort: men who took B6 or B12 on its own, rather than in a multivitamin, were linked to roughly 30 to 40 percent more lung cancer. At the highest doses the risk almost doubled (B6 more than 20 mg a day: hazard ratio 1.82, 95% CI 1.25 to 2.65; B12 more than 55 mcg a day: hazard ratio 1.98, 95% CI 1.32 to 2.97), and it was higher still in men who smoked; women showed no link [21]. The trials show little benefit in people who are not deficient. B6, B12 and folic acid lower homocysteine, but a Cochrane review of 15 trials found that lowering homocysteine did not prevent heart attacks or deaths and only slightly reduced strokes [18].

The weaker evidence points the same way. In two Swedish cohorts, high-dose B-vitamin supplements (about 10 times the recommended daily intake) taken on their own were linked to a 27% higher risk of cataract, and the link may be confined to younger people (under 60 years: hazard ratio 1.88, 95% CI 1.47 to 2.39) [20]. Vitamin and mineral supplements do not improve physical performance in people who eat an adequate diet [19].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### MTHFR Gene Mutations and B Vitamin Metabolism

The MTHFR enzyme turns folate into 5-MTHF (methylfolate), the main form in the blood. People with two copies of the 677C>T variant have a less active enzyme, higher homocysteine and a higher risk of neural tube defects; about 25% of Hispanics, 10% of Caucasians and Asians and 1% of African Americans carry two copies [10].

Riboflavin (B2) may help people with this genotype who have high blood pressure (see Blood Pressure below) [22]. Some products market methylfolate to MTHFR carriers. In women with two copies of the variant and in women without it, blood folate after folic acid was almost all in the methyl form, so people with the variant still convert folic acid [23]. The CDC recommends 400 mcg a day of folic acid, not methylfolate, for anyone who could become pregnant, even with the variant [10].

Brad's view is that the evidence for methylated B vitamins is weak, most methylated products are megadoses, and anyone who thinks they have an MTHFR variant should get tested by a doctor and plan treatment with their healthcare team [24].

### Thiamine (B1) Forms

- **Thiamine hydrochloride and thiamine mononitrate**: the standard supplement forms.
- **Benfotiamine**: a fat-soluble form. In 10 healthy young men it gave higher thiamine levels than thiamine mononitrate [25]. In a 3-week placebo-controlled trial in people with diabetic nerve damage, two 50 mg tablets four times a day improved the neuropathy score and pain, with no side effects attributed to benfotiamine [26]. In people with diabetic kidney disease, 900 mg a day for 12 weeks improved thiamine status but did not reduce protein in the urine [27].

### Riboflavin (B2) Forms

Supplements use riboflavin or riboflavin 5'-phosphate. The body can absorb at most about 27 mg of riboflavin from a single dose [28], so larger doses add little. Brad notes that excess riboflavin turns urine bright yellow [29].

### Niacin (B3) Forms

- **Nicotinic acid (niacin)**: 30 to 50 mg or more typically causes flushing [12]. At high doses it lowers cholesterol, but added to statins it has not reduced cardiovascular events [2][3].
- **Niacinamide (nicotinamide)**: does not cause flushing and has fewer side effects, which begin at much higher doses [12]. It does not lower cholesterol [12].
- **Inositol hexanicotinate (hexaniacinate)**: often labelled flush free; its absorption varies widely and averages 30% lower than other forms [12]. In a placebo-controlled trial in 23 people with primary Raynaud's disease, 4 g a day during cold weather led to shorter and fewer attacks [30].
- **Nicotinamide riboside (NR)**: an NAD+ precursor sold as a supplement but not labelled as a niacin source [12]. Its evidence is covered in the NAD-booster master. Long-term safety data are lacking, and some researchers have raised a theoretical concern that raising NAD+ could help existing cancers grow.
- **Extended-release niacin**: liver damage is more likely with extended-release forms [12].

### Vitamin B6 Forms

- **Pyridoxine hydrochloride**: the most common supplement form [16].
- **Pyridoxal-5-phosphate (P-5-P, PLP)**: the active coenzyme form. The gut removes the phosphate from phosphorylated forms of B6 before absorbing them [16], and absorption from supplements does not differ much between forms [16]. In nerve cells grown in the lab, pyridoxine caused cell death and blocked the action of the active form, which the authors suggest may explain why too much B6 causes symptoms like those of B6 deficiency [31]. No human trial shows that P-5-P avoids nerve damage. Brad would not rely on switching to P-5-P to avoid the nerve damage of high-dose B6 [29].

Most people in the United States get the recommended amount of B6 from food. Even so, national survey data from 2003 to 2004 found low blood levels of its active form (PLP less than 20 nmol/L) in 11% of people taking B6 supplements and 24% of those not taking them [16].

### Biotin (B7) Forms

Biotin is sold as d-biotin. The main concern is that it can distort laboratory tests that use biotin and streptavidin. In 6 healthy adults who took 10 mg a day for a week, 9 of 23 such tests gave wrong results, some falsely high and some falsely low [7]. The FDA reports that biotin has caused falsely low troponin results [8]. Ask your doctor or lab how long to stop biotin before a blood test.

### Folate (B9) Forms

- **Food folate**: found in leafy greens, legumes and many other foods. Only about 50% of folate in food is absorbed, against at least 85% of folic acid taken with food [10].
- **Folic acid**: the synthetic form used in supplements and fortified foods; taken on an empty stomach, nearly all of it is absorbed [10]. The US began requiring folic acid in enriched grain products in 1998 [10].
- **L-5-methyltetrahydrofolate (methylfolate, 5-MTHF)**: the main form in the blood. It is absorbed as well as or better than folic acid [10]. In a 12-week trial in 142 Malaysian women, plasma folate rose more with 1.13 mg of methylfolate a day than with 1 mg of folic acid, but both lowered homocysteine equally [32]. In heart patients, a single dose of methylfolate made of the natural and the unnatural isomer was better absorbed than folic acid, but the unnatural isomer was still detectable a week later, and the authors could not exclude harm from storing it [33]. In a pregnancy trial, a methylfolate prenatal vitamin kept folate status equal to folic acid and left less unmetabolised folic acid in the mother's blood; what this means for the baby is unknown, and some of the authors worked for Ritual, a supplement maker [34].
- **Folinic acid (leucovorin)**: a reduced form also used in medicine.

Folate labels use dietary folate equivalents (DFE), which give folic acid extra weight: 1 mcg DFE equals 1 mcg of food folate or 0.6 mcg of folic acid taken with food. So the upper limit for folic acid, 1,000 mcg, equals 1,667 mcg DFE [10]. Conversion factors for methylfolate have not been formally set [10].

### Vitamin B12 Forms

- **Cyanocobalamin**: the most common supplement form [17]. In people with kidney failure, high-dose cyanocobalamin can lead to cyanide building up, and one review suggests methylcobalamin for people with impaired kidney function [35].
- **Methylcobalamin**: marketed as the "active" form. A review concluded that methylcobalamin and adenosylcobalamin supplements are unlikely to be better than cyanocobalamin [36]. In 42 Romanian vegans, those taking cyanocobalamin had higher levels of active B12 (holotranscobalamin) than those taking methylcobalamin [37].
- **Hydroxocobalamin**: in healthy adults, cyanocobalamin raised active B12 more than twice as much as the same dose of hydroxocobalamin [38].
- **Adenosylcobalamin**: the other active coenzyme form.

NIH notes that no evidence shows absorption rates of B12 in supplements vary by form [17]. Absorption falls steeply once the intrinsic-factor route is full, at about 1 to 2 mcg: only about 2% of a 500 mcg dose and 1.3% of a 1,000 mcg dose is absorbed [17].

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Cognitive Function and Dementia

Raised homocysteine is linked to dementia, and B6, B12 and folate are needed to break it down [17]. Across 11 trials in about 22,000 people, B vitamins lowered homocysteine but did not change cognitive function [39].

**B vitamins and omega-3 (VITACOG):** In the VITACOG trial, 266 people aged 70 or older with mild cognitive impairment took folic acid 0.8 mg, vitamin B6 20 mg and vitamin B12 0.5 mg, or placebo, daily for 2 years [40][41]. The vitamins helped cognition in those whose homocysteine at the start was above the median of 11.3 µmol/L [42], and slowed shrinkage of the grey matter regions that Alzheimer's disease damages, a benefit confined to people with high homocysteine [40]. Later analyses found that in people with high omega-3 levels (above 590 µmol/L), the vitamins slowed brain shrinkage by 40.0% compared with placebo, and that they had no effect on cognitive decline when omega-3 was low [43][41]. These analyses were post hoc: omega-3 was measured, not randomised. Brad's reading is that B vitamins slowed brain shrinkage by 40% in people with high omega-3 and gave no benefit with low omega-3, a lead worth following rather than a finished answer [29]. A post hoc analysis of a 3-year trial (FACIT) in 791 adults aged 50 to 70 with raised homocysteine found the reverse: 800 µg of folic acid a day helped global cognition and processing speed in people with lower omega-3 status, but not in those with higher status [44].

**Other trials:** In older people with mild cognitive impairment and raised homocysteine, methylcobalamin 500 µg and folic acid 400 µg a day for 24 months did not reduce cognitive decline [45]. In a 6-month trial of 220 Chinese adults with cognitive impairment caused by cerebral small vessel disease, 800 mcg of folic acid a day (two 0.4 mg tablets) improved scores on two cognitive tests compared with placebo and lowered homocysteine [46]. In the Framingham cohort, people with the best B12 status from mid to late life had slower declines in memory, executive function and language; this is an association, not proof [47].

**World Health Organization:** As an intervention to reduce the risk of cognitive decline or dementia, WHO's July 2026 guidelines do not recommend B vitamins, vitamin E, omega-3 or multivitamins in the absence of a diagnosed deficiency, because there is no evidence of benefits that outweigh unexpected harmful effects [48]. Brad describes this as WHO recommending against them, and notes that the guideline does not mention the COSMOS trial and did not reassess supplements [29].

**B12 deficiency and reversible symptoms:** B12 deficiency can cause dementia, and numbness and tingling in the hands and feet, and these nerve symptoms can appear without anemia, so early diagnosis matters to avoid permanent damage [17]. B12 deficiency has been mistaken for progressive multiple sclerosis: one woman about to start a multiple sclerosis drug improved quickly and dramatically once given B12 by injection [127]. In another case, pernicious anemia went undiagnosed for 5 years while a patient's thinking and daily function declined, and the patient recovered remarkably after treatment [128]. A B12 result in the normal range does not always rule out deficiency: most labs call a level below 200 or 250 pg/mL low, and NIH reports expert advice to also check methylmalonic acid (MMA), the most sensitive marker, when serum B12 is between 150 and 399 pg/mL [17]. In a study without a placebo group, 39 people with B12 deficiency and cognitive impairment improved on a cognitive screening test (MMSE, from 20.5 to 22.9) after a median of 56 days of B12 [49].

**Warning, high folate with low B12:** In older US adults with low B12 status, a high blood folate level (above 59 nmol/L, the 80th percentile) was linked to anemia and cognitive impairment [50]. In UK Biobank participants, taking folate or folic acid alone was linked to a higher risk of Alzheimer's disease (HR 1.34) and vascular dementia (HR 1.61), but taking it together with other B vitamins was not [51].

### Depression and Mood

**Methylfolate as antidepressant add-on:** In two trials in people with major depression who had responded poorly to an SSRI, adding 15 mg a day of L-methylfolate worked better than placebo (about six people treated for one extra response), while 7.5 mg a day did not; side effects were no more common than with placebo [52]. Agitation and mania have been reported with L-methylfolate, so take it only under a doctor's care.

**B vitamins and mood:** Several trials of B6, B12 or folic acid, including trials in older men, in women of various ages and in people with raised methylmalonic acid, found no effect on depression or mood [53][54][55].

**Vitamin B6 and depression:** B6 deficiency can cause depression and confusion [16].

### Stroke Prevention

In a trial in China of adults with hypertension, combining 0.8 mg of folic acid with the blood pressure drug enalapril lowered the risk of a first stroke over a median of 4.5 years: 2.7% of the folic acid group had a stroke, against 3.4% on enalapril alone (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.68 to 0.93) [56]. The authors note that the results fit with a benefit of folate in people with hypertension and low starting folate levels [56]. A meta-analysis found that B vitamins reduced strokes mainly in trials without folic acid fortification of cereals [57], so the result may not apply to countries that fortify.

### Cardiovascular Disease and Homocysteine

A Cochrane review of 15 trials in 71,422 people found that lowering homocysteine with B vitamins did not reduce heart attacks (RR 1.02, 95% CI 0.95 to 1.10), though it was linked to slightly fewer strokes (RR 0.90, 95% CI 0.82 to 0.99) [18].

**Concerning findings in kidney disease:** In a trial of people with diabetic kidney disease, a tablet of folic acid (2.5 mg a day), B6 (25 mg a day) and B12 (1 mg a day) made kidney function decline faster than placebo by 36 months, and heart attack, stroke, revascularization or death occurred more often (HR 2.0, 95% CI 1.0 to 4.0) [11].

**Niacin breakdown products and cardiovascular risk:** The body breaks excess niacin down into two end products, 2PY and 4PY. In heart patients, higher blood levels of both were linked to more major cardiovascular events over 3 years, and in mice 4PY made blood vessel walls more inflamed [58]. This is an association, not proof of harm.

### Blood Pressure

**Riboflavin (B2) and MTHFR-related hypertension:** People with the MTHFR 677TT genotype tend to have higher blood pressure. In 31 heart patients with this genotype, riboflavin 1.6 mg a day for 16 weeks lowered systolic blood pressure by 9.2 mmHg and diastolic by 6.0 mmHg on average [22].

**Niacin and blood pressure:** In a cohort of 12,243 Chinese adults, dietary niacin had a J-shaped link with new high blood pressure: risk fell as intake rose to 15.6 mg a day and rose with intake beyond it [59].

### Cholesterol and Lipid Management (Niacin)

At drug doses, nicotinic acid raises HDL cholesterol by 10% to 30% and lowers LDL cholesterol by 10% to 25% and triglycerides by 20% to 50% [12]. However:

- In the AIM-HIGH trial of 3,414 people with heart disease on statins, 1,500 to 2,000 mg of extended-release niacin a day raised HDL cholesterol and lowered triglycerides but gave no extra clinical benefit, and the trial was stopped for lack of efficacy after a mean of 3 years [3]. NIH notes that this trial also saw more ischemic strokes [12].
- In the HPS2-THRIVE trial of 25,673 adults already taking statins, extended-release niacin with laropiprant did not reduce major vascular events, and it increased serious disturbances in diabetes control (3.7 percentage points more than placebo), infections, bleeding, and gastrointestinal, muscle and skin side effects [2].
- A 2017 Cochrane review of 23 niacin trials found that niacin did not reduce overall mortality (risk ratio 1.05, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.12) [60]. A 2018 review linked niacin taken with a statin to an increased risk of death from any cause [61]; NIH describes its result as 10% higher all-cause mortality with 1 to 3 g a day of extended-release nicotinic acid plus a statin [12].
- At 1,000 to 3,000 mg a day, nicotinic acid can cause blurred vision and macular edema, a build-up of fluid in the centre of the retina [12].

In 2018 the ACC/AHA stated that niacin is not recommended as an add-on to statins [12]. The same caution may apply to red yeast rice, which contains natural statin compounds. Brad would avoid high-dose niacin [62].

### Neuropathy and Nerve Health

**Vitamin B12 deficiency:** B12 deficiency can cause numbness and tingling in the hands and feet, sometimes without anemia [17]. It can also damage the spinal cord (subacute combined degeneration). A 53-year-old woman with a week of unsteady walking and falls had loss of myelin on her spinal MRI, and B12 injections led to significant recovery in her walking [126].

**Diabetic neuropathy (B12):** In a 1-year placebo-controlled trial of 90 people with type 2 diabetes and nerve damage who had taken metformin for at least four years, 1,000 mcg of methylcobalamin a day raised B12 levels and improved nerve conduction, pain and quality of life [63]. In a 16-week trial in 35 people with diabetic neuropathy and low B12, 2,000 mcg worked no better than 1,000 mcg and was linked to a decline in kidney function (estimated GFR) [64].

**Warning, high-dose B6 causes neuropathy:** Too much pyridoxine can cause the same nerve damage that B6 deficiency produces. Taking 1 to 6 g of pyridoxine a day for 12 to 40 months can cause severe, progressive sensory neuropathy, and reports describe nerve damage at doses below 500 mg a day [16]. A 73-year-old man with progressive neuropathy had a high blood B6 level while taking only a daily multivitamin with 6 mg of B6 [4]. Health Canada found a possible link between B6 natural health products and nerve damage at 10 mg a day or more [5]. The European Food Safety Authority set an upper limit of 12 mg a day for adults, including pregnant and breastfeeding women [6]. Symptoms usually stop if the supplement is stopped as soon as they appear [16][65], but some case reports describe recovery that was slow or incomplete.

### Neural Tube Defects and Pregnancy

Enough folic acid around conception prevents a large share of neural tube defects such as spina bifida [10]. US folic acid fortification is estimated to spare about 1,326 births a year (95% CI 1,122 to 1,531) from a neural tube defect [66].

- Women who could become pregnant should get 400 mcg of folic acid a day from supplements, fortified foods or both, on top of the folate in their diet [10]. The pregnancy RDA is 600 mcg DFE [10].
- Two observational studies link folic acid around pregnancy with less autism in children. In 45,300 Israeli children, maternal folic acid or multivitamin use was linked to a lower risk of autism spectrum disorder, both before pregnancy (RR 0.39) and during it (RR 0.27) [68]; in a Norwegian cohort of 85,176 children, folic acid around the start of pregnancy was linked to a lower risk of autistic disorder (OR 0.61) [67].

**Caution against excessive folate in pregnancy:** In a Chinese cohort, women who used 800 mcg a day or more of folic acid had more gestational hypertension (risk ratio 1.32, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.64) [70]. In a Spanish birth cohort, taking 1,000 mcg a day or more around conception was linked to lower scores on several tests of child development at age 4 to 5 [69].

### Skin Cancer Prevention (Niacin)

In an Australian placebo-controlled trial of 386 people who had had at least two non-melanoma skin cancers in the previous 5 years, nicotinamide 500 mg twice daily for 12 months lowered the rate of new non-melanoma skin cancers by 23%. The benefit did not last after people stopped taking it [71]. In a US veterans cohort of 33,822 people, taking 500 mg twice daily for longer than 30 days was linked to a 14% lower skin cancer risk overall and a 54% lower risk when started after a first skin cancer, with smaller benefits when started later [72]. In 13,108 adults from two US cohorts, nicotinamide use was not linked to more major cardiovascular events [73].

### Parkinson's Disease (Niacin)

In a small study, low-dose niacin shifted immune cells toward an anti-inflammatory profile in people with Parkinson's disease, along with better quality-of-life scores [74]. This is early research. The NOPARK trial of nicotinamide riboside, a related B3 compound, is covered in the NAD-booster master.

### Parkinson's Disease and Vitamin B12/B6

Pyridoxine (B6) can reverse the benefit of levodopa taken on its own; in seven patients also taking a dopa decarboxylase inhibitor, the class that includes carbidopa, pyridoxine made no difference [75].

### Acne and Pantothenic Acid

A placebo-controlled trial in 41 adults with mild to moderate facial acne found that a pantothenic acid-based supplement reduced total lesions, inflammatory lesions and the effect of acne on quality of life after 12 weeks. The authors called for further randomized trials [76].

### Rheumatoid Arthritis (Pantothenic Acid)

There is no good public evidence on this yet.

### Migraine Prevention (Riboflavin)

In a 3-month trial in 55 people with migraine, riboflavin 400 mg a day halved attacks in 59% of those taking it, against 15% on placebo [77]. A systematic review concluded that riboflavin reduced migraine frequency in adults [78]. This dose is far above the recommended intake; Brad notes that no upper limit has been set for riboflavin [79].

### Hearing Loss

There is no good public evidence on this yet.

### Glaucoma (Nicotinamide)

In a small 2-month trial in people with treated open-angle glaucoma, nicotinamide with pyruvate improved more visual field test points than placebo [80]. That trial used 1,000 to 3,000 mg of nicotinamide a day [80], and at 3,000 mg a day nicotinamide can cause nausea, vomiting and signs of liver toxicity [12]. High-dose nicotinamide is not advised outside clinical trials or in people with liver disease. Ask your eye doctor before trying it.

### Brittle Nails and Hair (Biotin)

In one clinic, 38% of women complaining of hair loss were deficient in biotin, and the author advises against giving biotin for hair loss unless deficiency has been shown [82]. For brittle nails, the evidence is a study of eight people measured before and after biotin treatment: nail thickness rose by 25% and splitting fell [81].

### Multiple Sclerosis (Biotin)

The larger of two trials, in 642 people with progressive multiple sclerosis, found that high-dose biotin (100 mg three times a day) did not improve disability or walking speed, and its authors concluded that high-dose biotin cannot be recommended for progressive multiple sclerosis, partly because it distorts lab tests [84]. It was funded by MedDay, the maker of the high-dose biotin product [84]. An earlier trial of 154 people had reported that the same treatment reversed disability in 13 (12.6%) of those treated and in none on placebo [83].

### Fibromyalgia (Thiamine)

There is no good public evidence on this yet.

### POTS (Thiamine)

In a review of 65 people with postural tachycardia syndrome (POTS), 6% were deficient in thiamine, and one of the four deficient patients improved markedly after taking oral thiamine [85].

### Fertility (Folate and Zinc)

Supplements for male fertility often contain folic acid and zinc on the strength of limited evidence. In a large US trial of couples planning infertility treatment, the men took 5 mg of folic acid and 30 mg of zinc, or placebo, daily for 6 months; the supplements did not improve semen quality or live birth rates, and slightly increased DNA damage in sperm [86].

### Muscle Cramps (B-Complex)

There is no good public evidence on this yet.

### Dosing by Condition

The doses below come from trials or from treating specific conditions, often under medical supervision; they are not everyday doses. Brad's view is to reach the recommended intake and avoid megadoses [29].

**Thiamine for heart failure on long-term furosemide:** In a small study, a 7-day course of intravenous thiamine, 100 mg twice daily, corrected thiamine deficiency in six patients with heart failure, and heart function improved in four of five who had an echocardiogram [87]. Whether oral thiamine prevents the deficiency has not been settled.

**Benfotiamine for diabetic neuropathy:** The BEDIP trial gave two 50 mg tablets four times a day for 3 weeks [26].

**Riboflavin for migraine prevention:** 400 mg a day [77].

**Riboflavin for MTHFR-related hypertension:** 1.6 mg a day [22].

**Niacin for cholesterol (under medical supervision only):** The median dose in trials was 2 g a day [60]. For people who cannot take statins, ACC/AHA describe starting extended-release nicotinic acid at 500 mg a day and raising it to at most 2,000 mg a day, with liver, glucose and uric acid checks [12]. It is not recommended alongside statins [12]. Brad would avoid high-dose niacin [62].

**Nicotinamide for skin cancer prevention (in those with history):** 500 mg twice daily [71].

**Pyridoxine for morning sickness:** 30 to 75 mg a day reduced nausea in two placebo-controlled trials, and ACOG recommends 10 to 25 mg three or four times a day [16]. These doses exceed the EFSA upper limit of 12 mg a day [6], and NIH advises pregnant women to consult a physician first because doses could approach the UL [16].

**Folic acid for women of childbearing age:** 400 mcg a day of folic acid, from supplements, fortified foods or both, on top of the folate in a varied diet [10]. Women who have had a baby with a neural tube defect are advised by some experts to take 4,000 to 5,000 mcg a day around conception, a dose above the UL that should be taken only under medical supervision [10].

**Folic acid for stroke prevention in low-folate populations:** 800 mcg a day [56].

**Methylfolate as antidepressant add-on:** 15 mg a day, under a doctor's supervision [52].

**B12 for deficiency correction:** Injections are the usual treatment, especially for pernicious anemia and severe deficiency, because they bypass problems with absorption [17]. In a Cochrane review, oral doses of 1,000 to 2,000 mcg normalised blood B12 about as well as injections [17]. In 26 adults with pernicious anemia, 1,000 mcg of oral cyanocobalamin a day ended deficiency in 88.5% within one month, and none were deficient by the end of follow-up [88]. In primary care, oral B12 was no less effective than injections at 8 weeks [89]. If you have nerve symptoms, follow your doctor's plan.

**B12 maintenance for vegans and vegetarians:** In vegans and vegetarians with marginal B12 status, 50 mcg a day under the tongue worked as well as 2,000 mcg once a week [90].

### Label Reading Notes

The Daily Value for vitamin B6 on US labels is 1.7 mg for adults and children aged 4 years and older [16]. Remember that 1,000 mcg = 1 mg, and 1,000 mg = 1 gram.

## 5. Dietary Sources

### Thiamine (B1): DV 1.2 mg

Good sources include cereals (especially whole grain), brown rice, green vegetables, potatoes, pasta, liver, pork and eggs [15]. For the thiamine content of specific foods, see the USDA's FoodData Central database.

### Riboflavin (B2): DV 1.3 mg

Good sources include dairy products, leafy vegetables, legumes, liver, kidneys, yeast and mushrooms [15].

### Niacin (B3): DV 16 mg

Poultry, beef and fish give about 5 to 10 mg per serving, nuts, legumes and grains about 2 to 5 mg, and niacin added to enriched and fortified foods is well absorbed [12].

### Pantothenic Acid (B5): DV 5 mg

Good sources include meat, whole grain cereals and broccoli [15].

### Vitamin B6: DV 1.7 mg

The NIH fact sheet lists canned chickpeas, beef liver, tuna, salmon, chicken breast, fortified breakfast cereals, potatoes, turkey and bananas among good food sources of vitamin B6 [16].

### Biotin (B7): DV 30 mcg

Good sources include eggs, liver, pork and leafy vegetables [15].

### Folate (B9): DV 400 mcg DFE

Folate occurs naturally in many foods, including vegetables (especially dark green leafy ones), fruits and fruit juices, nuts, beans, peas, seafood, eggs, dairy products, meat, poultry and grains; spinach, liver, asparagus and brussels sprouts are among the richest, and in the US enriched breads, cereals, flours, pastas and rice have folic acid added [10].

### Vitamin B12: DV 2.4 mcg

Vitamin B12 occurs naturally only in foods of animal origin. Plant foods do not contain it unless fortified, as many breakfast cereals and nutritional yeasts are, and vegans and vegetarians are at higher risk of deficiency, so they need fortified foods or a supplement [17].

## 6. Safety and Side Effects

### Thiamine (B1)

No upper limit has been set [15]. Kennedy's table lists alcohol misuse and obesity as risk factors for deficiency [15]. Three adolescents who did not take their supplements developed nerve damage from thiamine deficiency within months of a weight-loss operation [91]. Long-term furosemide, a loop diuretic, has been linked to thiamine deficiency in people with heart failure [87]. Thiamine can run low quickly in Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis, which can lead to Wernicke's encephalopathy [92], and a 36-year-old man developed Wernicke's encephalopathy after a 40-day water-only fast [93].

**GLP-1 drugs:** A study of US adverse-event reports and published cases found 15 cases of Wernicke's encephalopathy with GLP-1 drugs, most with semaglutide or tirzepatide, and most of them after weight loss, vomiting, poor appetite or malnutrition; 7 of 11 patients with follow-up had lasting nerve damage [94]. A review of six semaglutide cases found prolonged stomach symptoms and large weight loss before the brain symptoms began, with poor outcomes in several [95]. These are rare reports, not proof of cause. If you take a GLP-1 drug and have persistent vomiting or rapid weight loss, ask your doctor about thiamine.

### Riboflavin (B2)

No upper limit has been set [15]. Excess turns urine bright yellow [29]. Brad describes riboflavin deficiency as rare; strict vegans, people with thyroid disease and women taking hormonal birth control are among those at risk [79]. A newborn boy developed life-threatening low blood sugar and lactic acidosis from severe riboflavin deficiency; his mother had followed a strict vegan diet in pregnancy and took some supplements, but not riboflavin [96].

### Niacin (B3)

**Flushing:** The most common side effect of nicotinic acid, and the reason the US upper limit is 35 mg. The skin reddens and may burn, tingle or itch, and flushing can come with headache, rash, dizziness or lower blood pressure; taking nicotinic acid with food or raising the dose slowly may lessen it [12].

**Liver toxicity:** Taken for months or years, high-dose nicotinic acid can harm the liver, and extended-release forms are more likely to [12]. A 74-year-old woman developed acute liver failure after her usual immediate-release niacin was replaced with an extended-release form in hospital [13]. A 24-year-old woman developed acute liver failure after taking too much vitamin B3 [97].

**Glucose control:** At 1,000 to 3,000 mg a day, nicotinic acid can impair glucose tolerance [12]; in HPS2-THRIVE, niacin with laropiprant increased serious disturbances in diabetes control [2]. People taking diabetes medicines should have their glucose monitored on high-dose nicotinic acid, as their doses may need adjusting [12].

**Gout and monitoring:** The ACC/AHA recommend checking liver enzymes, fasting glucose or HbA1c, and uric acid before starting nicotinic acid, during dose increases and every 6 months, and stopping it if acute gout develops [12].

**Fainting:** A middle-aged man fainted after a sudden increase in his niacin dose [98]. At 1,000 to 3,000 mg a day, nicotinic acid can lower blood pressure enough to raise the risk of falls [12].

**Vision:** At 1,000 to 3,000 mg a day, nicotinic acid can cause blurred or impaired vision and macular edema [12].

**Blood counts:** In one case report, low platelets and anemia that had not responded to treatment corrected after extended-release niacin was stopped [99]. In hemodialysis patients taking nicotinamide, low platelets were among the most common side effects [12].

### Pantothenic Acid (B5)

No upper limit has been set [15].

### Vitamin B6

**UL: 100 mg (US), 12 mg (EFSA).**

The most important safety concern among B vitamins. Brad notes that the symptoms of too much B6 look like those of too little [29]. Severe effects are documented at 1 to 6 g a day [16], but cases exist at much lower doses:

- A 73-year-old man with worsening peripheral neuropathy had a high blood B6 level (259.9 nmol/L; reference range 20 to 125) while taking only a daily multivitamin with 6 mg of B6 [4].
- Three patients with nerve symptoms had all taken too much B6 from different sources, and their symptoms improved promptly once they stopped [65].

Health Canada advises being aware of added B6 in products such as energy drinks [5]. In a 2026 census of US supplement labels, 5.1% of adult products directed 100 mg a day or more, over half of them not sold as B6 or B-complex products, and 38.7% exceeded 12 mg a day [100]. NIH also lists painful skin lesions, sensitivity to sunlight, nausea and heartburn among the effects of too much B6 [16].

P-5-P may be safer than pyridoxine based on lab data, as pyridoxine itself appears to block the active form [31]. Brad would not rely on switching forms to avoid the problem [29].

**Regulation:** Australia's medicines regulator will make oral preparations with more than 50 mg but not more than 200 mg of B6 per recommended daily dose Pharmacist Only Medicines from 1 June 2027 [101]. Brad reports that Australia has required a warning label on products supplying more than 10 mg of B6 a day since 2022 [29]. Health Canada has updated its multivitamin monograph to include the nerve-damage risk [5].

**Hip fracture risk:** In two trials in heart patients, 40 mg of B6 a day was linked to more hip fractures over the extended follow-up (HR 1.42, 95% CI 1.09 to 1.83), though not during the trials themselves [104]. In postmenopausal nurses, a B6 intake of 35 mg a day or more was linked to more hip fractures (RR 1.29, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.59), and the risk was highest when intakes of both B6 and B12 were high (B12 20 mcg a day or more; RR 1.47, 95% CI 1.15 to 1.89) [105].

**Cancer risk:** In men, a 10-year average of more than 20 mg of supplemental B6 a day was linked to a higher lung cancer risk (HR 1.82, 95% CI 1.25 to 2.65), higher still in smokers; women showed no link [21]. In a cohort of older Iowa women, the risk of rectal cancer rose progressively with B6 intake [102]. In people treated for nasopharyngeal carcinoma, B6 intake above 8.6 mg a day during treatment was linked to poorer 5-year survival [103].

### Biotin (B7)

No upper limit has been set [15], but high doses create serious practical problems.

**Laboratory test interference** is the primary safety concern. Biotin in a blood sample can make tests that use biotin and streptavidin read falsely high or falsely low, according to how each test works [7]. The FDA reports falsely low troponin results, the test used to diagnose heart attacks [8]. Over-the-counter biotin led to wrong thyroid diagnoses in four people with thyroid disease, and the authors advise doctors to ask about biotin before thyroid tests [9]. As reported in a 2022 survey study, a 2017 FDA safety alert warned that this interference can lead to incorrect diagnoses and even death [124]. A 2026 review found that even over-the-counter doses of 5 to 20 mg a day can distort thyroid tests [125]. Stop biotin before blood tests and tell your doctor and the lab: the same review found that 24 to 72 hours off biotin is often enough for over-the-counter doses, and longer may be needed for high doses [125].

**Migraine:** A case report describes a woman with long-standing, well-controlled migraine who developed daily severe migraines after starting 10,000 mcg of biotin a day and returned to her baseline after stopping it [106].

### Folate (B9)

**UL: 1,000 mcg from synthetic forms (supplements and fortified foods).**

**Masking B12 deficiency:** Large amounts of folate can correct the anemia of B12 deficiency but not its nerve damage, so experts have worried that high folate intakes might mask the deficiency until the damage becomes permanent; concern has now shifted to large amounts of folate worsening the anemia and thinking problems of B12 deficiency [10]. In older adults with low B12, high blood folate was linked to anemia and cognitive impairment [50].

**Cancer concerns:** In a trial of folic acid to prevent bowel polyps, men given folic acid had a higher 10-year probability of a prostate cancer diagnosis than men given placebo (9.7% vs 3.3%; HR 2.63, 95% CI 1.23 to 5.65), while folate from the diet was not linked to higher risk [107]. In the same trial, people who kept taking folic acid had more sessile serrated polyps, a precancerous growth (RR 1.94, 95% CI 1.02 to 3.68) [108]. However, in a long study of US women, higher folate intake was linked to lower colorectal cancer risk after long latency periods, and high intake after US fortification began was not linked to higher risk [109].

**Peripheral neuropathy risk:** In older adults with normal B12, those with two copies of a transcobalamin gene (TCN2) variant had 6.9-fold higher odds of neuropathy than those with none (OR 6.9, 95% CI 1.31 to 36.36) when folate intake was more than 2 times the RDA (800 mcg) [110].

**Kidney damage:** Prolonged excessive folic acid intake may harm the kidneys; ask your doctor before taking high doses if you have kidney disease.

**Allergic reactions:** Allergic reactions to folic acid are rare but can be serious. They follow exposure to synthetic folic acid or its derivatives, not natural folate in food, and people allergic to folic acid may also react to antifolate drugs such as methotrexate [111].

### Vitamin B12

No upper limit has been set because of its low potential for toxicity [17]. It is generally safe, but some studies link high doses or high levels to harm, including lung cancer in men who took high-dose B12 on its own:

**Cancer associations:** In a large US cohort, men whose 10-year average B12 from single supplements was more than 55 mcg a day had almost twice the lung cancer risk of non-users (HR 1.98, 95% CI 1.32 to 2.97), higher still in smokers; women showed no link [21]. In the B-PROOF trial, older adults given 500 mcg of B12 and 400 mcg of folic acid a day for 2 to 3 years had more colorectal cancer than those on placebo (3.4% vs 2.0%; HR 1.77, 95% CI 1.08 to 2.90) and borderline more cancer overall (HR 1.25, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.53) [112]. NIH notes that the folic acid, not the B12, might explain this [17]. In women with breast cancer, taking B12 both before and during chemotherapy was linked to poorer disease-free survival (adjusted HR 1.83, 95% CI 1.15 to 2.92) and overall survival [113].

**Mortality associations:** In a Dutch cohort, higher blood B12 was linked to higher all-cause mortality (hazard ratio 1.25 per standard deviation increase) [114]. In US adults with type 2 diabetes, compared with B12 between 369.1 and 506.0 pg/mL, the risk of cardiovascular death was higher both with the lowest levels (below 369.1 pg/mL: HR 1.74, 95% CI 1.20 to 2.52) and with the highest (≥703.5 pg/mL: HR 2.32, 95% CI 1.60 to 3.35) [115]. High B12 levels may reflect disease rather than cause it.

**Insomnia:** In a study of 418 Chinese adults with type 2 diabetes, higher blood B12 was linked to insomnia (OR 1.61, 95% CI 1.06 to 2.45), and a level above 517.5 pg/mL predicted insomnia risk [117].

**Allergic reactions:** Allergy to B12 is rare, but anaphylaxis has followed B12 injections [118][119]. Among 29 patients seen at UK allergy centres for suspected B12 allergy, 18 had immediate reactions and 8 had anaphylaxis; some who reacted to one form of B12 tolerated another, and one reacted to polyethylene glycol in an oral B12 product rather than to B12 itself [118]. Systemic allergic dermatitis to the cobalt in cyanocobalamin supplements has been reported [120].

**Acne:** In five women, oral or injected B12 was followed by acne-like eruptions, which cleared 3 to 6 weeks after stopping [116].

## 7. Drug Interactions

### Drugs That Deplete B Vitamins

| Drug | B Vitamin Affected | Clinical Implications |
|------|:-:|----------|
| Proton pump inhibitors and H2 blockers | B12 | They reduce the stomach acid needed to release B12 from food [17]. Two or more years of use was linked to B12 deficiency (PPIs OR 1.65; H2 blockers OR 1.25) [121]. If you take them long term, discuss your B12 status with your doctor [17]. |
| Metformin | B12 | In a long-term trial, each year of metformin use raised the odds of B12 deficiency (OR 1.13 per year), and the authors advise considering routine B12 testing [14]. A 2026 Danish study using stricter criteria found deficiency in 2.3% of users and 1.3% of non-users, no meaningful excess, and suggested routine screening may be redundant [122]. If you take metformin long term, ask your doctor about checking B12 [14]. |
| Loop diuretics (furosemide) | B1 | Long-term furosemide has been linked to thiamine deficiency in heart failure [87]. |
| GLP-1 drugs (semaglutide, tirzepatide) | B1 | Rare reports of Wernicke's encephalopathy, mostly after vomiting or rapid weight loss [94][95]. |
| Anticonvulsants (phenytoin, carbamazepine, valproic acid) | B6, B9 | These drugs speed the breakdown of B6 and can lower blood levels of its active form; they can also lower folate [16][10]. |
| Cycloserine (Seromycin) | B6 | Increases loss of B6 in the urine, which may worsen its seizures and nerve side effects [16]. |
| Isoniazid | B3, B6 | Blocks the body's production of niacin from tryptophan; pellagra can occur but is preventable with more niacin [12]. Isoniazid may also deplete B6; ask your doctor whether you need B6. |
| Birth control pills | B2, B6 | Contraceptive medicines are listed as a risk factor for low B6 [15], and Brad notes that hormonal birth control may inhibit riboflavin absorption [79]. |
| Nitrous oxide (N2O) | B12, B9 | A patient with an MTHFR variant developed spinal cord damage after receiving nitrous oxide twice within 8 weeks [123]. |
| Penicillamine (Cuprimine) | B6 | May increase B6 needs; ask your doctor whether you need B6. |
| Sulfasalazine | B9 | Blocks folate absorption and can cause folate deficiency [10]. |
