## Current evidence position

Brad has not made a video on this topic; this is a summary of the evidence, not his view. NCCIH says there is not enough high-quality evidence to reach firm conclusions about milk thistle for any health condition, though a few small studies, mostly from Middle Eastern countries, suggest its extracts may help control blood sugar in type 2 diabetes [4][17]. For liver disease the results conflict: silymarin may lower liver enzymes in fatty liver disease, but a 2025 Cochrane review rated that evidence very uncertain, and NCCIH-funded trials in hepatitis C and in steatohepatitis found no benefit [4][23][27][30]. Trials have used silymarin, the main constituent of milk thistle extract, at doses from 140 mg three times a day in cirrhosis to 200 mg three times a day in type 2 diabetes [4][20][32]. It is usually well tolerated, but people allergic to ragweed, daisies and related plants, and anyone taking medicines such as warfarin, insulin or other diabetes drugs, should talk to their doctor first [4][21][55].

## Key safety points

- Warfarin: in one case report, a man's INR rose from 2.64 to 4.12 after he started a "liver cleanse" product with milk thistle (200 mg), and returned to normal after he stopped it [55]. If you take warfarin, talk to your doctor first.
- Diabetes medicines: silymarin lowered blood sugar in people already on diabetes drugs, and cut the insulin dose needed in insulin-treated patients [20][21]. Doses may need review, so check with your doctor first.
- If you take any medicine, NCCIH advises talking with your health care provider first, and a 2019 review urges caution with drugs that have a narrow safety margin [4][48].
- Allergy is more likely if you react to ragweed, chrysanthemums, marigolds or daisies [4]. Anaphylaxis is rare [64][65]: for swelling of the face or throat or trouble breathing, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department.
- Little is known about safety in pregnancy or while breastfeeding [4].
- Some products hold mycotoxins, pesticides or microbes, or a silymarin dose unlike the label [4][7].
- The most common side effects are digestive: bloating, nausea and gas [4].

## 1. Overview

Milk thistle (Silybum marianum) is a member of the Asteraceae (daisy) family with a 2,000-year history of medicinal use [5]. NCCIH describes it as a tall plant with large purple flowers, native to Europe, used historically for liver disorders and to increase breast milk [4]. Today it is sold as a dietary supplement and promoted for liver disorders, diabetes and other conditions [4].

The fruits (often called seeds) contain a mixture of flavonolignans known as silymarin, and silybin (also called silibinin) is its main component [5]. Silymarin also contains silychristin and silydianin [48][64].

A 2018 review describes anti-inflammatory, immunomodulating, antifibrotic, antioxidant and liver-regenerating actions relevant to liver disease [5]. It concluded that, despite encouraging preclinical data, better-designed randomized trials are still needed to show real value in liver disease [5].

In the United States, dietary supplements are not approved by the FDA before they are sold [4].

NCCIH says there is not enough high-quality evidence to reach definite conclusions about milk thistle for any health condition [4]. Results look most promising for blood sugar in type 2 diabetes, while trials in alcohol-related liver disease, hepatitis B and C, and fatty liver disease have been conflicting or too limited [4].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Seed Powder vs. Concentrated Extract

Supplements come as ground seed or as concentrated extracts. Silymarin is the main constituent of milk thistle extract [4], and most trials below gave a stated dose of silymarin rather than whole seed [20][30][32]. In a survey of US milk thistle botanicals, whole seeds carried the highest fungal levels, followed by cut herb [53].

### Testing Method Confusion (UV-VIS vs. HPLC)

Labels do not always match what is in the bottle. An analysis of 26 US and Czech supplements by U-HPLC-HRMS found large differences in silymarin content, often unlike the manufacturer's information, and differences between batches [7].

### Phospholipid Complexes and Enhanced Absorption

At customary doses silymarin has low bioavailability [14]. Binding silybin to phosphatidylcholine is one way around this: in 9 healthy volunteers, a silybin-phosphatidylcholine complex (IdB 1016) gave much higher blood silybin levels than plain silymarin at the same silybin dose [11]. Less than 3% of the dose appeared in the urine, and much of it is probably excreted in bile [11].

Silybin has a short terminal half-life, generally less than 4 hours, with either preparation [11]. Most trials in this article gave silymarin three times a day [20][30][32].

### Liquid Extracts

Liquid extracts are one more form. In a US retail survey no live fungi grew from them [53], but silymarin content differed widely between the milk thistle preparations tested in another analysis [7].

### Comparison of Key Forms

| Form | What the evidence shows |
|------|------|
| Standardized silymarin extract | The form used in most clinical trials [20][30][32] |
| Silybin-phosphatidylcholine complex | Much higher blood silybin levels than plain silymarin in healthy volunteers [11] |
| Whole seed or cut herb | Highest fungal levels in a US retail survey [53] |

### Standardization and Quality Concerns

Quality varies widely. Some products contain amounts of silymarin far from the label, and some are contaminated with pesticides, microorganisms or mycotoxins [4][7]. A 2020 review notes that mycotoxins in milk thistle supplements are not covered by European Union legislation and calls for strict monitoring [16].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Diabetes and Blood Sugar Control

Blood sugar control in type 2 diabetes has the most promising results, though the trials are small and do not all agree [4][17].

A 2016 meta-analysis of five randomized trials with 270 patients found that silymarin lowered fasting blood glucose by 26.86 mg/dL (95% CI -35.42 to -18.30) and HbA1c by 1.07 (95% CI -1.73 to -0.40), with no effect on blood lipids [17]. The authors judged the evidence low quality and made no recommendation [17].

A 2020 meta-analysis of sixteen studies with 1,358 patients with glucose or lipid problems found that silymarin lowered fasting glucose, HbA1c, HOMA-IR, total cholesterol, triglycerides, LDL cholesterol and C-reactive protein, and raised HDL cholesterol [18]. It had no effect on liver or kidney function tests [18].

A 2025 meta-analysis of six trials with 673 participants from Iran, Egypt and Italy found that silymarin improved HOMA-IR, a marker of insulin resistance, but not fasting insulin [19]. Its authors called the evidence limited [19].

For 4 months, 51 people with type 2 diabetes who stayed on their usual treatment took a 200 mg silymarin tablet or a placebo three times a day [20]. Silymarin lowered HbA1c, fasting blood sugar, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol and triglycerides compared with placebo [20].

In a 12-month open study of insulin-treated people with diabetes and alcoholic cirrhosis (two groups of 30), 600 mg of silymarin a day plus standard therapy lowered fasting blood glucose, HbA1c, fasting insulin and the amount of insulin needed, compared with standard therapy alone [21]. It was not placebo-controlled [21].

Not every trial is positive. In a 12-week open-label trial completed by 48 people with type 2 diabetes, three 140 mg silymarin capsules daily plus a diet plan did not change fasting blood sugar, HbA1c or blood lipids compared with the diet plan alone [22].

NCCIH notes that most of this research was done in Middle Eastern countries, and it is unclear whether the same results would be seen elsewhere [4].

Silymarin may modestly improve blood sugar in type 2 diabetes, but negative trials exist and the studies are small. It does not replace diabetes treatment, and because it can lower blood sugar further, people on diabetes medicines should talk to their doctor first [20][21].

### Metabolic Liver Disease (Fatty Liver / MASLD)

Silymarin has been studied often in metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), which used to be called NAFLD, and its inflamed form, MASH (formerly NASH) [23].

A 2025 Cochrane review included 17 trials with 2,069 participants [23]. Compared with no treatment or placebo, silymarin on its own may lower ALT (MD -7.21 U/L, 95% CI -10.62 to -3.80) and GGT, but the evidence is very uncertain [23]. Silymarin complexes (silymarin combined with agents such as vitamin E or phosphatidylcholine) did not lower liver enzymes, and no trial reported death or quality of life [23]. The reviewers called the benefits and harms of silymarin in MASLD unclear [23].

Earlier meta-analyses of eight trials each found that silymarin lowered AST and ALT more than control [24][25]. A 2021 analysis warned that the included studies had quality flaws and that it is not known whether lower enzymes mean healthier liver tissue [25].

A 2025 meta-analysis of 25 studies with 2,283 patients found that silymarin alone lowered LDL cholesterol, and that silymarin with a Mediterranean hypocaloric diet or lifestyle changes lowered ALT [26]. In the same analysis silymarin raised the rate of adverse effects (RR 1.98; 1.11 to 3.54), mostly digestive problems [26].

Some important caveats:

- A 55-study meta-analysis of liver injury of several causes found greater enzyme falls at doses less than 400 mg and courses of 2 months or less, and no effect in people with a BMI of 30 or more [40].
- A US trial funded by NCCIH gave Legalon 420 mg or 700 mg three times a day for 48 weeks to 78 people with NASH without cirrhosis, and found no benefit in the primary analysis [27]. The trial authors still called the effect inconclusive because many participants did not meet the biopsy entry criteria [27]. The journal later published a correction to the paper [27].

Silymarin may lower liver enzymes a little in fatty liver disease, but lower enzymes have not been shown to mean better liver tissue, and the certainty is low [23][25].

### Viral Hepatitis (Hepatitis B and C)

The evidence in viral hepatitis is largely negative [4][29].

A 2007 Cochrane review of 18 randomized trials in 1,088 patients with alcoholic and/or hepatitis B or C liver disease found that milk thistle had no significant effect on mortality (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.53 to 1.15), complications of liver disease or liver histology [29]. Liver-related deaths were lower across all trials (RR 0.50, 95% CI 0.29 to 0.88) but not in the high-quality trials (RR 0.57, 95% CI 0.28 to 1.19) [29]. The reviewers questioned any benefit and noted the lack of high-quality evidence [29].

An NCCIH-funded US trial randomized 154 people with chronic hepatitis C, previously treated unsuccessfully with interferon, to silymarin 420 mg, silymarin 700 mg or placebo, three times a day for 24 weeks [30]. Silymarin did not lower ALT more than placebo, and HCV RNA levels did not differ [30].

In a 7-day study in people with hepatitis C without cirrhosis, oral silymarin doses up to 2.1 g a day were safe and well tolerated, but did not meaningfully lower liver enzymes or HCV RNA [14].

NCCIH notes that two of its funded studies, one in hepatitis C and one in non-alcoholic steatohepatitis, did not show benefits from silymarin [4].

Well-designed trials have not shown that silymarin helps viral hepatitis B or C [4][29][30].

### Alcohol-Related Liver Disease

Evidence in alcohol-related liver disease is mixed, with an early positive trial and later reviews that found no clear benefit [4][29].

A 1989 double-blind trial gave 170 people with cirrhosis either placebo or silymarin 140 mg three times a day, with a mean observation of 41 months [32]. Four-year survival was 58 ± 9% with silymarin and 39 ± 9% with placebo (P = 0.036), and subgroup analysis suggested benefit in alcoholic cirrhosis and in milder (Child A) disease [32].

Later evidence has not confirmed this. The 2007 Cochrane review, which pooled alcoholic and viral liver disease, found no significant effect on overall mortality [29]. A 2025 meta-analysis found no significant liver-enzyme benefit in alcohol-related liver disease [40].

Silymarin is not a substitute for stopping alcohol and standard medical care, and NCCIH calls the trial results conflicting or too limited [4].

### Drug-Induced Liver Injury

Results for drug-induced liver injury (DILI) are mixed [4][40].

A meta-analysis of five randomized trials with 1,198 patients found that silymarin reduced liver injury from anti-tuberculosis drugs at week 4 (RR 0.33, 95% CI 0.15 to 0.75), with side effects similar to placebo [36].

In a randomized double-blind pilot trial of 50 children with acute lymphoblastic leukemia and liver toxicity, milk thistle for 28 days did not change liver tests at day 28 [37]. At day 56, AST was lower in the milk thistle group (P = .05) [37]. Milk thistle did not antagonize the chemotherapy drugs in laboratory tests [37].

A retrospective study of 180 people on cancer therapy who took silymarin (median starting dose 450 mg) found that, after 6 to 12 weeks, ALT, AST and bilirubin fell or held steady in 68.63, 65.85 and 53.25% of patients respectively [38]. Its authors suggested 300 to 450 mg as an effective dose, with little gain from more [38].

In mice given irinotecan, the highest silymarin dose was more harmful to the liver than the intermediate dose [39]. And the 2025 meta-analysis of 55 studies found no significant enzyme benefit in drug-induced liver injury [40].

Anti-tuberculosis treatment has the best trial evidence. Anyone on these drugs or chemotherapy should discuss milk thistle with the treating doctor.

### Menopausal Hot Flashes

One trial suggests milk thistle may reduce hot flashes [41].

In a double-blind trial, 80 women were randomized to placebo or 400 mg a day of milk thistle extract for 12 weeks [41]. In the milk thistle group, hot flashes fell from 4.32 to 1.31 a day and severity scores from 5.25 to 1.62, which was better than placebo at every step [41].

This is a single trial and needs repeating.

### Acne Vulgaris (Topical Use)

Topical silymarin has been tried for acne, but no placebo-controlled trial exists [42][43].

In an open-label pilot study, 22 Korean people with mild-to-moderate acne applied a 0.5% silymarin antioxidant serum twice a day for 4 weeks while continuing their usual acne medicines [42]. Acne grades and lesion counts fell, and no adverse events were linked to the serum [42].

In an observational study, 4,230 people aged 12 to 25 years used a cream containing milk thistle fruit extract twice a day over 8 to 12 weeks, alone or with acne treatment [43]. Mean acne grade fell by 36% ± 39% [43].

Both studies lacked placebo groups, and in both many people also used other acne treatments, so neither shows that milk thistle itself works.

### Neuroprotective Effects

A 2019 review lists anti-Alzheimer and anti-Parkinson effects among those reported for silymarin [48]. NCCIH says it is not known whether milk thistle helps conditions beyond those it has reviewed [4].

### Cancer (Preclinical)

Silymarin and silibinin show anti-cancer activity in laboratory work [6][45]:

- In hormone-refractory prostate cancer cells (LNCaP), silibinin lowered PSA and stopped cell growth through G1 arrest [45].
- Small trials have tested silymarin combined with selenium. In men with lower urinary tract symptoms (26 on treatment, 29 on placebo), 240 µg of selenium plus 570 mg of silymarin daily for 6 months improved symptom scores and lowered PSA compared with placebo [46]. In 37 men after radical prostatectomy, the same combination lowered LDL and total cholesterol [47]. These are combination results and cannot be credited to milk thistle alone.
- A 2022 patent review describes silymarin as a complementary treatment for skin, prostate and colorectal cancers and for chemotherapy side effects [6].

All cancer findings come from laboratory work or small studies. Milk thistle should not be used to treat cancer.

### Breast Milk Production (Galactagogue)

Milk thistle has been used historically to increase breast milk [4]. NCCIH says it is unclear whether it has any effect, and LactMed notes that no scientifically valid clinical trials support this use [4][64]. Little is known about whether it is safe during pregnancy or breastfeeding [4].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Standard Dosing

There is no agreed dose. Trials have used silymarin from 140 mg three times a day [32] to 700 mg three times a day [27][30]. Silybin's half-life is short, generally less than 4 hours [11].

### Dosing by Condition

| Condition | Silymarin dose studied | Duration | Notes |
|-----------|:-:|:-:|------|
| Type 2 diabetes | 200 mg three times a day | 4 months | With conventional therapy; lowered HbA1c [20] |
| Diabetes with alcoholic cirrhosis | 600 mg a day | 12 months | Open study; insulin need fell [21] |
| Cirrhosis | 140 mg three times a day | Mean 41 months | Better 4-year survival in one 1989 trial [32] |
| NASH without cirrhosis | 420 mg or 700 mg three times a day | 48 weeks | No benefit in the primary analysis [27] |
| Hepatitis C | 420 mg or 700 mg three times a day | 24 weeks | No benefit over placebo [30] |
| Drug-induced liver injury in cancer care | 300 to 450 mg | 6 to 12 weeks | Retrospective study [38] |
| Menopausal hot flashes | 400 mg a day of extract | 12 weeks | Single trial [41] |

### Dose-Response Considerations

Higher doses are not clearly better [38][40]:

- A 55-study meta-analysis found greater liver-enzyme falls at doses less than 400 mg and courses of 2 months or less [40].
- In cancer care, a dose of 300 to 450 mg appeared effective, and more gave little extra benefit [38].
- In mice, the highest silymarin dose harmed the liver more than an intermediate dose [39].
- Up to 2.1 g a day for 7 days was safe in hepatitis C, but gave no clinical benefit [14].

### How to Take

- **Divided doses:** most trials gave silymarin three times a day [20][30][32].
- **Extract vs. powder:** trials used silymarin extracts with a stated dose [20][30][32]; whole seed and cut herb products carried the most fungi in a US survey [53].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Safety Profile

NCCIH says milk thistle taken by mouth appears to be well tolerated [4]. A 2019 review found silymarin safe in humans at therapeutic doses, and well tolerated even at 700 mg three times a day for 24 weeks [48]. LiverTox, the NIH liver-injury database, states that milk thistle has not been implicated in causing liver injury [63].

### Common Side Effects

The most common side effects are digestive, such as bloating, nausea and gas [4]. Nausea and diarrhea have also been reported [48]. LactMed lists diarrhea, headache and skin reactions as mild side effects in adults [64]. In a 2025 meta-analysis of fatty liver trials, adverse effects were more common with silymarin, mostly digestive [26].

### Allergic Reactions

Milk thistle may cause allergic reactions, particularly in people allergic to related plants such as ragweed, chrysanthemum, marigold and daisy [4]. LactMed advises avoiding it with known allergy to the aster (Asteraceae) family, including daisies, artichokes, common thistle and kiwi, and notes that severe allergies and anaphylaxis are rarely reported [64]. A 2025 case report describes anaphylaxis after eating milk thistle seed [65].

If you get swelling of the face, lips or throat, or trouble breathing, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department.

### Hormonal Considerations

In ovariectomized rats, silymarin bound only to estrogen receptor beta; it had estrogen-like effects in bone but no effect, or an anti-estrogen effect, in the uterus [66]. Whether this matters in people with hormone-sensitive cancers is unknown, so they may wish to ask their doctor first.

### Thyroid Function

In a cell study, silymarin strongly blocked T3 uptake by MCT8, a thyroid hormone transporter, and silychristin was its most potent component [51]. The authors raised questions about effects on the thyroid hormone system [51]. Whether this affects thyroid levels or thyroid medicine in people is unknown, so people taking thyroid medicine may wish to check with their doctor.

### Contamination Concerns

Milk thistle products can be contaminated with fungi and the toxins they make (mycotoxins) [4][7][16].

**Yeast and mold:** in a survey of US milk thistle botanicals, a high percentage were contaminated with fungi, including potentially toxin-producing Aspergillus species [53]. Whole seeds carried the most, followed by cut herb, and no live fungi grew from capsules, soft gels, liquid extracts or seed tea bags [53].

**Mycotoxins:** in a study of herbal supplements, milk thistle products had the highest mycotoxin concentrations, up to 37 mg/kg in total [54]. An analysis of 26 US and Czech milk thistle supplements found mycotoxins and pesticides in every product tested, and substantial microbial contamination [7].

**NCCIH warning:** some milk thistle supplements contain silymarin amounts substantially different from the label or are contaminated with pesticides, microorganisms or mycotoxins [4].

### Pregnancy and Lactation

Little is known about whether milk thistle is safe during pregnancy or while breastfeeding [4]. A 2019 review cites one trial that found no problems in pregnancy, yet still advises caution [48]. LactMed notes that limited data show silymarin components are not excreted into breast milk in measurable amounts, and that milk thistle is unlikely to harm a breastfed infant [64].

### High-Dose Tolerance

In a 7-day study in people with hepatitis C without cirrhosis, oral silymarin up to 2.1 g a day (700 mg every 8 hours) was safe and well tolerated [14]. In a 24-week trial, 700 mg three times a day had an adverse event profile similar to placebo [30].

## 6. Drug Interactions

### CYP450 Enzyme Interactions

Laboratory studies suggest some milk thistle components can block liver enzymes that break down drugs, but human studies do not agree [57].

**CYP2C9 and warfarin:** in nine healthy volunteers, taking a standardized milk thistle extract for 14 days (three doses a day) had no significant effect on CYP1A2, CYP2C9, CYP2D6 or CYP3A4/5 activity [57]. Still, one case report describes a man on warfarin whose INR rose from 2.64 to 4.12 after he started a "liver cleanse" product with milk thistle (200 mg); it returned to normal after he stopped it [55].

**HIV drugs:** silymarin did not meaningfully change darunavir levels in 15 people taking darunavir-ritonavir [56], and had no apparent effect on indinavir levels in 10 healthy volunteers [58].

A 2019 review concluded that silymarin has few drug interactions and no major effect on CYP450 enzymes, but advises caution with drugs that have a narrow safety margin [48].

### Specific Drug Interactions

| Drug/Drug Class | Interaction | Clinical Significance |
|------|------|------|
| Warfarin | INR rise in one case report | Talk to your doctor first [55] |
| Insulin and other diabetes medicines | Blood sugar and insulin need fell in trials | Doses may need review [20][21] |
| Darunavir-ritonavir, indinavir | No meaningful change in drug levels | Low risk in available human studies [56][58] |
| Thyroid medicine | Silymarin blocked a thyroid hormone transporter in cells | Clinical relevance unknown [51] |

### General Recommendations

- People taking warfarin should talk to their doctor before using milk thistle [55].
- People on insulin or other diabetes medicines should talk to their doctor before starting, as blood sugar may fall [20][21].
- Anyone taking any medicine should talk with a health care provider before using milk thistle [4].
- The human enzyme studies so far are reassuring, but they are small [56][57][58].

## 7. Dietary Sources

Milk thistle is taken mainly as a herbal medicine. LiverTox notes it has been used for centuries as a food and as a medicinal herb for liver conditions [63].

### Traditional Preparations

Milk thistle seed is also sold in tea bags. In a US survey, no live fungi grew from them [53].

### Common Companion Ingredients in Liver Formulas

Milk thistle is often combined with other herbs in liver formulas. Artichoke leaf extract is one: a 2015 review describes antioxidant, bile-enhancing, liver-protective and lipid-lowering effects [61]. In a 6-week placebo-controlled trial of 247 people with functional dyspepsia, artichoke leaf extract improved symptoms and quality of life more than placebo [62].
