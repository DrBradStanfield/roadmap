The silent buildup of plaque in our arteries is a key risk factor for heart attacks and strokes. But what if you could reverse this dangerous buildup with the right way of eating? In this article, we’ll explore clinical trials that reveal which dietary patterns may help reverse plaque buildup and strengthen heart health.

We’ll walk through several major randomized, controlled trials—our gold standard for investigating these questions. Some of the trials included other lifestyle factors alongside diet, and we’ll address that. Then we’ll turn to indirect evidence from cholesterol studies that reinforces our conclusions. By the end, we’ll see a clear pattern of eating that can support healthy arteries and reduce the likelihood of serious cardiovascular events.

## **Section 1: The Diet Trials**

In this section, we’re going to look at five significant trials that examine how diet can influence plaque in the arteries, moving in chronological order from the oldest to the most recent. All participants in these trials had some level of heart disease risk, and all were placed in carefully designed groups to produce high-quality results. Each of these studies gives us a vital piece of the puzzle, helping us understand how changing our eating habits can affect heart health.

### **Trial 1: Lifestyle Heart Trial**

The first study we’ll consider is the **Lifestyle Heart Trial**, published in 1990. The question it asked was simple: can major lifestyle changes affect plaque buildup in the arteries after one year?

*   Study Participants: All had coronary artery disease with significant plaque buildup.
*   Intervention Group:
    *   Ate a low-fat vegetarian diet (fruits, vegetables, grains, legumes, egg whites, and non-fat dairy).
    *   Quit smoking.
    *   Added aerobic exercise.
    *   Received stress-management training and group support.

*   Control Group:
    *   Was not asked to make any specific changes.

Researchers measured the blood vessels in the heart using coronary angiography at the start and end of the trial. Here’s what happened in the intervention group:

*   **82%** of participants saw a reversal in the narrowing of their arteries.
*   Average blockage decreased from **40%** of the artery’s diameter to **37.8%**.
*   When blockages were more severe (>50%), the average regression was from **61.1% to 55.8%.**

By contrast, blockages in the control group worsened, going from an average of 42.7% to 46.1% \[1\].

Those in the low-fat group also saw dramatic improvements in their heart disease symptoms:

*   **91%** reduction in the frequency of chest pain (angina).
*   **41%** reduction in how long it lasted.
*   **28%** decrease in severity.

Meanwhile, the control group’s angina became more frequent (+165%), longer in duration (+95%), and more severe (+39%) \[1\].

Researchers followed up with the same group at the five-year mark. In the intervention group, the average plaque blockage continued to shrink from **37.8%** (at one year) to **37.3%** (at five years). In the control group, blockages worsened, going from **42.3**% (at one year) to **51.9%** (at five years) \[2\].

From this first study, we see that a low-fat, whole-food diet combined with other lifestyle changes can have a significant effect on plaque buildup. However, because so many lifestyle factors changed at once—quitting smoking, stress reduction, and exercise—we can’t be certain precisely how much of the benefit came from diet alone.

###  **Trial 2: STARS Trial**

Next up is the STARS trial, published in 1992. Like the Lifestyle Heart Trial, participants already had heart disease. This time, there were three groups:

1\. **Usual care (Control Group)**

2\. **Diet-Only Intervention Group****:** Adopted a low-fat diet high in fiber.

3\. **Diet + Medication Group****:** Adopted the same low-fat, high-fiber diet and took a cholesterol-lowering medication.

After 39 months, close to half of the control group experienced further plaque narrowing. By contrast, the arteries of participants in both intervention groups widened, although the increase was modest and reached statistical significance mainly in the diet + medication group. Even so, cardiovascular events dropped dramatically in both intervention groups compared to the control. The overall number of events was small, but the results hint that diet alone can move the needle on artery health.

This suggests that a low-fat diet plus added fiber can help cardiac health without other lifestyle changes. However, the previous trial that included additional lifestyle interventions saw a greater impact, leaving open the possibility that combining dietary changes with other healthy habits might be even more effective—or that a different diet altogether could be better.

###  **Trial 3: PREDIMED Study**

We come to the **PREDIMED** study, published in 2013. Researchers recruited individuals at high risk for heart disease and divided them into three groups:

*   **Control****:** A low-fat diet.
*   **Intervention Group 1****:** A Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra virgin olive oil (EVOO).
*   **Intervention Group 2****:** The same Mediterranean diet but supplemented with mixed nuts.

Instead of coronary angiography, these researchers used ultrasound to measure the thickness of the artery walls in the neck, known as carotid intima-media thickness (IMT). This thickness is a good surrogate for atherosclerotic plaque buildup.

After an average of 2.4 years:

*   The low-fat control group saw **increases** in arterial wall thickness.
*   The Mediterranean group with **nuts** had a **decrease** in average wall thickness.
*   The Mediterranean group with **olive oil** showed **no significant change** in thickness \[3\].

By demonstrating that a diet with higher unsaturated fat could outperform a low-fat diet in this context, PREDIMED delivered an important insight. But we still needed more data to confirm whether extra virgin olive oil (or other unsaturated fats) may bring unique benefits over the long haul.

### Trial 4: CORDIOPREV Trial

The **CORDIOPREV trial** gives us another look at the Mediterranean diet’s impact. Nearly 1,000 people with heart disease took part in this study. As in PREDIMED, researchers again used ultrasound to track changes in carotid artery thickness at baseline, then at five years, and again at seven years.

*   **Mediterranean Diet Group****:** Carotid artery thickness decreased sharply by the five-year mark, and even more by year seven.
*   **Low-Fat Group****:** No change was detected over the same timeframe \[4\].

These results further underscore the potential of a Mediterranean-style diet—one higher in unsaturated fats—to reduce arterial plaque markers and improve cardiovascular outcomes.

###  **Trial 5: DISCO Trial**

Our final study takes a look at a different dietary pattern called the **DASH diet** (Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension). The DASH diet is rich in:

*   Fruits
*   Vegetables
*   Whole grains
*   Low-fat dairy

It typically avoids saturated fats, cholesterol, starchy foods, and sweets. This trial had two groups:

1\. **DASH Diet + Medication**

2\. **Medication-Only**

Researchers used advanced imaging to measure total plaque volume in a specific segment of the coronary arteries at the start and after about a year.

*   In the **medication-only** group, overall plaque volume increased slightly.
*   In the **DASH + medication** group, there was **no significant overall change** in plaque volume—an improvement compared to the medication-only group.
*   For **non-calcified plaque**, which is softer and more dangerous, both groups saw reductions, but the reduction was greater in the group following the DASH diet plus medication \[5\].

Taken together, these five trials show that diets low in saturated fat, high in fiber, and often higher in unsaturated fats—combined with an overall healthy lifestyle—can help reverse or slow plaque buildup in our arteries. The early trials emphasized low-fat vegetarian patterns, whereas the more recent ones highlighted Mediterranean and DASH diets. They all reinforce that what we eat can substantially affect plaque, although the specific details (for example, how much and which type of fat) might vary.

## **Section 2: Indirect Evidence**

At this point, we have direct evidence from five major trials indicating that specific diets can reduce or stabilize plaque buildup. We’re also aware that cholesterol levels, particularly LDL cholesterol, play a huge role in whether plaque forms or regresses.

A meta-analysis of 31 studies found that treatments lowering LDL cholesterol significantly reduced plaque volume \[6\]. Another influential study, the PESA (Progression of Early Subclinical Atherosclerosis) study, noted that once LDL cholesterol surpasses about 50–60 mg/dL, plaque can begin to develop, and risk rises as LDL levels go higher \[7\].

In fact, some researchers speculate that an LDL level as low as 20–40 mg/dL might be optimal \[8\].

Because higher LDL is tied to more plaque, and lowering LDL can reduce plaque, it follows that a diet shown to lower LDL cholesterol should, in theory, help with plaque reduction. We already know that saturated fats tend to raise LDL cholesterol \[9\]. On the flipside, diets high in fiber and abundant in fruits, vegetables, and whole grains generally lower LDL.

One study in particular showed that a specific diet led to a 9.2% reduction in LDL cholesterol in just eight weeks \[10\]. Participants were split into three diets:

1.  **Control****:** A typical American diet.
2.  **More Fruit, Vegetables, and Whole Grains****:** Also reduced sweets somewhat.
3.  **DASH Diet****:** Rich in fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and low-fat dairy, while minimizing red meat, sweets, and saturated fats.

Unsurprisingly, the third group following the full DASH eating plan saw the most robust drop in LDL.

All this aligns well with what we’re seeing from trials directly measuring plaque. Diets that limit saturated fats, include healthy (unsaturated) fats, and feature plenty of fiber-rich foods can lower LDL cholesterol and, by extension, slow or reverse the accumulation of plaque.

##  **Section 3: The Best Diet Pattern**

Having walked through the direct and indirect evidence, we can now bring together these findings to outline a dietary pattern for heart health and plaque reduction.

### 6\. The Role of Fat

From the first two studies, we saw that a **low-fat** diet can help with arterial plaque. However, more recent studies, including PREDIMED and CORDIOPREV, show that a diet higher in **unsaturated** fats—particularly the Mediterranean style—can be just as effective, if not more so. The key difference lies in **avoiding saturated** **(and trans) fats** while **embracing healthy unsaturated** **fats** found in foods like:

*   Extra virgin olive oil
*   Avocados
*   Nuts and seeds
*   Fatty fish (if you choose to include animal proteins)

### 7\. The Importance of Fiber

In the trials we examined, participants often increased their intake of fruits, vegetables, legumes, and whole grains. These foods are loaded with fiber, which helps reduce LDL cholesterol \[11\]. Prioritizing fiber is a big part of why diets such as Mediterranean, DASH, and certain low-fat vegetarian approaches are all associated with improved heart health outcomes.

### 8\. Avoiding Sugar

Modern diets often include a significant amount of added sugars—from sweetened beverages to desserts and refined cereals. High sugar consumption is linked to increased cardiovascular disease risk \[12\]. 

The diets most successful in reducing plaque (Mediterranean, DASH, low-fat whole-food patterns) are relatively low in added sugars.

### 9\. Getting Enough Protein

Both the Mediterranean and DASH diets provide 15–18% of total calories from protein, which is more than the typical 10% found in many older diets. Adequate protein intake is essential for maintaining muscle mass, especially as we age, and studies show it may help reduce cardiovascular risk. But **protein source matters.**

Recent large-scale observational data over 30 years showed that individuals eating a higher ratio of **plant-based proteins** experienced a **27%** lower risk of blockages in the coronary arteries. Even more compelling, those who ate the most plant protein and had a higher overall protein intake had a **46%** lower risk of heart disease compared to those who ate the least plant protein and had the lowest total protein intake \[13\].

Why does this matter so much? Plant-based proteins come packaged with fiber and other beneficial nutrients, while many animal proteins can come with a heftier dose of saturated fat. That said, this does not necessarily mean no animal protein at all. Instead, it suggests limiting processed and fatty red meats in favor of leaner cuts, fish, or poultry—while focusing on boosting your overall plant protein sources, like beans, legumes, nuts, and seeds.

### 10\. Getting Enough Potassium

The Mediterranean and DASH diets both feature foods rich in potassium—like beans, leafy greens, peas, beets, many other vegetables, and low-fat dairy. Adequate potassium helps lower blood pressure, another key factor for cardiovascular health.

### 11\. Putting It All Together

From all of this, here’s a concise breakdown of what the research supports as the best eating strategy to prevent or help reverse artery clogging:

*   **Limit Saturated Fats****:** Found mainly in butter, high-fat dairy, and fatty cuts of meat.
*   **Embrace Unsaturated Fats****:** Focus on extra virgin olive oil, avocados, nuts, and seeds.
*   **Pack in Fiber****:** From fruits, vegetables, legumes, and whole grains.
*   **Cut Back on Added Sugars****:** Reduce sugary drinks and desserts.
*   **Aim for Adequate Protein****:** Around 15–18% of total calories, leaning toward plant-based sources.

Both the **Mediterranean** and **DASH** diets meet these criteria well, but they’re not the only options. You can adapt these principles to fit different culinary traditions. The main takeaway is to steer clear of saturated fats, choose healthy unsaturated fats, maximize fiber, and cut out added sugars. Doing so consistently stacks the odds in your favor for preventing the harmful buildup of plaque in your arteries.
