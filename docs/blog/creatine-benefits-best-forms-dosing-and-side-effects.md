## 1. Overview

Creatine is a non-essential amino acid naturally produced in the human body from other amino acids (arginine, glycine, and methionine), primarily in the kidneys and liver [1][2]. Approximately 95% of the body's creatine is stored in skeletal muscle tissue, with the remainder distributed across the heart, brain, and other tissues [1][2]. It plays a critical role in energy metabolism by facilitating the rapid regeneration of adenosine triphosphate (ATP) through the phosphocreatine system — the primary energy pathway for high-intensity, short-duration activities such as weightlifting and sprinting [2][3].

The body synthesizes approximately 1–2 grams of creatine per day endogenously, with dietary sources (primarily red meat and fish) providing an additional 1–2 grams in omnivores [1][4]. Total body creatine stores in a 70 kg individual are approximately 120 grams, predominantly as phosphocreatine in muscle [2]. Creatine is non-enzymatically degraded to creatinine at a rate of approximately 1.7% per day (about 2 grams), which is excreted by the kidneys [1][5].

Creatine monohydrate is the most common and best-studied supplemental form, with over 500 peer-reviewed studies supporting its safety and efficacy [6]. As a dietary supplement, it is classified as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the U.S. FDA at levels up to 5 grams per serving [7]. The International Society of Sports Nutrition (ISSN) considers creatine monohydrate the most effective ergogenic nutritional supplement available for increasing high-intensity exercise capacity and lean body mass during training [6][8].

Beyond muscle performance, emerging evidence suggests creatine may support cognitive function (particularly under stress or sleep deprivation), provide neuroprotective benefits, and serve as an adjunctive therapy in certain clinical conditions including depression, muscular dystrophies, and heart failure [1][9][10][11].

This article also covers branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) — leucine, isoleucine, and valine — which are often marketed alongside creatine as muscle-enhancing supplements. BCAAs are essential amino acids that make up approximately one-third of skeletal muscle protein. They are precursors in the synthesis of alanine and glutamine and are involved in several metabolic processes. During prolonged aerobic exercise, the body may break down muscle to release BCAAs for energy, which is the rationale behind BCAA supplementation for preventing muscle breakdown [12].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Creatine Forms

Not all creatine supplements deliver the same amount of usable creatine. Different chemical forms yield different percentages of free creatine per gram of compound. Creatine monohydrate remains the gold standard based on decades of research, cost-effectiveness, and near-complete bioavailability.

### Key Principles for Form Selection

**Creatine monohydrate is the recommended form.** It has the most extensive evidence base, the highest percentage of usable creatine among well-studied forms, near-complete bioavailability (~99%), and the lowest cost per gram [6][13][7].

**Creatine HCl** offers better solubility and dissolution in cold liquids, which may reduce GI discomfort in sensitive individuals. However, head-to-head studies in trained athletes show no significant differences in strength, body composition, or performance compared to monohydrate — even when the monohydrate group included a loading phase [14][15]. The higher solubility does not translate to superior bioavailability.

**Avoid creatine ethyl ester.** It has been shown to be less effective than monohydrate and may cause greater increases in creatinine, including a case report of elevated creatinine in a healthy 42-year-old taking high doses [13][16].

### Absorption and Bioavailability Factors

Creatine is absorbed primarily in the small intestine via the sodium- and chloride-dependent creatine transporter (CreaT), encoded by the SLC6A8 gene [20]. Bioavailability is approximately 99% for typical doses of 3–5 grams [20][21]. At higher single doses (>10 grams), the transporter becomes saturated, slowing absorption — which is why loading protocols split doses into 4–5 servings throughout the day [21].

**Co-ingestion with carbohydrates** (50–100 grams of simple sugars) stimulates insulin release, which enhances creatine transporter activity and increases muscle creatine accumulation by approximately 60% compared to creatine alone [22]. Taking creatine with a meal containing carbohydrates and protein provides a similar benefit without requiring pure sugar.

**Caffeine interaction:** A review of 20 clinical studies found that a single dose of caffeine taken approximately one hour before exercise does not interfere with creatine's ergogenic effects, and may even improve performance. However, chronic high-dose caffeine intake may blunt creatine's benefits — possibly by affecting muscle relaxation time or causing GI discomfort [23]. Prudent advice: avoid chronic high-dose caffeine if maximizing creatine's effects is a priority.

**Stability in solution:** Creatine monohydrate dissolved in water at neutral pH (6.5–7.5) remains stable for at least 8 hours at room temperature, with less than 1% conversion to creatinine. At refrigerated temperatures, only 0.6–1.4% converts after 52 days. Even in slightly acidic solutions (pH 4.5–5.5), only 4–12% converts after 3 days [7][24]. The common advice to "drink immediately after mixing" is therefore overly cautious for most situations.

**Creatine monohydrate powder** stored in the dark at room temperature maintains stability for years, with no detectable creatinine formation at temperatures up to 140°F (60°C) for up to 44 months [25].

### Vegetarian and Vegan Considerations

There are no plant sources of creatine. Vegetarians and vegans have approximately 20–30% lower intramuscular creatine concentrations compared to omnivores (90–110 vs. 120–140 mmol/kg dry muscle) [26]. Plasma creatine levels are 30–50% lower in these populations [27]. Supplementation produces amplified responses in vegetarians and vegans, with 20–40% greater increases in muscle phosphocreatine levels compared to omnivores [28]. All creatine monohydrate supplements are synthetically produced (from sarcosine and cyanamide) and are therefore inherently vegan [7].

### BCAA Composition

BCAA supplements designed for muscle recovery typically use a 2:1:1 ratio of leucine to isoleucine to valine. Leucine plays the most significant role in stimulating muscle protein synthesis [12][29]. BCAAs are found in virtually all protein-rich foods, including dairy, eggs, meat, chicken, fish, and legumes. A typical serving of whey protein contains approximately 5–6 grams of BCAAs, making isolated BCAA supplements unnecessary for those already consuming adequate protein [12].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Muscle Strength and Size

Creatine supplementation during resistance training consistently increases muscle strength and size beyond training alone. The effects are most pronounced for short-duration, high-intensity activities (weightlifting, sprinting) and do not extend to purely aerobic exercise such as distance running or cycling [6][12].

**Muscle hypertrophy in trained men:** An 8-week study in resistance-trained men found that creatine (loading dose of ~5.5 g four times daily for 1 week, then 2.2 g/day maintenance) combined with a 4-day/week resistance program increased upper body muscle size by 7.1% vs. 1.6% with placebo, trunk size by 3.2% vs. 0.7%, and lower body by 2.1% vs. 0.7% (Nunes, *Nutr Health* 2017) [30].

**Strength in older adults (positive):** An RCT of adults over 65 found that creatine monohydrate (5 g/day) combined with resistance training 3x/week for 3 months increased total body mass, lean body mass, and muscle strength vs. placebo (Brose, *J Gerontol A Biol Sci Med Sci* 2003) [31]. A study of older men (mean age 58) found that creatine (0.1 g/kg/day, ~9.5 g) with 2x/week resistance training for 2 months produced greater increases in leg press strength (54% vs. 35%) and total lower body strength (43% vs. 30%) than placebo, though upper body improvements were similar between groups (Bernat, *Appl Physiol Nutr Metab* 2019) [32].

**Strength in older adults (negative):** A 12-month study in 35 older men (mean age 57) using similar creatine doses in two divided doses daily found no strength advantage over placebo when both groups performed resistance exercise — both groups improved comparably (Candow, *Nutr Health* 2020) [33]. A 16-week study in 35 healthy untrained older adults (mean age 62) found that 5 g/day creatine with high-repetition resistance training did not significantly increase work capacity, muscle strength, balance, or muscle thickness vs. placebo plus exercise (Rusterholz, *Adv Exer Health Sci* 2026) [34].

**Prostate cancer patients on ADT:** A 12-week RCT in 30 previously untrained men (mean age 70) with prostate cancer undergoing androgen deprivation therapy found that creatine (5 g loading 4x daily for 5 days, then 5 g/day maintenance) with resistance exercise 3x/week did not result in greater increases in lean muscle mass, strength, or function compared to placebo (Fairman, *J Sci Med Sport* 2024) [35].

**Older adults without exercise:** Short-term supplementation (10 days, 8–25 g/day) without resistance exercise did not improve strength, endurance, or functionality in older adults (mean age 58) (Chami, *J Nutr Health Aging* 2018) [36].

**Psychological component:** A study of 15 men found that those told they were receiving creatine (but actually receiving placebo) completed the same total volume in squat press as those receiving actual creatine, and both outperformed a group told they were receiving placebo. This suggests that belief of benefit may partially account for some of creatine's strength effects (Aguiar, *Res Sports Med* 2022) [37].

**Meta-analyses of strength effects:** Comprehensive meta-analyses encompassing over 500 studies demonstrate consistent benefits for anaerobic sports and activities, with average performance enhancements of 10–15% for strength and power. In combination with resistance training, creatine produces average increases of 5–15% in one-repetition maximum (1RM) lifts over 4–12 weeks [28][38].

**Body composition effects:** Meta-analyses show creatine plus resistance training increases lean body mass by an additional 0.7–1.1 kg compared to placebo. A 2023 meta-analysis found a -1.19% reduction in body fat percentage in adults under 50. Initial weight gain of 1–3 kg during the first week is primarily intracellular water retention, not fat [28][39].

### Sarcopenia and Aging

Creatine supplementation combined with resistance training demonstrates efficacy in countering age-related muscle loss. A meta-analysis of RCTs involving 721 participants reported a mean gain of 1.37 kg in lean body mass over 7–52 weeks of combined creatine and resistance training [40]. Another meta-analysis of 357 older adults (aged 55–71) found a 1.33 kg increase in lean mass alongside improved physical performance measures such as chair stand times [41]. These effects are most consistent at doses of 3–5 g/day with structured resistance training programs lasting several weeks to months [42].

### Bone Density

Despite marketing claims, creatine has not been shown to increase bone density. Three studies of women given 1–5 g/day of creatine monohydrate for up to two years showed no bone benefits (Forbes, *J Diet Suppl* 2022) [43]. An RCT of older men performing resistance exercise 3x/week found no greater increase in bone density with creatine (6 g/day for 12 weeks with initial loading) vs. placebo (Chilibeck, *J Nutra Health Aging* 2005) [44]. A meta-analysis of five RCTs (n=193 older adults) showed no significant improvements in whole-body, hip, femoral neck, or lumbar spine bone mineral density following creatine supplementation during resistance training [45]. One study reported decreased bone resorption markers in urine with creatine plus resistance training (Candow, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2008), but this was based on a surrogate marker, not direct bone density measurement [46]. No studies have assessed whether creatine increases bone strength or reduces fracture risk.

### Memory and Cognition

Creatine plays a role in brain energy metabolism, where phosphocreatine supports rapid ATP regeneration during periods of heightened neuronal activity. Brain creatine levels are approximately 4–5 mmol/kg wet weight — substantially lower than in skeletal muscle [47]. Women tend to have lower baseline brain creatine levels than men, particularly in the frontal lobe, which may explain why cognitive benefits from supplementation appear more pronounced in females [48].

**Healthy young adults:** Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have not found convincing evidence that creatine generally improves cognition in healthy, rested young adults. Supplementing with 2–20 g/day for 5 days to 6 weeks does not appear to broadly improve brain function in this population (Prokopidis, *Nutr Rev* 2023; Avgerinos, *Exp Geront* 2018) [9][49]. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) concluded there was insufficient evidence for a cause-and-effect relationship between creatine intake and cognitive improvement in healthy people (EFSA, *EFSA J* 2024) [50].

**Selective benefits after mental fatigue:** A study in healthy young adults (mean age 24) found that 20 g/day for 7 days improved accuracy by 4.9% on a cognitive test performed after a mentally fatiguing task, though another cognitive measure (visuomotor response time) was not improved (Cutsem, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2020) [51].

**Sleep deprivation:** Evidence is mixed but suggestive. In elite rugby athletes, a single dose of ~4.5 or 9 g creatine 1.5 hours before testing prevented the declines in passing accuracy caused by sleep deprivation (Cook, *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 2011) [52]. In young adults, 5 g four times daily for 7 days before 24 hours of sleep deprivation lessened declines in verbal and spatial recall, choice reaction time, and static balance (McMorris, *Psychopharmacology* 2006) [53]. However, a subsequent study by the same group using 36 hours of sleep deprivation found improvement only on complex central executive tasks, not on other cognitive measures (McMorris, *Physiol Behav* 2007) [54]. A single high dose (0.35 g/kg, ~20 g) improved cognitive processing speed by 24.5% within 3.5 hours after one night of sleep deprivation, with effects lasting up to 9 hours, and increased brain high-energy phosphates on MRS (Gordji-Nejad et al., 2024) [55].

**Older adults:** A small study in the UK found that creatine monohydrate (5 g of Creapure 4x daily for 7 days) slightly improved long-term memory and certain intelligence tasks in elderly adults, but not attention, executive function, word fluency, reaction time, or mental fatigue (McMorris, *Aging Neuro Cog* 2007) [56]. A study of 36 healthy perimenopausal and menopausal women (mean age 50) found that 750 mg creatine HCl daily for 8 weeks improved alertness, executive control, and reaction time vs. placebo, but did not improve test accuracy, information processing, or cognitive inhibition. Higher (1,500 mg/day) and lower (400 mg/day) doses did not yield significant benefits (Korovljev, *J Am Nutr Assoc* 2025) [57].

**Vegetarians:** A double-blind crossover trial in 45 young adult vegetarians found that 5 g/day for 6 weeks significantly improved working memory (backward digit span) and intelligence/reasoning (Raven's Advanced Progressive Matrices, p < 0.0001) (Rae et al., 2003) [58].

**Mental fatigue reduction:** In a double-blind study, 8 g/day for 5 days reduced subjective mental fatigue and maintained performance during repeated mathematical calculations, with concurrent reductions in cerebral oxygenated hemoglobin suggesting enhanced cerebral energy efficiency [59].

**Meta-analytic summary:** A 2024 meta-analysis of 16 RCTs found significant positive effects on memory (SMD = 0.31), attention time, and processing speed, but no improvements in overall cognitive function or executive function. Benefits were more pronounced in females (processing speed SMD -0.87 vs. males SMD -0.35, non-significant), individuals with diseases, and those aged 18–60 [60]. A 2023 meta-analysis found creatine enhanced memory performance especially in older adults (ages 66–76) with no significant effect in younger groups [9].

### Depression

Creatine has been investigated as an adjunctive therapy for major depressive disorder (MDD), with the rationale that it may improve brain energy metabolism.

**Positive signals:** One trial demonstrated that creatine combined with escitalopram showed superiority over SSRI plus placebo, with a large effect size (Cohen's d = 1.13 at 8 weeks). Another trial showed CBT plus creatine outperformed CBT plus placebo. In some trials, benefits correlated with increased brain levels of N-acetylaspartate and phosphocreatine (Fares et al., *King's College London review* 2026) [10][61]. Benefits may appear faster in women, potentially due to lower baseline brain creatine levels [48].

**Sobering meta-analysis:** A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 trials (1,093 participants) found only a small effect on depressive symptoms (SMD -0.34, 95% CI -0.68 to -0.00), equivalent to approximately 2.2 points on the 17-item Hamilton Depression Rating Scale — below the minimal clinically important difference of 3.0 points. Evidence certainty was rated very low due to substantial heterogeneity (I² = 71.3%) and potential bias [11].

**Safety note:** Rare cases of hypomania/mania were reported in creatine-depression trials (2 out of 17 participants in one trial) [61].

**Synthesis:** Creatine shows some promise as an adjunct to SSRIs or psychotherapy for MDD, particularly in women. However, the overall effect size is small and evidence certainty is very low. It should not be used as a standalone treatment for depression, and more rigorous trials are needed [10][11][61].

### Fibromyalgia

An RCT of people with fibromyalgia found that creatine (loading dose of 5 g four times daily for 5 days, then 5 g once daily) for 16 weeks increased upper- and lower-body muscle strength by up to ~10% compared to placebo. However, other symptoms of fibromyalgia such as pain, sleep quality, and overall quality of life were not improved (Alves, *Arthritis Care Res* 2013) [62].

### Osteoarthritis

A small study of 40 adults (mean age 58) with knee osteoarthritis found that creatine (20 g/day for 1 week, then 5 g/day for 3 weeks) with physical therapy and daily exercise reduced pain by ~1 point (on a 0–10 scale) more than placebo. Creatine also slightly improved symptom severity (15 points out of 100), activities of daily living (~8 points out of 100), and ability to participate in recreational activities (~10 points out of 100), but not quality of life, range of motion, or strength (Osama, *J Clin Med* 2025) [63].

### Heart Failure and Cardiac Function

Creatine may improve exercise tolerance in congestive heart failure (CHF) patients. Phosphocreatine plays a protective role in cardiac tissue during ischemia by facilitating ATP shuttling and buffering. A systematic review of seven RCTs (n=243) in heart failure patients found modest benefits including improved 6-minute walk distance (+48.69 m in one study, p=0.005) and handgrip strength. However, effects on ejection fraction and peak VO₂ were inconsistent [64][65]. A 10-day loading regimen of 20 g/day did not significantly enhance ejection fraction, though it increased skeletal muscle phosphocreatine and endurance [65].

### Other Clinical Conditions

**McArdle disease:** Low-dose creatine supplementation (~4–5 g/day) has demonstrated minor improvements in exercise tolerance. Higher doses (150 mg/kg daily) may exacerbate symptoms [66].

**COPD:** Creatine (15 g/day for 14 days followed by 5 g/day for 10 weeks) may improve lean body mass and quality of life in COPD patients (Fuld, *Thorax* 2005) [67].

**Muscular dystrophies:** A Cochrane systematic review of 12 RCTs found that creatine (5–10 g/day for adults) yielded modest but statistically significant improvements in muscle strength (~8.5% overall) with no serious adverse effects, though long-term data are limited [68].

**Post-COVID-19 fatigue:** A small study of 12 people found that 4 g/day creatine for 6 months did not reduce fatigue or improve time to exhaustion vs. placebo, despite increasing creatine levels in muscle and brain. Some improvements in body aches and concentration difficulties were noted (Slankamenac, *Food Sci Nutr* 2023) [69].

**Parkinson's disease:** A large, multi-year RCT of nearly 1,000 patients receiving 10 g/day creatine (also taking dopaminergic drugs) was terminated early after interim results showed no benefit after at least 5 years of therapy. No detectable differences in adverse events were observed (NET-PD, *JAMA* 2015) [70].

**Huntington's disease:** The Phase III CREST-E trial (553 participants, 10 g/day) found no slowing of functional decline [71].

**Alzheimer's disease (preliminary):** A 2025 single-arm pilot study administered 20 g/day for 8 weeks to approximately 20 patients, reporting improved cognition and elevated brain creatine levels. These findings require confirmation in larger RCTs [72].

**Type 2 diabetes:** One RCT found that 5 g/day creatine for 12 weeks alongside resistance training significantly improved glycemic control via enhanced GLUT4 translocation and glucose uptake. However, a meta-analysis found no significant effects on fasting blood glucose or insulin resistance [73][74].

### BCAAs: Muscle Enhancement

The evidence for BCAA supplementation for muscle building is mixed and depends heavily on the setting.

**Around exercise:** BCAA supplementation before and after exercise may help reduce or delay muscle soreness, improve muscle recovery, and improve immune function after intense exercise (Negro, *J Sports Med Phys Fitness* 2008; Shimomura, *J Nutr* 2006) [75][76].

**Peri-surgical:** A study of adults aged 60–80 found that 20 g of essential amino acids (including 8 g BCAAs) twice daily for 1 week before and 2 weeks after total knee replacement reduced postoperative muscle loss and accelerated return of functional mobility vs. placebo (Dreyer, *J Clin Invest* 2013) [77]. A repeat study with longer supplementation (6 weeks post-surgery) showed reduced muscle loss in the quadriceps (-8.5% vs. -13.4%) and hamstrings (-7.4% vs. -12.2%) but no differences in functional mobility or strength (Dreyer, *JB JS Open Access* 2018) [78]. However, a Japanese study of 80 older individuals after lumbar spinal surgery found that 6 g EAAs (including 2.5 g BCAAs) plus vitamin D twice daily for 3 weeks post-surgery did not improve symptoms, physical function, muscle mass, or mobility (Minetama, *Spine J* 2023) [79].

**Prolonged bed rest:** A well-controlled study in 19 active middle-aged adults found that 4 g leucine with each of three meals during 14 days of continuous bed rest partially protected knee function (-7% vs. -15%) and endurance (-2% vs. -14%) vs. alanine control (English, *Am J Clin Nutr* 2016) [80]. However, two studies using single-leg immobilization (5 g leucine 3x daily) found no protection against muscle loss in either young or older adults (Edwards, *Am J Clin Nutr* 2020; Churchward-Venne, *Am J Clin Nutr* 2026) [81][82].

**Critically ill patients:** A study of 36 ICU patients found that 40 g BCAAs daily for 10 days reduced muscle loss by ~13% compared to standard nutrition alone (Wulandari, *Clin Nutr ESPEN* 2025) [83].

**Age-related muscle loss:** Leucine supplementation (7.5 g/day for 6–12 months) did NOT improve lower limb strength, balance, muscle mass, grip strength, walking speed, or quality of life in 109 adults aged 70+ with age-related muscle loss (Achison, *J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle* 2022) [84].

**Resistance training in older women:** 9 g BCAAs 3x/week (split pre- and post-exercise) for 8 weeks did NOT improve muscle strength in 30 sedentary postmenopausal women beyond placebo, though both groups improved from baseline (Bagheri, *Exp Gerontol* 2021) [85].

**Fatigue:** A study found a negligible effect of BCAAs on fatigue in older adults performing resistance training, while the placebo group actually showed a larger decrease in fatigue (Robbins, *Dietetics* 2025) [86].

**Synthesis on BCAAs:** The strongest evidence supports BCAA use around intense exercise for reducing soreness and supporting recovery. For muscle building and strength, BCAAs offer little benefit beyond adequate total protein intake (1.6–2.2 g/kg body weight). Leucine appears most important among the three BCAAs, but isolated BCAA supplementation is generally unnecessary when protein intake is sufficient [12][29].

### Other Muscle-Enhancing Supplements

**HMB (hydroxy methylbutyrate):** May slightly increase benefits of weight training in untrained individuals. A 3-week study found HMB (3 g/day) enhanced muscle strength in healthy untrained adults, with greater effects when combined with creatine (Jówko, *Nutrition* 2001) [87]. In older adults with age-related muscle loss after hip replacement, HMB (3 g/day for 3 months) with resistance training increased fat-free mass by 1.71 kg and grip strength by 2.05 kg (Han, *Orthop Surg* 2022) [88]. However, an analysis of 7 studies in mainly healthy older adults found no significant improvement in leg strength or muscle mass with HMB (Courel-Ibáñez, *Nutrients* 2019) [89].

**L-Glutamine:** Does not improve exercise performance but may reduce infection incidence in overtrained athletes (Castell, *Eur J Appl Physiol* 1996) and reduce markers of muscle damage after strenuous exercise (Cordova-Martinez, *Nutrients* 2021) [90][91].

**Taurine with BCAAs:** One study found that 2 g taurine plus 3.2 g BCAAs daily for 2 weeks before and 3 days after intense exercise reduced delayed-onset muscle soreness and muscle damage compared to placebo, although neither supplement alone provided benefit (Ra, *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 2013) [92].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Creatine Dosing Protocols

**Loading protocol (fastest saturation):** 0.3 g/kg/day (typically 20–25 g/day) divided into 4–5 doses for 5–7 days, followed by a maintenance dose. This achieves muscle creatine saturation within 5–7 days [6][8].

**Maintenance dose:** 0.05–0.15 g/kg/day (commonly 3–5 g/day) to sustain elevated levels. This compensates for endogenous degradation of ~2 g/day to creatinine [6][8].

**Loading-free protocol:** 3 g/day for approximately 28 days achieves similar saturation without a loading phase — a slower but equally effective approach [6][31].

**General health dose:** The ISSN recommends 2–3 g/day for general health benefits in all individuals [8].

**Emerging higher-dose considerations:** Recent expert commentary suggests that for brain health and cognitive support, minimum doses of ~4 g/day may be needed for noticeable increases in brain creatine, with higher amounts (up to 10 g/day) potentially needed for broader tissue saturation beyond muscle. A maintenance dose of 10 g/day has been used safely long-term in clinical studies. For bone health, a minimum effective dose of ~8 g/day has been proposed, though this remains preliminary [93].

### Timing and Practical Recommendations

**Timing:** Consistent daily intake is the primary factor for maintaining muscle creatine stores. On training days, taking creatine shortly before or after exercise may modestly optimize uptake due to increased blood flow and insulin sensitivity, though evidence is not conclusive for strict timing [6][94].

**Missing doses:** After saturation is achieved, missing occasional doses has minimal impact, as muscle creatine stores have a half-life of approximately 30 days [95].

**How to take:** Creatine monohydrate dissolves poorly in cold water. Mixing into warm water improves dissolution. Creatine HCl dissolves readily in cold liquids. Either can be added to protein shakes, smoothies, or warm beverages. Take with food containing carbohydrates and protein for optimal uptake [13][22][24].

**How to store:** Store creatine monohydrate powder in the dark at room temperature. Avoid exposure to high temperatures above 140°F (60°C) and direct light to minimize conversion to creatinine [25].

**Hydration:** There is no evidence-based requirement for increased water intake beyond normal hydration guidelines when supplementing with creatine. Creatine increases intracellular water retention but does not cause dehydration [6][96].

### BCAA Dosing

**For muscle recovery and soreness prevention:** 1–5 grams daily [12].

**Around surgery (knee replacement):** 20 g of essential amino acids (including ~8 g BCAAs) twice daily, starting 1 week before and continuing 2–6 weeks after surgery [77][78].

**During bed rest:** ~4 g leucine with each meal (three times daily) [80].

**BCAA ratio:** A 2:1:1 ratio of leucine:isoleucine:valine is generally recommended for muscle-related applications [12].

### Safety Thresholds

**EFSA and Spanish Agency for Food Safety:** Both have concluded that doses up to 3 g/day of creatine are likely safe for the general population. They advise against high loading doses due to insufficient evidence to confirm long-term safety at higher levels (EFSA, *EFSA J* 2004; AESAN, *Food Risk Assess Eur* 2024) [97].

**ISSN position:** Considers creatine safe in healthy populations at doses used in clinical studies (up to 10 g/day for 5+ years) [6][8].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Creatine Safety

Creatine monohydrate has been used safely in clinical studies in daily doses of 2–10 g for durations ranging from 7 weeks to 5 years [6][31][62][69][70]. The primary side effects are:

**Weight gain from water retention:** The most common effect. Creatine draws water into muscle cells (intracellular retention), causing 1–3 kg of weight gain, primarily during the loading phase. This is not fat gain and is generally temporary. Some users report temporary facial puffiness, which resolves within 1–4 weeks [6][96].

**Gastrointestinal symptoms:** Bloating, gas, belching, and diarrhea may occur, particularly at higher doses exceeding 10 g per serving. Incidence rates are approximately 5% for most symptoms — similar to placebo groups. Belching occurred in 16.9% of participants in one trial. These symptoms are more common during loading phases and can be minimized by splitting doses, taking with meals, and ensuring adequate hydration [98].

**Creatinine levels:** Creatine supplementation increases serum creatinine (a marker used to assess kidney function), but this does not indicate actual kidney damage. A meta-analysis of 12 studies (n=440) found that creatine temporarily increased creatinine during the first week, but the increase was not clinically meaningful in studies lasting longer than 12 weeks. Importantly, creatine did NOT significantly affect glomerular filtration rate (GFR), a more reliable measure of kidney function (Naeini, *BMC Nephrol* 2025) [99]. If you take creatine, inform your physician before blood tests measuring creatinine, or stop supplementation 2 weeks beforehand.

**Hair loss concerns debunked:** A 2009 study in rugby players found creatine increased DHT levels by 56% after a loading phase (van der Merwe, *Clin J Sport Med* 2009) [100]. However, the creatine group started with 23% lower DHT than placebo, and levels only increased to within the normal range. This finding has NOT been replicated. A subsequent 2025 RCT of 45 resistance-trained men given 5 g/day for 12 weeks found no differences in DHT levels, DHT-to-testosterone ratio, or hair growth between creatine and placebo groups (Mohammadyasin, *J Int Soc Sport Nutr* 2025) [101]. A comprehensive 2021 analysis of hormonal studies found no significant increases in total testosterone, free testosterone, or DHT with standard supplementation (Antonio, *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 2021) [101].

**Sleep disturbance:** Despite anecdotal reports, placebo-controlled studies using 5–20 g/day for 1–8 weeks have not shown creatine to cause insomnia or sleep disturbances [102].

**Muscle cramping and dehydration:** Anecdotal reports are not supported by RCTs. Multiple reviews indicate no increased incidence of cramping; some studies show creatine may actually reduce cramp frequency. Creatine promotes intracellular hydration and may improve thermoregulation during exercise in hot conditions [6][96].

**Arrhythmia:** A single case report of atrial fibrillation was associated with creatine use in a 30-year-old man (Kammer, *Pharmacotherapy* 2005) [103]. This has not been observed in clinical trials.

**Cancer risk:** There is no reliable evidence that creatine causes or increases cancer risk. A 2025 review concluded this claim is not substantiated. Some preclinical evidence suggests potential anticancer effects [104].

### Kidney Function

Creatine supplementation is generally safe for kidneys in healthy individuals. Clinical studies of up to 30 g/day for 1 year and 10 g/day for 5 years have not shown significant kidney damage (Poortmans, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 1999; Pline, *Ann Pharmacother* 2005) [99][105]. A 2025 systematic review and meta-analysis found a small but statistically significant increase in serum creatinine (mean difference: 0.07 µmol/L, p=0.03), but no significant differences in GFR, concluding the increase is metabolic rather than pathological [106].

**Kidney disease:** Creatine is generally NOT recommended for patients with CKD stage 3 or eGFR below 60 mL/min/1.73m² due to insufficient evidence of safety, potential to elevate serum creatinine (complicating diagnosis), and possible risks with preexisting kidney problems. The Mayo Clinic states it "might be unsafe" for people with kidney issues [5][107].

**Kidney stones:** There is no evidence creatine causes kidney stones. The speculation stems from the shared observation of elevated creatinine levels, but creatinine does not cause stones [108].

### BCAA Safety

BCAAs are generally considered safe at standard doses. Potential concerns include:

- Possible interference with Parkinson's disease medications
- Possible additive effects with type 2 diabetes medications
- Preliminary research suggests very high protein meals (>25 g) raising leucine levels might activate immune cells involved in atherosclerosis, though this has not been shown in clinical trials (Zhang, *Nat Metab* 2024) [109]

### Safety of Other Supplements

**Glutamine:** Generally safe. GI symptoms (upset stomach, gas, diarrhea) at doses of 10 g or more. Dry mouth, headache, and dizziness reported at 30 g/day or more. May trigger mania in people with bipolar disorder (2 case reports at 4 g/day). May cause MSG-like symptoms at 3 g or more without food in sensitive individuals [110].

**HMB:** Generally considered safe at standard doses [12].

**Warning about contaminated supplements:** Muscle-enhancement supplements have been found to contain undisclosed steroids and stimulants. Compared to men who never used such supplements, the risk of testicular cancer was 65% higher among those who had, 121% higher if used before age 25, and 177% higher if two or more supplements were used (Li, *Brit J Cancer* 2015). Stick to single-ingredient, third-party-tested products [111].

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy:** Human data are limited. Animal studies show maternal creatine may protect fetal brain from hypoxic-ischemic events. Preliminary pharmacokinetic trials (as of 2025) show oral creatine monohydrate is safe and well-tolerated in pregnant women, but interventional efficacy trials are scarce [112][113].

**Children and adolescents:** A comprehensive review found creatine well-tolerated in pediatric populations with no serious adverse effects. GRAS status by FDA. Recommended dose for youth: 3 g/day to minimize GI discomfort [114].

**Perimenopause and menopause:** Emerging evidence suggests creatine may help counteract estrogen-related muscle loss, reduce fatigue, and improve body composition when combined with resistance training. Safe alongside HRT with no reported interactions. Standard 3–5 g/day maintenance dosing [115].

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Creatine Interactions

| Interacting Substance | Concern | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Caffeine (chronic high-dose) | May blunt creatine's ergogenic effects through altered muscle relaxation or GI discomfort | Acute caffeine use (~1 hour pre-exercise) appears safe; avoid chronic high-dose caffeine [23] |
| Ephedra + Caffeine | A single case report of ischemic stroke in an athlete using all three | Avoid combining creatine with ephedra [116] |
| Nephrotoxic drugs (NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, ACE inhibitors, ciclosporin) | Theoretically increased kidney stress | Consult physician if using creatine with nephrotoxic medications [5] |
| Drugs that raise creatinine (trimethoprim, cimetidine) | Creatine further raises creatinine, potentially confusing kidney function assessment | Inform physician about creatine use before blood tests [99] |
| Levothyroxine | No documented direct interaction | Low-dose creatine (2 g/day) may help alleviate muscle pain associated with levothyroxine therapy (case report) [117] |

### BCAA Interactions

| Interacting Substance | Concern | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Parkinson's disease medications (levodopa) | BCAAs may interfere with drug absorption | Avoid concurrent use or separate timing [12] |
| Diabetes medications | Potential additive effects on blood sugar | Monitor glucose more closely [12] |

## 7. Dietary Sources

### Creatine in Food

Creatine occurs naturally almost exclusively in animal-derived foods. Plants contain negligible amounts. A typical omnivorous diet provides 1–2 g of creatine per day, depending on meat and fish consumption [1][4].

| Food (Raw) | Creatine (g/kg) | Per 4 oz (113 g) Serving |
|---|:-:|:-:|
| Herring | 6.5–10 | ~0.75–1.25 g |
| Pork | 5.0 | ~0.55 g |
| Beef | 4.5 | ~0.50 g |
| Salmon | 4.5 | ~0.50 g |
| Tuna | 4.0–5.0 | ~0.45–0.55 g |
| Chicken | 3.8–4.3 | ~0.43–0.49 g |

Data from industry nutrient reports and analyses [4][118][119].

### Impact of Cooking

Cooking reduces creatine content through non-enzymatic conversion to creatinine and leaching into cooking liquid:

- **Grilling:** ~19% reduction (e.g., lamb: 384.9 to 311 mg/100g) [120]
- **Boiling:** 27–32% loss (e.g., cod fillets: true retention 68–73%) [121]
- **Frying/baking:** Potentially exceeding 20–30% loss [121]

To minimize degradation: choose quick high-heat dry methods (e.g., grilling to medium-rare), consume pan juices and gravies, and avoid prolonged boiling. Eating cooked meat can temporarily elevate serum creatinine by 30–52% within 1–3 hours, which may transiently affect eGFR readings [122].

### Practical Perspective

Obtaining a supplemental dose of 5 g/day from food alone would require approximately 2–3 pounds of high-creatine meats or fish daily — impractical for most people. This is the fundamental rationale for creatine supplementation [4].

### BCAA Sources

BCAAs are found in all protein-rich foods. Dairy protein, eggs, meat, chicken, fish, and legumes are all significant sources. A typical serving of whey protein provides approximately 5–6 g of BCAAs, which is why isolated BCAA supplements add little value for those consuming adequate protein [12].
