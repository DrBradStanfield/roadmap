## 1. Overview

Creatine is a non-essential amino acid naturally produced in the human body from other amino acids, primarily in the kidneys and liver [1]. Approximately 95% of the body's creatine is stored in skeletal muscle tissue, with the remainder distributed across the heart, brain, and other tissues [1]. It helps supply muscles with energy, particularly for short-term, maximal exercise such as weightlifting and sprinting [1]. Through the phosphocreatine system, it acts as an immediately available energy buffer for cells and tissues with high and intermittent energy demands [2].

When creatine is metabolized, the body creates a waste product called creatinine, which is normally removed by the kidneys [1].

Creatine monohydrate is the most common and best-studied supplemental form [1][3], and there are over 500 peer-reviewed publications on creatine supplementation [4]. As a dietary supplement, it is classified as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the U.S. FDA at levels up to 5 grams per serving [5].

Creatine may also improve exercise tolerance in people with congestive heart failure (CHF) and muscle strength in people with muscular dystrophies. It does not improve memory in young adults and has shown very limited benefits on cognition in older adults, including menopausal women [1].

This article also covers branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs) — leucine, isoleucine, and valine — which are often marketed alongside creatine as muscle-enhancing supplements. BCAAs are essential amino acids that make up approximately one-third of skeletal muscle protein. They are precursors in the synthesis of alanine and glutamine and are involved in several metabolic processes. During prolonged aerobic exercise, the body may break down muscle to release BCAAs for energy, which is the rationale behind BCAA supplementation for preventing muscle breakdown [6].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Creatine Forms

Not all creatine supplements deliver the same amount of usable creatine. Different chemical forms yield different percentages of free creatine per gram of compound. Creatine monohydrate yields the largest amount of free, usable creatine compared to other forms [1].

### Key Principles for Form Selection

**Creatine monohydrate is the recommended form.** It yields the most free, usable creatine per gram among common forms and is the most extensively studied [1][3].

**Creatine HCl** dissolves better than monohydrate at room temperature, but there doesn't appear to be evidence that it is better absorbed or leads to greater muscle creatine storage [1]. Head-to-head studies in trained athletes show no significant differences in strength, body composition, or performance compared to monohydrate, even when the monohydrate group included a loading phase [7][8].

**Avoid creatine ethyl ester.** It may be more likely to increase creatinine levels, including a case report of elevated creatinine in a healthy 42-year-old taking high doses [1][9].

### Absorption and Bioavailability Factors

Creatine monohydrate does not appear to be broken down during normal digestion, and nearly 99% of an oral dose is either taken up by muscle or excreted in urine [3].

**Co-ingestion with carbohydrates:** In a 5-day study of 24 men, taking 93 g of simple sugars 30 minutes after each 5 g creatine dose raised muscle total creatine 60% more than creatine alone, an effect that appears to be insulin mediated [13]. Other studies report that creatine uptake depends on sodium rather than carbohydrates, suggesting that adding sugars or carbohydrates may not be necessary [1].

**Caffeine interaction:** A review of 20 clinical studies found that a single dose of caffeine taken approximately one hour before exercise does not interfere with creatine's ergogenic effects, and may even improve performance. However, chronic high-dose caffeine intake may blunt creatine's benefits — possibly by affecting muscle relaxation time or causing GI discomfort [14]. Prudent advice: avoid chronic high-dose caffeine if maximizing creatine's effects is a priority.

**Stability in solution:** Creatine monohydrate dissolved in water at neutral pH (6.5–7.5) remains stable for at least 8 hours at room temperature with no sign of conversion to creatinine, and about 1% or less converts by 24 hours. At refrigerated temperatures, only 0.6–1.4% converts after 52 days. Even in slightly acidic solutions (pH 4.5–5.5), only 4–12% converts after 3 days [1][15]. Even so, ConsumerLab advises drinking it soon after mixing, since creatine begins to break down once in liquid [1].

**Creatine monohydrate powder** stored in the dark at room temperature maintains stability for years, with no detectable creatinine formation at temperatures up to 140°F (60°C) for up to 44 months [16].

### Vegetarian and Vegan Considerations

There are no plant sources of creatine. Vegetarians and vegans tend to have lower creatine levels in skeletal muscle than omnivores [1]. In one study, muscle total creatine at baseline was 117 mmol/kg in vegetarians and 130 mmol/kg in non-vegetarians [17]. Creatine supplementation appears to be more effective for improving muscle size and strength in people with low initial creatine levels [1]. All creatine monohydrate supplements are synthetically produced (from sarcosine and cyanamide) and are therefore inherently vegan [5].

### BCAA Composition

BCAA supplements designed for muscle recovery typically use a 2:1:1 ratio of leucine to isoleucine to valine. Leucine plays the most significant role in stimulating muscle protein synthesis [6][18]. Dietary sources of BCAAs include dairy protein, eggs, meat, chicken, fish, and legumes [6].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Muscle Strength and Size

Creatine may temporarily increase muscle size and, according to some (but not all) studies, improve muscle strength and endurance during repeated high-intensity exercise of short duration, such as weightlifting and sprinting [1]. It is not of benefit in purely aerobic exercise such as distance running or cycling [1].

**Muscle hypertrophy in trained men:** An 8-week study in resistance-trained men found that creatine (loading dose of ~5.5 g four times daily for 1 week, then 2.2 g/day maintenance) combined with a 4-day/week resistance program increased upper body muscle size by 7.1% vs. 1.6% with placebo, trunk size by 3.2% vs. 0.7%, and lower body by 2.1% vs. 0.7% (Nunes, *Nutr Health* 2017) [19].

**Strength in older adults (positive):** An RCT of adults over 65 found that creatine monohydrate (5 g/day) combined with resistance training 3x/week for 3 months increased total body mass, lean body mass, and muscle strength vs. placebo (Brose, *J Gerontol A Biol Sci Med Sci* 2003) [20]. A study of older men (mean age 58) found that creatine (0.1 g/kg/day, ~9.5 g) with 2x/week resistance training for 2 months produced greater increases in leg press strength (54% vs. 35%) and total lower body strength (43% vs. 30%) than placebo, though upper body improvements were similar between groups (Bernat, *Appl Physiol Nutr Metab* 2019) [21].

**Strength in older adults (negative):** A 12-month study in 35 older men (mean age 57) using similar creatine doses in two divided doses daily found no strength advantage over placebo when both groups performed resistance exercise — both groups improved comparably (Candow, *Nutr Health* 2020) [22]. A 16-week study in 35 healthy untrained older adults (mean age 62) found that 5 g/day creatine with high-repetition resistance training did not significantly increase work capacity, muscle strength, balance, or muscle thickness vs. placebo plus exercise (Rusterholz, *Adv Exer Health Sci* 2026) [23].

**Prostate cancer patients on ADT:** A 12-week RCT in 30 previously untrained men (mean age 70) with prostate cancer undergoing androgen deprivation therapy found that creatine (5 g loading 4x daily for 5 days, then 5 g/day maintenance) with resistance exercise 3x/week did not result in greater increases in lean muscle mass, strength, or function compared to placebo (Fairman, *J Sci Med Sport* 2024) [24].

**Older adults without exercise:** Short-term supplementation (10 days) with daily doses ranging from 8 g to 25 g without resistance exercise did not improve strength, endurance, or functionality in older adults (mean age 58) (Chami, *J Nutr Health Aging* 2018) [25].

**Psychological component:** A study of 15 men found that those told they were receiving creatine (but actually receiving placebo) completed the same total volume in squat press as those receiving actual creatine, and both outperformed a group told they were receiving placebo. This suggests that belief of benefit may partially account for some of creatine's strength effects (Aguiar, *Res Sports Med* 2022) [26].

**Body composition effects:** Creatine may cause some weight gain from water retention. The gain may be small (about 1 lb or less) and is temporary [1].

### Sarcopenia and Aging

Creatine combined with resistance training has been studied as a way to counter age-related muscle loss. A meta-analysis of RCTs involving 721 participants reported a mean gain of 1.37 kg in lean body mass over 7–52 weeks of combined creatine and resistance training [27]. Another meta-analysis of 357 older adults found greater gains in fat-free mass, chest press and leg press strength, and 30-second chair stand performance than resistance training alone [28]. Still, the evidence is mixed on whether creatine helps older adults who do resistance training. If it does, the benefits are modest [1].

### Bone Density

Despite marketing claims, creatine has not been shown to increase bone density. Three studies of women given 1–5 g/day of creatine monohydrate for up to two years showed no bone benefits (Forbes, *J Diet Suppl* 2022) [29]. An RCT of older men performing resistance exercise 3x/week found no greater increase in bone density with creatine (6 g/day for 12 weeks with initial loading) vs. placebo (Chilibeck, *J Nutra Health Aging* 2005) [30]. One study reported decreased bone resorption markers in urine with creatine plus resistance training (Candow, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2008), but this was based on a surrogate marker, not direct bone density measurement [31]. No studies have assessed whether creatine increases bone strength or reduces fracture risk.

### Memory and Cognition

Creatine helps supply energy to brain cells [1]. One review reports that females have 70–80% lower endogenous creatine stores than males and suggests supplementation may be even more effective for females by supporting brain energy [32].

**Healthy young adults:** Systematic reviews and meta-analyses have not found convincing evidence that creatine generally improves cognition in healthy, rested young adults. Supplementing with 2–20 g/day for 5 days to 6 weeks does not appear to broadly improve brain function in this population (Prokopidis, *Nutr Rev* 2023; Avgerinos, *Exp Geront* 2018) [33][34]. The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) concluded there was insufficient evidence for a cause-and-effect relationship between creatine intake and cognitive improvement in healthy people (EFSA, *EFSA J* 2024) [35].

**Selective benefits after mental fatigue:** A study in healthy young adults (mean age 24) found that 20 g/day for 7 days improved accuracy by 4.9% on a cognitive test performed after a mentally fatiguing task, though another cognitive measure (visuomotor response time) was not improved (Cutsem, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2020) [36].

**Sleep deprivation:** Evidence is mixed, and any benefit appears to be modest [1]. In elite rugby athletes, a single dose of ~4.5 or 9 g creatine 1.5 hours before testing prevented the declines in passing accuracy caused by sleep deprivation (Cook, *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 2011) [37]. In young adults, 5 g four times daily for 7 days before 24 hours of sleep deprivation lessened declines in verbal and spatial recall, choice reaction time, and static balance (McMorris, *Psychopharmacology* 2006) [38]. However, a subsequent study by the same group using 36 hours of sleep deprivation found improvement only on complex central executive tasks, not on other cognitive measures (McMorris, *Physiol Behav* 2007) [39]. A single high dose (0.35 g/kg) taken during sleep deprivation improved cognitive performance and processing speed in tests at 3, 5.5, and 7.5 h after the dose and changed brain high-energy phosphate measures (Gordji-Nejad, *Sci Rep* 2024) [40].

**Older adults:** A small study in the UK found that creatine monohydrate (5 g of Creapure 4x daily for 7 days) slightly improved long-term memory and certain intelligence tasks in elderly adults, but not attention, executive function, word fluency, reaction time, or mental fatigue (McMorris, *Aging Neuro Cog* 2007) [41]. A study of 36 healthy perimenopausal and menopausal women (mean age 50) found that 750 mg creatine HCl daily for 8 weeks improved alertness, executive control, and reaction time vs. placebo, but did not improve test accuracy, information processing, or cognitive inhibition. Higher (1,500 mg/day) and lower (400 mg/day) doses did not yield significant benefits (Korovljev, *J Am Nutr Assoc* 2025) [42].

**Vegetarians:** A double-blind crossover trial in 45 young adult vegetarians found that 5 g/day for 6 weeks significantly improved working memory (backward digit span) and intelligence/reasoning (Raven's Advanced Progressive Matrices, p < 0.0001) (Rae et al., 2003) [43].

**Mental fatigue reduction:** In a double-blind study, 8 g/day for 5 days reduced mental fatigue during repeated simple mathematical calculations and reduced the task-evoked rise in cerebral oxygenated hemoglobin, which is compatible with greater oxygen use in the brain [44].

**Meta-analytic summary:** A 2024 meta-analysis of 16 RCTs found significant positive effects on memory (SMD = 0.31), attention time, and processing speed, but no improvements in overall cognitive function or executive function. Benefits were more pronounced in individuals with diseases, those aged 18–60, and females [45]. A 2023 meta-analysis found creatine enhanced memory performance especially in older adults (ages 66–76) with no significant effect in younger groups [33].

### Depression

Creatine has been investigated as an adjunctive therapy for major depressive disorder (MDD), with the rationale that it may improve brain energy metabolism. One review reports positive effects of creatine on mood and cognition in pre-clinical and clinical studies, possibly by restoring brain energy levels [32]. If you are being treated for depression, talk to your doctor before adding creatine.

### Fibromyalgia

An RCT of people with fibromyalgia found that creatine (loading dose of 5 g four times daily for 5 days, then 5 g once daily) for 16 weeks increased upper- and lower-body muscle strength by up to ~10% compared to placebo. However, other symptoms of fibromyalgia such as pain, sleep quality, and overall quality of life were not improved (Alves, *Arthritis Care Res* 2013) [46].

### Osteoarthritis

A small study of 40 adults (mean age 58) with knee osteoarthritis found that creatine (20 g/day for 1 week, then 5 g/day for 3 weeks) with physical therapy and daily exercise reduced pain by ~1 point (on a 0–10 scale) more than placebo. Creatine also slightly improved symptom severity (15 points out of 100), activities of daily living (~8 points out of 100), and ability to participate in recreational activities (~10 points out of 100), but not quality of life, range of motion, or strength (Osama, *J Clin Med* 2025) [47].

### Heart Failure and Cardiac Function

Creatine may improve exercise tolerance in congestive heart failure (CHF) patients [1].

### Other Clinical Conditions

**McArdle disease:** Low-dose creatine supplementation (~4–5 g/day) has demonstrated minor improvements in exercise tolerance. Higher doses (150 mg/kg daily) may exacerbate symptoms [48].

**COPD:** Creatine (15 g/day for 14 days followed by 5 g/day for 10 weeks) may improve lean body mass and quality of life in COPD patients (Fuld, *Thorax* 2005) [49].

**Muscular dystrophies:** Creatine may improve muscle strength in people with muscular dystrophies [1].

**Post-COVID-19 fatigue:** A small study of 12 people found that 4 g/day creatine for 6 months did not reduce fatigue or improve time to exhaustion vs. placebo, despite increasing creatine levels in muscle and brain. Some improvements in body aches and concentration difficulties were noted (Slankamenac, *Food Sci Nutr* 2023) [50].

**Parkinson's disease:** A large, multi-year RCT of nearly 1,000 patients receiving 10 g/day creatine (also taking dopaminergic drugs) was terminated early after interim results showed no benefit after at least 5 years of therapy. No detectable differences in adverse events were observed (NET-PD, *JAMA* 2015) [51].

**Huntington's disease:** In a 16-week trial of 64 people with Huntington disease, 8 g/day was well tolerated and raised serum and brain creatine levels [52].

**Alzheimer's disease (preliminary):** A 2025 single-arm pilot study administered creatine for 8 weeks to approximately 20 patients, reporting improved cognition and elevated brain creatine levels. These findings require confirmation in larger RCTs [53].

### BCAAs: Muscle Enhancement

The evidence for BCAA supplementation for muscle building is mixed and depends heavily on the setting.

**Around exercise:** BCAA supplementation before and after exercise may help reduce or delay muscle soreness, improve muscle recovery, and improve immune function after intense exercise (Negro, *J Sports Med Phys Fitness* 2008; Shimomura, *J Nutr* 2006) [54][55].

**Peri-surgical:** A study of adults aged 60–80 found that 20 g of essential amino acids (including 8 g BCAAs) twice daily for 1 week before and 2 weeks after total knee replacement reduced postoperative muscle loss and accelerated return of functional mobility vs. placebo (Dreyer, *J Clin Invest* 2013) [56]. A repeat study with longer supplementation (6 weeks post-surgery) showed reduced muscle loss in the quadriceps (-8.5% vs. -13.4%) and hamstrings (-7.4% vs. -12.2%) but no differences in functional mobility or strength (Dreyer, *JB JS Open Access* 2018) [57]. However, a Japanese study of 80 older individuals after lumbar spinal surgery found that 6 g EAAs (including 2.5 g BCAAs) plus vitamin D twice daily for 3 weeks post-surgery did not improve symptoms, physical function, muscle mass, or mobility (Minetama, *Spine J* 2023) [58].

**Prolonged bed rest:** A well-controlled study in 19 active middle-aged adults found that 4 g leucine with each of three meals during 14 days of continuous bed rest partially protected knee function (-7% vs. -15%) and endurance (-2% vs. -14%) vs. alanine control (English, *Am J Clin Nutr* 2016) [59]. However, two studies using single-leg immobilization (5 g leucine 3x daily) found no protection against muscle loss in either young or older adults (Edwards, *Am J Clin Nutr* 2020; Churchward-Venne, *Am J Clin Nutr* 2026) [60][61].

**Critically ill patients:** A study of 36 ICU patients found that 40 g BCAAs daily for 10 days, added to standard nutrition and electrical muscle stimulation, reduced muscle loss by about 13% compared to standard nutrition and electrical stimulation alone (Wulandari, *Clin Nutr ESPEN* 2025) [62].

**Age-related muscle loss:** Leucine supplementation (7.5 g/day for 6–12 months) did NOT improve lower limb strength, balance, muscle mass, grip strength, walking speed, or quality of life in 109 adults aged 70+ with age-related muscle loss (Achison, *J Cachexia Sarcopenia Muscle* 2022) [63].

**Resistance training in older women:** 9 g BCAAs 3x/week (split pre- and post-exercise) for 8 weeks did NOT improve muscle strength in 30 sedentary postmenopausal women beyond placebo, though both groups improved from baseline (Bagheri, *Exp Gerontol* 2021) [64].

**Fatigue:** A study found a negligible effect of BCAAs on fatigue in older adults performing resistance training, while the placebo group actually showed a larger decrease in fatigue (Robbins, *Dietetics* 2025) [65].

**Synthesis on BCAAs:** BCAA use around intense exercise may help reduce soreness and support recovery. Overall, the research on BCAA supplements for muscle enhancement is mixed, and benefits may depend on the setting [6]. Leucine appears most important among the three BCAAs [6][18].

### Other Muscle-Enhancing Supplements

**L-Glutamine:** Does not improve exercise performance but may reduce infection incidence in overtrained athletes (Castell, *Eur J Appl Physiol* 1996) and reduce markers of muscle damage after strenuous exercise (Cordova-Martinez, *Nutrients* 2021) [66][67][68].

**Taurine with BCAAs:** One study found that 2 g taurine plus 3.2 g BCAAs daily for 2 weeks before and 3 days after intense exercise reduced delayed-onset muscle soreness and muscle damage compared to placebo, although neither supplement alone provided benefit (Ra, *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 2013) [69][70].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Creatine Dosing Protocols

**Loading protocol (fastest saturation):** A common approach is a loading dose of 15 to 20 g of creatine per day for 5 to 14 days, followed by a maintenance dose. On this regimen, maximum creatine storage should occur within 2 to 4 weeks [1]. In one study, 20 g/day for 6 days raised muscle total creatine by about 20% [71].

**Maintenance dose:** 2 to 5 g of creatine per day [1]. In the same study, 2 g/day maintained the higher level for a further 30 days [71].

**Loading-free protocol:** In the same study, 3 g/day produced a similar 20% rise in muscle total creatine, more gradually, over a period of 28 days [71]. Some studies have shown benefit using a constant dose (such as 5 grams daily) rather than a larger loading dose [1].

**General health dose:** The ISSN position stand suggests that habitual low dietary creatine intake (for example, 3 g/day) throughout life may provide significant health benefits [72].

**Emerging higher-dose considerations:** Recent expert commentary suggests that for brain health and cognitive support, minimum doses of ~4 g/day may be needed for noticeable increases in brain creatine, with higher amounts (up to 10 g/day) potentially needed for broader tissue saturation beyond muscle [73]. A maintenance dose of 10 g/day has been used safely long-term in clinical studies.

### Timing and Practical Recommendations

**Timing:** Consistent daily intake is the primary factor for maintaining muscle creatine stores. In one four-week study of male bodybuilders, taking 5 grams right after workouts appeared slightly better than right before for fat-free mass and strength, though no statistically significant differences were found [74].

**Missing doses:** Once supplementation stops, muscle creatine falls gradually and returns to baseline after about 30 days, so missing an occasional dose probably has little impact [71].

**How to take:** Creatine monohydrate dissolves poorly in cold water. Mixing into warm water improves dissolution. Creatine HCl dissolves readily in cold liquids. Either can be added to protein shakes, smoothies, or warm beverages [1].

**How to store:** Store creatine monohydrate powder in the dark at room temperature. Avoid exposure to high temperatures above 140°F (60°C) and direct light to minimize conversion to creatinine [16].

**Hydration:** ConsumerLab advises drinking about 8 cups (64 ounces) of plain water per day while taking creatine, on top of water from other drinks, fruits, and vegetables [1]. Creatine increases water retention in muscle but generally does not seem to cause dehydration [1][75].

### BCAA Dosing

**For muscle recovery and soreness prevention:** 1–5 grams daily [6].

**Around surgery (knee replacement):** 20 g of essential amino acids (including ~8 g BCAAs) twice daily, starting 1 week before and continuing 2 weeks after surgery (6 weeks in a repeat study) [56][57].

**During bed rest:** ~4 g leucine with each meal (three times daily) [59].

**BCAA ratio:** A 2:1:1 ratio of leucine:isoleucine:valine is generally recommended for muscle-related applications [6].

### Safety Thresholds

**EFSA and Spanish Agency for Food Safety:** Both have concluded that doses up to 3 g/day of creatine are likely safe for the general population. EFSA advises against high loading doses because there is insufficient evidence to confirm their safety (EFSA, *EFSA J* 2004; AESAN, *Food Risk Assess Eur* 2024) [76].

**ISSN position:** The ISSN position stand describes short- and long-term creatine supplementation as safe and well tolerated in healthy people [72]. However, much of the research has been done in athletes, and some experts believe that evidence of safety in athletes cannot be extrapolated to the general population [1].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Creatine Safety

Little is known about potential long-term adverse effects of creatine, and there is some concern that long-term use at high doses (20 grams/day) may adversely affect the kidneys and cardiovascular system [1]. Creatine monohydrate has been used safely in clinical studies in daily doses of 2–10 g for durations ranging from 7 weeks to 5 years [1]. The primary side effects are:

**Weight gain from water retention:** The most common effect. Creatine draws water into muscle cells (intracellular retention), causing weight gain, primarily during the loading phase. The gain may be small (about 1 lb or less) [1]. This is not fat gain and is generally temporary. In women, bloating from water retention of nearly 1 liter, without weight gain, has been reported during the second half of the menstrual cycle with creatine compared to placebo (Moore, *Nutrients* 2023) [1].

**Gastrointestinal symptoms:** Despite anecdotal reports, creatine generally does not seem to cause diarrhea [1].

**Creatinine levels:** Creatine supplementation increases serum creatinine (a marker used to assess kidney function), but this does not indicate actual kidney damage. A 2025 meta-analysis of 12 studies (177 people taking creatine and 263 controls) found a small but statistically significant rise in serum creatinine, likely due to metabolic turnover rather than kidney impairment. Creatine did not significantly change glomerular filtration rate (GFR), a more reliable measure of kidney function (Naeini, *BMC Nephrol* 2025) [77]. A blood test for cystatin C, instead of or in addition to a creatinine test, can be more accurate in some people because it is less affected by age, sex, and muscle size [1]. If you take creatine, inform your physician before blood tests measuring creatinine, or stop supplementation 2 weeks beforehand.

**Hair loss concerns:** A 2009 study in rugby players found creatine increased DHT levels by 56% after a loading phase (van der Merwe, *Clin J Sport Med* 2009) [78]. However, the creatine group started with 23% lower DHT than placebo, and levels only increased to within the normal range. This finding has NOT been replicated. A subsequent 2025 RCT of 45 resistance-trained men given 5 g/day for 12 weeks found no differences in DHT levels, DHT-to-testosterone ratio, or hair growth between creatine and placebo groups (Mohammadyasin, *J Int Soc Sport Nutr* 2025) [79]. A 2021 review noted that no clinical study of creatine has reported hair loss or balding as a side effect (Antonio, *J Int Soc Sports Nutr* 2021) [1][4].

**Sleep disturbance:** Despite anecdotal reports, placebo-controlled studies using 5 to 6 g daily for 6 to 8 weeks, or 20 g daily for 1 week, have not shown creatine to cause insomnia or sleep disturbances [80].

**Muscle cramping and dehydration:** Despite anecdotal reports, creatine generally does not seem to cause muscle cramping or dehydration [1]. A systematic review found no evidence that creatine hinders the body's ability to dissipate heat or harms body fluid balance during exercise [75].

**Arrhythmia:** A single case report of atrial fibrillation was associated with creatine use in a 30-year-old man (Kammer, *Pharmacotherapy* 2005) [81].

**Airways, allergies and sinusitis:** Creatine may cause some reduction in airflow, which may matter mainly for people with allergies, asthma, or sinusitis. In a study of 19 young male soccer players taking a 5-gram daily maintenance dose for 8 weeks, airway inflammation rose mildly and was significant only in players with allergic conditions (Simpson, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 2019). A pulmonologist also reported a patient whose severe sinusitis with polyps worsened while taking creatine [1].

### Kidney Function

Creatine supplementation is generally safe for kidneys in healthy individuals. Clinical studies of up to 30 g/day for 1 year and 10 g/day for 5 years have not shown significant kidney damage (Poortmans, *Med Sci Sports Exerc* 1999; Pline, *Ann Pharmacother* 2005) [1][82].

**Kidney disease:** Many experts consider the risk of kidney injury from short-term creatine use to be low even among people with existing kidney problems, but long-term safety in this group remains unclear, so it seems prudent to consult your doctor before using creatine [1]. The Mayo Clinic states it "might be unsafe" for people with kidney issues [83].

**Kidney stones:** There is no evidence creatine causes kidney stones. The speculation stems from the shared observation of elevated creatinine levels, but creatinine does not cause stones [1].

### BCAA Safety

BCAAs are generally considered safe at standard doses [6]. Potential concerns include:

- Possible interference with Parkinson's disease medications
- Possible additive effects with type 2 diabetes medications
- Preliminary research suggests very high protein meals (>25 g) raising leucine levels might activate immune cells involved in atherosclerosis, though this has not been shown in clinical trials (Zhang, *Nat Metab* 2024) [84]

### Safety of Other Supplements

**Glutamine:** Generally safe. GI symptoms (upset stomach, gas, diarrhea) at doses of 10 g or more. Dry mouth, headache, and dizziness reported at 30 g/day or more. May trigger mania in people with bipolar disorder (2 case reports at up to 4 g/day). May cause MSG-like symptoms at 3 g or more without food in sensitive individuals [85][68].

**Warning about contaminated supplements:** Muscle-enhancement supplements have been found to contain undisclosed steroids and stimulants. Compared to men who never used such supplements, the risk of testicular cancer was 65% higher among those who had, 121% higher if used before age 25, and 177% higher if two or more supplements were used (Li, *Brit J Cancer* 2015). Stick to single-ingredient, third-party-tested products [86][6].

### Special Populations

**Children and adolescents:** A comprehensive review found creatine well-tolerated in pediatric populations with no serious adverse effects. GRAS status by FDA. Recommended dose for youth: 3 g/day to minimize GI discomfort [87].

**Perimenopause and menopause:** One review suggests post-menopausal women may gain muscle size and function at high doses of creatine (0.3 g for each kilogram of body weight per day) [32].

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Creatine Interactions

| Interacting Substance | Concern | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Caffeine (chronic high-dose) | May blunt creatine's ergogenic effects through altered muscle relaxation or GI discomfort | Acute caffeine use (~1 hour pre-exercise) appears safe; avoid chronic high-dose caffeine [14] |
| Ephedra + Caffeine | A single case report of ischemic stroke in an athlete using all three | Avoid combining creatine with ephedra [88] |
| Nephrotoxic drugs (NSAIDs, aminoglycosides, ACE inhibitors, ciclosporin) | Theoretically increased kidney stress | Consult physician if using creatine with nephrotoxic medications |
| Drugs that raise creatinine (trimethoprim, cimetidine) | Creatine further raises creatinine, potentially confusing kidney function assessment | Inform physician about creatine use before blood tests [1] |

### BCAA Interactions

| Interacting Substance | Concern | Recommendation |
|---|---|---|
| Parkinson's disease medications | BCAAs might interfere with some medications used to treat Parkinson disease | Avoid concurrent use or separate timing [6] |
| Type 2 diabetes medications | BCAAs might have additive effects with some type 2 diabetes medications | Monitor glucose more closely [6] |

## 7. Dietary Sources

### Creatine in Food

Food sources of creatine include meat, poultry, and fish [1].

### Impact of Cooking

Cooking reduces creatine content. In one study of beef steaks, losses were at least partly due to conversion to creatinine and probably also included some loss in cooking juices [89].

### Practical Perspective

A single 5 g dose of creatine equals the creatine in about 1.1 kg of fresh, uncooked steak, so getting a supplemental dose from food alone is impractical for most people [90].

### BCAA Sources

Dietary sources of BCAAs include dairy protein, eggs, meat, chicken, fish, and legumes [6].
