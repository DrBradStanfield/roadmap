## Key safety points

- If you have kidney disease, talk to your doctor before taking creatine [1]. Brad does not recommend it for people with severe kidney problems [2].
- Creatine raises blood creatinine [3], so a creatinine-based kidney test can look worse than it is [2]. Tell your doctor you take it; a cystatin C test can help judge kidney function [4][5].
- If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting. Avoid combining creatine with ephedra: one man had a stroke after taking both [6].
- If you have bipolar disorder, check with your doctor first: in a small trial, two people with bipolar depression taking creatine switched to hypomania or mania [7].
- Creatine has not been fully studied in pregnancy [8], and research in teenagers is very limited [9]. Ask your doctor first if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, or before giving it to a child or teenager.
- Single doses over 10 g may cause diarrhea, so split any loading phase into smaller servings [10].
- A loading phase usually adds 1 to 3 kg of body weight, mostly water [10].
- Creatine may affect the airways, mainly in people with allergies [11].

## 1. Overview

The body makes creatine in the kidneys and liver from the amino acids arginine, glycine and methionine [10]. About 95% of it is in skeletal muscle, with small amounts in the brain and testes [1]. About half of the daily need comes from food [1]. Each day about 1 to 2% of muscle creatine breaks down to creatinine, a waste product that leaves in urine [1].

Creatine monohydrate is the most studied supplemental form, and there is little to no evidence that newer forms work better or are safer [12]. More than 500 peer-reviewed papers have studied creatine supplements [10].

The best evidence is for muscle. With resistance training, creatine adds strength and lean mass [12][13], though one trial suggests part of the early gain is water [14]. It has also been studied in medical conditions, including Parkinson's and Huntington's disease, fibromyalgia, osteoarthritis and aging [1]. For memory, two meta-analyses found benefits [15][16], but both counted several tests from the same people as separate results [17], and in 2024 the European Food Safety Authority concluded that a cause-and-effect link with better cognition has not been established [18].

This article also covers branched-chain amino acids (BCAAs: leucine, isoleucine and valine), which are often sold alongside creatine. BCAAs are essential amino acids: the body cannot make them, so they must come from food [102]. Exercise speeds the breakdown of BCAAs in muscle, which may raise the need for them [106].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Creatine Forms

Creatine is sold in many chemical forms. Apart from creatine anhydrous, creatine monohydrate powder has the highest percentage of creatine (87.9%) [10]. An analysis of 175 creatine products sold online found 16 forms other than monohydrate; about 88% of the products using other forms had limited to no evidence for absorption, benefit or safety, and they cost more per gram [19].

### Key Principles for Form Selection

**Creatine monohydrate is the recommended form.** It has the most creatine per gram of the common forms and the most research behind it [10][12].

**Creatine HCl:** Head-to-head trials disagree. An 8-week trial found no benefit of HCl over monohydrate for strength or muscle size [20]. A 4-week trial in soccer players found larger performance gains with HCl and ethyl ester [21]. A single trial is not enough to switch forms.

**Avoid creatine ethyl ester.** In a case report it raised blood creatinine to levels that looked like kidney failure, although the kidneys were healthy; the levels returned to normal after it was stopped [22].

### Absorption and Bioavailability Factors

Creatine monohydrate is not broken down during normal digestion, and nearly 99% of a dose is either taken up by muscle or passed in urine [12].

**Co-ingestion with carbohydrates:** In 24 men, taking 93 g of simple sugar 30 minutes after each 5 g dose, four times a day for 5 days, raised muscle creatine 60% more than creatine alone, an effect that appeared to work through insulin [27].

**Caffeine interaction:** Research is scarce, but caffeine may blunt creatine's effect on exercise. A drug-level interaction is unlikely; slower muscle relaxation or stomach upset may explain it [28].

**Stability in solution:** Dissolved creatine slowly turns into creatinine, faster in acidic drinks. After 3 days at 25 °C, about 4% converted at pH 5.5, 12% at pH 4.5 and 21% at pH 3.5 [1].

**Creatine monohydrate powder:** In stress tests, heat was the main factor that broke creatine down [29]. Store the powder away from heat.

### Vegetarian and Vegan Considerations

Creatine comes mainly from red meat and seafood [1]. In one study, muscle creatine at the start was 117 mmol/kg in vegetarians and 130 mmol/kg in meat eaters, and vegetarians gained more muscle creatine, lean tissue and work performance on creatine [30]. In 20 active vegan adults, 4 weeks of creatine (0.1 g/kg a day) improved jump performance and added 0.3 kg of skeletal muscle and 1.4 kg of body water, with no change in sprint or strength tests [31].

### BCAA Composition

BCAA products combine leucine, isoleucine and valine. Trials have often used a 2:1:1 ratio of leucine to isoleucine to valine [103], or a similar high-leucine mix [104]. Of the three, leucine is the one mainly responsible for switching on muscle protein synthesis [101].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Muscle Strength and Size

Creatine raises muscle creatine and phosphocreatine by about 15 to 40%, improves short high-intensity exercise, and with training leads to greater gains in strength, power and muscle mass [12]. Gains are consistent for intense efforts of 30 seconds or less [28].

**Meta-analyses:** In 35 trials, creatine with resistance training added 1.10 kg of lean mass, whatever the age; without exercise it had no significant effect [13]. Men gained 1.46 kg, while women's gain of 0.29 kg was not significant [13]. In adults under 50, creatine with resistance training added 4.43 kg of upper-body and 11.35 kg of lower-body strength; men gained significantly, women did not, though few women were studied (40 women in 2 studies, plus 9 in a mixed study) [32].

**Muscle hypertrophy in trained men:** In 43 resistance-trained men, 8 weeks of creatine with training increased upper-limb lean tissue by 7.1% vs 1.6% with placebo, lower limbs by 3.2% vs 0.7%, and trunk by 2.1% vs 0.7% [33]. The men took 0.3 g/kg a day in four doses for a week, then 0.03 g/kg a day [33].

**Young adults, short courses:** In 40 young adults, 20 g a day for 1 week raised mean and peak power on a 30-second cycling test from before to after, with no effect on any cognitive task [34].

**Psychological component:** In 15 men, believing they had taken creatine gave the same acute squat performance as taking it [35]. Belief may explain part of the short-term effect.

**Body composition effects:** In 63 adults, 5 g a day added 0.51 kg of lean mass in a 7-day run-in before training; over the next 12 weeks of resistance training both groups gained about 2 kg, with no extra gain from creatine [14]. The authors think the early gain reflects water in the measurement and suggest that more than 5 g a day may be needed to add lean mass [14]. The trial did not measure strength, which is the main reason Brad still takes 5 g a day [36].

**Recovery from injury and disuse:** Creatine does not protect muscle during strict immobilization, but may help once loading resumes [37]. In 22 young adults whose leg was in a cast for 2 weeks, creatine during 10 weeks of rehabilitation sped the recovery of thigh muscle size and knee-extension power [38]. In 20 athletes with patellar tendinopathy, adding creatine to 8 weeks of rehabilitation brought earlier pain relief and better jump performance, though pain at 8 weeks was similar with placebo [39].

### Sarcopenia and Aging

Creatine with resistance training has been studied as a way to slow age-related muscle loss. A meta-analysis of 22 trials in 721 older adults found greater gains in lean tissue (1.37 kg) and in chest press and leg press strength over 7 to 52 weeks [40]. Another meta-analysis of 357 older adults found greater gains in fat-free mass, chest and leg press strength, and the 30-second chair stand [41]. Brad reads this evidence as showing that creatine with resistance training improves strength and lean mass in older adults [42].

**Positive trials:** In adults over 65, 5 g a day with resistance training for 14 weeks raised fat-free mass and some measures of strength more than placebo [43]. In untrained older men, 0.1 g/kg a day with 8 weeks of high-velocity training improved leg press strength more than placebo [44]. In 64 adults aged 45 to 65, 5 g twice a day for 12 weeks increased lean mass and strength with or without an exercise and diet programme [45]. In 103 older adults, 3 g a day added to 16 weeks of resistance training gave extra gains in strength and function and lowered inflammation markers [46].

**Trials with no added benefit:** In older men, 12 months of creatine (0.1 g/kg a day) with supervised training gave no more gain in bone, muscle or strength than placebo [47]. In 30 men aged 59 to 84 with prostate cancer on androgen deprivation therapy, creatine added nothing to 12 weeks of resistance exercise [48]. Without training, 10 days of creatine at either dose had no effect on muscle performance in older adults [49].

**Postmenopausal women:** A meta-analysis of 7 trials in 608 postmenopausal women found small gains in lean mass (0.37 kg) and leg press strength (7.5 kg), seen when 5 g a day or more was combined with resistance training; trials at 3 g a day or less without training showed no effect [50].

### Bone Density

Creatine has not been shown to raise bone density. A review describes the research on creatine alone for bone in older adults as a small body of work [51]. In two 2-year trials in postmenopausal women, 3 g a day in 200 women with osteopenia did not change bone density, bone markers, or falls and fractures [52], and creatine during resistance training and walking in 237 women did not change bone density, though it maintained a measure of hip bone geometry linked to bending strength [53]. In shorter trials with resistance training, creatine added a small gain in arm bone mineral content (12 weeks) [54] and lowered a urine marker of bone breakdown (10 weeks) [55]. A meta-analysis in postmenopausal women found bone density unchanged overall [50].

### Memory and Cognition

Supplements can raise brain creatine, and cognitive benefit seems most likely when brain creatine is low, as with sleep loss or aging [56][57]. Women have 70 to 80% lower creatine stores than men [58].

**Meta-analyses:** A 2023 meta-analysis of 8 trials found better memory overall (SMD 0.29), mainly in older adults aged 66 to 76 (SMD 0.88), with no effect in people aged 11 to 31 [15]. A 2024 meta-analysis of 16 trials in 492 adults found better memory (SMD 0.31), attention and processing speed, but no gain in overall cognition or executive function; benefit was greater in people with diseases, those aged 18 to 60, and women [16]. It rated the certainty of evidence moderate for memory and low for the other outcomes [16]. Both meta-analyses counted several correlated tests from the same people as separate results, which inflates precision [17]. The 2023 authors replied to a letter on this point [59]; Brad's current guidance reads their reanalysis as finding no clear overall memory benefit except in older adults aged 66 to 76 (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Regulators and earlier reviews:** In 2024 the European Food Safety Authority concluded that a cause-and-effect link between creatine and better cognitive function has not been established; a working-memory effect seen at 20 g a day for 5 to 7 days was not seen at 2.2 to 14 g a day [18]. A 2018 systematic review found that creatine may help short-term memory and reasoning, did not change cognition in young people, and helped vegetarians more than meat eaters [60]. In 45 young vegetarians, 5 g a day for 6 weeks improved working memory and reasoning [61].

**Newer trials (2026):** Results are split. In 40 young adults, 1 week at 20 g a day had no effect on any cognitive task [34]. In 51 perimenopausal women, creatine with 6 weeks of training did not change cognitive test results compared with placebo; sleep consistency was higher in an exploratory analysis [62]. In 48 women, 3 g a day for 28 days improved several cognitive measures in both young and older women, in an exploratory analysis [63]. In adults aged 45 to 65, selected cognitive and memory markers improved, which the authors call exploratory [45].

**Older adults:** In 36 perimenopausal and menopausal women, creatine HCl at 1,500 mg a day for 8 weeks improved reaction time and raised frontal brain creatine more than placebo [71]. In 32 older people, 5 g four times a day for 1 week improved all memory tasks tested except backward number recall [70].

**Sleep deprivation:** One dose of 0.2 g/kg during sleep deprivation reduced the decline by up to 12% [69], and in an earlier study by the same group a dose of 0.35 g/kg improved cognitive performance and processing speed [68]. In young adults who took 5 g four times a day for 7 days, creatine reduced the decline in several tasks and mood after 24 hours without sleep [66]; a later study by the same group found a benefit only on a complex executive task at 36 hours [67]. In elite rugby players, one dose of 50 or 100 mg/kg 1.5 hours before testing prevented the fall in passing accuracy caused by short sleep [65].

**Selective benefits after mental fatigue:** In 14 young adults, 20 g a day for 7 days improved accuracy on a long, tiring mental task by 4.9%, but did not prevent the fatigue-related slowing of visuomotor response time [64].

### Depression

Added to escitalopram in 52 women with depression, 5 g a day for 8 weeks improved depression scores more than placebo [72]. Added to cognitive behavioural therapy in 100 adults, creatine lowered depression scores more than placebo at 8 weeks, in a pilot trial [73]. If you are being treated for depression, talk to your doctor before adding creatine. In 35 people with bipolar depression, two of the 17 taking 6 g a day switched to hypomania or mania early in the trial [7]. If you have bipolar disorder, check with your doctor before taking creatine.

### Heart Failure and Cardiac Function

Creatine has been studied in heart and brain ischemia [1]. A meta-analysis of 8 trials found no effect on total, LDL or HDL cholesterol or triglycerides, with low or very low certainty [77]. In 12 older adults, 4 weeks of creatine improved flow-mediated dilation, a measure of blood vessel function, in a pilot crossover trial [76].

### Osteoarthritis

In 40 adults aged 40 to 70 with knee osteoarthritis, creatine added to 4 weeks of physical therapy and resistance exercise improved pain, function and strength more than placebo, but not quality of life or range of motion [75].

### Fibromyalgia

In a 16-week trial in fibromyalgia, creatine increased leg press and chest press strength more than placebo (+9.8% and +1.2% vs -0.5% and -7.2%), but did not improve pain, sleep or quality of life [74].

### Other Clinical Conditions

**Parkinson's disease:** A trial of 1,741 people with early, treated Parkinson's disease, given 10 g a day or placebo, was stopped early for futility after an interim analysis of 955 participants; the median follow-up was 4 years [81].

**Huntington's disease:** In 64 people with Huntington disease, 8 g a day for 16 weeks was well tolerated and raised blood and brain creatine [82].

**COPD:** In 38 people with COPD, creatine (5.7 g three times a day for 2 weeks, then once a day during pulmonary rehabilitation) increased fat-free mass, muscle strength and health status, but not whole-body exercise capacity [79].

**McArdle disease:** A Cochrane review found that low-dose creatine (60 mg/kg a day) caused no side effects, while high-dose creatine (150 mg/kg a day) worsened muscle pain [78].

**Post-COVID-19 fatigue:** In 12 patients, 4 g a day for 6 months raised muscle and brain creatine more than placebo. In the creatine group, general fatigue fell from baseline at 3 months and several symptoms improved by 6 months; the abstract does not report a difference from placebo in fatigue [80].

**Alzheimer's disease and other brain conditions:** Early clinical data suggest possible effects in Alzheimer's disease, depression and mild traumatic brain injury, but the findings are preliminary and inconsistent [57].

**Uric acid:** In one open-label study of 40 young men, 1 week at 20 g a day lowered blood uric acid [83]. There was no placebo group, and no controlled trial has tested this.

### BCAAs: Muscle Enhancement

The largest and longest trial, in older adults with sarcopenia, found no benefit from leucine [113]; smaller and shorter trials around surgery, bed rest and critical illness found some protection of muscle [104][107][110].

**Older adults and sarcopenia:** In 145 adults aged 70 or older with sarcopenia, leucine 2.5 g three times a day did not improve physical performance or muscle mass over 12 months, and a meta-analysis in the same paper found no evidence that leucine improves physical performance [113]. An earlier review noted that short-term studies suggested leucine might help older people build muscle, but that no long-term trials had confirmed it [115].

**Around surgery:** In 80 patients after surgery for lumbar spinal stenosis, BCAAs plus vitamin D twice a day for 3 weeks improved knee strength but not symptoms, muscle mass, gait speed or mobility [109]. In 39 adults having a knee replacement who took 20 g of essential amino acids twice a day from 7 days before to 6 weeks after surgery, the quadriceps shrank less (-8.5% vs -13.4%), as did the hamstrings (-7.4% vs -12.2%), but strength and mobility did not differ [108]. In an earlier trial of 28 older adults who took the same dose until 2 weeks after a knee replacement, thigh muscle loss was smaller than with placebo, mobility returned faster, and the leg that was not operated on also lost less muscle [107]. Both knee trials used a mix of essential amino acids, not BCAAs alone.

**Critical illness:** In 40 critically ill patients, 40 g a day of high-leucine BCAAs for 10 days was linked with no loss of thigh muscle thickness, against a 13% loss in the control group [104]. The authors say the supplement may limit muscle loss; the trial was small [104].

**Resistance training in older women:** In 30 postmenopausal women, 9 g a day of BCAAs during 8 weeks of resistance training did not add to the gains in muscle mass and strength from training alone [114].

**Bed rest:** In 19 middle-aged adults on 14 days of bed rest, leucine (0.06 g per kg of body weight with each meal) limited the loss of knee strength (-7% vs -15%) and endurance (-2% vs -14%) compared with an alanine control [110]. Two trials of leg immobilization found no protection: 5 g three times a day over 3 days in young and older adults [112], and 15 g a day of leucine over 7 days in young men [111].

**Fatigue and function:** In a pilot trial of 20 older adults, BCAAs (100 mg per kg of body weight a day) with 8 weeks of exercise improved strength, mobility and endurance more than exercise with placebo [103]. On the trial's registered main outcome, the Fatigue Assessment Scale (scored 10 to 50), fatigue fell in both groups, by 5.6 points with BCAAs and 4.1 points with placebo [121]. The paper's larger contrast in fatigue (-45% vs +92%) came from another measure [103]. A pilot this small needs confirming.

**Around exercise:** Reviews report that BCAAs taken before and after exercise may reduce muscle damage and delayed-onset muscle soreness and may affect immune function, though they do not seem to improve athletic performance [105][106].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Creatine Dosing Protocols

**Loading protocol (fastest saturation):** The quickest way to fill muscle creatine stores may be about 0.3 g/kg a day for 5 to 7 days (for example, 5 g four times a day), then 3 to 5 g a day [1]. In one study, 20 g a day for 6 days raised muscle creatine by about 20% [84]. High loading doses are optional; people with kidney concerns should skip them and ask their doctor.

**Maintenance dose:** Once stores are full, 3 to 5 g a day generally maintains them, though larger athletes may need 5 to 10 g a day [1]. In the same study, 2 g a day maintained the higher level for a further 30 days [84].

**Loading-free protocol:** 3 g a day gave the same 20% rise more gradually, across 28 days [84]. Without loading, 3 to 5 g a day needs at least 4 weeks to fill the muscles [10]. Brad recommends 5 g a day without loading, which fills the muscles more slowly and with less chance of tummy upset [2], and his guidance gives 3 to 5 g a day (Brad's current guidance, 2 Nov 2026).

**Dose for muscle gain:** In postmenopausal women, gains were seen at 5 g a day or more with resistance training, not at 3 g a day or less without training [50].

**General health dose:** The ISSN position stand suggests that a habitual low intake (for example, 3 g a day) throughout life may bring health benefits [1].

**Higher doses:** In the Parkinson's trial, 10 g a day for years caused no more adverse events than placebo [81].

### Timing and Practical Recommendations

**Timing:** Taking creatine every day matters more than the time of day. In a 4-week study of 19 male bodybuilders, 5 g right after workouts looked slightly better than right before, but the differences were not statistically significant [85].

**Missing doses:** After stopping, muscle creatine returns to baseline in about 30 days, so a missed dose probably matters little [84].

**Hydration:** Creatine adds water to muscle [10], but a systematic review found no evidence that it harms heat tolerance or fluid balance [86].

### BCAA Dosing

There is no established dose of BCAAs for muscle. Doses used in the trials above include:

**Around surgery (knee replacement):** 20 g of essential amino acids twice a day, from 1 week before to 2 weeks after surgery (6 weeks in a second trial) [107][108].

**During bed rest:** 0.06 g of leucine per kg of body weight with each meal [110].

**With resistance training:** 9 g of BCAAs a day, which did not add to the effect of training [114].

### Safety Thresholds

**ISSN position:** Short- and long-term use, up to 30 g a day for 5 years, has been safe and well tolerated in healthy people and in a number of patient groups from infants to older adults [1].

**EFSA:** In 2004, a European Food Safety Authority panel judged that up to 3 g a day of high-purity supplemental creatine, close to the amount the body normally turns over each day, is unlikely to pose any risk [122][123]. The panel also advised avoiding high loading doses, because the evidence left doubts about their safety for the kidneys [122]. Brad recommends 5 g a day without loading [2], and the ISSN found no harm in healthy people at up to 30 g a day for 5 years [1].

**Side effects across trials:** In 684 randomised trials with more than 12,800 people, side effects were infrequent, mostly mild, and did not rise in a clinically meaningful way with dose or duration [87].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Creatine Safety

The ISSN position stand concludes that use up to 30 g a day for 5 years is safe and well tolerated in healthy people and in a number of patient groups [1]. The main effects to know about:

**Weight gain from water retention:** A loading phase of 20 g a day for 5 to 7 days usually adds 1 to 3 kg of body weight, mostly water [10]. This is not fat. In women, loading (5 g four times a day for 5 days) in the luteal phase, the later part of the cycle, raised total body water by 0.83 L without changing body weight [88].

**Gastrointestinal symptoms:** The ISSN found no evidence that creatine raises stomach upset [1], but single doses over 10 g may cause diarrhea, so a loading phase is best split into smaller servings [10].

**Creatinine levels:** Creatine raises blood creatinine, a marker used to judge kidney function. In a meta-analysis of 20 trials, creatinine rose by 0.13 mg/dL, while urea and eGFR did not change [3]. Another meta-analysis of 12 studies found a small rise in creatinine and no significant change in kidney filtration [89]. A rise still needs checking by your doctor. Tell your doctor you take creatine before a kidney blood test.

**Hair loss concerns:** In a 2009 study in rugby players, DHT rose 56% after a week of loading [90], but DHT started 23% lower in the creatine group than the placebo group [10]. A 2025 trial in 45 resistance-trained men given 5 g a day for 12 weeks found no difference in DHT, the DHT-to-testosterone ratio, or hair growth [91].

**Mood trials:** In 52 women with depression taking escitalopram, adverse events were no more common with creatine than with placebo [72]. In bipolar depression, two of 17 people taking creatine switched to hypomania or mania [7]. If you have bipolar disorder, check with your doctor before taking creatine.

**Muscle cramping and dehydration:** The ISSN found no evidence that creatine causes cramping or dehydration [1], and a systematic review found no evidence that it harms heat tolerance or fluid balance [86].

**Arrhythmia:** A 2005 case report describes a 30-year-old man with no notable medical history who came to an emergency department in atrial fibrillation and was taking creatine monohydrate [92]. One case cannot show that creatine caused it.

**Airways and allergies:** In 19 elite youth soccer players (loading for 1 week, then 5 g a day for 7 weeks), creatine showed a trend toward mild airway inflammation and a larger fall in lung function after a breathing challenge, more so in players with allergies [11]. Creatine also worsened allergic lung inflammation in mice [93].

**Stroke with ephedra:** A sportsman had an ischemic stroke after taking ma huang (ephedra) extract and creatine monohydrate [6]. Avoid combining them.

### Kidney Function

In healthy people, creatine does not appear to harm the kidneys. Long-term users (10 months to 5 years) had normal kidney filtration compared with controls [94], and a review found creatinine only slightly affected by use for up to 5.6 years, in young healthy people [95]. In healthy men taking about 10 g a day for 3 months, cystatin C, a kidney marker not made from creatine, fell rather than rose [4]. Reviewers have called for more data in older adults and people with kidney disease [95].

**Kidney disease:** If you have kidney disease, talk to your doctor before using creatine; this has long been advised as a precaution [1]. Brad does not recommend creatine for people with severe kidney problems, and for patients with significant kidney disease he reduces the dose or stops it [2].

**Kidney-harming medicines and high doses:** A 2004 review found that in people with kidney disease, or who take medicines that can harm the kidneys, creatine may raise the risk of kidney problems [119]. Medicines often linked with kidney harm include NSAID painkillers, aminoglycoside antibiotics, ACE inhibitors and the antifungal amphotericin B [125]. If you take such a medicine, check with your doctor first. Few trials had tested more than 10 g a day over the long term, and acute kidney failure was reported in a 20-year-old man taking 20 g a day for 4 weeks [119].

**Kidney tests:** Because creatine raises creatinine, a creatinine-based test can make kidney function look worse than it is [2], and large doses may falsely raise creatinine [95]. A cystatin C test, alone or combined with creatinine, can help judge kidney function when creatinine may mislead [5][4].

### BCAA Safety

If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting BCAAs. In people with Parkinson's disease, oral leucine or isoleucine reversed the effect of levodopa, probably by competing with it for transport into the brain [96]. If you take levodopa, ask your doctor before taking BCAAs. Leucine strongly stimulates insulin release and has improved glucose control in people with type 2 diabetes [115][116], so BCAAs might add to the effect of diabetes medicines; if you take them, ask your doctor first, as you may need closer glucose checks. In 39 older adults taking essential amino acids for about 7 weeks around knee surgery, kidney and liver blood tests did not differ from placebo [108]. Early research suggests that very protein-rich meals (more than about 25 g of protein) raise leucine enough to activate immune cells involved in atherosclerosis; this comes from short human studies and mice, not from trials of heart disease [117].

### Safety of Other Supplements

**Glutamine:** Mania has been reported with L-glutamine [97]. If you have bipolar disorder, check with your doctor before taking it. In a small trial of single doses in 14 healthy men, gut symptoms such as discomfort and nausea rose with the dose, though most were mild [118]. If you plan to take high doses of glutamine, ask your doctor or pharmacist first. More on glutamine: [glutamine](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/glutamine-benefits-forms-dosing-and-side-effects).

**Muscle-building supplements and testicular cancer:** In a case-control study of 356 men with testicular germ cell cancer and 513 controls, men who had ever used muscle-building supplements had higher odds of the cancer (odds ratio 1.65); the link was stronger in early users, men who used more types, and men who used them for longer [98]. A case-control study cannot show cause. Some products sold for muscle building contain undeclared drugs: of 92 muscle-building supplements named in US FDA warnings from 2007 to 2016, 82 contained synthetic steroids or steroid-like ingredients [124]. Choose single-ingredient products, and choose a product that an independent lab has tested.

### Special Populations

**Children and adolescents:** Research on creatine in adolescents is extremely limited, especially on its safety and benefit in this age group [9].

**Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** Creatine use in human pregnancy has not been fully evaluated [8]. If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, ask your doctor before taking it.

**Women:** Women have 70 to 80% lower creatine stores than men [58]. In muscle meta-analyses, women's gains were smaller or not significant [13][32], though few women were studied; postmenopausal women gained a little lean mass and strength at 5 g a day or more with training [50].

## 7. Dietary Sources

### Creatine in Food

Creatine is found mainly in red meat and seafood, and about half the daily need comes from food [1]. A pound of uncooked beef or salmon provides about 1 to 2 g [1].

### Impact of Cooking

Cooking lowers creatine in beef. In one study of steaks, losses were at least partly due to conversion to creatinine and probably also included some loss in cooking juices [99].

### Practical Perspective

A single 5 g dose equals the creatine in about 1.1 kg of fresh, uncooked steak, so getting a supplement-sized dose from food is impractical for most people [100].

### BCAA Sources

Because BCAAs are essential amino acids, they must come from food, as part of the protein in the diet [102][116].
