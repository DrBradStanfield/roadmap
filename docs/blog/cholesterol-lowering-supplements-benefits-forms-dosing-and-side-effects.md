## Current view (reviewed 12 November 2026)

No single food or supplement will unclog arteries, though a heart-healthy diet, with other risk factors also controlled, can slow plaque build-up and in some cases shrink it a little ([Brad, 10 Sep 2024](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/foods-help-unclog-arteries)). Brad says some studies have indicated that phytosterols, plant molecules similar to cholesterol, can help lower cholesterol, and he lists foods high in them, such as mango, citrus, cauliflower, nuts and legumes ([Brad, 10 Sep 2024](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/foods-help-unclog-arteries)). He lists fiber, including psyllium husk supplements, among the parts of the diet that may help lower LDL cholesterol ([Brad, 10 Sep 2024](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/foods-help-unclog-arteries)). He also says that supplementing niacin above the recommended daily intake may be inadvisable, after a study linked niacin's breakdown products to heart attacks ([Brad, 10 Sep 2024](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/foods-help-unclog-arteries)). For high LDL cholesterol, Brad starts people on rosuvastatin 5 mg, adds ezetimibe 10 mg if that is not enough, and switches people who cannot take statins to a PCSK9 inhibitor ([Brad, 9 Nov 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/we-finally-know-how-to-remove-plaque-from-arteries)). He takes rosuvastatin 5 mg and ezetimibe 10 mg himself ([Brad, 9 Nov 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/we-finally-know-how-to-remove-plaque-from-arteries)).

## Key safety points

- Red yeast rice contains monacolin K, the same compound as the statin lovastatin, and can cause statin-like muscle, kidney and liver harm. Do not combine it with a statin, gemfibrozil, cyclosporine or strong CYP3A4 inhibitors such as clarithromycin, avoid grapefruit juice, and do not use it in pregnancy or breastfeeding. Check with your doctor first if you have liver disease [17][67].
- If you take a blood thinner or antiplatelet drug, ask your doctor first: high-dose fish oil may prolong bleeding time, and policosanol and pantethine reduced platelet clumping in small studies [73][65][58].
- People with sitosterolemia, a rare inherited disorder, should not take plant sterol supplements [51][74].
- Supplements do not replace prescribed cholesterol medicine. Plant sterols lower LDL cholesterol, but no trial has shown that they prevent heart attacks or strokes [2][5].
- Take psyllium with enough fluid, because it can swell and cause choking, and take other medicines at least 2 hours before or after it [66]. Do not take it if you have trouble swallowing. If you get chest pain, vomiting, or trouble swallowing or breathing after a dose, get medical help at once [66].
- High-dose niacin can cause flushing and raise blood sugar and uric acid, and severe liver damage has occurred when sustained-release niacin replaced immediate-release niacin [68].
- Plant sterols lower blood beta-carotene and some other carotenoids [50].

## 1. Overview

LDL cholesterol and apoB, a count of the particles that carry cholesterol into the artery wall, are causal risk factors for heart disease, and trials that lower LDL cholesterol show lower rates of heart disease [75]. Statins are the main medicine for lowering it: rosuvastatin 5 mg on its own lowers LDL by about 45%, and the top dose of 40 mg by about 63% [75]. Some people cannot take a statin, and others need more LDL lowering than their medicine gives, so many turn to supplements.

The most studied options are plant sterols and stanols (phytosterols), soluble fiber (psyllium and oat beta-glucan), red yeast rice and omega-3 fatty acids. Sterols and stanols block cholesterol absorption in the gut [8], soluble fibers lower total and LDL cholesterol by about 5% to 10% [4], and red yeast rice contains a statin [17].

Supplements lower LDL cholesterol far less than statins. In a 28-day trial in 190 adults at raised risk of heart disease, rosuvastatin 5 mg daily lowered LDL cholesterol 35.2% more than placebo, while none of six supplements (fish oil, cinnamon, garlic, turmeric, plant sterols and red yeast rice) lowered it significantly more than placebo [6]. The trial was funded by AstraZeneca [6]. In a meta-analysis, plant sterols and stanols at a mean daily dose of 2.15 g lowered LDL cholesterol by 8.8% on average [25], but no trial has tested whether they prevent heart attacks, strokes or deaths [5]. Ordinary foods give far less than the roughly 2 g a day used in the trials, so the trial results come from fortified foods and supplements.

The evidence varies by supplement. Plant sterols and stanols and soluble fiber have the strongest support from meta-analyses. Red yeast rice lowers LDL cholesterol, but its strength varies widely between products and it carries statin risks [17][19]. Omega-3 fatty acids mainly lower triglycerides, not LDL cholesterol [22]. The evidence for the rest is weaker. Reviews disagree on garlic and artichoke [2][44][71][72]. Berberine lowered LDL cholesterol more than placebo in a network meta-analysis, but its trials are of limited quality [76][88]. Pantethine rests on one small trial [23], and policosanol does not appear to work [64][76].

These products are add-ons to lifestyle change (a heart-healthy diet, regular exercise, weight management), not substitutes for prescribed medicines [2]. In the UK, NICE advises against recommending plant stanols or sterols to prevent heart disease [77].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Plant Sterols and Stanols (Phytosterols)

Plant sterols and stanols are compounds structurally similar to cholesterol, found in small amounts in vegetable oils, nuts, grains and other plant foods. The main plant sterols are beta-sitosterol, campesterol and stigmasterol [62]. Plant stanols are the saturated forms of sterols.

These compounds are available in two primary chemical forms:

**Free (nonesterified) sterols and stanols:** The base molecular form. In 2000 the FDA concluded that 800 mg per day or more of free plant sterols consistently lowered blood total and LDL cholesterol. Its 2010 proposed rule would raise it to 2 g a day, and it found the evidence for nonesterified phytosterols in supplements limited and inconsistent [63]. Until a final rule, the FDA said it would consider using enforcement discretion, case by case, for claims that follow the proposed rule [63]. The current health claim covers only plant sterol esters (at least 1.3 g a day) and plant stanol esters (at least 3.4 g a day) [62].

**Esterified sterols and stanols:** Sterols or stanols bonded to a fatty acid, which makes them dissolve better in fats and oils (useful in spreads and softgels). The ester form is heavier: approximately 1,300 mg of plant sterol esters provides roughly 800 mg of free sterols. Similarly, 3,400 mg of plant stanol esters provides approximately 2,000 mg of free stanols [63].

The form also seems to matter in supplements. In one trial, tablets giving 1.8 g a day of free sterols and stanols for 6 weeks lowered LDL cholesterol by 4.9% and total cholesterol by 2.8% compared with placebo (Maki et al., 2012) [10]. In a second trial, softgel capsules giving 1.8 g a day of esterified sterols and stanols lowered LDL cholesterol by 9.2%, total cholesterol by 7.4% and triglycerides by 9.1% (Maki et al., 2013) [11]. These were separate trials, but they suggest that esterified forms in softgels may work better than free forms in tablets. The FDA's 2010 review reached a similar view: supplements with phytosterol esters lowered cholesterol as well as foods did [63].

**Delivery vehicles matter.** Sterols and stanols have been studied in margarine/spreads, yogurt drinks, orange juice, capsules, and tablets. The food form may substantially affect how much LDL cholesterol falls [51]. A meta-analysis of randomized trials found larger LDL reductions when sterols were added to fat spreads, mayonnaise, salad dressing, milk and yogurt than to foods such as croissants, muffins, orange juice and cereal bars, and a single morning dose had no significant effect [26].

For the FDA health claim, at least 80% of the sterols in plant sterol esters, by combined weight, must be beta-sitosterol, campesterol or stigmasterol [62].

### Soluble Fiber Forms

**Psyllium husk** (from *Plantago ovata* seeds) is the most studied soluble fiber for cholesterol lowering. It is sold as a bulk powder, capsules or wafers. Water-soluble fibers that form a thick gel lower total and LDL cholesterol by about 5% to 10% [4].

**Beta-glucan** is a soluble fiber found in oats and barley. It is available through food sources (oatmeal, oat bran) or as a purified supplement. Trials that lowered LDL cholesterol used at least 3 g a day [14][15].

**Acacia fiber** and other soluble fibers have less cholesterol-lowering data than psyllium or beta-glucan.

### Red Yeast Rice

Red yeast rice is rice fermented with a yeast. Its active compound, monacolin K, is structurally identical to the prescription statin lovastatin [17]. Product quality varies enormously. In a test of 28 US brands, two had no detectable monacolin K, and in the rest the amount varied more than 60-fold, from 0.09 to 5.48 mg per 1200 mg of red yeast rice [19]. The FDA has found that red yeast rice products containing more than trace amounts of monacolin K are unapproved new drugs and cannot be sold legally as dietary supplements [2]. Some products are also contaminated with citrinin, which can damage the kidneys [17]. Red yeast rice has its own page.

### Omega-3 Fatty Acids

The two main omega-3 fatty acids for blood lipids are eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA). They come in fish oil supplements (EPA and DHA), krill oil, algal oil (a vegetarian DHA source) and prescription products, some of which contain EPA alone. Their main effect on lipids is to lower triglycerides rather than LDL cholesterol [22].

### Policosanol

Policosanol is a blend of long-chain fatty alcohols, mostly from sugar cane wax. As the evidence section below shows, independent trials have not found that it lowers cholesterol.

### Pantethine

Pantethine is a form of vitamin B5 (pantothenic acid). In rat liver cells it lowered the activity of an enzyme the liver uses to make cholesterol [41].

### Other Forms

**Garlic** supplements come in aged garlic extract, garlic powder, and garlic oil forms. **Artichoke leaf extract** (*Cynara scolymus*) is available as standardized capsules. **Niacin** (vitamin B3) in high doses lowers LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, but in people already on a statin it did not prevent heart attacks or strokes [42][68][87]. **Berberine** is an alkaloid from plants such as *Berberis vulgaris*. The evidence for garlic, artichoke and berberine is mixed or of limited quality [2][88].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Plant Sterols and Stanols: LDL Cholesterol Reduction

Plant sterols and stanols lower LDL cholesterol by reducing how much cholesterol the gut absorbs [8]. The liver partly makes up for it: in one trial a stanol margarine cut cholesterol absorption by 45% while the body's own cholesterol production rose 39% [78].

**Dose-response meta-analysis:** A dose-response meta-analysis of 84 randomized controlled trials found that phytosterols lowered LDL cholesterol by 8.8% (0.34 mmol/L) on average at a mean dose of 2.15 g a day; the effect rose with dose, and the dose-response curves for plant sterols and stanols did not differ significantly [25]. A later meta-analysis of 124 studies found that 0.6 to 3.3 g a day lowered LDL cholesterol by 6% to 12%, with the effect still rising up to about 3 g a day [7]. A separate meta-analysis of 41 trials found that intake of 2 g/day reduced LDL cholesterol by approximately 10%, with higher doses providing little additional benefit [51]. A 2026 umbrella review of 14 meta-analyses rated the evidence for LDL lowering as high certainty, with falls of 0.26 to 0.36 mmol/L and no change in HDL cholesterol [79]. People with higher LDL cholesterol at the start saw larger falls [25].

**Sterols versus stanols:** A 2011 meta-analysis of 113 publications found larger maximal LDL reductions for plant stanols (16.4%, or 17.1% as stanol esters) than for plant sterols (8.3%, or 8.4% as sterol esters), with stanol intakes in excess of 2 g/day giving further dose-dependent reductions [27]. This differs from the dose-response meta-analysis above, which found no significant difference between sterols and stanols [25]. In their free forms, sterols and stanols lowered LDL cholesterol equally in one trial [46].

**Long-term trial:** In a one-year Finnish trial in 153 adults with mildly raised cholesterol, a margarine giving 1.8 or 2.6 g of stanol per day lowered LDL cholesterol by 14.1%, against 1.1% with a plain margarine. It was well tolerated, and triglycerides and HDL cholesterol did not change [80].

**Free vs. esterified forms in supplements:** As noted above, the free-sterol tablet lowered LDL cholesterol by 4.9% in adults on a therapeutic lifestyle changes diet [10], and the esterified softgel lowered it by 9.2% [11]. Neither changed HDL cholesterol significantly [10][11]. In 15 people without diabetes and 14 with type 2 diabetes given 1.8 g/day of sterols over 21 days, LDL cholesterol fell from baseline by 15.1% and 26.8%, respectively [45].

**People with obesity:** In a 2026 trial in adults with overweight or obesity, 2 g of plant sterols a day for 8 weeks did not lower LDL cholesterol compared with placebo [81]. The trial was small, so this needs confirming.

**Low-dose sterol supplementation with diet adherence:** In a crossover trial in 50 Italian adults with high cholesterol, a once-a-day drink with 2.5 g of phytosterols for 3 weeks lowered LDL cholesterol by 7.8 mg/dL from baseline, and the fall was larger in people who kept more closely to a Mediterranean diet (Cicero et al., *Nutrients*, 2023) [28]. Diet quality may change how much sterols help.

**Yogurt drink delivery:** In a Spanish trial in 182 adults with high cholesterol, a yogurt drink with 2 g of plant stanols a day lowered LDL cholesterol by 11.0% at 12 months, a fall 13.7 mg/dL larger than with plain yogurt (Parraga-Martinez et al., *Rev Esp Cardiol*, 2015) [29].

**Comparison to statin in a direct trial:** In a 28-day trial in 190 adults at raised cardiovascular risk, plant sterols did not lower LDL cholesterol more than placebo, while rosuvastatin 5 mg a day did (Laffin et al., *J Am Coll Cardiol*, 2023) [6].

**Triglycerides:** Pooled data from 12 trials (935 people) found that sterols lowered triglycerides by a modest 6.0%, with larger falls in people whose triglycerides started higher [82]. The authors worked for Unilever [82].

**Heart attacks and strokes:** No randomized trial has shown that plant sterols or stanols prevent heart attacks, strokes or deaths [5]. In a 12-week trial in 240 adults, 3 g a day of plant sterols in a spread lowered LDL cholesterol by 6.7% but did not change artery function [52]. A European expert panel says sterol foods may be considered for people with high cholesterol who do not need medicine, as an add-on when statins fall short, and for adults and children over 6 years with familial hypercholesterolaemia [5]. NICE in the UK advises against recommending them to prevent heart disease [77].

### Plant Sterols Combined with Statins

Adding plant stanols or sterols to a statin lowers LDL cholesterol further. A stanol ester spread (5.1 g a day) taken with a statin for 8 weeks cut LDL cholesterol by 10% more than a placebo spread (Blair et al., *Am J Cardiol*, 2000) [30]. In a small trial in statin-treated patients, a stanol margarine with dietary advice cut LDL cholesterol by 15.6%, against 7.7% with standard care (Castro Cabezas et al., *J Am Diet Assoc*, 2006) [31]. In a 2002 trial, sterol-ester margarine added to a statin gave an LDL reduction equal to doubling the dose of statin (Simons, *Am J Cardiol*, 2002) [32]. In 10 women with heart disease taking simvastatin, a stanol margarine lowered LDL cholesterol by a further 16% [78]. A European expert panel suggests them as an add-on for people at high risk who miss their LDL target on a statin or cannot take one [5].

### Plant Sterols Combined with Fish Oil

In a 4-week trial in 332 adults with high cholesterol (85 men and 247 women), a low-fat spread gave 2.5 g of plant sterols a day with 0, 0.9, 1.3 or 1.8 g of EPA plus DHA from fish oil. LDL cholesterol was 11.5% to 14.7% lower in the sterol groups than with a control spread, and triglycerides were 9.3% to 16.2% lower in the fish oil groups, falling further as the fish oil dose rose (Ras et al., *J Nutr*, 2014) [35].

### Plant Sterols Combined with Curcumin

In an Australian trial in adults with high cholesterol, 2 g of phytosterols plus 200 mg of curcumin a day for 4 weeks lowered total cholesterol by 11.0% and LDL cholesterol by 14.4%, against 4.8% and 8.1% with phytosterols alone. Curcumin alone gave small, non-significant falls, and HDL cholesterol and triglycerides did not change (Ferguson et al., *Metabolism*, 2018) [36].

### Plant Sterols Combined with Red Yeast Rice

In an 8-week Italian trial in 90 adults with moderately high cholesterol, 800 mg of phytosterols a day did not change lipids significantly, red yeast rice with 5 mg of monacolins cut LDL cholesterol by 20.5%, and the two together cut it by 27.0%, more than red yeast rice alone (Cicero et al., *Nutr Metab*, 2017) [33]. A US trial found no added benefit: in 187 people taking red yeast rice 1,800 mg twice daily, adding 900 mg of phytosterol tablets twice daily did not lower LDL cholesterol further at 12, 24 or 52 weeks (Becker et al., *Am Heart J*, 2013) [34]. Combining the two may help, but these trials disagree.

### Soluble Fiber: Psyllium

Water-soluble fibers that form a thick gel, such as psyllium, lower total and LDL cholesterol by about 5% to 10% [4].

**Meta-analysis (2025):** A 2025 meta-analysis of 41 randomized trials (2,049 people) found that psyllium lowered LDL cholesterol (weighted mean difference -8.55) and total cholesterol (-9.05); the paper does not state the unit. Dose and duration had no significant linear effect on the results [12].

**Earlier meta-analyses:** In 8 trials of 10.2 g of psyllium a day added to a low-fat diet, LDL cholesterol fell by 7% and total cholesterol by 4% compared with placebo (Anderson et al., *Am J Clin Nutr*, 2000) [13]. A 2009 meta-analysis of trials using 3 to 20.4 g a day found that larger doses lowered cholesterol more [37]. A 2018 meta-analysis of 28 trials (1,924 people) found that a median dose of about 10.2 g of psyllium lowered LDL cholesterol by 0.33 mmol/L [59].

**With a statin:** In a 12-week trial in 68 patients, 15 g of psyllium a day added to simvastatin 10 mg lowered LDL cholesterol as much as simvastatin 20 mg alone [83].

Dr Brad Stanfield's [MicroVitamin+ Powder](https://drstanfield.com/products/microvitamin-powder) no longer contains psyllium: its current v9 formula, the same in every market, uses 5 g of acacia fiber instead, which supports overall fiber intake and gut health.

### Soluble Fiber: Beta-Glucan (Oats and Barley)

Meta-analyses of randomized trials found that at least 3 g a day of oat beta-glucan lowered LDL cholesterol by 0.25 mmol/L and total cholesterol by 0.30 mmol/L [14]. A later meta-analysis of 58 trials found that a median 3.5 g a day lowered LDL cholesterol by 0.19 mmol/L [15].

### Red Yeast Rice

Red yeast rice lowers cholesterol through monacolin K, which is structurally identical to lovastatin [17].

**Meta-analysis (2024):** A systematic review and meta-analysis of 14 double-blind trials lasting 4 to 24 weeks found red yeast rice extract lowered total cholesterol by a mean of 37.43 mg/dL and LDL cholesterol by 35.82 mg/dL, but did not significantly change HDL cholesterol or triglycerides. It was not associated with an increase in life-threatening side effects, and the authors call for more research in specific groups (Trogkanis et al., *Nutrients*, 2024) [70]. In a 24-week trial in people who had stopped statins because of muscle pain, red yeast rice lowered LDL cholesterol without raising muscle enzyme levels or pain [84].

**Dose matters:** Products with considerable amounts of monacolin K may lower blood cholesterol. Products with very little monacolin K may not, and some contain less than the amount known to lower cholesterol in clinical trials [17].

**However, product variability is a major concern.** In a test of 28 brands sold by mainstream US retailers, two had no detectable monacolin K. Following the labels' daily servings, the rest would give anywhere from 0.09 to 10.94 mg of monacolin K a day, a more than 120-fold range [19]. A product may therefore hold too little monacolin K to work, or an unpredictably large dose.

**Regulatory status:** The FDA has found that red yeast rice products with more than trace amounts of monacolin K are unapproved new drugs and cannot be sold legally as dietary supplements [2].

### Omega-3 Fatty Acids

Omega-3 fatty acids mainly lower triglycerides rather than LDL cholesterol [22].

**Triglyceride reduction:** In people with very high triglycerides, prescription EPA plus DHA at 4 g a day lowers triglycerides by 30% or more [22].

**LDL cholesterol:** In people with very high triglycerides, EPA plus DHA products can raise LDL cholesterol, while EPA alone did not raise it [22][73].

### Pantethine

In a trial in 32 adults at low to moderate cardiovascular risk who were eligible for statins, pantethine (600 mg a day for 8 weeks, then 900 mg a day for 8 more weeks) lowered LDL cholesterol by 11% from baseline, while LDL rose 3% with placebo; total cholesterol also fell significantly compared with placebo at 16 weeks (Evans et al., *Vasc Health Risk Manag*, 2014) [23]. The trial was funded by Daiichi Fine Chemical, and some of its authors were employees of that company or of Kyowa Hakko USA [23]. The evidence base for pantethine remains small.

In rat liver cells, pantethine lowered the activity of HMG-CoA reductase, a key enzyme in cholesterol production (Cighetti et al., *Biochim Biophys Acta*, 1988) [41]. Sterols and stanols work differently, by blocking cholesterol absorption [8].

### Policosanol

**Most positive studies come from a single Cuban research group.** More than 80 trials, performed mostly by a single research institute, suggest that policosanol at 5 to 40 mg a day lowers lipids about as well as statins [64]. However, a randomized trial in Germany found that Cuban sugar cane policosanol at 10, 20, 40 or 80 mg a day for 12 weeks did not lower LDL cholesterol more than placebo [64]. Independent Canadian trials of Cuban sugar cane policosanol also found no effect on LDL cholesterol [85][86]. A network meta-analysis of 131 trials found that every supplement tested lowered LDL cholesterol more than placebo except policosanols [76]. A US government review finds no clear evidence that policosanol lowers cholesterol [2].

The evidence for policosanol as a cholesterol-lowering agent should be considered doubtful at best.

### Garlic

Garlic supplements have been extensively studied, and the results for LDL cholesterol are mixed. A 2013 meta-analysis of 39 trials found garlic taken for longer than 2 months lowered LDL cholesterol by about 9 mg/dL in people with total cholesterol above 200 mg/dL [71]. A 2012 meta-analysis of 26 trials found garlic lowered total cholesterol and triglycerides but did not change LDL cholesterol [72]. A 6-month trial found no cholesterol-lowering benefit from raw garlic or garlic supplements [24]. In a 28-day trial, garlic did not lower LDL cholesterol more than placebo [6].

### Niacin (Vitamin B3)

In high doses, niacin can raise HDL cholesterol and lower LDL cholesterol and triglycerides [42][68]. In the AIM-HIGH trial, adding extended-release niacin (1,500 to 2,000 mg a day) to a statin in 3,414 people with heart disease gave no extra clinical benefit, and the trial stopped early for lack of efficacy [42]. In the larger HPS2-THRIVE trial, extended-release niacin with laropiprant added to a statin did not significantly cut major vascular events and caused more serious problems with diabetes control [87]. The niacin label now states that it has not been shown to reduce cardiovascular events or deaths in people already taking a statin, and it lists flushing, digestive upset, liver problems and higher blood sugar among its harms [68].

### Artichoke Leaf Extract

Artichoke leaf extract (*Cynara scolymus*) results disagree. A meta-analysis of 9 trials (702 people) found it lowered LDL cholesterol by 14.9 mg/dL [44], but a US government review says it has not been found to be effective [2]. Evidence is limited by small trials and different extract preparations.

### Berberine

Berberine, an alkaloid from several plant species, has shown cholesterol-lowering potential in some studies, mostly from China. A meta-analysis of 27 trials found that berberine with lifestyle change lowered total and LDL cholesterol more than lifestyle change alone, with no serious side effects reported, but the trials were of limited quality [88]. Berberine has its own page.

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Plant Sterols and Stanols

Under the current FDA health claim, eating at least these daily amounts with a diet that is low in saturated fat and cholesterol may lower heart disease risk [62]. The free-sterol row reflects the FDA's 2000 review and 2010 proposed rule [63]:

| Form | Minimum Daily Dose | Notes |
|------|:-:|-------|
| Plant sterol esters | 1,300 mg (equivalent to ~800 mg free sterols) | Divided over 2+ servings with meals |
| Plant stanol esters | 3,400 mg (equivalent to ~2,000 mg free stanols) | Divided over 2+ servings with meals |
| Free sterols | Not in the current claim; the FDA's 2000 review found 800 mg a day or more effective, and its 2010 proposed rule would set 2 g a day | The 2010 proposal found the evidence for free sterols in supplements limited and inconsistent; until a final rule, the FDA said it would consider enforcement discretion for claims that follow the proposal |

Trials have used up to about 3 g a day: 1.8 g/day of sterols lowered LDL cholesterol over 21 days [45], and the LDL-lowering effect keeps rising up to about 3 g a day [7]. A dose-response meta-analysis described the LDL-lowering effect with a nonlinear curve that rises with dose toward a maximum [25].

**Take them with meals:** Take plant sterols and stanols with meals, because they work by blocking cholesterol absorption in the intestine. In a study the FDA reviewed, a sterol-enriched yogurt lowered cholesterol significantly more when eaten with a meal than when eaten fasting [63]. In another trial, a sterol yogurt drink lowered LDL cholesterol by 9.3% to 9.5% when taken with a meal, but only by 5.1% to 6.9% without one; the trial was sponsored by Unilever [89]. A meta-analysis found that a single morning dose had no significant effect on LDL cholesterol [26]. One small trial disagreed: a sterol yogurt eaten as an afternoon snack lowered total cholesterol, while the mealtime dose did not differ from control, and neither changed LDL cholesterol significantly [90].

### Psyllium

Trials have used 3 to 20.4 g a day of psyllium [37]; the most common trial dose is about 10.2 g a day [13][59]. The US FDA health claim for psyllium is based on 7 g or more a day of soluble fiber from psyllium seed husk [98]. Mix each dose with at least 8 ounces (a full glass) of water or other fluid [66]. The label advises new users to start with 1 serving a day and increase gradually, which may help with bloating and gas [66].

### Beta-Glucan

Trials that lowered LDL cholesterol used at least 3 g a day of oat beta-glucan [14], from oats, oat bran or purified supplements.

### Red Yeast Rice

Because monacolin K content varies more than 60-fold between products [19], the dose on a label says little about the statin dose inside. In a trial in people who had stopped statins because of muscle pain, red yeast rice 1,800 mg twice a day lowered LDL cholesterol [84]. Consult a healthcare provider before use, as red yeast rice acts as an unregulated statin [17].

### Omega-3 Fatty Acids

To lower high triglycerides, prescription omega-3 products are used at 4 g a day [22]; a 2002 American Heart Association statement recommended 2 to 4 g a day of EPA plus DHA [22]. For people with heart disease, the American Heart Association recommends about 1 g a day of EPA plus DHA, preferably from oily fish [99]. Higher doses should be used under medical supervision due to potential bleeding risk [73].

### Policosanol

Trials from the Cuban group used 5 to 40 mg a day [64]. A German trial of 10, 20, 40 or 80 mg a day found no effect beyond placebo, so even these doses may not be effective [64].

### Pantethine

The sole positive study used 600 mg/day for 8 weeks followed by 900 mg/day for an additional 8 weeks [23]. Evidence is too limited for a firm dosing recommendation.

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Plant Sterols and Stanols

At about 2 to 2.5 g a day, products with added plant stanol or sterol esters lowered LDL cholesterol without any reported side effects [8]. In a 10-week trial, a very high intake of plant stanols as esters left liver enzymes, blood cells and vitamins A and D unchanged, but lowered blood beta-carotene (Gylling et al., *Clin Nutr*, 2010) [49]. Long-term safety data are limited, especially in children [91].

**Sitosterolemia contraindication:** Sitosterolemia (also called phytosterolemia) is a rare inherited disorder in which plant sterols build up in the body, and it is linked to a marked increase in atherosclerosis [51]. Its treatment includes cutting phytosterols from the diet [74], so people with sitosterolemia should not take beta-sitosterol or other plant sterol supplements.

**Gastrointestinal effects:** Some people get digestive upset with beta-sitosterol. See your doctor if it does not settle.

**Sexual side effects:** A few men taking beta-sitosterol for prostate symptoms have reported erectile problems or a lower sex drive. Tell your doctor if this happens.

**Liver enzymes:** Raised liver enzymes have been reported; if you have liver disease, ask your doctor first. A trial of a very high stanol intake found liver enzymes unchanged [49].

**Carotenoid depletion:** A meta-analysis of randomized trials found that about 2.5 g a day of sterols or stanols lowered blood beta-carotene and some other carotenoids, though levels stayed within normal ranges, and did not affect vitamins A and D (Baumgartner et al., *Eur J Nutr*, 2017; Katan et al., *Mayo Clin Proc*, 2003) [50][51].

**Elevated plant sterol blood levels:** Sterol-enriched foods raise blood sitosterol by about 31% and campesterol by about 37%, though plant sterols stay below 1% of the sterols in the blood [92]. Experts have asked whether this adds heart risk, given the marked atherosclerosis seen in inherited phytosterolemia, and judged the risk largely hypothetical [51]. A genetic study suggested that higher sitosterol levels may raise coronary risk [93], while a German expert review found no sign of harm in observational studies and argued that such genetic studies cannot settle the question [94]. In a 12-week trial in 240 adults, 3 g a day of plant sterols in a low-fat spread lowered LDL cholesterol by 6.7% but neither improved nor worsened artery function (Ras et al., *Am J Clin Nutr*, 2015) [52].

### Soluble Fiber (Psyllium)

Psyllium is generally well tolerated; in one trial, simvastatin plus 15 g of psyllium was well tolerated [83]. Common side effects include:
- Mild bloating, gas, and cramping, particularly when starting treatment or with inadequate fluid intake
- These symptoms typically resolve after 1-2 weeks as the gut microbiome adjusts
- Always take psyllium with at least 8 ounces (a full glass) of water or other fluid; without enough fluid it can swell and block the throat or esophagus and cause choking [66]
- Do not take psyllium if you have trouble swallowing [66]
- If you get chest pain, vomiting, or trouble swallowing or breathing after a dose, get medical help at once [66]

### Red Yeast Rice

Red yeast rice carries the most significant safety concerns among cholesterol-lowering supplements because monacolin K is structurally identical to lovastatin [17]:

- **Muscle damage:** Products with significant monacolin K can have the same potential muscle side effects as statins, including myopathy and rhabdomyolysis [17][67].
- **Liver injury:** Such products can also cause liver damage, as statins can [17]. The lovastatin label rules lovastatin out in active liver disease and urges caution with heavy alcohol use or past liver disease [67], so check with your doctor first if either applies to you.
- **Kidney damage and citrinin:** Products can also cause kidney damage, and some contain citrinin, which can harm the kidneys; in a 2021 analysis of 37 products, only one had citrinin below the European Union limit [17].
- **Other side effects:** Digestive problems such as diarrhea, nausea and stomach pain have been reported [17].
- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** Red yeast rice products are not recommended for people who are pregnant or breastfeeding [17].

### Omega-3 Fatty Acids

Fish oil supplements are generally well tolerated at typical doses:
- **Common mild side effects:** Fish oil can cause a fishy aftertaste, bad breath, heartburn, nausea, diarrhea or rash
- **High-dose risks:** Omega-3 fatty acids may prolong bleeding time [73], and doses of 2 to 15 g a day of EPA and/or DHA might increase bleeding time [99]; very high doses may also raise stroke risk in some cases, so ask your doctor before taking high doses
- **LDL increase:** EPA plus DHA products may raise LDL cholesterol [22][73]

### Policosanol

In a German trial, policosanol was tolerated well without serious adverse events [64]. In a small trial by the Cuban research group behind most of the positive policosanol trials, policosanol reduced platelet aggregation in healthy volunteers; nose and gum bleeding were reported among those taking aspirin, alone or with policosanol [65]. Other reports describe complaints that may include stomach upset, thirst or weight loss; migraine, dizziness, drowsiness, poor sleep or irritability; painful urination; and skin rash. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

### Pantethine

Limited safety data. The available studies suggest it is generally well tolerated, but the evidence base is too small for comprehensive safety assessment [23]. In one small 1981 study, pantethine reduced platelet hyperaggregation, so it may increase the risk of bleeding [58]. A few people report loose stools or wind with pantethine. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

### Niacin

High-dose niacin can cause [68]:
- **Flushing:** The most common side effect: warmth, redness, itching or tingling of the skin
- **Gastrointestinal upset:** Nausea, vomiting, diarrhea
- **Hepatotoxicity:** Raised liver enzymes; severe liver damage has occurred when sustained-release niacin replaced immediate-release niacin at the same dose
- **Hyperglycemia:** Higher fasting blood glucose, and worse glucose control in people with diabetes [68][87]
- **Hyperuricemia:** Raised uric acid, so use it with caution if you are prone to gout [68]

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Sterols/Stanols and Statins

Adding sterols or stanols to a statin lowers LDL cholesterol further (by 10% in one trial) [30][31][32], and an expert panel found no adverse signals at 2 g a day [5]. Sterols lower blood beta-carotene and some other carotenoids [50][51].

### Red Yeast Rice and Statins

Red yeast rice should NOT be combined with prescription statins. Because monacolin K is identical to lovastatin, taking both adds to the risk of statin side effects, including myopathy, rhabdomyolysis and liver damage [17][67]. Red yeast rice can have the same drug interactions as statins [17]. The lovastatin label rules out strong CYP3A4 inhibitors such as itraconazole and clarithromycin, says to avoid gemfibrozil, and urges caution with other fibrates or with niacin at 1 g a day or more, because these drugs can also cause muscle damage; cyclosporine and grapefruit juice raise lovastatin levels [67]. Do not combine red yeast rice with a statin, gemfibrozil, cyclosporine, or strong CYP3A4 inhibitors such as clarithromycin or itraconazole, and avoid grapefruit juice; check with your doctor first before combining it with other fibrates or niacin [67].

### Omega-3 Fatty Acids and Anticoagulants

Omega-3 fatty acids may prolong bleeding time. The prescription omega-3 label advises that people also taking an anticoagulant or antiplatelet drug, such as warfarin, clopidogrel or aspirin, be monitored periodically [73].

### Soluble Fiber and Medication Absorption

Bulk-forming fibers like psyllium may affect how well other medicines work. US psyllium labels advise taking prescription medicines by mouth at least 2 hours before or 2 hours after psyllium [66].

### Policosanol and Anticoagulants

Policosanol may inhibit platelet aggregation and should be used cautiously with antiplatelet or anticoagulant drugs [65]. In one small trial, nose and gum bleeding were reported in volunteers taking aspirin, alone or with policosanol [65].

### Beta-Sitosterol and Thrombin Inhibitors

In laboratory and mouse studies, beta-sitosterol blocked thrombin, a clotting enzyme, and thinned the blood of mice when given by mouth (Gogoi et al., *J Nat Prod*, 2018) [56]. An added effect with anticoagulants, such as dabigatran, is a theoretical risk; it has not been studied in people.

## 7. Dietary Sources

While supplements can help lower cholesterol, dietary modifications remain the foundation of cholesterol management. Several foods naturally contain cholesterol-lowering compounds.

### Plant Sterol- and Stanol-Rich Foods

Most fruits and vegetables contain some phytosterols, and mango, citrus, cauliflower, nuts and legumes are high in them [95]. Even so, the trials used about 2 g a day [5], far more than ordinary foods supply. That is why trials use fortified spreads, yogurt drinks, milk and supplements [26].

### Soluble Fiber-Rich Foods

Foods rich in soluble fiber, such as whole oats, whole barley, legumes, peas, beans, flax seeds, apples and citrus fruit, have also been linked to lower cholesterol [4].

### Omega-3 Fatty Acid-Rich Foods

Oily fish such as mackerel, sardines and salmon are the richest food sources of EPA and DHA; flax and chia seeds give a plant omega-3 [95].

### Cholesterol-Lowering Dietary Patterns

Beyond individual foods, overall dietary patterns lower cholesterol:

- **Portfolio diet:** Combines plant sterols, viscous fiber, plant protein such as soy, and nuts. Added to a cholesterol-lowering diet, it cut LDL cholesterol by about 17% across 7 trial comparisons [96]. In a 1-month trial in 46 adults with high cholesterol, it lowered LDL cholesterol by 28.6%, close to the 30.9% seen with lovastatin [97].
- **Reducing saturated fat and trans fat intake** is fundamental: replace saturated fats, such as those in red meat and full-fat dairy, with unsaturated fats from nuts, seeds, avocados and olive oil [95].
