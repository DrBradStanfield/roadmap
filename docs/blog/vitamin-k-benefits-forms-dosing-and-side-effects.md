## 1. Overview

Vitamin K is a group of fat-soluble vitamins sharing a common 2-methyl-1,4-naphthoquinone ring structure. The designation "K" comes from the German word "Koagulation," reflecting its discovery in the 1920s by Danish biochemist Henrik Dam as an anti-hemorrhagic factor essential for blood clotting [1][2]. In 1943, Dam and American biochemist Edward Doisy shared the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine for discovering vitamin K and elucidating its chemical structure [2].

The vitamin K family includes two naturally occurring forms [1][3][4]:

- **Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone)** — The primary plant-derived form, found at high concentrations in green leafy vegetables and at lower levels in vegetable oils. Phylloquinone has a phytyl side chain and accounts for approximately 75-90% of dietary vitamin K intake in Western diets. A synthetic version, phytonadione, is the form most commonly used in medical settings.
- **Vitamin K2 (menaquinones)** — A group of bacterial-origin compounds designated MK-4 through MK-13 based on the length of their polyisoprenoid side chains. The most well-studied subtypes are MK-4 (menaquinone-4), MK-7 (menaquinone-7), and MK-9. Menaquinones are found in modest amounts in fermented foods, some cheeses, butter, and meats. Gut bacteria also synthesize menaquinones, primarily long-chain forms (MK-9 to MK-13), though the extent of their contribution to human vitamin K status remains uncertain [5][6].

A third form, vitamin K3 (menadione), is a synthetic analog that served historically as a provitamin. Menadione was shown to cause hepatic cell damage in laboratory studies and has been banned from use in human dietary supplements by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration. It remains permitted in animal feed [1][7].

### Biochemical Functions

Vitamin K functions as a coenzyme for gamma-glutamyl carboxylase (GGCX), an enzyme that catalyzes the post-translational conversion of specific glutamate (Glu) residues to gamma-carboxyglutamate (Gla) residues in vitamin K-dependent proteins [3][8]. This gamma-carboxylation enables these proteins to bind calcium ions, which is essential for their biological activity. The reaction requires the reduced (hydroquinone) form of vitamin K and consumes CO2 and O2, oxidizing vitamin K to its epoxide form. The vitamin K epoxide reductase complex subunit 1 (VKORC1) then recycles the epoxide back to the active hydroquinone, creating the "vitamin K cycle" that allows repeated use of vitamin K molecules [8][9].

Key vitamin K-dependent proteins include [3][8][10]:

- **Coagulation factors** — Prothrombin (factor II) and factors VII, IX, and X are procoagulant proteins synthesized in the liver. Anticoagulant proteins C and S are also vitamin K-dependent. Human prothrombin contains 10 Gla residues that must be carboxylated for proper function.
- **Osteocalcin** — A non-collagenous bone protein synthesized by osteoblasts, constituting 10-20% of non-collagenous bone protein. Carboxylated osteocalcin binds calcium and hydroxyapatite crystals, contributing to bone mineralization and matrix stabilization. Undercarboxylated osteocalcin (ucOC) is a biomarker of suboptimal vitamin K status in bone [10][11].
- **Matrix Gla protein (MGP)** — Expressed in vascular smooth muscle cells, bone, and cartilage. Carboxylated MGP is a potent inhibitor of vascular calcification, binding calcium deposits and preventing their accumulation in arterial walls. Undercarboxylated MGP (ucMGP) and its dephosphorylated form (dp-ucMGP) are biomarkers of functional vitamin K insufficiency. Dp-ucMGP levels exceeding 500 pmol/L are associated with a 2- to 3-fold increased risk of cardiovascular disease events and mortality [12][13][14].
- **Growth arrest-specific protein 6 (Gas6)** — Involved in cell signaling, cell growth, and apoptosis [3].

### Absorption, Transport, and Metabolism

Ingested vitamin K is incorporated into mixed micelles via the action of bile salts and pancreatic enzymes, and absorbed by enterocytes of the small intestine (primarily the jejunum and ileum) through passive diffusion or via the Niemann-Pick C1-like 1 (NPC1L1) transporter [1][15][16]. Absorption requires dietary fat; consuming vegetables with fat significantly enhances phylloquinone absorption compared to fat-free conditions [16].

Following absorption, vitamin K is incorporated into chylomicrons, secreted into the lymphatic system, transported to the liver, and repackaged into very low-density lipoproteins (VLDL) and low-density lipoproteins (LDL) for distribution to tissues [1][3][15]. Vitamin K is present in the liver, brain, heart, pancreas, and bone [3][17].

Phylloquinone (K1) has a short plasma half-life of approximately 1-2 hours, reflecting rapid hepatic uptake and utilization. In contrast, MK-7 has an extended half-life of approximately 72 hours (up to 3 days), allowing prolonged circulation and resulting in 7- to 8-fold higher serum accumulation during daily intake [18][19]. MK-4 is absorbed as efficiently as MK-7 but has a shorter half-life similar to K1 [1].

Phylloquinone is preferentially taken up by the liver, while menaquinones (particularly MK-4 and MK-7) are directed to extrahepatic tissues including bones and arterial walls [15][19]. The body also converts phylloquinone to MK-4 via a hepatic pathway involving cytochrome P450 enzyme CYP4F2 and UbiA prenyltransferase domain-containing protein 1 (UBIAD1). This conversion is unidirectional — there is no established reverse pathway [20][21].

Unlike other fat-soluble vitamins, vitamin K is not stored in substantial amounts. The total body pool is estimated at 17-194 mcg (mean approximately 88 mcg), with a rapid turnover rate of about 1.5 days [22]. The body retains only about 30-40% of an oral physiological dose, while approximately 20% is excreted in urine and 40-50% in feces via bile [3][17].

### Vitamin K Status Assessment

Vitamin K status is not routinely assessed except in individuals taking anticoagulants or with bleeding disorders. The only clinically significant indicator is prothrombin time (PT), which measures the time for blood to clot; prolongation greater than 1.5 times normal with normal platelet count and fibrinogen suggests vitamin K deficiency [3][23]. In healthy people, fasting plasma phylloquinone concentrations range from 0.29 to 2.64 nmol/L (0.2 to 1.0 ng/mL), though plasma levels below this range do not necessarily indicate clinical deficiency because they do not capture the contribution of menaquinones [3][24].

More sensitive markers include [23][24][25]:

- **PIVKA-II (protein induced by vitamin K absence-II)** — Also known as undercarboxylated prothrombin. Elevated levels (above 2 ng/mL) reflect inadequate carboxylation before overt coagulation changes occur. Particularly useful in newborn screening.
- **Percentage of undercarboxylated osteocalcin (%ucOC)** — Levels exceeding 20% signal suboptimal vitamin K status for bone health.
- **Desphospho-uncarboxylated MGP (dp-ucMGP)** — Elevated levels indicate functional vitamin K insufficiency for vascular health.

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Vitamin K1 (Phylloquinone)

Phylloquinone is the primary dietary form of vitamin K, comprising 75-90% of total vitamin K intake in typical Western diets [15]. It features a phytyl side chain (a 20-carbon, partially unsaturated isoprenoid chain derived from chlorophyll) attached to the naphthoquinone ring [26]. In its pure form, K1 appears as a yellow, viscous oil that is fat-soluble but poorly soluble in water [26].

Phylloquinone from plant foods is tightly bound to chloroplasts, substantially limiting its bioavailability. The body absorbs only 4-17% as much phylloquinone from spinach as from a tablet [1][3]. Absorption from oils and dietary supplements is considerably higher, with the free form achieving approximately 80% absorption [1]. Consuming vegetables with fat improves phylloquinone absorption, but the amount absorbed is still lower than from oils or supplements [1].

Food processing affects phylloquinone content: boiling vegetables can reduce K1 content by 20-50% through leaching into cooking water, while steaming preserves more of the nutrient [27]. K1 content in plants can also vary seasonally, with higher levels often observed during summer months [27].

### Vitamin K2 as MK-7 (Menaquinone-7)

MK-7 is a long-chain menaquinone with seven isoprenoid units in its side chain. It is primarily obtained from natto (fermented soybean), which contains approximately 1,100 mcg per 100 g [1][28]. MK-7 is also produced industrially: traditionally from natto fermentation (often labeled "natural MK-7"), and more recently through synthetic processes using flower extracts such as geraniol or farnesol [1].

The key pharmacokinetic advantage of MK-7 is its extended plasma half-life of approximately 72 hours, compared to 1-2 hours for both K1 and MK-4 [18][19]. This results in substantially more stable serum levels and 7- to 8-fold higher serum accumulation during prolonged daily intake compared to K1 [18]. A study by Schurgers et al. (2007) suggested that if taken on a daily basis, 25 mcg of MK-7 may be more potent than 100 mcg of K1 because it persists in the body for much longer [18].

MK-7 from fermented and animal sources generally achieves higher absorption rates (approximately 20-30%) than phylloquinone from leafy greens (5-10%), facilitated by its association with dietary fats [27][28]. Supplementation with MK-7 at doses of 180-360 mcg daily for 3-12 months has been shown to increase carboxylated osteocalcin levels, reduce ucOC by up to 60%, boost carboxylated MGP by approximately 70%, and decrease dp-ucMGP by 50-86% [10][12].

Soy-derived MK-7 is the most common supplement form. For individuals with soy allergy, MenaQ7 (derived from chickpeas) is available as a soy-free alternative [1]. A similar-sounding ingredient, MenaQ7 Natto MK-7, is derived from soy, so label reading is important [1].

### Vitamin K2 as MK-4 (Menaquinone-4)

MK-4 is the shortest-chain menaquinone, with four isoprenoid units. It is produced in certain animal tissues and occurs in small amounts in meats, egg yolks (approximately 15-32 mcg per 100 g), and some dairy products [1][28]. MK-4 is unique among menaquinones in that the body can synthesize it from phylloquinone through a hepatic conversion pathway involving CYP4F2 and UBIAD1, without requiring bacterial action [20][21].

MK-4 is absorbed as efficiently as MK-7 but has a significantly shorter half-life, similar to K1 (1-2 hours) [1][18]. Despite this shorter half-life, MK-4 accumulates preferentially in extrahepatic tissues and has been the form most extensively studied for bone health at pharmacological doses. In Japan and other parts of Asia, MK-4 at a dose of 45 mg (45,000 mcg) is approved as a pharmacological treatment for osteoporosis [3][29].

### Other Menaquinone Forms

Longer-chain menaquinones (MK-8 through MK-13) are produced by gut bacteria and are present in fermented foods. Cheeses provide 10-50 mcg per 100 g of longer-chain menaquinones like MK-8 and MK-9 [28]. These forms likely contribute to vitamin K status but data on their specific bioavailability and clinical effects in humans are limited [1][6]. The gut microbiota's output of vitamin K is estimated to supply 10-50% of the human requirement, predominantly as long-chain menaquinones, though absorption is limited because synthesis occurs in the distal colon, beyond the primary sites of nutrient uptake [5][30].

### Comparative Pharmacokinetics

| Property | K1 (Phylloquinone) | K2 as MK-4 | K2 as MK-7 |
|----------|-------------------|-------------|-------------|
| Plasma half-life | 1-2 hours | 1-2 hours | ~72 hours |
| Primary tissue uptake | Liver (hepatic) | Extrahepatic (bone, arteries) | Extrahepatic (bone, arteries) |
| Serum accumulation during daily use | Baseline | Moderate | 7-8-fold higher than K1 |
| Absorption from food | 4-17% (leafy greens) | ~20-30% (animal foods) | ~20-30% (fermented foods) |
| Absorption from supplements | ~80% | High | High |
| Typical supplement dose | 100-1,000 mcg | 100-45,000 mcg | 90-375 mcg |

A longer half-life does not automatically translate to greater clinical activity. For example, MK-4 at pharmacological doses (45 mg/day) has demonstrated bone benefits in multiple trials, while MK-7 at lower doses (90-375 mcg) has shown mixed results for bone outcomes despite its more favorable pharmacokinetics [1].

### Fat-Soluble Vitamin Interactions

A laboratory experiment using intestinal cells from mice found that uptake of vitamin K was reduced by approximately half in the presence of vitamins A, D, and E — likely due to competition for absorption among fat-soluble vitamins [31]. Interestingly, vitamin K did not significantly reduce uptake of the other fat-soluble vitamins. While this has not been confirmed in human studies, it may be prudent to take vitamin K at least 3 hours apart from high-dose vitamin D supplements, and to note that combined formulas are rarely clinically tested for ingredient absorption [1][31]. Large doses of vitamin E may specifically antagonize vitamin K, making it less effective [32].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Bone Health

#### Overview of the Vitamin K–Bone Connection

Low vitamin K consumption or impaired vitamin K status is associated with lower bone mass and higher risk of hip fracture in older individuals [33][34]. Vitamin K is required for the carboxylation of osteocalcin, which is essential for proper calcium binding and bone mineralization. High serum levels of undercarboxylated osteocalcin are associated with lower bone mineral density (BMD) [3][10][11]. However, whether supplemental vitamin K meaningfully strengthens bones or reduces fracture risk remains an active area of debate, with results varying by form, dose, and study population.

A 2006 systematic review and meta-analysis by Cockayne et al. included 13 randomized controlled trials (predominantly conducted in Japan involving postmenopausal women, with durations of 6-36 months) [29]. Twelve of the 13 trials found that supplementation with either phytonadione (K1) or MK-4 improved bone mineral density. Seven trials provided fracture data for meta-analysis — all used MK-4 at either 15 mg/day (1 trial) or 45 mg/day (6 trials). MK-4 supplementation significantly reduced rates of hip fractures, vertebral fractures, and all nonvertebral fractures [29].

However, subsequent trials have produced more mixed results, particularly for lower doses and when participants are already receiving adequate calcium and vitamin D.

#### Vitamin K1 Bone Trials

**Braam et al. (2003):** A study of 162 healthy postmenopausal women in the Netherlands found that very high-dose vitamin K1 (1,000 mcg/day) plus calcium, vitamin D (320 IU), and magnesium for 3 years resulted in significantly less bone loss compared to the same supplements without K1 or placebo [35].

**Booth et al. (2008):** A larger, subsequent 3-year study using a lower dose of K1 (500 mcg/day) plus calcium (600 mg) and vitamin D (400 IU) failed to show any benefit for bone loss compared to the same calcium and vitamin D without vitamin K. This study included 381 postmenopausal women receiving either 1 mg K1, 45 mg MK-4, or placebo daily for 12 months (all with calcium and vitamin D supplements). Both K1 and MK-4 groups had significantly lower undercarboxylated osteocalcin, but there were no significant differences in BMD at the lumbar spine or proximal femur among any groups [36][37].

**Cheung et al. (2008):** A study of 440 postmenopausal women in Canada (average age 59) found that extremely high-dose K1 (5,000 mcg/day) for 2-4 years did not prevent bone loss compared to placebo, but it did reduce the incidence of vertebral fractures — a secondary outcome. Over the study period, only 9 women in the vitamin K group experienced a fracture compared to 20 in the placebo group. No adverse events were reported [38].

**Crohn's disease:** Vitamin K deficiency can occur in people with Crohn's disease due to malabsorption, and low vitamin K levels have been associated with low BMD in this population. However, a study giving K1 to people with Crohn's, low bone mass, and vitamin K deficiency found no improvement in bone mass [39].

#### Vitamin K2 (MK-4) Bone Trials

Several small clinical trials in Japan, Indonesia, and China found that very high-dose MK-4 (45,000 mcg/day = 45 mg/day) taken for 1-3 years improved bone mineral density and reduced fracture risk in postmenopausal women with osteoporosis, without toxic effects [40]. One of these studies demonstrated that 45,000 mcg of MK-4 was the minimum effective dose — a lower dose of 15,000 mcg did not show benefit [41].

In Japan and other parts of Asia, this pharmacological dose of MK-4 (45 mg) is approved as a treatment for osteoporosis [3][29]. The European Food Safety Authority has also approved a health claim stating that "a cause and effect relationship has been established between the dietary intake of vitamin K and the maintenance of normal bone" [42]. The U.S. FDA has not authorized a similar health claim.

#### Vitamin K2 (MK-7) Bone Trials

**Knapen et al. (2013):** A 3-year, placebo-controlled study of 244 healthy postmenopausal women in the Netherlands found that MK-7 at 180 mcg/day decreased the age-related decline in bone mineral content and density at the lumbar spine and femoral neck, but not the total hip [43]. Bone strength was also favorably affected. Improvements were statistically significant after 3 years, but not earlier. Notably, calcium and vitamin D supplements were not given as part of this study. A 2025 meta-analysis of RCTs confirmed these general findings, showing that vitamin K supplementation (including MK-7) enhances bone mineral density in middle-aged and older adults, with more pronounced effects in women [44].

**Ronn et al. (2020):** A study of 142 postmenopausal women with osteopenia in Denmark found that MK-7 at 375 mcg/day plus vitamin D3 (1,520 IU) and calcium (800 mg) for 3 years did not slow the decrease in BMD at the hip, femoral neck, or lumbar spine compared to vitamin D3 and calcium alone. Markers of bone turnover and bone microarchitecture were also similar between groups [45].

**Zhang et al. (2020):** A study of 311 older men and women in China using MK-7 at 90 mcg/day found no bone density benefit for men, nor for men and women combined. Among women, there was no benefit at the lumbar spine or hip, but loss of density at the femoral neck was slightly less than in the placebo group. The study found no additional benefit from adding vitamin D (400 IU) and calcium (500 mg) to vitamin K, nor benefit from a lower daily dose (50 mcg). A weakness was that the study was only single-blind [46].

#### Summary of Bone Evidence

The overall evidence suggests that vitamin K plays a role in bone metabolism through osteocalcin carboxylation, but the clinical significance of supplementation for fracture prevention remains uncertain. Very high-dose MK-4 (45 mg/day) appears to have the strongest evidence in Asian postmenopausal women with osteoporosis. MK-7 at 180 mcg/day showed benefit in one well-designed 3-year trial when calcium and vitamin D were not co-administered, but results are less promising when added on top of adequate calcium and vitamin D supplementation. The administration of vitamin D and/or calcium along with vitamin K may partly explain the discrepancy between studies — some researchers have noted the importance of considering baseline vitamin D status when comparing results [37].

### Cardiovascular Health

#### Observational Evidence

Low serum vitamin K levels have been associated with atherosclerosis. In the Rotterdam Study, a prospective cohort of 4,807 men and women aged 55 and older in the Netherlands, higher dietary menaquinone (K2) intake was inversely associated with coronary heart disease mortality [47]. Participants in the upper tertile of menaquinone intake (>32.7 mcg/day) had a 57% lower risk of coronary heart disease mortality compared to those in the lower tertile (<21.6 mcg/day). Phylloquinone (K1) intake had no effect on any outcome [47]. A separate analysis of 564 postmenopausal women confirmed that dietary menaquinone intake (but not phylloquinone) was inversely associated with coronary calcification [48].

These observational associations are supported by the known biological mechanism: vitamin K-dependent carboxylation of matrix Gla protein (MGP) is essential for preventing vascular calcification. Undercarboxylated MGP cannot exert this protective effect, contributing to medial and intimal calcification [12][13].

#### MK-7 Cardiovascular Trials

Despite the promising observational data, most clinical trials of MK-7 supplementation have failed to demonstrate significant cardiovascular benefit:

**Knapen et al. (2015):** A double-blind study found that 180 mcg/day of MK-7 (MenaQ7, NattoPharma ASA) taken for 3 years reduced arterial stiffness in healthy postmenopausal women, especially in those with high arterial stiffness at baseline. However, there was no improvement in endothelial dysfunction, which is more closely associated with cardiovascular events such as heart attack [49].

**de Vries et al. (2025):** The same dose of MK-7 (180 mcg/day) did not show meaningful benefit in a 1-year study of 87 postmenopausal women (average age 64). Although vascular stiffness did not worsen as much with MK-7 compared to placebo based on Young's modulus, there were no improvements in vascular stiffness index, pulse wave velocity, blood pressure, carotid artery wall thickness, or carotid artery diameter. A separate group of 78 pre- and perimenopausal women (average age 57) also showed no significant improvements in any cardiovascular measure [50].

**Bartstra et al. (2021):** A post-hoc analysis of a 6-month study among people with type 2 diabetes and history of cardiovascular disease found that 360 mcg/day of MK-7 did not slow the progression of arterial calcification compared to placebo [51].

**Witham et al. (2020):** A double-blind randomized study of 159 people with stage 3 or 4 chronic kidney disease (average age 66) found that 400 mcg/day of MK-7 for 12 months did not reduce vascular stiffness or calcification, or improve blood pressure, compared to placebo [52].

**Kurnatowska et al. (2015):** A study of 40 people (average age 58) with chronic kidney disease (stages 3-5) showed that 90 mcg/day of MK-7 plus 400 IU of vitamin D for approximately 9 months reduced the increase in carotid artery intima-media thickness by about 7.9% compared to vitamin D alone, but there was no significant difference in progression of coronary artery calcification [53].

**Diederichsen et al. (2022):** Despite improving vitamin K status to near normal ranges, supplementation with 750 mcg/day of MK-7 plus 1,000 IU of vitamin D for 2 years did not slow the progression of aortic valve calcification or stenosis in 333 men and women (average age 71) with mild aortic valve calcification. Supplementation also did not reduce the rate of heart valve replacement, cardiovascular events, or all-cause mortality. Most participants were taking statin and/or ACE inhibitor or beta-blocker medications [54].

**Hasific et al. (2025):** Further analysis of the Diederichsen cohort confirmed that MK-7 plus vitamin D for 2 years did not reduce inflammation of epicardial or pericoronary adipose tissue, nor did it significantly reduce blood markers of general inflammation [55].

**Hasific et al. (2023) — subgroup analysis:** However, a post-hoc analysis of the same trial found a potential benefit among those who started with the highest levels of coronary artery calcification. In this subgroup, MK-7 plus vitamin D reduced the progression of calcification compared to placebo, and fewer people experienced adverse cardiovascular events (myocardial infarction, coronary revascularization, all-cause mortality) — 1.9% versus 6.7% in the placebo group [56].

#### Vitamin K1 Cardiovascular Trials

**Shea et al. (2009):** A study in Boston showed that people with pre-existing coronary artery calcification who took a multivitamin including K1 (500 mcg) along with calcium and vitamin D for 3 years had 6% less progression of calcification compared to those who received the same calcium and vitamin D plus a multivitamin without K1. However, among people without pre-existing calcification, an equal percentage developed calcification regardless of K1 supplementation [57].

**Bellinge et al. (2021):** A study in Australia investigated vitamin K1 supplementation in men and women (average age 66) with type 1 or type 2 diabetes and moderate coronary artery calcification. Participants taking 10 mg of K1 once daily for 3 months showed a 65% decrease in the risk of developing new calcified lesions in the coronary arteries and a 73% decrease in the aorta compared to placebo. These results were similar for those taking or not taking colchicine [58].

#### Summary of Cardiovascular Evidence

The overall cardiovascular evidence is mixed. Observational studies consistently link higher dietary K2 intake with reduced coronary heart disease risk, and the biological mechanism through MGP is well-established. However, most interventional trials of MK-7 supplementation have failed to demonstrate significant benefit in the general population or in people with chronic kidney disease. The strongest signal for benefit is among people with pre-existing, extensive coronary artery calcification, where both K1 and K2 may slow progression. For people without existing calcification, supplementation does not appear to prevent its development.

### Nocturnal Leg Cramps

A randomized, placebo-controlled trial by Tan et al. (2024) studied 199 older adults in China who took 180 mcg of MK-7 every night for 8 weeks [59]. Compared to placebo, those given MK-7 experienced a dramatic reduction in nocturnal leg cramps:

- **Frequency:** Reduced from 2.6 per week at baseline to approximately 1 per week
- **Severity:** Significant reduction in cramp intensity
- **Duration:** Reduced from over 1 minute at baseline to under 10 seconds

Reductions were apparent within 1 week of supplementation, with further improvements over the first 4 weeks that continued for the rest of the study. No adverse events related to vitamin K2 were identified. The proposed mechanism involves vitamin K's effects on calcium channels in cells, reducing excessive muscular contractions [59][60].

### Cancer

Observational evidence on vitamin K and cancer risk is mixed and does not establish causation:

**Nimptsch et al. (2010):** A multi-year population study in Germany found that people who consumed larger amounts of vitamin K2-containing foods (such as cheese) had a statistically lower risk of dying from cancer. Men (but not women) also had a significant decrease in cancer incidence, particularly prostate and lung cancers. No such associations were found for K1 intake [61].

**Wang et al. (2020):** A larger population study in the United States among 51,662 women followed for a median of 13.6 years found that higher dietary intake of K2 (from butter and cheeses) was associated with a 26% higher risk of breast cancer and a 71% increased risk of death from breast cancer [62]. No associations were found for K1.

These studies reflect dietary K2 intake from foods (primarily dairy), not supplemental vitamin K, and cannot distinguish the effect of vitamin K from confounding factors in high-fat dairy consumption.

Emerging preclinical research suggests potential anti-cancer mechanisms for vitamin K. A 2024 study demonstrated that a vitamin K precursor induces oxidative stress in prostate cancer cells, inhibiting proliferation via PI3K/AKT signaling disruption [63]. Downregulation of VKORC1L1 (a vitamin K cycle enzyme) has been shown to activate ferroptosis pathways, suppressing tumor growth in models of pancreatic and prostate cancers [64]. These are early-stage findings that have not yet been translated into human clinical data.

### COVID-19

A blood marker of low vitamin K levels (dp-ucMGP) has been associated with COVID-19 infection and unfavorable outcomes:

**Dofferhoff et al. (2020):** Patients with COVID-19 had significantly higher dp-ucMGP levels (indicating low vitamin K) compared to controls without COVID-19. Dp-ucMGP levels were significantly higher in patients with unfavorable outcomes (ventilation and/or death) compared to those discharged without ventilation, even after adjusting for age, gender, and medication use. Reduced vitamin K status was also associated with accelerated elastin breakdown [65].

**Linneberg et al. (2020, preprint):** Average dp-ucMGP levels were significantly higher among hospitalized COVID-19 patients (1,022 pmol/L) compared to controls (509 pmol/L). Those who died had dp-ucMGP of 1,445 pmol/L versus 877 pmol/L in survivors. Every doubling of dp-ucMGP increased death risk by 50% [66].

The proposed mechanisms include increased vitamin K consumption during severe illness depleting reserves, and the potential for low vitamin K to increase risk of abnormal blood clotting, venous thromboembolism, and elastin breakdown (contributing to lung damage) [65]. However, there is currently no direct evidence that vitamin K supplementation can prevent or treat COVID-19. Other conditions common in COVID-19 patients with poor outcomes (type 2 diabetes, hypertension) are independently associated with reduced vitamin K levels, making it difficult to establish causality [65][66].

### Blood Clotting

Vitamin K1 is well-established as effective in preventing and treating poor blood clotting (hypoprothrombinemia) caused by vitamin K deficiency or induced by medications [18]. Vitamin K2 has also been shown effective and may be more potent for clotting support than K1 [18]. Symptomatic vitamin K deficiency is rare in adults, typically resulting from severe malnutrition or malabsorption, or prolonged therapy with broad-spectrum antibiotics [1][3].

Among people on warfarin with unexplained INR instability, daily low-dose oral K1 (100-200 mcg) was previously recommended to improve clotting stability under medical supervision [67]. However, experts now advise against this practice after analysis of clinical trials showed that vitamin K supplementation did not reduce major bleeding events or other complications related to blood clotting in warfarin users [68][69].

### Inflammatory Bowel Disease

Emerging evidence from NHANES data analysis (2025) suggests associations between vitamin K intake and reduced inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) risk in certain subgroups [70]. This is an early observational finding requiring further investigation.

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Adequate Intakes

No Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) has been established for vitamin K due to insufficient data. Instead, the Food and Nutrition Board at the Institute of Medicine established Adequate Intakes (AIs) based on intake levels in healthy populations [3]:

| Age/Sex Group | AI (mcg/day) |
|---------------|-------------|
| Birth to 6 months | 2.0 |
| 7-12 months | 2.5 |
| Children 1-3 years | 30 |
| Children 4-8 years | 55 |
| Children 9-13 years | 60 |
| Adolescents 14-18 years | 75 |
| Males 19+ years | 120 |
| Females 19+ years | 90 |
| Pregnancy (all ages) | 75-90 |
| Lactation (all ages) | 75-90 |

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) sets a different AI of 1 mcg per kg body weight per day, equating to approximately 70 mcg/day for average adults [71]. In Germany, the Deutsche Gesellschaft fur Ernahrung recommends 60-80 mcg/day for adults [72].

There is no Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) established for vitamin K because of its low potential for toxicity. The FNB stated that "no adverse effects associated with vitamin K consumption from food or supplements have been reported in humans or animals" [3].

### Typical Intake in Populations

NHANES 2011-2012 data show that among U.S. adults age 20 and older, average daily vitamin K intake from foods is 122 mcg for women and 138 mcg for men. When supplements are included, intake rises to 164 mcg for women and 182 mcg for men [73]. Approximately two-thirds of the U.S. population has vitamin K intake below the AI, though the clinical significance of this is unclear given that overt deficiency is extremely rare and the AI is only an estimate [3][73].

### Doses Used in Clinical Research

The doses used in clinical trials far exceed the AI and vary substantially by form:

**Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone):**
- Bone health: 500-5,000 mcg/day (most trials)
- Cardiovascular (calcification): 500-10,000 mcg/day
- Neonatal prophylaxis: 0.5-1 mg single intramuscular dose

**Vitamin K2 as MK-4:**
- Bone health (pharmacological): 45,000 mcg/day (45 mg) — the dose approved in Japan for osteoporosis treatment
- Lower doses (15,000 mcg/day) have not shown benefit for bone [40][41]

**Vitamin K2 as MK-7:**
- Bone health: 90-375 mcg/day for 1-3 years [43][45][46]
- Cardiovascular health: 90-750 mcg/day for 6 months to 3 years [49][51][52][54]
- Nocturnal leg cramps: 180 mcg/day for 8 weeks [59]
- Over-the-counter supplements: typically 100-200 mcg per capsule [1]

### Practical Dosing Considerations

Dietary intake of approximately 250 mcg/day from food is associated with decreased hip fracture risk among older adults [33][34]. For MK-7 bone benefits, 180 mcg/day required 3 years of continuous use to produce statistically significant improvements in BMD [43].

Due to MK-7's longer half-life, doses may need to be adjusted when switching between vitamin K forms. However, a longer half-life does not automatically translate to greater clinical activity — MK-4 has shown bone benefits at high doses that MK-7 has not consistently replicated [1].

Most multivitamin/mineral supplements provide less than 75% of the Daily Value (120 mcg) for vitamin K. Dedicated vitamin K supplements typically provide a wider dose range, with some containing up to 4,050 mcg (5,063% DV) or more [7].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Safety Profile

Vitamins K1 and K2 are generally considered safe with a low potential for toxicity [1][3][74]. No Tolerable Upper Intake Level has been established. Typical supplemental doses of K1 up to 10 mg orally and MK-4 up to 45 mg/day have not been associated with toxicity in clinical studies. The European Food Safety Authority has similarly not established an upper intake level due to lack of evidence of adverse effects [74].

Vitamin K3 (menadione) is a different matter. It has demonstrated hepatotoxicity in laboratory studies and can cause hemolytic anemia at doses exceeding 5 mg/kg body weight. It has been banned for use in human supplements by the FDA [1][7][75].

### Specific Safety Considerations

**Soy allergy:** The MK-7 form of vitamin K2 is typically derived from natto, which is made from soy. People with soy allergy should seek chickpea-derived MK-7 (such as certain MenaQ7 formulations). Note that labels should be read carefully, as "MenaQ7 Natto MK-7" is soy-derived while "MenaQ7" from chickpeas is not [1].

**Sleep concerns:** Despite anecdotal reports that MK-7 may worsen sleep, a clinical study in Japan of 115 adults (average age 47) who took 100 mcg of MK-7 daily for 12 weeks showed no worsening of sleep parameters compared to non-supplementing controls [76]. Furthermore, some research has linked short sleep durations (less than 7 hours per night) with inadequate vitamin K intake from foods and supplements among women aged 19-50, though no such association was found in men [77].

**Breast cancer:** A U.S. population study found an association between higher dietary K2 intake (from butter and cheeses) and increased breast cancer risk [62]. However, this was observational and may reflect confounding from high-fat dairy consumption rather than vitamin K itself. There does not appear to be convincing evidence that supplementation with soy-based MK-7 increases breast cancer risk or recurrence [1].

**Thyroid function:** Although there is some concern that soy can affect thyroid function, there does not appear to be evidence of soy-based vitamin K supplements affecting thyroid function [1][78].

**Parenteral administration:** Intravenous vitamin K1 has been associated with rare anaphylactoid reactions (approximately 3 per 10,000 doses), attributed to the solubilizing agent Cremophor EL rather than vitamin K itself. These reactions can manifest as flushing, hypotension, or cardiovascular collapse. Oral administration does not carry this risk [79][80].

**Neonatal high-dose concerns:** High-dose intravenous K1 (e.g., 25-30 mg) in neonates has been linked to hyperbilirubinemia and hemolytic anemia, particularly in premature infants. Standard prophylactic doses of 0.5-1 mg do not pose this risk [79][81].

### Newborn Vitamin K Prophylaxis

Newborns are particularly vulnerable to vitamin K deficiency due to limited placental transfer of the vitamin, sterile gut lacking vitamin K-producing bacteria, and low vitamin K content of breast milk [81][82][83]. Plasma levels of vitamin K-dependent clotting factors in newborns are approximately 20-50% of adult values [82].

Without prophylaxis, the incidence of classical vitamin K deficiency bleeding (VKDB) can reach up to 1.7% (about 1 in 60 births), while late VKDB occurs in 4-7 per 100,000 births [82][84]. Late VKDB (occurring at 2-12 weeks of age) can manifest as sudden intracranial hemorrhage, which carries approximately 20% mortality [81][85].

The American Academy of Pediatrics recommends a single intramuscular dose of 0.5-1 mg phylloquinone (K1) at birth, which reduces the risk of late VKDB by 81-fold and nearly eliminates classical VKDB [84][86][87]. For parents who decline intramuscular administration, oral prophylaxis (three doses of 2 mg each — at birth, 1 week, and 1 month) achieves 70-90% efficacy but carries higher residual risk, particularly for late-onset cases [88].

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Warfarin and Vitamin K Antagonists

The most significant drug interaction is with warfarin (Coumadin) and similar vitamin K antagonists (phenprocoumon, acenocoumarol, tioclomarol) [3][8]. Warfarin exerts its anticoagulant effect by inhibiting VKORC1, reducing the availability of active vitamin K by approximately 90% and decreasing synthesis of functional clotting factors [89].

Vitamin K supplementation can counteract warfarin's effects by restoring clotting factor synthesis. This interaction can occur at remarkably low doses: MK-7 may interfere with warfarin at doses as low as 10 mcg/day due to its long half-life [18][90]. Some experts suggest that people taking warfarin should avoid MK-7 supplements entirely [90]. Although K1 supplements up to 100 mcg/day may not cause clinically relevant disturbances, experts now recommend against routine low-dose vitamin K supplementation for warfarin users due to limited evidence of benefit and potential for interaction at research doses (100-200 mcg/day) [68][69].

People taking warfarin should maintain consistent vitamin K intake from food without large fluctuations. One study found that people consuming less than 250 mcg of vitamin K daily from food required a warfarin dose of 4.4 mg, while those consuming more required 5.8 mg [3].

Genetic variations in VKORC1 significantly influence warfarin sensitivity. The -1639G>A polymorphism, which has an allele frequency of 22-38% in Asian populations, can necessitate 20-50% lower warfarin doses [91].

### Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs)

Newer blood-thinning drugs such as dabigatran (Pradaxa) and rivaroxaban (Xarelto) work through different mechanisms than warfarin and are not thought to be affected by vitamin K intake [92][93]. Unlike warfarin, which inhibits vitamin K recycling, these drugs directly inhibit specific clotting factors (thrombin for dabigatran; factor Xa for rivaroxaban).

### Antiplatelet Drugs

Aspirin and clopidogrel (Plavix) prevent platelets from aggregating, a mechanism distinct from vitamin K's role in clotting factor synthesis. There is no evidence of vitamin K interacting with these drugs [94].

### Antibiotics

Prolonged broad-spectrum antibiotic therapy can reduce vitamin K status by destroying vitamin K-producing bacteria in the gut. Cephalosporin antibiotics (such as cefoperazone) may have a compounded effect because they can also inhibit VKORC1, disrupting the vitamin K cycle directly [6][95][96]. However, vitamin K supplements are usually not needed unless antibiotic use extends beyond several weeks and is accompanied by poor dietary vitamin K intake [95].

### Bile Acid Sequestrants

Cholestyramine (Questran) and colestipol (Colestid) are used to reduce cholesterol but can also reduce absorption of vitamin K and other fat-soluble vitamins by binding bile acids needed for vitamin K absorption [95][97]. Vitamin K status should be monitored with long-term use of these drugs [97].

### Orlistat

Orlistat (Alli, Xenical), a weight-loss drug that reduces dietary fat absorption by about 30%, can also reduce absorption of fat-soluble vitamins including vitamin K [98]. Combining orlistat with warfarin therapy may cause a significant increase in prothrombin time [99]. Clinicians usually recommend that patients taking orlistat take a multivitamin supplement containing vitamin K [98][100].

### Statins

Some research suggests that statins may decrease vitamin K levels [101]. However, supplementing with vitamin K does not appear to improve heart-related outcomes (including coronary artery calcification and aortic valve calcification) in most statin users, although it may reduce the progression of calcification among people with the highest levels of existing calcification [54][56].

### St. John's Wort

St. John's wort induces cytochrome P450 enzymes (CYP3A4 and CYP2C9), which can accelerate warfarin metabolism and reduce its plasma levels, thereby decreasing anticoagulant activity and increasing thrombosis risk [102]. This is an indirect interaction relevant for people managing vitamin K intake alongside warfarin therapy.

Vitamin K is fat-soluble, so full-fat dairy products contain significantly more vitamin K than reduced-fat or non-fat versions. Full-fat cheddar cheese contains approximately 281 mcg total vitamin K per 100 g, while reduced-fat cheddar contains just 49 mcg. In general, reduced-fat or non-fat dairy products contain only 5-22% of the vitamin K found in their full-fat counterparts [1][103].

While turnip roots contain negligible vitamin K (0.1-0.2 mcg per 100 g), turnip greens are rich in K1 (300-500+ mcg per 100 g) — a distinction sometimes confused in nutrition sources [28].

### Impact of Gut Microbiota

Gut bacteria — particularly from the Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes phyla — synthesize menaquinones as part of their electron transport chains [5][30][104]. Key producers include Lactobacillus species (producing MK-7 and MK-8) and Bacteroides species (producing MK-10 and MK-11) [30]. The gut microbiota's contribution is estimated at 10-50% of the human vitamin K requirement, but absorption is limited because synthesis occurs in the distal colon [5][30].

Factors influencing microbial vitamin K production include antibiotic use (which can reduce synthesis by depleting producer bacteria) and high-fiber diets (which promote growth of vitamin K-synthesizing bacteria) [95][105].
