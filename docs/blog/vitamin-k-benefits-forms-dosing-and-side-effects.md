## Current view (reviewed 10 November 2026)

Two dedicated trials found that high-dose vitamin K2 as MK-7, 360 mcg in one and 720 mcg with vitamin D in the other, slowed the build-up of calcium in the coronary arteries after two years ([Brad, 3 Sep 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/why-arent-cardiologists-prescribing-vitamin-k2)). Calcium is how a blocked artery ends, not how it starts, and Brad reports that soft, non-calcified plaque did not differ from placebo in either trial, so there is potentially less calcium on the scan but the same amount of the dangerous plaque ([Brad, 3 Sep 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/why-arent-cardiologists-prescribing-vitamin-k2)). Nobody has shown whether these high doses reduce heart attacks, and at 360 to 720 mcg a day, three to eight times the adequate intake, Brad says K2 should be thought of as a drug, which has to prove that it is safe and that it reduces heart attacks before anyone prescribes it ([Brad, 3 Sep 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/why-arent-cardiologists-prescribing-vitamin-k2)). On current evidence, he sees no compelling reason to take vitamin K2 for heart health ([Brad, 29 Jun 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/does-vitamin-k2-actually-protect-your-heart-new-trial)). His advice is to reach the adequate intake of total vitamin K, 90 to 120 mcg a day: people who do not eat leafy greens, cheese, natto or eggs could consider a low-dose K2 supplement, but he would advise against taking more than about 90 to 120 mcg ([Brad, 3 Sep 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/why-arent-cardiologists-prescribing-vitamin-k2)). Meeting vitamin K needs is a good idea for bone strength: 180 mcg a day for three years slowed bone density loss at the spine and the neck of the hip in postmenopausal women, but fewer fractures have not been shown ([Brad, 3 Sep 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/why-arent-cardiologists-prescribing-vitamin-k2)). If you take warfarin, be extremely cautious with vitamin K2, because MK-7 moved the clotting test in at least 40 percent of volunteers at just 10 mcg a day, and some specialists advise avoiding it entirely; apixaban, rivaroxaban and dabigatran are not affected ([Brad, 3 Sep 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/why-arent-cardiologists-prescribing-vitamin-k2)).

## Key safety points

- If you take warfarin or a similar blood thinner, keep your vitamin K intake from food and supplements steady [1], and do not start, stop or change a vitamin K supplement without your prescriber or anticoagulation clinic [61].
- A daily dose of 10 mcg of MK-7 lowered the INR enough to matter in at least 40% of volunteers taking a warfarin-like drug, and the study authors advise avoiding MK-7 supplements on these drugs [2].
- Vitamin K is not expected to change the effect of apixaban, rivaroxaban or dabigatran [3], even as a reversal treatment [73][74][75].
- Orlistat taken with warfarin might cause a significant increase in prothrombin time, and orlistat, bile acid sequestrants and long courses of antibiotics can lower vitamin K status [1].
- MK-7 is often made from natto, a fermented soybean food, so people with a soy allergy should check the source on the label [4][1].
- Vitamin K3 (menadione) damaged liver cells in laboratory studies and is no longer used in supplements [1].
- No upper limit is set and no harm has been reported from vitamin K in food or supplements, but Brad advises against taking more than about 90 to 120 mcg [1][3].
- In trials, K1 and MK-7 caused no more side effects than placebo; drug-level doses of MK-4 may cause stomach discomfort and diarrhea [5][6][7].

## 1. Overview

Vitamin K is the generic name for a family of fat-soluble compounds with a common chemical structure of 2-methyl-1,4-naphthoquinone [1].

The vitamin K family includes two naturally occurring forms [1]:

- **Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone):** The main dietary form, present mostly in green leafy vegetables and also in vegetable oils and some fruits. Phytonadione is a man-made form of K1 [1].
- **Vitamin K2 (menaquinones):** A group of compounds, mostly of bacterial origin, designated MK-4 through MK-13 by the length of their side chain. MK-4, MK-7 and MK-9 are the most studied. Menaquinones are present in modest amounts in animal-based and fermented foods, and gut bacteria also make them [1].

A third form, menadione, sometimes called vitamin K3, is synthetic. It damaged liver cells in laboratory studies in the 1980s and 1990s, so it is no longer used in dietary supplements or fortified foods [1].

### Biochemical Functions

Vitamin K functions as a coenzyme for vitamin K-dependent carboxylase, an enzyme required for the synthesis of proteins involved in blood clotting and bone metabolism [1]. This enzyme, gamma-glutamyl carboxylase (GGCX), converts glutamate (Glu) residues to gamma-carboxyglutamate (Gla), a change required for the activity of vitamin K-dependent proteins [8]. The reaction uses reduced vitamin K, CO2, and O2 as co-substrates and converts vitamin K to vitamin K epoxide [8]. The epoxide must be recycled to vitamin K before it can be reused, a reaction catalyzed by vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKOR) [8], the enzyme that warfarin targets [9].

Key vitamin K-dependent proteins include:

- **Coagulation factors:** Prothrombin (clotting factor II) is a vitamin K-dependent protein in plasma that is directly involved in blood clotting [1].
- **Osteocalcin:** A vitamin K-dependent protein in bone that may be involved in bone mineralization or turnover [1]. The share of osteocalcin that is not carboxylated (undercarboxylated osteocalcin) falls when vitamin K intake rises, and trials often use it as a marker of vitamin K status [10].
- **Matrix Gla protein (MGP):** Present in vascular smooth muscle, bone, and cartilage. MGP is the focus of considerable research because it might help reduce abnormal calcification [1]. Some trials measure dp-ucMGP, the inactive form of MGP in blood, which falls when vitamin K is supplemented [11][12].

### Absorption, Transport, and Metabolism

Like other fat-soluble vitamins, ingested vitamin K is incorporated into mixed micelles by bile and pancreatic enzymes and absorbed in the small intestine [1]. It is then carried in chylomicrons to the liver and repackaged into very low-density lipoproteins. It is present in the liver and other tissues, including the brain, heart, pancreas, and bone [1].

MK-7 has a much longer half-life than K1, which gives more stable serum levels and 7- to 8-fold higher accumulation during prolonged intake [4]. At nutritional doses, MK-4 was not detectable in the blood of healthy women after a single dose or a week of daily doses, while MK-7 was well absorbed and raised blood levels [13].

The body converts phylloquinone to MK-4 through a process that does not involve bacterial action [1].

Vitamin K is rapidly metabolized and excreted. Based on phylloquinone measurements, the body retains only about 30-40% of an oral physiological dose, while about 20% is excreted in the urine and 40-50% in the feces via bile [1]. This rapid turnover explains vitamin K's low blood levels and tissue stores compared with the other fat-soluble vitamins [1].

### Vitamin K Status Assessment

Vitamin K status is not routinely assessed except in people who take anticoagulants or have bleeding disorders. The only clinically significant indicator is prothrombin time, the time it takes for blood to clot [1].

In healthy people, fasting plasma phylloquinone concentrations have been reported to range from 0.29 to 2.64 nmol/L [1]. People slightly below this range have no clinical signs of deficiency, possibly because plasma phylloquinone does not measure the menaquinones from the diet and the large bowel [1].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Vitamin K1 (Phylloquinone)

Phylloquinone is the main dietary form of vitamin K and is present primarily in green leafy vegetables [1].

Phylloquinone in plant foods is tightly bound to chloroplasts, so it is less bioavailable than that from oils or dietary supplements. For example, the body absorbs only 4-17% as much phylloquinone from spinach as from a tablet [1]. Eating vegetables with some fat improves absorption, but the amount absorbed is still lower than from oils [1].

The absorption rate of phylloquinone in its free form is about 80%, but its absorption rate from foods is much lower [1].

### Vitamin K2 as MK-7 (Menaquinone-7)

Natto, a Japanese food made from fermented soybeans, has high amounts of menaquinones [1], and the MK-7 in supplements is often derived from natto [4].

In a study by Schurgers et al. (2007), MK-7 stayed in the blood much longer than K1 and induced more complete carboxylation of osteocalcin [4].

According to the NIH fact sheet, limited research suggests that long-chain menaquinones may have higher absorption rates than phylloquinone from green vegetables [1].

Labels do not always match contents. In one analysis, most MK-7 supplements held less of the active all-trans form than declared: 5.5 to 49 mcg per pill, and one product had none [14]. Another study found that finished MK-7 products varied in purity and often differed from their stated content, and that purer raw material was more stable [15]. Choose a product that an independent lab has tested.

### Vitamin K2 as MK-4 (Menaquinone-4)

MK-4 occurs in small amounts in meat and eggs [1]. Menatetrenone, the MK-4 used as an osteoporosis drug in Japan, is synthetic [7]. MK-4 is unique in that the body makes it from phylloquinone through a conversion that does not involve bacteria [1]. Laboratory work has identified UBIAD1 as a human enzyme that makes MK-4 [16].

At nutritional doses, MK-4 taken by mouth did not raise blood MK-4 levels in one study [13]. At pharmacological doses it is used to treat osteoporosis in parts of Asia (see Bone Health).

### Other Menaquinone Forms

Almost all menaquinones, in particular the long-chain forms, are produced by bacteria in the human gut [1]. In four volunteers on warfarin, menaquinone delivered into the lower small bowel improved clotting measures within 24 hours, direct evidence that this part of the gut can absorb it [17]. Fermented foods such as cheese also contain menaquinones, but their forms and amounts likely vary with the bacterial strains and fermentation conditions [1]. Data on the bioavailability of different forms of vitamin K from food are very limited [1].

### Comparative Pharmacokinetics

| Property | K1 (Phylloquinone) | K2 as MK-4 | K2 as MK-7 |
|----------|-------------------|-------------|-------------|
| Half-life | Shorter than MK-7 | No data | Longer; 7- to 8-fold higher serum accumulation during prolonged intake |
| Absorption | Free form about 80%; much lower from foods | Not detectable in blood at nutritional doses in one study [13] | Well absorbed as a supplement |

### Fat-Soluble Vitamin Interactions

In mouse intestine and human intestinal cells in the laboratory, vitamins D, E, and K competed with each other for uptake, and vitamin A also lowered uptake of the others [18]. No human study has tested whether this matters for supplements. Vitamin E may interfere with vitamin K activity, especially blood clotting, and why vitamin E supplements cause bleeding in some people and not others is not understood [19].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Bone Health

#### Overview of the Vitamin K–Bone Connection

In observational studies, postmenopausal women with low bone density had lower blood vitamin K levels [20], and people with low vitamin K intake had more hip fractures [21][22]. Vitamin K is required for the carboxylation of osteocalcin, one of the main proteins in bone, so deficiency could reduce bone mineralization [1]. Some research links high levels of undercarboxylated osteocalcin with lower bone mineral density (BMD) [1]. However, it is unclear whether supplementing with any form of vitamin K reduces the risk of osteoporosis [1].

A 2006 meta-analysis by Cockayne et al. included 13 randomized controlled trials, most in Japan in postmenopausal women, lasting 6 to 36 months. Twelve of the 13 found that phytonadione (K1) or MK-4 improved bone mineral density. Seven trials had fracture data, all using MK-4 at 15 mg/day (1 trial) or 45 mg/day (6 trials), and MK-4 significantly reduced hip, vertebral and all nonvertebral fractures [1][23].

That fracture result now carries a caution. In 2018, JAMA Internal Medicine noted that the meta-analysis's conclusions "were based on data from trials that have since been indicated as problematic or retracted" [24]. One MK-4 fracture trial was retracted because it contained fabricated data [25], and a statistical review of 33 trials from the same research group found results that may be unreliable, including hip fracture reductions far larger than in other trials [26]. Later meta-analyses did not find a clear fracture benefit (see below).

#### Vitamin K1 Bone Trials

**Booth et al. (2008):** In 452 men and women aged 60 to 80 who took daily vitamin D (400 IU) and calcium (600 mg), a multivitamin with 500 mcg of K1 for three years made no difference to bone density at the hip, spine or whole body compared with the same multivitamin without K1 [28].

**Cheung et al. (2008):** In a trial of 440 postmenopausal women with osteopenia, 5,000 mcg of K1 a day did not change bone density compared with placebo over two to four years [5]. Fewer women taking K1 had clinical fractures (nine versus 20), but the trial was not powered to examine fractures and the numbers were small [5]. Vitamin K was well tolerated, with no significant differences in adverse effects [5].

**Binkley et al. (2009):** 381 postmenopausal women took 1 mg K1, 45 mg MK-4, or placebo daily for 12 months, all with calcium and vitamin D [1]. Both vitamin K groups had lower undercarboxylated osteocalcin, but bone density at the lumbar spine and hip did not differ among the groups [10].

**Braam et al. (2003):** In a placebo-controlled trial of 181 healthy postmenopausal women aged 50 to 60 years, adding daily vitamin K1 (1,000 mcg) to calcium, magnesium, zinc and vitamin D for three years reduced bone loss at the femoral neck compared with placebo or the same minerals and vitamin D alone; the lumbar spine showed no difference [27].

**Crohn's disease:** Vitamin K deficiency is common in inflammatory bowel disease, more so in Crohn's disease than in ulcerative colitis, but its link with bone disease is controversial [29].

#### Vitamin K2 (MK-4) Bone Trials

A 2019 meta-analysis of 18 trials with 8,882 patients found that MK-4 (menatetrenone) slightly improved lumbar spine density, but the drop in vertebral fractures was not significant (RR 0.87, 95% CI 0.64 to 1.20), and its benefit for fractures was uncertain [30]. Adverse events were more common than with placebo (RR 1.47), though serious adverse events were not [30].

Eight randomized trials, six in Japan and one each in Indonesia and China, gave postmenopausal women with osteoporosis 45,000 mcg of MK-4 a day. In these small trials, MK-4 alone modestly raised spine bone density and lowered fracture rates, mainly of the spine [7]. A Japanese dose-finding study compared several daily doses and found 45 mg the lowest dose that improved bone measures [7]. The author of that review also co-authored retracted MK-4 trials [25].

In Japan and other parts of Asia, a pharmacological dose of MK-4 (45 mg) is used as a treatment for osteoporosis [1]. The European Food Safety Authority has approved a health claim stating that "a cause and effect relationship has been established between the dietary intake of vitamin K and the maintenance of normal bone" [1]. The U.S. FDA has not authorized a health claim for vitamin K [1].

#### Vitamin K2 (MK-7) Bone Trials

**Meta-analysis (2022):** Pooling 16 trials with 6,425 postmenopausal women, Ma and colleagues found that vitamin K2 improved lumbar spine density, but fracture rates did not differ overall (RR 0.96); a benefit appeared only after one heterogeneous study was excluded (RR 0.43). Adverse reactions did not differ from control (RR 1.03) [6].

**Knapen et al. (2013):** Healthy postmenopausal women who took 180 mcg of MK-7 a day for three years kept more bone at the lumbar spine and femoral neck, by both mineral content and density, than those taking placebo; the total hip showed no difference [31].

The trial enrolled 244 healthy postmenopausal women, who took placebo or MK-7 for 3 years [31]. Bone strength was also favorably affected, and MK-7 lessened the loss of vertebral height in the lower thoracic spine [31].

**Ronn et al. (2020):** In 142 postmenopausal women with osteopenia, all taking vitamin D3 (1,520 IU) and calcium (800 mg) a day, adding 375 mcg of MK-7 for three years made no difference to bone density, which fell at every site in both groups [32]. Bone turnover markers and bone microarchitecture also changed alike with MK-7 and placebo [32].

**Zhang et al. (2020):** A one-year trial in China randomized 311 men and postmenopausal women aged 50 to 75 years to placebo, MK-7 at 50 mcg/day or 90 mcg/day, or 90 mcg/day with calcium (500 mg) and vitamin D (400 IU). Loss of density at the femoral neck was smaller in postmenopausal women given 90 mcg/day than with placebo, but there was no effect in men, and adding vitamin D and calcium brought no extra benefit [33].

#### Summary of Bone Evidence

Vitamin K takes part in bone metabolism through osteocalcin carboxylation, but it is unclear whether supplementation with any form of vitamin K reduces the risk of osteoporosis [1]. Trials of K1 and MK-7 have mixed results for bone density, and the fracture benefit rests mainly on MK-4 trials whose data have been questioned [24][30][6].

Giving vitamin D and/or calcium along with vitamin K could partly explain why some studies have found a bone benefit and others have not [1]. A meta-analysis of eight trials with 971 people found that vitamin K combined with vitamin D raised total bone density compared with a normal diet or no treatment [34]. Trials that added K to vitamin D and calcium, or vitamin D and calcium to K, found no extra benefit from the addition [32][33]. In two 2026 analyses of vitamin D trials, people's vitamin K status did not change the effect of vitamin D on bone and heart markers or on bone density [35][36].

### Cardiovascular Health

#### Observational Evidence

In Dutch observational studies, people who ate more vitamin K2 had less coronary calcification and were less likely to die of coronary heart disease [37][38].

In the Rotterdam Study, a prospective cohort of 4,807 men and women age 55 years and older from the Netherlands, menaquinone intake was inversely associated with severe aortic calcification [1].

People in the top third of menaquinone intake (>32.7 mcg/day) had a 57% lower risk of death from coronary heart disease than those in the bottom third (<21.6 mcg/day) [1]. The middle third also had a lower risk, but it was not statistically significant (RR 0.73, 95% CI 0.45 to 1.17) [37]. Phylloquinone (K1) intake was not related to any outcome [37].

A separate observational study in the Netherlands included 564 postmenopausal women [1]. In this study, dietary menaquinone (but not phylloquinone) intake was inversely associated with coronary calcification [38].

Vascular calcification is one of the risk factors for coronary heart disease because it reduces aortic and arterial elasticity [1]. In 6,778 adults followed for a median of 7.6 years, faster progression of coronary calcium was associated with more coronary heart disease events [39]. A hypothesis based on animal data suggests that inadequate vitamin K status leads to undercarboxylated MGP, which could increase vascular calcification and the risk of coronary heart disease. The full biological function of MGP is unclear [1].

#### MK-7 Cardiovascular Trials

The MK-7 trials below differ in who took part, the dose and what they measured. They are listed newest first.

**Hasific et al. (2026), DANCODE:** In 398 adults (median age 71) with a coronary calcium score of at least 400 AU, the score rose by a mean of 196 AU in 24 months with MK-7 (720 mcg/day) and vitamin D3 (25 mcg/day), against 248 AU with placebo [40]. In 143 participants who had CT angiography, total plaque volume rose alike in both groups and non-calcified plaque volume did not differ; calcified plaque grew less with the supplement [40].

**Vossen et al. (2026), VitaK-CAC:** In a Dutch placebo-controlled trial, 180 people with coronary artery disease symptoms and calcium scores between 50 and 400 AU took 360 mcg of MK-7 or placebo daily for two years [41]. The median calcium score rose from 145 to 214 AU with placebo and from 135 to 184 AU with MK-7, a significant difference; calcium mass gave a similar result, and there were no significant adverse effects [41]. The rise in calcium score correlated with the number of non-calcified plaques that became partly calcified, and the authors said the meaning of this for plaque stability remains to be determined [41].

**Hasific et al. (2025):** A substudy of the Danish valve trial (below) found that 24 months of MK-7 plus vitamin D, despite lowering dp-ucMGP, had no effect on inflammation in the fat around the heart and coronary arteries or on blood markers of inflammation [42].

**de Vries et al. (2025):** In a post hoc analysis of 165 women with low vitamin K status who took 180 mcg of MK-7 daily or placebo for one year, vascular stiffness rose less with MK-7 in post-menopausal women (by 9.4% against 49.1%) [43]. In the full report, pulse wave velocity and carotid wall thickness did not improve with MK-7 in post-menopausal women, and pre- and perimenopausal women showed no significant result [43]. The study was funded by the supplement maker Gnosis by Lesaffre; some authors were employees of the company or of Lesaffre International, and others received research funding from it [43].

**Haroon et al. (2023):** In 178 people on haemodialysis, MK-7 (360 mcg three times a week) for 18 months did not change coronary calcium scores, and major cardiac events plus deaths did not differ (HR 0.98, 95% CI 0.50 to 1.94) [44].

**Hasific et al. (2023), subgroup analysis:** In 304 men without known ischemic heart disease from the Danish valve trial, MK-7 (720 mcg/day) with vitamin D (25 mcg/day) did not significantly slow coronary calcium progression overall, but progression was slower in those who started with scores of at least 400 AU [45]. Non-calcified plaque volume did not differ significantly. Fewer people given supplements had a safety event (a heart attack, a coronary revascularization or a death from any cause: 1.9% vs. 6.7%), which the authors called hypothesis-generating [45]. The trial was funded by Danish foundations, and the tablets were supplied free by the makers [45].

**Diederichsen et al. (2022):** In 365 men (mean age 71) with aortic valve calcification, 720 mcg of MK-7 plus 25 mcg of vitamin D a day for 24 months lowered dp-ucMGP but did not slow the build-up of valve calcium compared with placebo, and aortic and coronary calcification did not differ either [12]. Valve surgery, cardiovascular events and deaths from any cause were similar in both groups [12].

**Zwakenberg et al. (2019) and Bartstra et al. (2021):** In 68 people with type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease, 360 mcg/day of MK-7 for six months did not change calcification on CT, and active calcification measured by PET tended to increase compared with placebo, although not significantly (P = 0.06) [46][47]. A pre-specified post hoc analysis found no effect on arterial calcification elsewhere in the body or on bone density [47].

**Witham et al. (2020):** In a double-blind trial in 159 adults with stage 3b or 4 chronic kidney disease, mean age 66, a year of 400 mcg of vitamin K2 a day did not improve pulse wave velocity, a measure of arterial stiffness, or blood pressure compared with placebo; an updated meta-analysis in the same paper found no effect of vitamin K on vascular stiffness or calcification [48].

**Knapen et al. (2015):** In a double-blind trial of 244 healthy postmenopausal women, 180 mcg of MK-7 (MenaQ7) a day for three years lowered arterial stiffness compared with placebo, and the effect was larger in women whose arteries were stiffest at the start [11]. MK-7 lowered dp-ucMGP by 50% but did not change blood markers of inflammation or endothelial dysfunction [11].

**Kurnatowska et al. (2015):** In 42 people with stage 3 to 5 chronic kidney disease not on dialysis, carotid intima-media thickness (thickening of the artery wall) rose less over about 270 days with 90 mcg of MK-7 a day added to 400 IU of vitamin D daily than with the vitamin D alone, but coronary calcification did not differ significantly between the groups [49].

#### Vitamin K1 Cardiovascular Trials

**Shea et al. (2009):** In a three-year trial in 388 healthy older men and women, calcification progressed 6% less in those with existing coronary calcification who took a multivitamin with 500 mcg of K1 a day than in those given the multivitamin alone [50]. Across all participants, there was no significant difference in coronary artery calcification between the groups [50].

**Bellinge et al. (2021):** A post hoc analysis of a double-blind trial in Western Australia gave people with diabetes and existing coronary calcification 10 mg of K1 or placebo daily for three months. K1 lowered the odds of new calcifying lesions on PET scans in the coronary arteries (OR 0.35, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.78) and the aorta (OR 0.27, 95% CI 0.08 to 0.94) compared with placebo [51].

#### Summary of Cardiovascular Evidence

In observational studies, diets richer in K2 were associated with less coronary calcification and fewer deaths from coronary heart disease [37][38]. In the two trials built to test coronary calcium, MK-7 slowed its rise, in people with moderate scores (360 mcg alone) and with severe scores (720 mcg with vitamin D) [41][40]. Most other MK-7 trials found no effect on calcification, and one found that active calcification tended to rise [46][12][44][48]. Neither K1 nor K2 has been shown to reduce major cardiovascular events or deaths; where events were counted, the studies were underpowered and the results neutral [52].

A slower rise in calcium score is a scan result, not an outcome. Statins, which lower heart attack risk, increased coronary calcification while shrinking plaque in pooled ultrasound trials [53]. So whether slowing calcification with vitamin K helps the heart is unknown.

### Nocturnal Leg Cramps

In a placebo-controlled trial by Tan et al. (2024), 199 people in China aged 65 and older who had night cramps took 180 mcg of MK-7 or placebo daily for eight weeks [54]. With MK-7, cramps fell from a mean of 2.60 to 0.96 a week, while the placebo group averaged 3.63 a week, and cramps were also less severe and shorter [54]. The trial found no adverse events related to vitamin K2 [54]. This is a single trial.

How vitamin K might ease cramps is unclear; in one laboratory study, vitamin K3 relaxed mouse uterine muscle by acting on calcium and potassium channels [55].

### Cancer

Observational evidence on vitamin K and cancer risk is mixed and does not establish cause and effect [56][57]:

**Wang et al. (2021):** A U.S. cohort study found that higher dietary intake of K2 was associated with a higher risk of breast cancer (HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.52) and of death from breast cancer (HR 1.71, 95% CI 0.97 to 3.01), though the interval for death includes no difference [57]. The authors said their findings need replicating in other studies [57].

**Nimptsch et al. (2010):** In the EPIC-Heidelberg cohort in Germany, people who ate the most vitamin K2 (menaquinones), mostly from cheese, were less likely to die of cancer [56]. The link with lower cancer incidence was stronger in men than in women, mainly for prostate and lung cancers [56].

Neither study found a link with phylloquinone (K1) intake. Observational studies like these cannot show cause and effect [56][57].

### COVID-19

High blood levels of dp-ucMGP, which point to low vitamin K status, have been linked with COVID-19 and with poor outcomes in people in hospital [58][59]:

**Linneberg et al. (2021):** In a cohort of 138 patients in hospital with COVID-19 and 138 population controls, average dp-ucMGP levels were higher in patients (1,022 pmol/L) than in controls (509 pmol/L) [58]. Those who died had levels of 1,445 pmol/L versus 877 pmol/L in those who survived [58]. After adjustment for sex and age, each doubling of dp-ucMGP was linked with a higher risk of death (HR 1.49, 95% CI 1.03 to 2.24), but this link weakened and was no longer statistically significant after adjustment for other health conditions [58].

**Dofferhoff et al. (2020):** Patients in hospital with COVID-19 had higher dp-ucMGP levels (indicating low vitamin K) than historic controls, and levels were higher still in patients with poor outcomes [59]. Higher dp-ucMGP was also linked with faster breakdown of elastic fibers [59].

No trial has tested vitamin K supplements for COVID-19.

### Blood Clotting

Bleeding is the classic sign of vitamin K deficiency, though it occurs only in severe cases [1]. Clinically significant vitamin K deficiency in adults is very rare and is usually limited to people with malabsorption disorders or those taking drugs that interfere with vitamin K metabolism [1]. In healthy people eating a varied diet, getting so little vitamin K that standard clotting tests change is almost impossible [1].

For people taking warfarin, guidelines suggest against routine daily low-dose vitamin K supplements as a way to improve INR control or clinical outcomes [60][61].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Adequate Intakes

Data were insufficient to set an Estimated Average Requirement or Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) for vitamin K. Instead, the Food and Nutrition Board (FNB) at the Institute of Medicine set Adequate Intakes (AIs) based on vitamin K intakes in healthy population groups [1]:

| Age/Sex Group | AI (mcg/day) |
|---------------|-------------|
| Birth to 6 months | 2.0 |
| 7-12 months | 2.5 |
| Children 1-3 years | 30 |
| Children 4-8 years | 55 |
| Children 9-13 years | 60 |
| Adolescents 14-18 years | 75 |
| Males 19+ years | 120 |
| Females 19+ years | 90 |
| Pregnancy (all ages) | 75-90 |
| Lactation (all ages) | 75-90 |

The FNB set no Tolerable Upper Intake Level (UL) because vitamin K has a low potential for toxicity [1]. The FNB stated that "no adverse effects associated with vitamin K consumption from food or supplements have been reported in humans or animals" [1].

### Typical Intake in Populations

Data from the 2011-2012 National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES) show that among U.S. children and teens age 2-19 years, the average daily vitamin K intake from foods is 66 mcg [1].

In adults age 20 and older, the average daily vitamin K intake from foods is 122 mcg for women and 138 mcg for men. When supplements are included, intake rises to 164 mcg for women and 182 mcg for men [1].

Some analyses of NHANES data from 2003-2006 and 2007-2010 found that only about one-third of the U.S. population had a vitamin K intake above the AI [1]. The significance of this is unclear because the AI is only an estimate of need, and reports of vitamin K deficiency in adults are very rare [1].

### Doses Used in Clinical Research

Most trial doses are well above the AI, and they vary widely by form, from 50 mcg a day of MK-7 to 45 mg a day of MK-4 [7][33]:

**Vitamin K1 (phylloquinone):**
- Bone health: 5,000 mcg/day for 2 to 4 years, which did not improve bone density [5]
- Cardiovascular (calcification): 10 mg a day for three months in people with diabetes [51]
- Newborn prophylaxis: a single intramuscular dose of 0.5 to 1 mg at birth [1]

**Vitamin K2 as MK-4:**
- Bone health (pharmacological): 45 mg/day, the dose used to treat osteoporosis in Japan and other parts of Asia [1]
- Dose finding: in one Japanese study, 45,000 mcg a day was the lowest dose that improved bone measures, and 15,000 mcg was not enough [7]

**Vitamin K2 as MK-7:**
- Bone health: 90-375 mcg/day [31][32][33]
- Cardiovascular health, lowest trial dose: 90 mcg of MK-7 with 400 IU of vitamin D daily over about 270 days in kidney disease [49]
- Cardiovascular health, highest trial dose: 720 mcg of MK-7 with vitamin D for 24 months [12][40]
- Nocturnal leg cramps: 180 mcg every day for eight weeks [54]

### Practical Dosing Considerations

Higher dietary vitamin K intake is associated with a lower risk of hip fractures, though not with higher bone density [22]. In one cohort of older men and women, those with the highest intake (a median of 254 mcg a day) had a lower risk of hip fracture than those with the lowest [22].

Brad advises against taking more than about 90 to 120 mcg a day. The 360 to 720 mcg doses that slowed coronary calcium are three to eight times the adequate intake [3].

Most multivitamin/mineral supplements provide less than 75% of the Daily Value for vitamin K [1].

Supplements containing only vitamin K, or vitamin K with a few other nutrients, have a wider range of doses, with some providing 4,050 mcg (5,063% of the DV) or another very high amount [1].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Safety Profile

The Food and Nutrition Board reported no adverse effects from vitamin K in food or supplements in humans or animals [1]. No Tolerable Upper Intake Level has been established because of vitamin K's low potential for toxicity [1].

In trials, K1 and MK-7 caused no more adverse effects than placebo, and a meta-analysis of vitamin K2 trials found no difference in adverse reactions [5][54][6].

Iwamoto's review of the MK-4 osteoporosis trials reported no toxicity at the drug dose of 45,000 mcg a day [7]. Possible side effects of this drug-level dose are stomach discomfort and diarrhea, and it should not be used with warfarin [7]. A meta-analysis found more adverse events with MK-4 than with placebo, but not more serious ones [30].

Vitamin K3 (menadione) is different: it damaged liver cells in laboratory studies, so it is no longer used in dietary supplements or fortified foods [1].

### Specific Safety Considerations

**Soy allergy:** The MK-7 in supplements is often derived from natto [4], which is made from fermented soybeans [1]. If you have a soy allergy, check the label or ask the maker about the source before starting.

**Breast cancer:** A U.S. cohort study found an association between higher dietary K2 intake and increased breast cancer risk [57]. An association does not prove cause and effect, and the authors said the findings need replicating [57].

**Thyroid function:** A review found little evidence that soy foods or soy isoflavones harm thyroid function in healthy people with adequate iodine, though soy foods may increase the dose of thyroid hormone that people with hypothyroidism need [63].

**Sleep concerns:** Short sleep (less than 7 hours per night) has been linked with lower vitamin K intake from foods and supplements in adults aged 19 to 50 [62]. This is an association in U.S. survey data, not evidence that vitamin K affects sleep [62].

### Newborn Vitamin K Prophylaxis

Newborns are vulnerable to vitamin K deficiency because of low placental transfer of phylloquinone, low clotting factor levels, and low vitamin K content of breast milk [1].

Vitamin K deficiency in the first weeks of life can cause vitamin K deficiency bleeding (VKDB), formerly known as classic hemorrhagic disease of the newborn [1]. Late VKDB occurs at 2-12 weeks of age, especially in exclusively breastfed infants, and can appear as sudden intracranial bleeding, which has a high mortality rate [1].

To prevent VKDB, the American Academy of Pediatrics recommends a single intramuscular dose of 0.5 to 1 mg of vitamin K1 at birth [1].

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Warfarin and Vitamin K Antagonists

Vitamin K can have a serious and potentially dangerous interaction with warfarin (Coumadin) and similar anticoagulants such as phenprocoumon, acenocoumarol, and tioclomarol [1]. These drugs antagonize the activity of vitamin K, leading to depletion of vitamin K-dependent clotting factors [1]. Vitamin K epoxide reductase (VKOR), the enzyme that recycles vitamin K, is the target of warfarin [9].

People taking warfarin and similar anticoagulants need to keep their vitamin K intake from food and supplements consistent, because sudden changes can increase or decrease the anticoagulant effect [1]. NIH advises people who take these medicines to discuss their vitamin K status with their health care providers [1]. Check with the doctor or anticoagulation clinic that manages your anticoagulant before you start, stop or change a vitamin K supplement, because a significant change in vitamin K intake should prompt more frequent INR checks [61].

In 15 healthy volunteers taking acenocoumarol, a warfarin-like drug, daily MK-7 doses of 10 and 20 mcg lowered the INR to a clinically relevant degree in at least 40% and 60% of them, and a daily 45 mcg lowered the average INR by about 40% [2]. The authors concluded that people on these drugs should avoid MK-7 supplements [2]. An earlier study warned that 50 mcg a day or more of MK-7 may interfere with anticoagulant treatment [4]. MK-4 at the drug dose used for osteoporosis is contraindicated with warfarin [7].

Guidance for warfarin care stresses consistent dietary vitamin K rather than avoiding it, and says a significant change in dietary vitamin K should prompt more frequent INR checks [61]. Natto is very rich in MK-7 and is avoided by people on warfarin [64].

### Direct Oral Anticoagulants (DOACs)

Vitamin K intake is not expected to change the effect of the newer blood thinners apixaban, rivaroxaban and dabigatran [3]; the label of each says vitamin K is not expected to affect its anticoagulant activity, even as a reversal treatment [73][74][75]. Some foods and supplements, such as St John's wort, can interact with these drugs [65]. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting a supplement.

### Antiplatelet Drugs

Brad notes that aspirin is not affected by vitamin K2 [66]. Clopidogrel (Plavix) works differently from warfarin: it stops platelets from clumping by blocking a receptor on them, rather than acting on vitamin K [72]. Its label warns that taking it with an anticoagulant such as warfarin raises the risk of bleeding [72]. If you take clopidogrel or another blood thinner, ask your doctor or pharmacist before starting vitamin K.

### Antibiotics

Antibiotics can destroy vitamin K-producing bacteria in the gut, potentially decreasing vitamin K status. This effect might be more pronounced with cephalosporin antibiotics such as cefoperazone, because these antibiotics might also inhibit the action of vitamin K in the body [1]. Vitamin K supplements are usually not needed unless antibiotic use is prolonged (beyond several weeks) and accompanied by poor vitamin K intake [1].

### Bile Acid Sequestrants

Cholestyramine (Questran) and colestipol (Colestid) reduce cholesterol by preventing reabsorption of bile acids. They can also reduce absorption of vitamin K and other fat-soluble vitamins, although the clinical significance of this is not clear [1]. Vitamin K status should be monitored in people taking these drugs, especially when they are used for many years [1]. One person on cholestyramine for over 25 years developed bleeding from low vitamin K [67].

### Orlistat

Orlistat (Alli, Xenical), a weight-loss drug, reduces the body's absorption of dietary fat and can also reduce absorption of fat-soluble vitamins such as vitamin K [1]. Combining orlistat with warfarin therapy might cause a significant increase in prothrombin time [1][68]. Clinicians usually recommend that patients taking orlistat take a multivitamin supplement containing vitamin K [1].

### Statins

A perspective article by Okuyama and colleagues proposes that statins may inhibit the body's synthesis of vitamin K2; this idea is not established [69].

### Impact of Gut Microbiota

Substantial quantities of long-chain menaquinones made by gut bacteria are present in the large bowel, but little is known about how they are absorbed and transported [1]. Although the amount of vitamin K the body obtains this way is unclear, the NIH fact sheet says experts believe these menaquinones satisfy at least some of the body's requirement for vitamin K [1]. Antibiotics can reduce vitamin K status by destroying vitamin K-producing bacteria in the gut [1].
