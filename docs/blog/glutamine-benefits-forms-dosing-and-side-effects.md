## Current evidence position

Brad has not made a video on this topic; this is a summary of the evidence, not his view. Glutamine is the most abundant amino acid in the body, and healthy people make their own, though needs may outstrip supply during critical illness [1][2]. In athletes, a 2019 meta-analysis of 25 trials found that glutamine had no effect on immune function, aerobic performance or body composition [34]. The clinical evidence is narrower: in an 8-week trial in post-infectious IBS-D, 5 g three times a day improved symptom scores in 79.6% of people versus 5.8% on placebo, and pharmaceutical-grade L-glutamine cut sickle cell pain crises to a median of 3.0 versus 4.0 [24][44]. In critically ill adults with multiorgan failure glutamine was linked to higher mortality, and people with kidney or liver disease, active cancer or bipolar disorder should talk to their doctor before taking it [49][72][85][60][74].

## Key safety points

- Many cancer cells use glutamine as a main fuel, and in rats glutamine raised methotrexate levels in tumors. With active cancer or chemotherapy, take glutamine only if your oncologist agrees [60][61][78].
- In critically ill adults with multiorgan failure, glutamine was linked to higher mortality [49].
- The body turns glutamine into ammonia, and in cirrhosis a glutamine dose raises blood ammonia. With liver disease, ask your doctor first [12][85].
- A patient who used 18 g of L-glutamine on a daily basis developed acute kidney injury; the authors advise caution in older people with reduced kidney function [72].
- A 1984 case report titled "L-glutamine and mania" raised concern for people with bipolar disorder. If that applies to you, talk to your doctor first [74].
- A 2008 risk assessment found strong evidence of safety up to 14 g a day in healthy adults; data above that are too limited to be sure of long-term safety [83].
- A high single dose caused more nausea, discomfort and belching than lower doses, though most symptoms were mild [23].

## 1. Overview

L-glutamine is the most abundant amino acid in the body and in plasma [1][80]. It is a fuel for fast-dividing cells such as enterocytes and lymphocytes, a regulator of acid-base balance, a carrier of nitrogen between tissues and a building block for nucleic acids, amino sugars and proteins [2]. Most of the body's own glutamine comes from skeletal muscle [4]. Because the body makes it, glutamine is classed as a nonessential amino acid [2].

During critical illness, the body's needs may exceed what it can make, which is why glutamine is called "conditionally essential" [2][4]. Plasma and muscle glutamine levels fall with sepsis, injury, burns, surgery and endurance exercise, and in overtrained athletes [10]. Normal plasma glutamine after an overnight fast is 500 to 750 micromol/L [32]. In intensive care, a low plasma glutamine level at admission predicted later death [6].

Glutamine has many roles in the body [2][5]:

- **Cellular energy production:** Glutamine is a preferred fuel for enterocytes (intestinal lining cells) and lymphocytes [2].
- **Gut barrier integrity:** In intestinal cells grown in the lab, removing glutamine weakened the barrier between cells and lowered the tight junction protein claudin-1 [8].
- **Immune function:** Lymphocytes and macrophages need glutamine to grow and to make cytokines, and macrophages need it to engulf bacteria [10][55].
- **Nitrogen transport:** Glutamine carries nitrogen between tissues, including to the liver and kidney [2][11].
- **Acid-base homeostasis:** In the kidney, each glutamine molecule broken down yields 2 ammonium and 2 bicarbonate ions, and ammonia passed in urine helps the body excrete acid [12].
- **Biosynthetic precursor:** Glutamine is a precursor for nucleotides, nucleic acids and glutathione [5].
- **Muscle protein metabolism:** Muscle holds more free glutamine than any other free amino acid [32].

Foods that contain protein supply glutamine. In the Nurses' Health Study, women ate an average of 6.84 g of glutamine a day [15].

The biologically active form is L-glutamine. In this article, "glutamine" means L-glutamine.

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Free-Form L-Glutamine

L-glutamine has been made by fermentation since the late 1960s; the fermentation broth is purified to a crystalline powder [18]. Supplements sell it as a powder or in capsules.

### Dipeptide Forms

Free glutamine has limited solubility and is unstable in solution, which has kept it out of most intravenous feeding solutions [4][20]. Dipeptides that pair glutamine with another amino acid are stable and highly soluble, and suit parenteral nutrition [20]. In lab studies, these dipeptides could stand in for glutamine to support immune cells [10].

In rats doing resistance exercise, an oral alanyl-glutamine dipeptide restored glutamine levels in plasma and muscle [66].

### Bioavailability and Absorption

The human small intestine absorbs glutamine well. In healthy volunteers, glutamine infused into the jejunum was nearly fully absorbed, and plasma glutamine rose with the dose [87]. Glutamine crosses cell membranes on several transporter families (SLC1, 6, 7 and 38), including sodium-dependent co-transporters [21].

### Stability Considerations

Glutamine breaks down slowly in solution. In a 1991 study, breakdown was faster at room temperature than at 4 degrees C and slower still when frozen; it also varied with pH and the type of solution, while light and oxygen made little difference [86].

Glutamine breaking down in solution forms ammonia, not glutamate [86].

### Comparison with Glutamate and MSG

Glutamine and glutamate (glutamic acid) are closely related. Many of glutamine's functions in cells depend on its conversion to glutamate [5].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS-D)

The strongest clinical evidence for glutamine outside hospital comes from a randomized trial in diarrhea-predominant IBS (IBS-D) that began after a gut infection.

In this 8-week trial, adults with post-infectious IBS-D and a leaky gut took 5 g of glutamine three times a day or placebo. The main goal, a drop of at least 50 points on the IBS Severity Scoring System, was reached by 43 (79.6%) in the glutamine group and 3 (5.8%) in the placebo group [24].

Key findings from this trial [24]:
- **Symptom severity** scores fell further with glutamine than with placebo
- **Bowel movement frequency** and stool form improved with glutamine
- **Intestinal permeability** (measured by urinary lactulose/mannitol ratio) returned to normal in the glutamine group only
- **Adverse events** were low and similar in the two groups
- **Dosing protocol:** 5 g of glutamine three times a day for 8 weeks [24]

Lab studies in intestinal cells suggest one mechanism: glutamine helps maintain the tight junctions between cells [8].

The trial was randomized, double-blind and placebo-controlled. Its authors called for larger trials to confirm the result [24].

### Intestinal Permeability ("Leaky Gut")

Glutamine has also been tested for gut barrier function in other settings.

**NSAID-induced permeability:** In healthy volunteers given the NSAID indomethacin, glutamine taken as 3x7 g daily for 1 week beforehand did not prevent the rise in gut permeability. Several doses taken close in time to the NSAID did lower it [25].

**Exercise-induced permeability:** In a study of active men running for 60 minutes at 30 degrees C, glutamine taken 2 hours before the run likely lowered a marker of gut permeability at doses from 0.25 g per kg of fat-free mass, with no effect on gut symptoms [26].

**Inflammatory bowel disease (IBD):** A 2016 Cochrane review found only two small trials (42 patients) of glutamine for active Crohn's disease. In one, 4/9 on a glutamine-enriched diet reached remission versus 5/9 on a standard diet (RR 0.80, 95% CI 0.31 to 2.04), and neither trial found a change in gut permeability [29]. The reviewers found the evidence too thin for firm conclusions [29].

### Exercise and Muscle Recovery

Glutamine is a popular sports supplement, but well-controlled studies in healthy, well-nourished people give little support for the usual reasons to take it [33].

**Muscle damage markers:** In a crossover study of 12 professional basketball players, 6 g a day of glutamine for 20 days lowered aspartate transaminase, creatine kinase and myoglobin in blood, which are signs of muscle damage, compared with placebo [30]. In a crossover trial in healthy young adults, 0.3 g per kg a day after eccentric exercise sped strength recovery and lowered soreness [36].

**Post-exercise infection risk:** Plasma glutamine falls after endurance events and prolonged exercise, and heavy training can leave levels low [32]. In a 1996 study, 81% of athletes given glutamine drinks after exercise reported no infection over the next 7 days, versus 49% given placebo [31]. Later studies found that keeping plasma glutamine steady did not prevent the changes in immune function after exercise [33].

**Performance and body composition:** A 2019 meta-analysis found glutamine had no effect on immune function, aerobic performance or body composition in athletes, though it was linked to greater weight loss [34]. In a 6-week resistance training trial in young adults, glutamine had no effect on strength, lean mass or muscle protein breakdown [35].

**Practical bottom line:** For healthy athletes who eat enough protein, glutamine is unlikely to improve performance or body composition [33][34].

### Oral Mucositis (Chemotherapy-Related)

Some trials suggest glutamine lessens oral mucositis, the painful inflammation and ulcers of the mouth lining that cancer treatment can cause. Results are mixed.

**Swish-and-swallow protocol:** In breast cancer patients on anthracycline chemotherapy, a glutamine oral suspension (Saforis) taken 3 times a day lowered the rate of clinically significant mucositis (38.7% vs. 49.7%) compared with placebo [38].

**Oral dosing protocol:** In head and neck cancer patients on chemoradiotherapy, 10 g three times a day lowered mucositis severity in one trial of 40 patients [39]. A trial with 25 patients per arm using the same dose found no significant difference in mucositis, although skin dermatitis was reduced [40].

**Chemotherapy-induced peripheral neuropathy:** In a non-randomized study, 12 patients given 10 g of glutamine three times a day for 4 days after high-dose paclitaxel had less severe nerve damage than 33 patients who did not take it [42]. The authors said randomized trials were needed [42].

These applications should only be undertaken under the guidance of an oncologist, as glutamine's role in cancer metabolism raises concerns about tumor support (see Cancer Metabolism section below).

### Sickle Cell Disease

L-glutamine was approved by the U.S. FDA for sickle cell disease in 2017 [82].

In a phase 3 trial of 230 patients aged 5 to 58 years with sickle cell anemia or sickle beta-zero-thalassemia, L-glutamine at 0.3 g per kilogram of body weight per dose, taken twice daily for 48 weeks, gave these results compared with placebo [44]:
- Median pain crises: 3.0 versus 4.0 [44]
- Median hospitalizations: 2.0 versus 3.0 [44]

Two thirds of patients in both groups also took hydroxyurea [44]. An earlier small study in adults found that 30 g a day of oral glutamine for 4 weeks raised the NAD redox potential of sickle red blood cells from 47.2% to 62.1% [45]. A 2019 systematic review judged that only one randomized trial gives strong evidence for this use [82].

### Critical Care and Surgical Recovery

The evidence for glutamine in critically ill patients has changed over time.

**Early positive evidence:** A 2002 review of 14 randomized trials found glutamine was associated with a non-significant trend toward lower mortality (RR 0.78, 95% CI 0.58 to 1.04) and fewer infections, with the benefit mostly in high-dose, parenteral glutamine [47]. A 2014 review of 26 trials of parenteral glutamine found lower hospital mortality (RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.51, 0.90) [48].

**REDOXS trial (a cautionary result):** In 1223 critically ill adults with multiorgan failure, glutamine given intravenously and by tube showed a trend toward higher 28-day mortality (32.4% vs. 27.2%; adjusted odds ratio, 1.28; 95% confidence interval [CI], 1.00 to 1.64), and in-hospital and 6-month mortality were significantly higher [49]. The META-PLUS trial tested a feed enriched with several nutrients, including glutamine, and found no fewer infections and higher 6-month mortality in the medical subgroup (hazard ratio of 1.57 (95% CI, 1.03-2.39)) [50].

**Current guidelines:** The 2019 ESPEN guideline on nutrition in intensive care gives specific attention to glutamine [51].

**Perioperative supplementation:** In the 2002 review, surgical patients given glutamine had shorter hospital stays (-3.5 days; 95% CI, -5.3 to -1.7) [47].

### HIV/AIDS Wasting

In a 1999 double-blind trial in 26 AIDS patients with more than 5% weight loss, a glutamine-antioxidant mixture (40 g/d) for 12 weeks increased body weight by 2.2 kg, versus 0.3 kg with a glycine placebo [54]. Because the supplement combined glutamine with antioxidants, the trial cannot show the effect of glutamine alone. Immune cells such as lymphocytes use glutamine at high rates [55].

### Cancer Cachexia

The 2020 ASCO guideline on cancer cachexia reviewed 20 systematic reviews and 13 additional trials. It found benefit only for dietary counselling, progesterone analogs and corticosteroids; glutamine is not among its recommendations [57].

### Long COVID Fatigue

The only study here is in acute COVID-19, not long COVID. In a 2020 comparison of 30 hospital patients who took oral L-glutamine and 30 who did not, hospital stay was 8.9 versus 10.4 days, and four patients without L-glutamine needed intensive care versus none with it [59]. The study was not randomized.

### Cancer Metabolism — An Important Caveat

While glutamine is being studied for reducing chemotherapy side effects, there is concern that it may support tumor growth. Some cancer cells show "glutamine addiction" and use glutamine as their main mitochondrial fuel [60].

Cancer biologists have recognized glutamine as a tumor nutrient since the 1950s. Glutamine supports almost every core metabolic task of fast-growing tumor cells [13][61].

Blocking glutamine use has been tested as a cancer treatment. In the CANTATA trial in 444 patients with advanced kidney cancer, adding the glutaminase inhibitor telaglenastat to cabozantinib did not improve outcomes [84].

The clinical implication for supplementation is that people with active cancer should not take glutamine supplements without their oncologist's approval [60][61].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### General Dosing Guidelines

Glutamine is a nonessential amino acid, and food supplies it: women in the Nurses' Health Study averaged 6.84 g a day [2][15]. A 2008 risk assessment found strong evidence of safety for supplements up to 14 g/d in healthy adults [83].

### Dosing by Indication

| Indication | Dose | Duration | Notes |
|------------|------|----------|-------|
| IBS-D (post-infectious) | 5 g three times daily | 8 weeks | One randomized trial [24] |
| NSAID-induced permeability | Several doses close in time to the NSAID | Acute use | Pre-treatment for a week did not work [25] |
| Oral mucositis (chemotherapy) | 10 g three times daily | During chemoradiotherapy | Mixed results; oncologist supervision only [39][40] |
| Sickle cell disease (prescription) | 0.3 g per kilogram of body weight per dose, twice daily [44] | 48 weeks in the trial [44] | FDA-approved [82]. The FDA label recommends 5, 10 or 15 g per dose by body weight (up to 30 g a day) [88] |
| Critical care (parenteral) | Hospital use only | During ICU stay | Higher mortality in multiorgan failure [49] |
| Perioperative recovery | Hospital use only | Perioperative period | Shorter hospital stay in pooled trials [47] |
| Exercise recovery (muscle damage) | 6 g/day | 20 days in one study | Lower muscle damage markers [30] |
| General supplementation | No set dose | Variable | No clear evidence of benefit in well-nourished athletes [34] |

### Practical Dosing Notes

- **Divide doses:** In a small trial, the highest single dose caused more mild gut symptoms than lower doses [23].
- **Mix just before use:** The prescription label says to mix the powder immediately before taking it [88]. Glutamine breaks down slowly in solution, faster at room temperature than in the fridge [86].

### Pediatric Dosing

The phase 3 sickle cell trial included patients aged 5 to 58 years, all on the same weight-based dose [44].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Safety Profile

Acute intakes of about 20-30 g seem to be without ill effect in healthy adults, and one study reported no harm when athletes consumed 28 g of glutamine every day for 14 days [33]. A 2008 risk assessment found strong evidence of no adverse effects at intakes up to 14 g/d in healthy adults and set that as the observed safe level; higher intakes have been tested without harm, but the data are too limited for a confident conclusion on long-term safety [83].

### Common Side Effects

Most reported side effects involve the gut. In healthy men, the highest single dose (0.9 g per kg of fat-free mass) caused more discomfort, nausea, belching and upper gut pain than the lowest dose, though most symptoms were mild [23].

In the sickle cell trial, low-grade nausea, noncardiac chest pain, fatigue and musculoskeletal pain were more common with L-glutamine than with placebo [44].

### Side Effects at Higher Doses

On the label for prescription L-glutamine, dosed at 10 to 30 g a day by weight, the most common side effects in its trials (each in more than 10% of patients) were constipation, nausea, headache, abdominal pain, cough, pain in the arms or legs, back pain and chest pain; several were nearly as common on placebo [88].

### Kidney Injury

Kidney injury from glutamine has been reported. A 2026 case report described acute kidney injury from tubular damage in a patient who used 18 g of L-glutamine on a daily basis [72]. The authors thought ammonia made in the kidney from glutamine was the most likely cause, and they advise cautious use of L-glutamine in elderly patients with reduced kidney function [72]. The pharmacokinetics of prescription L-glutamine have not been studied in people with kidney or liver impairment, and its sickle cell trial excluded people with renal insufficiency or uncontrolled liver disease [88].

### Psychiatric Effects

A 1984 case report, titled "L-glutamine and mania," raised concern for people with bipolar disorder [74]. If you have bipolar disorder, talk to your doctor before taking glutamine.

### Hepatic Encephalopathy and Liver Disease

The body breaks glutamine down to ammonia [12]. In 238 people with cirrhosis, an oral glutamine challenge raised blood ammonia, and a large rise predicted later hepatic encephalopathy [85]. People with liver disease should talk to their doctor before taking glutamine. If someone with liver disease becomes confused or very drowsy, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).

### Cancer Safety Considerations

As discussed under Cancer Metabolism above, glutamine supplementation raises concerns about promoting tumor growth in glutamine-dependent cancers. Patients with active cancer should only use glutamine under oncologist supervision [60][61].

### Safety in Pregnancy and Lactation

There are no data on prescription L-glutamine in pregnancy or breastfeeding, and its sickle cell trial excluded women who were pregnant or breastfeeding [88]. Pregnant and breastfeeding women should talk to their doctor before taking glutamine.

### Tolerability at High Doses

Doses of up to 0.65 g/kg body mass have been tolerated by patients without abnormal plasma ammonia levels [33]. The sickle cell trial gave 0.3 g per kilogram of body weight per dose, twice daily, for 48 weeks [44]. The FDA label sets 5, 10 or 15 g per dose by body weight, so 30 g a day at most [88].

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Medications That May Interact with Glutamine

| Drug | Interaction | Clinical Implication |
|------|-------------|---------------------|
| **Methotrexate** | In rats, glutamine gave a threefold increase in methotrexate in tumors and less in the gut; a glutamine-enriched diet also improved survival from methotrexate gut injury [78][79] | Use only under oncologist supervision |
| **Chemotherapy agents** | Glutamine may reduce mouth ulcers from chemotherapy but may also fuel tumor cells [38][60] | Use only under oncologist supervision |

If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

### Amino Acid Competition

Most glutamine transporters also carry other neutral or cationic amino acids [21]. Glutamine transporters may also be involved in drug interactions, but the clinical significance at supplement doses is unclear [80].

### Monitoring Recommendations

- **Plasma glutamine levels:** Normal plasma glutamine after an overnight fast is 500 to 750 micromol/L [32]. A low level at ICU admission predicted later death [6].
- **Kidney function:** Older people with reduced kidney function should use glutamine with caution [72].
- **Liver function and ammonia:** People with cirrhosis respond to a glutamine load with a rise in ammonia [85].

## 7. Dietary Sources

Glutamine is found in protein-rich foods; by one method, it makes up 4.8% of the protein in meat [15].

### Top Food Sources

Across the foods in one database, glutamine content ranged from 0.01 to 9.49 g/100 g of food [15].

### Practical Notes on Dietary Glutamine

- **Average intake:** Women in the Nurses' Health Study ate an average of 6.84 g a day [15].
- **Protein-rich diet may be sufficient:** In healthy, well-nourished people, well-controlled studies give little support for glutamine supplements [33].

### Special Dietary Considerations

In critical illness, the body may not make enough glutamine [4]. Glutamine in that setting is a decision for the hospital team (see Critical Care and Surgical Recovery).
