## 1. Overview

Selenium is an essential trace mineral required for the biosynthesis of 25 distinct selenoproteins in humans [1][2]. These selenoproteins incorporate selenium in the form of selenocysteine — the 21st amino acid in the genetic code — and play critical roles in antioxidant defense, thyroid hormone metabolism, DNA synthesis, reproduction, and protection from oxidative damage and infection [1][3][4]. The selenium concentration in the thyroid gland is higher than in any other organ in the body, reflecting its importance in thyroid function [5].

Key selenoprotein families include:

- **Glutathione peroxidases (GPx):** A family of antioxidant enzymes that reduce hydroperoxides and lipid hydroperoxides, protecting cells from oxidative damage. GPx4 is particularly important in spermatogenesis, protecting developing sperm and contributing to the structural integrity of the sperm midpiece [6][7].
- **Thioredoxin reductases (TrxR):** Maintain the redox state of thioredoxin and other proteins, supporting DNA synthesis, apoptosis regulation, and antioxidant defenses [6].
- **Iodothyronine deiodinases (DIO1, DIO2, DIO3):** Essential for thyroid hormone metabolism — specifically the conversion of the prohormone thyroxine (T4) to the biologically active triiodothyronine (T3), or its inactivation [5][7].
- **Selenoprotein P:** The primary selenium transport protein in plasma, used as a functional biomarker of selenium status alongside glutathione peroxidase 3 [1][8].

Selenium deficiency is uncommon in North America, where the average daily selenium intake from foods and beverages is approximately 108 mcg — well above the 55 mcg Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) [9]. However, deficiency is more prevalent in regions with selenium-depleted soils, including Northern and Central Europe (England, Wales, Scotland, Ireland, Germany, Poland) and certain areas of China [10][11]. Globally, an estimated 0.5 to 1 billion people are at risk of selenium deficiency [12]. Vegetarians and vegans are at particular risk in low-selenium regions, and researchers have noted that "the most pronounced difference in nutritional status between British and American vegetarians is selenium status" [13].

Selenium deficiency alone rarely causes overt illness but produces biochemical changes that predispose affected individuals to additional health problems [1][14]. Severe deficiency is associated with two endemic diseases:

- **Keshan disease:** An endemic cardiomyopathy first identified in 1935 in selenium-poor areas of China, mainly affecting women of childbearing age and preschool children. A 2018 systematic review and meta-analysis of 41 studies found that selenium supplementation reduces the risk of Keshan disease by 86% [15].
- **Kashin-Beck disease:** An osteoarthritis-like condition affecting cartilage and bone, commonly presenting in childhood and puberty in low-selenium regions of China, Tibet, Siberia, and North Korea [1][16].

Subclinical selenium deficiency may contribute to weakened immune function (reduced T-cell proliferation and antibody production), hypothyroidism (particularly when compounded by iodine deficiency), and impaired thyroid hormone metabolism [1][5][17].

Plasma and serum selenium concentrations are the most commonly used measures of selenium status. Concentrations of 8 mcg/dL or higher are generally considered sufficient for selenoprotein synthesis [1][8]. The average serum selenium concentration in the US population is approximately 12.7 mcg/dL [18]. Status is lower in people who smoke, possibly because smoking increases oxidative stress [19]. Approximately 28% to 46% of the body's total selenium content is found in skeletal muscle, and selenium homeostasis is maintained primarily through urinary excretion [1][20].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

Selenium compounds are generally well absorbed by humans. The form of selenium determines its absorption rate, metabolic pathway, and tissue distribution, but all common supplemental forms achieve absorption rates of 80-90% or higher [1][21].

### Common Supplement Forms

| Form | Absorption | Key Characteristics |
|------|:-:|------|
| **Selenomethionine** | >90% | Organic form; the predominant form in plant foods. Non-specifically incorporated into body proteins (methionine pool). Most studied supplemental form. Used in the SELECT and PREADVISE trials [1][22]. |
| **Selenium-enriched yeast** | >90% | Brewer's yeast grown in selenium-rich broth; contains predominantly selenomethionine. Used in the Nutritional Prevention of Cancer Trial. Believed slightly better absorbed than inorganic selenite [10][23]. |
| **Sodium selenite** | >80% | Inorganic form. Well absorbed but metabolized differently than organic forms — rapidly reduced to selenide rather than being incorporated into the methionine pool. Used in some thyroid trials [1][21]. |
| **Sodium selenate** | >90% | Inorganic form. High absorption, then reduced to selenite in vivo. Used in fertilizer fortification programs (e.g., Finland) [1][21][24]. |
| **Selenium glycinate** | ~80% | Selenium complexed with the nonessential amino acid glycine. Promoted as better absorbed and utilized than other forms, but this has not been demonstrated in human studies. Research in broiler chickens showed it was similar to selenite in raising muscle selenium levels [10]. |
| **Se-methyl-L-selenocysteine** | High (animal data) | Naturally occurring organic form representing ~80% of total selenium in cruciferous vegetables (broccoli, Brussels sprouts, cabbage, garlic, onion, leek). Readily absorbed and bioavailable based on animal studies. Toxicity appears comparable to or possibly higher than other selenium compounds. Limited human supplement data — EFSA advises caution until more is known [10][25]. |

### Key Differences Between Organic and Inorganic Forms

Organic selenium (selenomethionine, selenium yeast) is incorporated non-specifically into body proteins wherever methionine is used, creating a selenium "reserve" in tissues. This means organic selenium has a longer biological half-life and can be released when needed for selenoprotein synthesis [1][22].

Inorganic selenium (selenite, selenate) is rapidly metabolized to a common intermediate (selenide) that is then used directly for selenoprotein synthesis. It does not accumulate in the methionine pool and has a shorter half-life [1][22].

A randomized clinical trial comparing selenomethionine with selenium yeast (both at 200 mcg/day) in healthy men found that both forms increased plasma selenium, but selenium yeast produced more favorable changes in oxidative stress biomarkers [22].

### Practical Form Selection

For general supplementation, selenomethionine and selenium-enriched yeast have the most clinical evidence behind them. Selenite is commonly used in multivitamins and has adequate absorption. Selenium glycinate is a reasonable option, though its claimed absorption advantages over other forms lack human evidence [10].

### Factors Affecting Absorption

Selenium absorption is largely not affected by selenium status, meaning the body absorbs a similar proportion regardless of whether stores are adequate or depleted [1][4]. However, certain digestive conditions (Crohn's disease, short-bowel syndrome, ulcerative colitis) may impair selenium absorption, and medications that reduce stomach acid may also reduce absorption of some forms [10].

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Cancer

The relationship between selenium and cancer has been extensively studied, with results that have evolved significantly over the past three decades.

**Early evidence suggesting benefit:**

The observation that cancer incidence is much higher in parts of China with selenium-depleted soil gave rise to the hypothesis that selenium deficiency promotes cancer [10][11]. The landmark Nutritional Prevention of Cancer Trial (NPC Trial), which began recruitment in 1983, randomized 1,312 men and women with histories of nonmelanoma skin cancer to 200 mcg/day selenium as selenium yeast or placebo for a mean of 4.5 years. While selenium did not prevent recurrence of skin cancer, a secondary analysis found that men who took selenium had a 49% lower risk of prostate cancer over ~7.5 years of follow-up. The effect was strongest in men with the lowest baseline selenium concentrations (below 10.6 mcg/dL), with no benefit in men whose plasma selenium exceeded 12.3 mcg/dL (Clark, *JAMA*, 1996; Reid, *Cancer Epidemiol Biomarkers Prev*, 2002) [26][27].

A study of African-American women found that those with the highest supplemental selenium intake (≥20 mcg/day) had approximately 30% lower risk of ovarian cancer compared to non-supplementers, after adjusting for age, contraceptive use, and family history. This association did not exist for selenium from foods nor for other antioxidants (Terry, *J Nutr*, 2017) [28].

For colorectal cancer, the NPC Trial also reported a 58% reduction in colorectal cancer incidence among participants with low baseline selenium levels [29].

**Large trials showing no benefit:**

The subsequent Selenium and Vitamin E Cancer Prevention Trial (SELECT) — the largest trial of selenium for cancer prevention — randomized 35,533 men aged 50+ from the US, Canada, and Puerto Rico to 200 mcg/day selenium (as selenomethionine), 400 IU/day vitamin E, both, or placebo. The trial was discontinued after 5.5 years when analyses showed no association between selenium supplementation and prostate cancer risk [30]. An additional 1.5 years of follow-up after supplement discontinuation confirmed the null finding [31]. Crucially, a case-cohort analysis of 1,739 prostate cancer cases from SELECT found that selenium supplementation actually increased the risk of high-grade prostate cancer by 91% among men with already high selenium levels (≥60th percentile of toenail selenium), while having no effect in men with low baseline selenium [32].

**Evidence of potential harm with high-dose supplementation:**

A study following men with non-metastatic prostate cancer found that those supplementing with 140 mcg or more of selenium per day were 160% more likely to die from prostate cancer compared to non-users, though all-cause mortality was not higher. The researchers cautioned that men with prostate cancer should not take more than 140 mcg per day from supplements (Kenfield, *J Natl Cancer Inst*, 2015) [33].

**Systematic reviews:**

A 2018 Cochrane Review of 83 studies on selenium and cancer prevention found mixed results [34]. Among 70 observational cohort studies, participants with the highest baseline selenium status had:
- 28% lower overall cancer risk
- 24% lower cancer mortality
- 33% lower bladder cancer risk
- 18% lower lung cancer risk
- 18% lower colorectal cancer risk
- 16% lower prostate cancer risk (in men)
- No association between selenium status and breast cancer risk in women

However, the certainty of evidence for these observational findings was very low due to study design weaknesses and confounders. Among the 10 randomized, placebo-controlled trials (27,232 participants, 94% male), supplementation with 200-500 mcg/day selenium for 2-10.3 years had little to no effect on cancer incidence or mortality, with high certainty of evidence for prostate, colorectal, lung, and bladder cancers [34].

A separate Cochrane Review specifically examining selenium for skin and colorectal cancer concluded that selenium supplements do not decrease the risk of either cancer type, regardless of baseline selenium status (Filippini, *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*, 2018) [35].

The US FDA has allowed qualified health claims that selenium "may reduce the risk of certain forms of cancer" but specifies that this evidence is "limited and not conclusive." In 2009, FDA determined it is "highly unlikely" that selenium supplements reduce prostate cancer risk and "highly uncertain" for bladder and thyroid cancers [36].

**Synthesis:** The available evidence suggests that correcting genuine selenium deficiency may reduce cancer risk, but supplementing beyond adequate levels offers no cancer prevention benefit and may increase risk of certain cancers (particularly high-grade prostate cancer in men with already-adequate selenium). The cancer prevention hypothesis applies predominantly to populations with low baseline selenium — not to the average North American adult.

### Thyroid Disease

The thyroid gland contains more selenium per gram of tissue than any other organ, reflecting selenium's central role in thyroid hormone synthesis and metabolism [5]. Selenoproteins — specifically the deiodinases (DIO1, DIO2, DIO3) — catalyze the conversion of T4 to the active T3. Additionally, glutathione peroxidase and thioredoxin reductase protect the thyroid from hydrogen peroxide generated during thyroid hormone synthesis [5][7].

**Epidemiological associations:**

Epidemiological studies have found inverse associations between selenium status and thyroid disease risk, but interestingly, this effect has been observed only in women [37][38]. A study of 1,900 middle-aged adults found that higher serum selenium was protective against goiter in women but not men [39]. Similarly, a cross-sectional study in Denmark (n=805) with mild iodine deficiency found the same sex-specific pattern — lower selenium was associated with larger thyroid volume only in women [38].

**Hashimoto's thyroiditis (chronic autoimmune thyroiditis):**

This condition affects 1-2% of the population, particularly middle-aged women [40]. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 trials (n=1,494, mainly women) evaluated selenium supplementation (80-200 mcg/day as selenomethionine or sodium selenite for 3-12 months) for Hashimoto's thyroiditis [41]. Key findings:
- In patients already on levothyroxine, selenium supplementation lowered thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb) levels.
- Selenomethionine was more effective than sodium selenite at lowering TPOAb, possibly due to higher absorption.
- In newly diagnosed patients not on levothyroxine, selenium lowered both TPOAb and thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb) levels at 3 months, but not at 6 or 12 months.
- Baseline selenium status was deficient to marginal (3.7-8.5 mcg/dL) in the five trials that reported it.

However, a systematic review of 9 controlled trials (n=679) found that selenium supplementation (80-200 mcg/day for 3-12 months) did not improve TSH levels, thyroid echogenicity, or health-related quality of life [40]. The authors concluded that the evidence does not support selenium supplementation specifically for autoimmune thyroiditis.

Grokipedia reports that selenium supplementation at 200 mcg/day reduces TPOAb by 20-40% in Hashimoto's patients, with effects stemming from selenium's role in selenoproteins that regulate inflammation and thyroid hormone metabolism [42].

**Pregnancy and thyroid autoimmunity:**

Up to 50% of pregnant women who are TPOAb-positive in the first trimester will develop postpartum thyroiditis [5]. Results from clinical trials have been mixed:
- One trial gave 200 mcg/day selenomethionine to 151 TPOAb-positive pregnant women from week 12.5 through 12 months postpartum. Only 28.6% developed postpartum thyroid disorders versus 48.6% on placebo, and 11.7% developed permanent hypothyroidism versus 20.3% on placebo [43].
- Another trial (83 mcg/day selenomethionine) also reported reductions in thyroid antibodies at 6 months postpartum [44].
- However, a third trial (60 mcg/day selenium yeast) in TPOAb-positive pregnant women with mild to moderate iodine deficiency showed no reduction in TPOAb levels [45].

In 2017, the American Thyroid Association issued a weak recommendation against selenium supplementation for pregnant women who are TPOAb-positive, based on moderate quality evidence [46].

**Healthy adults:**

A randomized trial of 100, 200, or 300 mcg/day selenium yeast for 6 months in 368 healthy adults (aged 60-74 years, mean baseline selenium 9.13 mcg/dL) found no effect on thyroid function despite plasma selenium increases in all groups [47].

**Selenium-iodine interaction:**

Selenium deficiency can exacerbate iodine deficiency, increasing the risk of congenital hypothyroidism by impairing deiodinase enzymes needed for thyroid hormone activation [1][48]. Endemic myxedematous hypothyroidism, which occurs in regions with very low selenium (e.g., Central Africa), is thought to develop during gestation or early childhood [1].

**Synthesis:** Selenium is clearly important for normal thyroid function, and correcting deficiency in selenium-poor populations may protect against thyroid disease. For Hashimoto's thyroiditis, selenium supplementation can lower antibody markers (particularly TPOAb), but this does not consistently translate to improved thyroid function or quality of life. The evidence is strongest in populations with low baseline selenium.

### Cardiovascular Disease

Selenoproteins help reduce inflammation and prevent lipid oxidation and platelet aggregation — mechanisms that theoretically could reduce cardiovascular risk [49][50].

**Observational evidence:**

A 2006 meta-analysis of 25 observational studies (primarily men over 40) found an inverse association between selenium concentrations and coronary heart disease risk: men with the highest selenium had 15% lower risk (Flores-Mateo, *Am J Clin Nutr*, 2006) [51]. However, subsequent studies have yielded conflicting findings. A study in 1,042 middle-aged adults found that higher plasma selenium was actually associated with higher total and non-HDL cholesterol [52]. A study of 13,887 US adults showed no association between baseline serum selenium and cardiovascular mortality over 12 years [53]. An 18-year prospective study in 3,112 young adults similarly found no association between toenail selenium and subclinical atherosclerosis measures [54].

A 2025 meta-analysis of cohort studies reported that selenium intake above 55 mcg/day correlated with a 20-30% lower cardiovascular disease risk, with glutathione peroxidase activity identified as a key mediator of endothelial protection [55].

**Clinical trials:**

A trial of 474 healthy adults (aged 60-74, mean baseline selenium 9.12 mcg/dL) found that selenium supplementation at 100 and 200 mcg/day lowered total cholesterol by 8.5-9.7 mg/dL and non-HDL cholesterol by 7.7-10.4 mg/dL, while 300 mcg/day increased HDL by 2.3 mg/dL. However, the clinical significance was noted as unclear [56].

The SELECT trial assessed cardiovascular risk as a secondary outcome in 17,488 men and found no effect of 200 mcg/day selenium on cardiovascular events over 7-12 years [31]. A Cochrane Review of SELECT and 11 smaller trials (total n=19,715) also found that selenium supplementation did not reduce fatal or nonfatal cardiovascular events [50].

One notable finding relates to selenium as part of antioxidant mixtures: a meta-analysis of 43 trials found that antioxidant formulas containing selenium reduced cardiovascular mortality by 23% compared to antioxidant formulas without selenium, though selenium alone had no effect [57]. This suggests potential synergistic benefits when selenium is combined with other antioxidants.

Selenium does not appear to be helpful for preventing heart disease when used alone. One fairly large double-blind study also failed to find that selenium supplementation improved general sense of well-being (Rayman, *Biol Psychiatry*, 2006) [58].

**Synthesis:** Observational associations between higher selenium status and lower cardiovascular risk are inconsistent, and clinical trial evidence does not support selenium supplementation alone for cardiovascular disease prevention. However, selenium as part of an antioxidant combination may confer modest cardiovascular mortality benefit.

### Cognitive Decline and Alzheimer's Disease

Selenoproteins have antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities relevant to brain health, and serum selenium concentrations decline with age [59][60]. Chronic selenium deficiency has been correlated with cognitive decline [61][62].

**Observational evidence:**

An analysis of NHANES data (2011-2014) showed that higher whole blood selenium and higher selenium intakes were associated with higher cognitive scores in older adults [18][63]. A study of 1,012 men and women in Italy (mean age 75 years) found that those in the lowest quartile of plasma selenium (<6.67 mcg/dL) had worse neuro-motor performance than those in the highest quartile (>8.23 mcg/dL) [60]. A 2024 meta-analysis of trace elements in Alzheimer's disease patients revealed significantly lower serum selenium levels (by 15-25 mcg/L) compared to controls [64]. Another 2024 study linked higher selenium status to better global and attention-related cognitive function [65].

However, not all findings are consistent. A study of 154 healthy people in Australia (85% selenium replete, average plasma selenium 16.9 mcg/dL) found no association between selenium and cognitive outcomes [66].

**Clinical trial evidence:**

The PREADVISE trial (Prevention of Alzheimer's Disease with Vitamin E and Selenium) — the first large-scale primary prevention trial — randomized 7,540 cognitively healthy men aged 60+ to 200 mcg/day selenomethionine, 400 IU/day vitamin E, both, or placebo for 5.4 years. No difference in the incidence of Alzheimer's disease or dementia was found among any of the groups (Kryscio, *JAMA*, 2017) [67].

A post hoc analysis of the SU.VI.MAX study (n=4,447, aged 45-60) found that a daily antioxidant supplement containing 100 mcg selenium (plus vitamin C, vitamin E, beta-carotene, and zinc) for 8 years was associated with higher episodic memory and semantic fluency scores 6 years after supplementation ended [68]. However, selenium's independent contribution cannot be determined from this study.

A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis of 11 clinical trials evaluating selenium supplements (alone or with other nutrients) for 12-24 weeks in adults aged 69-89 with mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer's disease found no improvement in cognitive test scores [69].

**Synthesis:** The mechanistic rationale for selenium in brain health is strong, and observational studies consistently show associations between low selenium and cognitive decline. However, clinical trial evidence does not support selenium supplementation for preventing or treating cognitive decline in adults with adequate selenium levels.

### Immune Function and Infectious Disease

Selenium deficiency may contribute to a weakened immune system through impaired T-cell proliferation and antibody production [1][17].

**COVID-19:**

A small study in Germany found that selenium deficiency was common among people diagnosed with COVID-19, with approximately 39-43% having levels below the 2.5th percentile. Selenium deficiency was more common in individuals who died from COVID-19 compared to survivors (64-70% vs 32-39%, respectively). However, it is not known whether selenium deficiency is a risk factor for COVID-19 or a consequence of the illness (Moghaddam, *Nutrients*, 2020) [70]. Recent research (2024-2025) on selenium nanoparticles has demonstrated strong antiviral activity against SARS-CoV-2 in vitro, with up to 90% inhibition of viral replication [71].

**HIV:**

People living with HIV often have low selenium concentrations, possibly due to malabsorption or inadequate intake [72]. Observational studies have found associations between lower selenium and worse HIV outcomes, including faster disease progression, cardiomyopathy, and in pregnant women, HIV transmission to offspring [73][74]. However, clinical trials have shown limited benefit:
- A trial in Rwanda (n=300 HIV-positive adults) found that 200 mcg/day selenium slowed CD4+ T-cell decline by 43.8%, but had no effect on the composite endpoint of CD4 <350 or ART initiation [75].
- A trial in Iran (n=146 HIV-positive adults on ART) found no benefit of 200 mcg/day selenium on CD4 counts [76].
- Two Cochrane Reviews concluded insufficient evidence that selenium supplementation benefits people with HIV [77][78].
- For pregnant women with HIV, a trial in Nigeria (n=90) found that 200 mcg/day selenium reduced preterm birth risk by 68% but did not affect HIV markers [79].

**Synthesis:** Adequate selenium status supports immune function, and deficiency clearly impairs immunity. However, selenium supplementation above adequate levels does not appear to provide meaningful immune benefits in well-nourished populations.

### Male Fertility

The selenoprotein phospholipid hydroperoxide glutathione peroxidase (GPx4) is a major structural component of mature sperm, and both low and high semen selenium concentrations have been associated with male infertility [80][81].

**Evidence of benefit:**

A 3-month trial in Scotland in 69 men with reduced sperm motility and low selenium plasma concentrations (mean 8.1 mcg/dL) found that 100 mcg/day selenomethionine improved plasma selenium, sperm motility, and the odds of conception versus placebo. Adding vitamins A, C, and E provided no additional benefit [82]. Another 3-month trial of 200 mcg/day selenium yeast in 70 men with idiopathic infertility reported improved total and progressive sperm motility, though this trial lacked a placebo group [83].

**Evidence of no benefit:**

A trial in 54 healthy men given 300 mcg/day selenium yeast for 48 weeks found no differences in sperm concentration or motility versus placebo, and semen volume declined in both groups [84].

**Synthesis:** Selenium supplementation may improve sperm motility in selenium-deficient men with reduced fertility, but it does not appear to benefit men with adequate selenium status. There is preliminary evidence that selenium may be helpful, particularly in combination with vitamin E, but more research is needed.

### Bone Health

Greater bone mineral density has been associated with higher selenium levels in the blood of postmenopausal women (Hoeg, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab*, 2012) [85]. This is consistent with selenium's role in reducing inflammation and oxidative stress that contribute to bone loss.

### Rheumatoid Arthritis

Low selenium levels have been associated with the development of rheumatoid arthritis, although selenium supplements do not appear to help once the disease has developed (Tarp, *Analyst*, 1995) [86].

### Diabetic Neuropathy

There is weak preliminary evidence that selenium might be helpful for diabetic neuropathy (Kahler, *Z Gesamte Inn Med*, 1993) [87].

### Achilles Tendinopathy

Prophylactic use of selenium along with vitamin E may slightly reduce the severity (but not incidence) of Achilles tendinopathy caused by fluoroquinolone antibiotics such as ciprofloxacin and levofloxacin [10].

### Nutrient Interactions

**Selenium and iodine:** Selenium deficiency can exacerbate iodine deficiency, increasing the risk of congenital hypothyroidism by impairing the deiodinase enzymes required for thyroid hormone activation [1][48].

**Selenium and vitamin E:** These nutrients work synergistically in antioxidant protection. Selenium (via glutathione peroxidase) helps regenerate vitamin E from its oxidized form, amplifying defense against lipid peroxidation [88]. However, the SELECT trial revealed a complex interaction: vitamin E increased prostate cancer risk by 46-111% in men low in selenium, while having no such effect in men with adequate selenium — suggesting that selenium status modulates the risk profile of vitamin E supplementation [32].

**Selenium and coenzyme Q10 (CoQ10):** Selenium facilitates CoQ10 recycling and supports the formation of antioxidant enzymes. A randomized trial found that combined supplementation with selenium and CoQ10 improved cardiovascular health outcomes, with more pronounced effects in selenium-deficient populations [89][90].

### Tolerable Upper Intake Levels (ULs)

The UL for selenium is **400 mcg/day** for adults and adolescents 14+ (combined dietary and supplemental intake). ULs are lower for younger age groups: 45 mcg (0-6 months), 60 mcg (7-12 months), 90 mcg (1-3 years), 150 mcg (4-8 years), and 280 mcg (9-13 years) [1][91].

In 2023, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) set a lower UL of **255 mcg/day** for all adults, including pregnant and lactating women, based on systematic reviews linking excess selenium to alopecia. Children's ULs range from 70 to 230 mcg/day depending on age [92].

### Practical Dosing Considerations

**Most adults in the US and Canada do not need selenium supplements.** The average US adult already consumes approximately 108 mcg/day from food alone — nearly double the RDA [9]. Adding a selenium supplement on top of adequate dietary intake could push total intake toward or above the UL.

**For general supplementation (to fill potential gaps):** A modest dose of 25-55 mcg from a multivitamin is reasonable and unlikely to cause harm. Since it is selenium deficiency that is associated with disease risk, meeting the RDA should be sufficient to capture any benefit [10].

**For Hashimoto's thyroiditis:** Studies have used 80-200 mcg/day (as selenomethionine or sodium selenite), though benefits have been most evident at 200 mcg/day in populations with low baseline selenium [41][42].

**For HIV patients:** 200-250 mcg/day of L-selenomethionine has been studied for 12-24 months, though evidence of clinical benefit is limited [10][75].

**For male fertility (in deficient men):** 100-200 mcg/day for at least 3 months [82][83].

**Study dosing:** A higher dose of 200 mcg/day (typically as 0.5 gram high-selenium brewer's yeast tablet) has been used in many clinical trials [10].

### Important Safety Note Regarding Dosing

When considering selenium supplementation, it is critical to account for dietary intake. Most US adults already consume approximately 100-108 mcg of selenium daily from food [9]. Adding 200 mcg from a supplement would bring total intake to approximately 300-308 mcg/day. A study in Denmark found that adults who took 300 mcg/day selenium for 5 years had an 11% greater risk of death from all causes compared to placebo, with no benefit from lower doses. The researchers concluded that even 200 mcg/day supplemental selenium could result in toxicity in populations where selenium levels are already high, such as in the US (average blood selenium 137 mcg/L) (Rayman, *Free Radic Biol Med*, 2018) [93].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Selenosis (Chronic Toxicity)

Chronically high selenium intake leads to selenosis, with effects similar for organic and inorganic forms [1][94]. The earliest clinical signs are:

- **Hair and nail brittleness and loss** — the primary indicator used to establish the UL of 400 mcg/day [1][91]
- **Garlic odor on the breath** and metallic taste in the mouth — early warning signs [1][94]
- **Skin rash, nausea, diarrhea, fatigue, irritability**
- **Nervous system abnormalities** (depression, nervousness, emotional instability) — typically beginning at approximately 900 mcg/day [10]

A case report from Turkey described a 48-year-old woman consuming 150 mcg/day from a supplement plus an estimated 650-850 mcg/day from nuts, resulting in blood selenium of 1,300 mcg/L (normal range: 23-190 mcg/L). Within 6 months of discontinuing selenium and nut consumption, hair loss stopped, hair density increased, and nail abnormalities improved dramatically (Civas, *J Cosmet Dermatol*, 2023) [95].

### Acute Toxicity

Acute selenium toxicity can result from consuming misformulated supplements. In 2008, 201 people experienced severe adverse reactions from a liquid dietary supplement containing 200 times the labeled amount of selenium [96]. Acute toxicity can cause severe gastrointestinal and neurological symptoms, respiratory distress syndrome, myocardial infarction, kidney failure, cardiac failure, and, in rare cases, death [1][96].

At the cellular level, excess selenium binds to thiol groups in proteins, causing non-specific substitution of sulfur atoms, disrupting protein structure and function, and generating reactive oxygen species that damage lipids, proteins, and DNA [97][98].

### Diabetes Risk

One of the most concerning findings from selenium research is the association with type 2 diabetes:

- The SELECT trial found that among people with higher baseline selenium, those given 200 mcg/day were **2.7 times more likely to develop type 2 diabetes** compared to placebo. No increase was seen in those with lower baseline selenium (Stranges, *Ann Intern Med*, 2007) [99].
- A study of 21,334 men and women in Italy found that those with the highest dietary selenium intakes (median 77-88 mcg/day — still below the UL) had a **64% greater risk** of being hospitalized for type 2 diabetes over 8 years, independent of BMI (Vinceti, *Nutr Metab Cardiovasc Dis*, 2021) [100].

These findings reinforce the principle that selenium supplementation should be reserved for those who are deficient or at risk of deficiency, not used indiscriminately.

### Prostate Cancer Risk in Men with High Selenium

The SELECT trial found that 200 mcg/day selenium increased the risk of high-grade prostate cancer by 91% among men with already-high selenium levels, while having no effect in selenium-low men [32]. This was accompanied by a significant increase in hair loss and dermatitis with selenium supplementation (Lippman, *JAMA*, 2009) [30]. Another study found that men with non-metastatic prostate cancer who supplemented with 140+ mcg/day selenium were 160% more likely to die from prostate cancer (Kenfield, *J Natl Cancer Inst*, 2015) [33]. The researchers cautioned that men 55+ should avoid selenium supplementation at doses exceeding recommended daily intakes [32].

### All-Cause Mortality at High Doses

A 15-year follow-up study of Danish adults with low baseline selenium found that those who took 300 mcg/day selenium for 5 years had an 11% greater risk of all-cause mortality compared to placebo. Lower doses (100 mcg, 200 mcg) had no effect on mortality (Rayman, *Free Radic Biol Med*, 2018) [93].

### U-Shaped Risk Curve

The overall evidence points to a U-shaped relationship between selenium status and health outcomes: both too little and too much selenium are harmful. The therapeutic window is narrow. People with adequate-to-high selenium status (serum selenium ≥122 mcg/L) may be adversely affected by supplementation and should not take selenium supplements (Rayman, *Lancet*, 2012) [101].

### Maximum Safe Doses

Maximum safe doses of selenium for individuals with severe liver or kidney disease have not been established [10]. For the general population, the US UL of 400 mcg/day and the more conservative EFSA UL of 255 mcg/day provide the safety boundaries. These ULs apply to combined dietary plus supplemental intake [1][91][92].

### Special Populations

**Brazil nut caution:** Brazil nuts contain 68-91 mcg per nut (average 544 mcg per ounce of 6-8 nuts). Regular consumption can easily exceed the UL and should be monitored carefully [1][96].

**Kidney dialysis patients:** Selenium concentrations are often significantly lower in patients undergoing hemodialysis, partly because the procedure removes selenium from blood. These patients also face dietary restrictions that may limit selenium intake [102][103].

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Cisplatin

Cisplatin, an inorganic platinum chemotherapy agent used for ovarian, bladder, lung, and other cancers, can reduce selenium levels in hair and serum. Whether these reductions have clinically significant impact is not known [104][105]. Some small studies suggest selenium supplementation may reduce cisplatin's toxicity, but a Cochrane Review concluded the evidence is insufficient [106].

### Medications That May Reduce Selenium Absorption

Certain digestive conditions and medications that reduce stomach acid may impair selenium absorption [10]. Proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), H2 receptor antagonists, and antacids may potentially reduce absorption of some selenium forms, though this interaction is not well characterized for selenium specifically.

### General Guidance

Individuals taking medications on a regular basis should discuss their selenium status with their healthcare provider, particularly those on chemotherapy regimens or long-term acid-suppression therapy [1][106].

### Practical Notes

- **Brazil nuts are uniquely rich** in selenium but highly variable — a single nut provides roughly 68-91 mcg. Just 1-2 Brazil nuts daily can meet or exceed the RDA without supplementation, but regular consumption of a full ounce can exceed the UL [1][96].
- **Animal sources are more consistent** than plant sources because animals maintain predictable tissue selenium concentrations through homeostatic mechanisms, and formulated livestock feeds contain consistent selenium levels [1][108].
- **Selenium in plant foods varies widely** by soil selenium content. For example, wheat grown in selenium-rich Midwestern US soils contains substantially more selenium than wheat from selenium-poor European soils [1][9].
- **Selenium in plant-based foods is more bioavailable** than that in meat, eggs, and dairy. However, because of soil variability, the total amount consumed from plants is less predictable [10].
- **Drinking water** is not a nutritionally significant source of selenium in most regions [1].
- **In selenium-deficient regions** such as Finland, national programs have fortified fertilizers with sodium selenate since 1984, effectively raising population selenium intake without exceeding safe limits — a strategy unique to Finland [24].

### Regional Considerations

Selenium intakes and serum concentrations in the US and Canada vary somewhat by region due to differences in soil selenium content. Residents of the Midwestern and Western US have higher concentrations than those in the South and Northeast [109]. However, the extensive transport and distribution of food typically ensures adequate selenium intake even in lower-selenium areas [1].

### Groups at Higher Risk of Inadequacy

- **People in selenium-deficient regions** (parts of Europe, China, sub-Saharan Africa)
- **Vegetarians and vegans** (especially in low-selenium regions like the UK)
- **People undergoing kidney dialysis** (hemodialysis removes selenium from blood)
- **People living with HIV** (possibly due to malabsorption or inadequate intake)
- **Smokers** (lower selenium status, possibly due to increased oxidative stress)
