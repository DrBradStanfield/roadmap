## Current view (reviewed 22 October 2026)

Brad describes selenium as critical for thyroid function, reproduction, DNA synthesis, and protection from infection and free-radical damage ([Brad, 7 Jan 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/trace-minerals-vitamins-fad)). He notes that deficiency is rare in the developed world and usually comes from dialysis, HIV, or local foods that are low in selenium ([Brad, 7 Jan 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/trace-minerals-vitamins-fad)). The recommended intake is 55 mcg for adults, with slightly more, 60 to 70 mcg, in pregnancy and breastfeeding ([Brad, 7 Jan 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/trace-minerals-vitamins-fad)). Because trace mineral needs are very low and toxic levels are much higher, he sees a small amount in a multivitamin and mineral supplement as a way to be sure of getting enough ([Brad, 7 Jan 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/trace-minerals-vitamins-fad)). His biggest concern is megadosing: almost all trace minerals become toxic at megadose levels, and supplements, unlike food, can easily go well over the limits ([Brad, 7 Jan 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/trace-minerals-vitamins-fad)). So check that a supplement does not give you hundreds of times more than you need ([Brad, 7 Jan 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/trace-minerals-vitamins-fad)).

## Key safety points

- If you have prostate cancer, be cautious: 140 mcg a day or more after diagnosis was linked to a 2.60-fold higher risk of dying from it [33].
- The US upper limit for adults is 400 mcg from food and supplements combined; Europe's EFSA sets 255 mcg a day [1]. In a small Danish trial, 300 mcg a day for 5 years raised deaths over the whole follow-up (hazard ratio 1.59) [93].
- People with adequate or high selenium status might be harmed by supplements and should not take them [101]. In SELECT, high-grade prostate cancer rose by 91% with selenium in men whose selenium status was already higher [30][32].
- Selenium supplements may raise the risk of type 2 diabetes (hazard ratio 1.55 with 200 mcg a day in the NPC trial) [99][34].
- Cisplatin chemotherapy can lower selenium levels. If you have chemotherapy or take medicines regularly, discuss selenium with your doctor [1][106].
- Chronic excess (selenosis) causes hair loss and brittle or lost nails; garlic breath and a metallic taste can come first [1].
- Brazil nuts hold 68 to 91 mcg per nut and could cause toxicity if eaten regularly [1]. Content varies: in one trial, two nuts a day averaged 53 mcg (possible range 20 to 84 mcg) [111].
- Labels can be wrong: in 2008 a liquid supplement with 200 times its labelled selenium caused 201 cases of poisoning [96].

## 1. Overview

Selenium is an essential trace mineral and part of 25 selenoproteins in humans [1][2]. Selenoproteins are involved in thyroid hormone metabolism, DNA synthesis, reproduction, and protection from oxidative damage and infection [1]. Selenium concentration is higher in the thyroid than in any other organ, which reflects its role in thyroid function [1].

Key selenoprotein families include:

- **Glutathione peroxidases (GPx):** Antioxidant enzymes. With thioredoxin reductase, they help protect the thyroid from the hydrogen peroxide made during hormone synthesis [1]. The phospholipid form is a major structural part of the midpiece of mature sperm [80].
- **Thioredoxin reductases (TrxR):** Enzymes involved in redox homeostasis [2].
- **Iodothyronine deiodinases (DIO1, DIO2, DIO3):** Enzymes involved in the conversion of the prohormone thyroxine (T4) to the active triiodothyronine (T3) [1][2].
- **Selenoprotein P:** One of the two main selenoproteins in plasma. With glutathione peroxidase 3, it can serve as a biomarker of selenium status, although inflammation can affect both [1].

In the United States and Canada, selenium deficiency is very rare; intakes are well above the 55 mcg Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) even in low-selenium regions; for people aged 2 years and older, average daily intake from foods and beverages is 108 mcg [1][9]. The lowest intakes in the world are in parts of China, and average intakes are also low in some European countries, especially among people who follow vegan diets [1]. New Zealand intakes were low in the past but rose after the country imported more high-selenium wheat [1]. Vegetarians and vegans may take in less selenium than others [1].

Selenium deficiency alone rarely causes overt illness, but it produces biochemical changes that might make people more likely to become ill under other stresses [1]. Severe deficiency is associated with two endemic diseases:

- **Keshan disease:** An endemic cardiomyopathy first identified in 1935 in parts of China with low-selenium soil, mainly affecting women of childbearing age and preschool children [1]. Large trials of sodium selenite tablets in China sharply reduced its incidence, and a 2018 meta-analysis of 41 studies found a risk ratio of 0.14 (95% CI 0.12 to 0.16) with selenium supplements [14][15].
- **Kashin-Beck disease:** A type of osteoarthritis that commonly presents in childhood and puberty in certain low-selenium and low-iodine areas of China, Tibet, Siberia, and North Korea [1]. In low-quality trials, sodium selenite improved bone findings on X-ray in affected children [16].

Plasma and serum selenium concentrations are the most commonly used measures of selenium status. Concentrations of 8 mcg/dL or higher are usually considered sufficient for selenoprotein synthesis in healthy people [1]. The mean serum selenium concentration in the US population aged 8 to 80 years is 12.7 mcg/dL [1]. Status is lower in people who smoke, possibly because smoking increases oxidative stress [1]. Approximately 28% to 46% of the body's total selenium content is found in skeletal muscle, and selenium homeostasis is maintained primarily through urinary excretion [1].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

Dietary selenium is readily absorbed. The body absorbs up to about 90% of the selenium from the common supplement forms: selenomethionine, selenium-enriched yeast, sodium selenite, and sodium selenate [1].

### Common Supplement Forms

| Form | Absorption | Key Characteristics |
|------|:-:|------|
| **Selenomethionine** | Up to about 90% | Organic form; the main form of selenium in foods. Used in the SELECT and PREADVISE trials [1]. |
| **Selenium-enriched yeast** | Up to about 90% | Yeast grown in a high-selenium medium; contains predominantly selenomethionine. Used in the Nutritional Prevention of Cancer Trial [1]. |
| **Sodium selenite** | Up to about 90% | Inorganic form. Used in some thyroid trials [1]. |
| **Sodium selenate** | Up to about 90% | Inorganic form [1]. |
| **Selenium glycinate** | Not reported | Selenium bound to the amino acid glycine. |
| **Se-methyl-L-selenocysteine** | Not established | EFSA found the data submitted to it too weak to show that this form is safe and bioavailable as a supplement source of selenium, under the proposed conditions of use [92]. |

### Key Differences Between Organic and Inorganic Forms

Once absorbed, selenomethionine and inorganic selenium are rapidly converted to a common intermediate that the body uses to make selenocysteine, the form of selenium in selenoproteins [1].

The forms differ in how they raise blood levels. In a 16-week trial in selenium-replete adults given about 200 to 600 mcg a day, selenomethionine and selenium yeast raised plasma selenium in step with the dose, while sodium selenite did not; no form raised selenoprotein P or glutathione peroxidase activity [8].

In a 9-month randomized trial in 69 healthy men, selenomethionine (200 mcg a day) and selenium yeast (200 or 285 mcg a day) all raised plasma selenium, but only the higher yeast dose lowered two urine markers of oxidative stress [22].

### Practical Form Selection

Selenomethionine and selenium yeast are the forms used in the large cancer and dementia prevention trials, and sodium selenite was used in some thyroid trials [1].

### Factors Affecting Absorption

Selenium absorption is largely not affected by selenium status, meaning the body absorbs a similar proportion whether stores are adequate or depleted [1].

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Cancer

The relationship between selenium and cancer has been extensively studied, with results that have evolved significantly over the past three decades.

**Early evidence suggesting benefit:**

Epidemiological studies suggested that people with higher selenium status have a lower risk of several types of cancer [1]. The Nutritional Prevention of Cancer (NPC) Trial gave 1,312 men and women with a history of nonmelanoma skin cancer 200 mcg/day of selenium as selenium yeast or placebo for a mean of 4.5 years [1]. Selenium did not reduce basal or squamous cell skin cancer [26]. In secondary analyses, it reduced total cancer incidence (RR 0.63, 95% CI 0.47 to 0.85) and cancer mortality, with fewer lung, colorectal, and prostate cancers [26]. By the end of blinded treatment, the reduction in prostate cancer held (RR 0.51, 95% CI 0.29 to 0.87) but was confined to men with lower baseline plasma selenium (less than 123.2 ng/mL) [27].

In a US case-control study in African-American women, those with the highest supplemental selenium intake (more than 20 mcg a day) had lower odds of ovarian cancer than women taking none (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.46 to 0.97), and dietary selenium showed no link [28].

**Large trials showing no benefit:**

The Selenium and Vitamin E Cancer Prevention Trial (SELECT) randomized 35,533 men in the United States, Canada, and Puerto Rico to 200 mcg a day of selenium as L-selenomethionine, 400 IU a day of vitamin E, both, or placebo [30]. After a median of 5.46 years, selenium had not prevented prostate cancer (HR 1.04, 99% CI 0.87 to 1.24) [30]. Longer follow-up after the supplements stopped again showed no significant effect of selenium (HR 1.09, 99% CI 0.93 to 1.27) [31]. In a case-cohort analysis of 1739 prostate cancer cases from SELECT, selenium had no effect in men with lower baseline toenail selenium but high-grade prostate cancer rose by 91% in men whose selenium status was higher [32].

**Evidence of potential harm with high-dose supplementation:**

In 4459 men with non-metastatic prostate cancer followed after diagnosis, those taking 140 mcg a day or more of supplemental selenium had a 2.60-fold higher risk of dying from prostate cancer (95% CI 1.44 to 4.70) than non-users, with no link to overall mortality. The authors advised caution about these supplements in men with prostate cancer [33].

**Systematic reviews:**

A 2018 Cochrane Review included 83 studies on selenium and cancer [34]. In its 70 observational cohort studies, the highest selenium exposure was linked to lower cancer incidence (OR 0.72, 95% CI 0.55 to 0.93) and lower cancer mortality (OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.59 to 0.97), but the certainty of this evidence was very low [34].

In its 10 randomized trials with 27,232 participants, selenium did not reduce cancer incidence (RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.93 to 1.10 in trials with low risk of bias), with high-certainty evidence for prostate, colorectal, lung, and bladder cancers [34].

Starting in 2003, the US FDA allowed qualified health claims that selenium "may reduce the risk of certain forms of cancer" but noted that this evidence is "limited and not conclusive." In 2009, FDA determined it is "highly unlikely" that selenium supplements reduce prostate cancer risk and "highly uncertain" that they reduce the risk of bladder cancer in women or thyroid cancer [1][36].

**Synthesis:** Well-conducted trials found no cancer benefit from extra selenium, and some found harm, such as more high-grade prostate cancer in men whose selenium was already higher [32][34]. The NPC trial raised the idea that people with low selenium could cut their cancer risk by taking more, but the 2018 Cochrane Review judged this idea unconfirmed [34].

### Thyroid Disease

Selenium concentration is higher in the thyroid than in any other organ. Selenoproteins catalyze the conversion of T4 to the active T3, and glutathione peroxidase and thioredoxin reductase protect the thyroid from the hydrogen peroxide made during thyroid hormone synthesis [1].

**Epidemiological associations:**

Epidemiological studies have linked low selenium status to a higher risk of thyroid disease, but only in women [1]. In 1,900 middle-aged adults, higher serum selenium was associated with protection against goiter in women but not men, and a cross-sectional study of 805 adults in Denmark with mild iodine deficiency found the same pattern for thyroid volume [1].

**Hashimoto's thyroiditis (chronic autoimmune thyroiditis):**

This condition affects 1 to 2 percent of the population, particularly middle-aged women [1]. A 2016 systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 controlled trials examined selenium supplementation for chronic autoimmune thyroiditis [41]. Key findings:
- In patients taking levothyroxine, selenium lowered thyroid peroxidase antibody (TPOAb) levels after 3 months, and this was consistent at 6 and 12 months [41].
- In newly diagnosed patients not taking levothyroxine, selenium lowered TPOAb and thyroglobulin antibody (TgAb) levels at 3 months, but not at 6 or 12 months [41].
- People taking selenium were more likely than controls to report adverse effects, and the quality of evidence was generally low [41].
- Trials used 80 to 200 mcg a day of selenomethionine or sodium selenite, and selenomethionine lowered TPOAb more than sodium selenite [1].

However, a 2017 meta-analysis of nine controlled trials found that selenium did not change TSH levels, thyroid ultrasound findings, or health-related quality of life in patients not taking levothyroxine [40]. The authors concluded that the evidence does not yet document clinical benefit.

**Pregnancy and thyroid autoimmunity:**

Up to 50% of pregnant women who are TPOAb or TgAb positive in the first trimester will develop postpartum thyroiditis [1]. Results from clinical trials have been mixed:
- In one trial, 77 TPOAb-positive women took 200 mcg a day of selenomethionine during pregnancy and after birth, and 74 took placebo. Postpartum thyroid dysfunction (28.6 vs 48.6%) and permanent hypothyroidism (11.7 vs 20.3%) were less common with selenium [43].
- In 45 pregnant women with thyroiditis, 83 mcg a day of L-selenomethionine lowered thyroid antibodies after birth compared with placebo [44].
- However, in 230 UK pregnant women with mild-to-moderate iodine deficiency, 60 mcg a day of selenium had no more effect than placebo on TPOAb [45].

In 2017, the American Thyroid Association issued a weak recommendation against selenium supplementation for TPOAb-positive pregnant women, based on moderate quality evidence [1][46].

**Healthy adults:**

A randomized trial gave 100, 200, or 300 mcg/day selenium as selenium yeast or placebo for 6 months to UK adults aged 60 to 74 years [1][47]. It randomized 501 people, and in the 368 whose blood was analysed, thyroid function did not change, despite higher plasma selenium [47].

**Selenium-iodine interaction:**

Selenium deficiency could exacerbate iodine deficiency, potentially increasing the risk of congenital hypothyroidism in infants [1]. Endemic myxedematous hypothyroidism, which occurs in regions with very low selenium (such as Central Africa), is thought to develop during gestation or early childhood [1].

**Synthesis:** Selenium is clearly important for normal thyroid function, and correcting deficiency in selenium-poor populations may protect against thyroid disease. In chronic autoimmune thyroiditis, selenium supplementation has generally lowered thyroid antibodies without apparent improvement in the course of the disease [5]. The evidence is strongest in populations with low baseline selenium.

### Cardiovascular Disease

Selenoproteins help reduce inflammation and prevent lipid oxidation and platelet aggregation, so experts have hypothesized that selenium supplements could reduce cardiovascular risk [1].

**Observational evidence:**

A 2006 meta-analysis pooled 25 observational studies [1]. In its cohort studies, the highest selenium concentrations were linked to lower coronary heart disease risk (RR 0.85, 95% CI 0.74 to 0.99), but the authors judged the validity of this association uncertain (Flores-Mateo, *Am J Clin Nutr*, 2006) [51]. Later studies conflicted. In 1042 British adults, higher plasma selenium was associated with higher total and non-HDL cholesterol [52]. In 13,887 US adults followed for up to 12 years, serum selenium was not associated with cardiovascular mortality [53]. An 18-year study of 3112 young American adults found no association between toenail selenium and subclinical atherosclerosis [54].

**Clinical trials:**

In a trial of 501 UK adults aged 60 to 74 years, 100 and 200 mcg a day of high-selenium yeast for 6 months lowered total cholesterol by 8.5 and 9.7 mg/dL compared with placebo, while 300 mcg a day raised HDL cholesterol by 2.3 mg/dL; the authors called the clinical significance unclear [56].

The SELECT trial assessed cardiovascular risk as a secondary outcome in 17,488 men and found that 200 mcg a day of selenium did not affect cardiovascular events over 7 to 12 years [1]. A Cochrane Review of 12 trials with 19,715 participants also found that selenium supplements did not reduce fatal or nonfatal cardiovascular events [50].

A 2020 meta-analysis of 43 trials found no association of selenium alone with cardiovascular disease, but antioxidant mixtures that included selenium were linked to lower cardiovascular mortality (RR 0.77, 95% CI 0.62 to 0.97), with no such link for mixtures without selenium [57]. These were combination products, so the share due to selenium cannot be separated.

**Synthesis:** Observational associations between higher selenium status and lower cardiovascular risk are inconsistent, and clinical trial evidence does not support selenium supplementation alone for cardiovascular disease prevention. Antioxidant mixtures containing selenium were linked to lower cardiovascular mortality in one meta-analysis, but this result may not reflect selenium alone.

### Cognitive Decline and Alzheimer's Disease

Selenoproteins have antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activities relevant to brain health, and serum selenium concentrations decline with age [1][59]. Low selenium status has been associated with cognitive decline [61][62][101].

**Observational evidence:**

In 2068 older Americans in NHANES 2011 to 2014, higher whole blood selenium was associated with higher cognitive scores [110]. In 1681 adults aged at least 65 years from the same survey, adequate selenium intake was linked to higher cognitive scores, but the link was no longer significant after adjusting for energy intake [63]. In 1,012 older adults in Italy, lower plasma selenium was associated with poorer performance on tests of coordination [60]. However, a study of 154 older adults in Australia, 85% of whom had adequate selenium intake, found no association between plasma selenium and cognitive performance [66].

**Clinical trial evidence:**

The PREADViSE trial randomized 7540 men aged at least 60 years, recruited at SELECT sites, to vitamin E, selenium, both, or placebo. Neither supplement prevented dementia (HR 0.83, 95% CI 0.60 to 1.13 for selenium) (Kryscio, *JAMA Neurol*, 2017) [67].

A post hoc analysis of the SU.VI.MAX trial found better episodic memory scores years after people stopped a daily antioxidant supplement that included selenium [68]. The 4,447 French participants, aged 45 to 60 years, took 100 mcg selenium with vitamin C, vitamin E, beta-carotene, and zinc for 8 years, and were tested 6 years after supplementation ended [1]. However, selenium's independent contribution cannot be determined from this study.

A 2022 systematic review of 11 clinical trials in people with mild cognitive impairment or Alzheimer's disease reported that selenium, alone or with other nutrients, raised selenium levels and improved scores on some cognitive tests, and the authors called for more detailed studies [69]. NIH reads the same trials differently: in its summary, cognitive test scores did not improve [1].

In a 6-month trial, 501 UK adults aged 60 to 74 received 100, 200 or 300 mcg of selenium a day as high-selenium yeast or placebo, and selenium did not improve mood or quality of life (Rayman, *Biol Psychiatry*, 2006) [58].

**Synthesis:** The mechanistic rationale for selenium in brain health is strong, and observational studies often show associations between low selenium and cognitive decline. However, the large prevention trial found no benefit, and clinical trial evidence does not support selenium supplementation for preventing cognitive decline in adults whose selenium levels are adequate.

### Immune Function and Infectious Disease

Low selenium status has been associated with poor immune function [101].

**COVID-19:**

In Germany, a small study measured selenium in 166 serum samples from 33 people with COVID-19. Selenium was below the 2.5th percentile of a reference population in 43.4% of samples, and it was lower in people who died than in survivors; because the study was observational, causality remains unknown (Moghaddam, *Nutrients*, 2020) [70].

**HIV:**

People living with HIV often have low selenium concentrations, possibly due to malabsorption or inadequate intake [1][72]. In observational studies, lower selenium was linked to higher mortality in children with HIV and in children born to mothers with HIV [73][74]. Clinical trials have shown limited benefit:
- In a trial in Rwanda in 300 adults with HIV not yet on treatment, a daily 200 mcg selenium tablet for 24 months slowed CD4 cell decline by 43.8%, but had no effect on the composite outcome of treatment eligibility or starting treatment [75].
- In a trial in Iran in 146 adults on antiretroviral therapy, 6 months of daily selenium (200 mcg) did not significantly improve CD4 counts [76].
- Two Cochrane Reviews concluded that evidence was insufficient to show that selenium supplementation benefits people with HIV [1].
- In a trial in Nigeria in pregnant women with HIV, a daily tablet of 200 mcg selenium lowered the risk of preterm delivery (RR 0.32, 95% CI 0.11 to 0.96) [79].

**Synthesis:** Low selenium status is linked to poor immune function, but trials have not shown clear immune benefits from extra selenium in well-nourished people.

### Male Fertility

The selenoprotein phospholipid hydroperoxide glutathione peroxidase is a major structural component of mature sperm [80]. In men from infertile couples, both low and high semen selenium levels were associated with poorer reproductive outcomes [81].

**Evidence of benefit:**

In a 3-month trial in Scotland, 69 subfertile men with reduced sperm motility took selenium, selenium with vitamins A, C, and E, or placebo. Selenium raised plasma selenium; sperm motility rose significantly only when the two selenium groups were pooled, and 5 men (11%) in the selenium groups fathered a child, against none on placebo; the added vitamins had no effect [82]. Another 3-month trial in 70 men with idiopathic infertility reported better total and progressive sperm motility with 200 mcg a day of selenium, but it compared selenium with coenzyme Q10 and had no placebo group [83].

**Evidence of no benefit:**

In 42 healthy men, 300 mcg a day of selenium yeast for 48 weeks did not change sperm count or motility, and semen volume declined in both the selenium and placebo groups [84].

**Synthesis:** Selenium may improve sperm motility in subfertile men with low selenium, but a trial in healthy men found no benefit. More research is needed.

### Bone Health

In a European cohort of 1144 healthy postmenopausal women, higher selenium levels were associated with higher hip bone mineral density and lower bone turnover, independent of thyroid status (Hoeg, *J Clin Endocrinol Metab*, 2012) [85].

### Nutrient Interactions

**Selenium and iodine:** Selenium deficiency could exacerbate iodine deficiency, potentially increasing the risk of congenital hypothyroidism in infants [1].

**Selenium and vitamin E:** In a SELECT case-cohort analysis, vitamin E alone had no effect on prostate cancer risk in men with higher selenium status, but in men with lower selenium status it increased the risk of total (63%), low-grade (46%, not statistically significant), and high-grade (111%) prostate cancer [32].

### Tolerable Upper Intake Levels (ULs)

The UL for selenium is **400 mcg/day** for adults and adolescents 14+ (combined dietary and supplemental intake). ULs are lower for younger age groups: 45 mcg (0-6 months), 60 mcg (7-12 months), 90 mcg (1-3 years), 150 mcg (4-8 years), and 280 mcg (9-13 years) [1][91].

In 2023, the European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) set a lower UL of **255 mcg/day** for all adults, including pregnant and lactating women, based on systematic reviews linking excess selenium to alopecia. Children's ULs range from 70 to 230 mcg/day depending on age [1][92].

### Practical Dosing Considerations

**Most adults in the US and Canada do not need selenium supplements.** Selenium deficiency is very rare there, and intakes are well above the RDA [1]. Adding a selenium supplement on top of adequate dietary intake could push total intake toward the UL.

**Typical supplement doses:** Many multivitamin/mineral supplements contain 55 mcg of selenium, while selenium-only supplements typically contain 100 to 400 mcg [1].

**For Hashimoto's thyroiditis:** Trials used 80 to 200 mcg a day as selenomethionine or sodium selenite [1]. Selenium lowered thyroid antibodies, but clinical benefit is not documented [40][41].

**For HIV patients:** Trials used 200 mcg/day for 9 to 24 months, with limited evidence of clinical benefit [1].

**For male fertility (in deficient men):** Trials used 100 mcg a day as selenomethionine or 200 mcg a day as selenium yeast for 3 months [1].

### Important Safety Note Regarding Dosing

When considering selenium supplementation, it is critical to account for dietary intake, which already exceeds the RDA for most people in the United States [1]. In a Danish trial in a population of relatively low selenium status, 300 mcg a day of selenium for 5 years increased all-cause mortality over the whole follow-up. The trial was small and not designed to study deaths, but the researchers advised avoiding total selenium intakes over 300 mcg a day and high-dose supplements [93].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Selenosis (Chronic Toxicity)

Chronically high selenium intake leads to selenosis, with similar effects for organic and inorganic forms [1]. The main signs are:

- **Hair loss and nail brittleness or loss**: the most common signs, and the basis for the UL of 400 mcg/day [1][91]
- **Garlic odor on the breath** and a metallic taste in the mouth: early indicators of excess intake [1]
- **Skin rash, nausea, diarrhea, fatigue, irritability** [1]
- **Nervous system abnormalities** [1]

A 2024 letter described hair loss and nail dystrophy probably caused by selenium in a supplement (Civas, *J Cosmet Dermatol*, 2024) [95].

### Acute Toxicity

Acute selenium toxicity has resulted from misformulated products that contain very large amounts of selenium [1]. In 2008, a liquid dietary supplement that contained 200 times its labeled selenium concentration caused 201 cases in 10 states; common symptoms were diarrhea (78%), fatigue (75%), hair loss (72%), joint pain (70%), and nail discoloration or brittleness (61%), and fingernail discoloration and loss (52%), fatigue (35%), and hair loss (29%) lasted 90 days or longer [96]. Acute toxicity can cause severe gastrointestinal and neurological symptoms, acute respiratory distress syndrome, myocardial infarction, kidney failure, cardiac failure, and, in rare cases, death [1].

If someone may have taken a large overdose of selenium, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).

### Diabetes Risk

One of the most concerning findings from selenium research is the association with type 2 diabetes:

- In a secondary analysis of the NPC trial in 1202 people without diabetes, 200 mcg a day of selenium raised the incidence of type 2 diabetes over an average of 7.7 years (hazard ratio 1.55, 95% CI 1.03 to 2.33), and the risk was highest in people in the top third of baseline plasma selenium (hazard ratio 2.70, 95% CI 1.30 to 5.61) [99].
- In SELECT, the selenium group had a statistically nonsignificant increase in type 2 diabetes (relative risk 1.07, 99% CI 0.94 to 1.22) [30], and the 2018 Cochrane Review found a slightly increased risk with supplementation across trials [34].
- In 21,335 Italian adults followed for a median of 8.2 years, risk of hospitalization for type 2 diabetes was 64% greater in the top quarter of dietary selenium intake than in the other three [100].

Supplementing people who already have adequate intake might increase their risk of type 2 diabetes [101]. These findings support reserving selenium supplements for people who are deficient or at risk of deficiency.

### Prostate Cancer Risk in Men with High Selenium

In SELECT, selenium had no effect on prostate cancer in men with lower selenium status, but high-grade cancer rose by 91% in men whose status was higher [32]. Selenium supplementation in SELECT also increased alopecia (RR 1.28, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.62) and grade 1 to 2 dermatitis (RR 1.17, 95% CI 1.0 to 1.35) [50]. Men with non-metastatic prostate cancer who took 140 mcg a day or more had a 2.60-fold higher risk of prostate cancer death (Kenfield, *J Natl Cancer Inst*, 2015) [33]. The SELECT researchers advised men to avoid selenium supplements above recommended dietary intakes [32].

### All-Cause Mortality at High Doses

In the Danish PRECISE trial, 491 adults aged 60 to 74 years with relatively low selenium status took 100, 200, or 300 mcg a day of selenium yeast or placebo for 5 years. Over the whole follow-up, 300 mcg a day raised all-cause mortality (hazard ratio 1.59, 95% CI 1.02 to 2.46), while 100 and 200 mcg a day had no lasting effect [93]. At the end of the 5 years of treatment, the hazard ratio for 300 mcg a day was 1.62 (95% CI 0.66 to 3.96), which was not significant. The authors noted that the trial was not designed to study deaths and its sample size was limited [93].

### U-Shaped Risk Curve

The overall evidence points to a U-shaped relationship between selenium status and health. Extra selenium may help people whose status is low, but people whose status is already adequate or high might be harmed and should avoid selenium supplements [101].

### Maximum Safe Doses

For the general population, the US UL of 400 mcg/day and the more conservative EFSA UL of 255 mcg/day provide the safety boundaries. These ULs apply to combined dietary plus supplemental intake, and the US ULs do not apply to people taking selenium under a physician's care [1][91][92].

### Special Populations

**Brazil nut caution:** Brazil nuts contain 68 to 91 mcg of selenium per nut and could cause selenium toxicity if eaten regularly [1]. USDA lists an average of 544 mcg per ounce, but other analyses vary widely [1].

**Kidney dialysis patients:** Selenium concentrations are often lower in people on long-term hemodialysis, partly because the procedure removes some selenium from the blood [1][102]. However, status varies: in 198 people starting hemodialysis in Canada, low selenium was uncommon and many had concentrations above the 95th percentile of healthy people [103].

**Kidney or liver disease:** If you have severe kidney or liver disease, ask your doctor before taking selenium.

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Cisplatin

Cisplatin, an inorganic platinum chemotherapy agent used for ovarian, bladder, lung, and other cancers, can reduce selenium levels in hair and serum. Whether these reductions have a clinically significant impact is not known [1]. Some small studies suggest selenium supplementation may reduce cisplatin's toxicity, but a Cochrane Review concluded that the evidence that selenium eases chemotherapy side effects is insufficient [1][106].

### Other medicines

If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

### General Guidance

People who take medications regularly should discuss their selenium status with their healthcare provider [1].

### Practical Notes

- **Brazil nuts are uniquely rich** in selenium but highly variable. In a New Zealand trial, two Brazil nuts a day supplied an average of 53 mcg (possible range 20 to 84 mcg) and raised selenium status as well as 100 mcg of selenomethionine [111].
- **Animal sources are more consistent** than plant sources because animals maintain predictable tissue selenium concentrations through homeostatic mechanisms, and formulated livestock feeds contain consistent selenium levels [1].
- **Selenium in plant foods varies widely** by geographic location, because soil selenium, soil pH, and organic matter all affect it [1].
- **Drinking water** is not a nutritionally significant source of selenium in most regions [1].

### Regional Considerations

Selenium intakes and serum concentrations in the US and Canada vary somewhat by region due to differences in soil selenium content. Residents of the Midwestern and Western US have higher concentrations than those in the South and Northeast [1]. However, the extensive transport of food typically allows people in low-selenium areas to get enough [1].

### Groups at Higher Risk of Inadequacy

- **People in selenium-deficient regions** (parts of China and some European countries)
- **Vegetarians and vegans** (especially in low-selenium regions)
- **People undergoing kidney dialysis** (hemodialysis removes some selenium from blood)
- **People living with HIV** (possibly due to malabsorption or inadequate intake)
- **Smokers** (lower selenium status, possibly due to increased oxidative stress)
