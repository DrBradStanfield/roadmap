We hear "cancer breakthrough", but people keep dying.

Or the "breakthrough" only gives people a few extra months.

Why then am I excited about a cancer therapy that just completed its final Phase 3 study? And why do I think we are now on an exponential curve to curing cancer?

Here's why.

## The Cancer That Showed Us

In 2004, Sharon Belvin was 22 when she was told she had stage 4 melanoma. She went through chemotherapy, and her cancer didn't respond [1, 2].

At the time, that was the usual story. For melanoma that had spread around the body, half of people died within a year. And no drug had ever been shown, in a large randomized trial, to help people with it live longer [3].

So in late 2004, her doctor at Memorial Sloan Kettering in New York, Jedd Wolchok, offered her a trial. The drug was ipilimumab. It works by taking a brake off the immune system, so its T cells can attack the cancer.

She had four doses. A year later, Dr Wolchok told her, "Sharon, you no longer have cancer." [1]

But that's one person. So what did ipilimumab do in a big randomized trial?

In 2010, the results came out. Median survival went from 6.4 months to 10 [3].

Sounds like another "breakthrough" that only gives people a few months. But look closer. The median is just the halfway point. It doesn't tell you about the people at the far end.

When researchers pooled the long-term data on ipilimumab, about one in five people were alive at three years. And after that, the line on the graph went almost flat. Most of the people who made it to three years were still alive years later, and some were followed for ten [4].

So researchers kept going. They added a second drug that takes off a different brake, called PD-1. And in 2024, the final results of the big trial of that combination were published, this time in people getting their first treatment, after ten years of follow-up.

In that trial, ipilimumab on its own gave a median of about 20 months. The combination gave about six years. And 43 percent of people were still alive at ten years [5].

Now, look at the people who were still alive, with their cancer not growing, three years in. Ninety-six percent of them did not die of melanoma by the ten-year mark [5].

And Sharon? About twenty years later, she's still cancer-free, and she's had children [2].

Look at the trajectory that we are on. Of the progress that's already happened, let alone new therapies that have just started. Across the US, five-year survival for melanoma that has spread went from 16 percent in the mid-1990s to 35 percent. Because of new treatments, and combinations of them. Because of finding cancer's weak spots, developing therapies to directly target those weaknesses, and then layering those attacks. So what we have to do is keep finding those weaknesses [6].

Which brings us to lung cancer.

## KRAS: The Sequel to the Sequel

Some lung cancers, and more than nine in ten pancreatic cancers, are driven by faulty versions of a protein called KRAS. For about thirty years, nobody could make a drug that stuck to it.

What changed, in 2013, is that chemists found a hidden pocket in one faulty version, one that hadn't shown up in earlier pictures of the protein, and built a molecule to fit it [7].

So, back to KRAS. The first of these drugs latch on to the faulty version only, and leave the normal protein alone [7].

In lung cancer, the first drug for one faulty version, called G12C, was approved in 2021, and a second at the end of 2022. Then in October of this year, 2026, and this is the Phase 3 trial that I'm excited about, a newer one, divarasib, went head to head against them, in people whose cancer had come back after earlier treatment. So this isn't some obscure mouse experiment that will never work in humans, or a tiny human trial that will fail in the big leagues. This is the big-league trial.

Median survival was 17.7 months with divarasib, against 12 with the older drugs. And severe side effects were less common, 25 percent against 35. Remember, this strategy of layering on months of survival is how melanoma went from months to years [8].

For pancreatic cancer, a drug called daraxonrasib, which blocks several faulty versions of KRAS at once, was approved in August 2026. There's been a lot of breakthroughs this year. In people whose pancreatic cancer had spread and kept growing after their first treatment, median survival was 13.2 months with daraxonrasib, against 6.7 with chemotherapy [9].

So again, we've found another cancer weakness and exploited it. And we can keep combining these therapies. More on that at the end.

So that's KRAS. But what about a lung cancer that hadn't moved in thirty years?

## Thirty Years of the Same Chemotherapy

Small-cell lung cancer is a different kind of lung cancer. When it has spread, the standard treatment for decades was the same chemotherapy combination. A 2020 paper said that the life expectancy of someone diagnosed with it hadn't changed in thirty years [10].

Median survival was somewhere between 7 and 10 months. Then in 2018, adding immunotherapy to the chemotherapy took it from about 10 months to about 12 [11, 12].

Two more months. "A few extra months" again.

Then came a drug called tarlatamab, which targets a protein on the surface of most of these cancer cells. In a trial that reported in October, patients had their first chemotherapy and immunotherapy, and if the cancer hadn't grown, they either carried on with immunotherapy alone or added tarlatamab.

Median survival was 21.1 months with tarlatamab, against 12.7 without it. See how the survival months keep extending [13]?

Still, all of these are months. What happens when the weakness is the cancer's own mistakes?

## When the Weakness Is the Cancer's Own Mistakes

In 2018, Sascha Roth noticed some blood when she went to the toilet. She was a runner in her thirties, and otherwise she felt fine [14].

It was rectal cancer.

Her plan was the standard one. Chemotherapy, then radiation, then most likely surgery. Expecting the radiation, surgeons moved her ovaries up under her ribs, to try to protect her fertility.

Then she joined a small trial in New York. Six months of an immunotherapy drip, and nothing else. When it finished, her doctor told her, "We looked at your scans. There is absolutely no cancer." [14]

So why did it work? Her tumour had a broken proofreading system. Every time a cell copies its DNA it makes small mistakes, and a team of proteins called mismatch repair fixes them. In some tumours that's broken, and the mistakes pile up. In one study, these tumours carried well over a thousand mutations on average, against fewer than a hundred in others [15]. Each mutation can make a protein fragment your immune system has never seen. So these tumours stand out.

The drug she had, dostarlimab, takes off that PD-1 brake, so the immune system can see what's already there.

She was the first patient. In 2022, the team reported the first 12 people to finish. All 12 had a complete response. No tumour on any scan, camera test or biopsy, and none needed chemotherapy, radiation or surgery [16].

A colorectal cancer specialist who wasn't involved, Alan Venook, told the New York Times that was "unheard-of" [14].

By 2025, it was 49 people with rectal cancer, and all 49 had a complete response and skipped surgery. Two years on, almost all of them were still free of the cancer. The cancer did come back in a few people in the wider program, and so far none of them has needed the rectum removed [17].

Then, on October 19th, the FDA approved dostarlimab for people like Sascha, for rectal cancer that hasn't spread to other organs, stage 2 or 3.

It's what's called an accelerated approval. The FDA approved it on early results, and it still has to be shown to hold up. The label says the long-term risk of the cancer coming back is unknown [18]. And this only applies to a small slice, roughly 3 in 100 rectal cancers [19], up to about 1 in 10 in some estimates [20].

So every drug so far has needed someone to find one weakness, then build one drug for it. What if we built the treatment from the weaknesses in your own cancer?

## The Vaccine, Now With the Numbers

Remember, every tumour has its own mutations, and some of them make protein fragments that your healthy cells don't have. If your immune system could learn to spot those fragments, it could go after the cancer.

So doctors take a sample of the tumour and a sample of blood, and read the DNA in both. Then a computer system compares them, finds the mutations only the cancer has, and predicts which ones the immune system is most likely to recognize. Moderna, the company that makes it, has described this as AI. It picks up to 34 [21, 22].

Those 34 are written into a strand of mRNA, wrapped in tiny fat particles and injected. Your cells read the instructions, make the fragments, and your immune system learns what to hunt for. So every patient gets a different vaccine, made from their own cancer [21].

In August, all we had was the companies saying the big trial had worked. On November 11th, the full results were published.

The trial was in 1,137 people whose high-risk melanoma had been removed by surgery. Everyone got the immunotherapy drug pembrolizumab. Two thirds also got the vaccine. After two years, 82 percent of the vaccine group were free of recurrence, against 70 percent without it [23].

So that's four results in a few weeks, each built on a different weakness. So what's coming next?

## Layering the Attacks

Remember what happened in melanoma. One drug that took off one brake. Then a second drug on top of it. That's what researchers are now doing with several of the weaknesses in this article.

In small-cell lung cancer, a trial is now adding tarlatamab to the very first round of chemotherapy and immunotherapy. Another will test it earlier, when the cancer is still confined to the chest [24, 25].

In lung cancer with that G12C version, a trial aiming for 600 people is testing divarasib with immunotherapy as a first treatment, in place of chemotherapy [26].

In pancreatic cancer, daraxonrasib is now being tested as a first treatment, on its own and with chemotherapy. It's being tested after surgery and chemotherapy, to see whether it stops the cancer coming back. And it's being paired with a second KRAS drug, zoldonrasib, built for one specific faulty version [27, 28, 29].

And the personalized vaccine is being tested in lung, kidney and bladder cancer. In bladder cancer, one trial combines it with BCG, the tuberculosis vaccine I mentioned in [part one](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/we-are-witnessing-cancers-downfall), and another with a different bladder cancer drug [30, 31, 32, 33].

None of these has reported yet, and some will fail. One of the vaccine trials, in a different skin cancer, has already been stopped, for what the company called business reasons [34].

## Where This Could Go

So here's my take.

I don't think "a few extra months" is the right way to judge a new cancer drug. The question I'd ask is whether it's found a new weakness, and can we combine that new attack vector with existing therapies? In melanoma, that's where the people who keep going came from, though every new drug still has to show it can do the same.

And right now, we're finding cancer's weaknesses, exploiting them, and combining the attacks, in cancer after cancer.

And here's one of the key reasons I think this could speed up. Before you can build a drug, you need the shape of the protein you're aiming at, and for most proteins, getting that took years of lab work. Now AI tools like AlphaFold, which won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry, can predict the shape of almost any protein. Drugs designed with the help of AI are already in human trials. None has been approved yet. But I think building a drug to fit the next weakness should get faster [35, 36].

That's why I think we could be at the start of an exponential curve, where each weakness we find gives the next treatment something to build on, until most cancers are something we can cure, or at least control for decades.

### Other studies

A few more findings from the research behind this article, which didn't make it into the video:

- In the dostarlimab rectal cancer trial behind the approval (AZUR-1), independent reviewers judged 47% of 116 evaluable patients to be in a sustained complete response at 12 months [18], against 68% of 144 by the trial's own doctors [37]; the data are still maturing. About one in four patients (39 of 154) went on to surgery or another treatment [37]. Six patients died, four of them with cancer that grew without first responding [18].

- In a planned analysis split at six months, the personalized vaccine's benefit appeared only after six months [23]. The vaccine isn't approved.

- The tarlatamab survival figures come from an interim analysis, measured from the start of maintenance in people whose cancer hadn't grown during first treatment. Cytokine release syndrome, mainly mild (grade 1 or 2), was seen in nearly half the patients on tarlatamab [13]. In an earlier trial against chemotherapy after first treatment, 30.3% were alive at two years with tarlatamab, against 14.9% with chemotherapy [38].

- Across all cancers in the US, the death rate has fallen 34% since 1991, averting about 4.8 million deaths, and the American Cancer Society says the contribution of treatment advances has accelerated over the past decade. Five-year survival for rectal cancer that has spread rose from 8% to 18% since the mid-1990s. But the fall in melanoma deaths slowed, from nearly 6% a year in 2013 to 2017 to 1.2% a year in 2017 to 2023 [6].

- Accelerated approvals don't always hold up. Of 46 cancer drug approvals granted this way from 2013 to 2017, fewer than half (43%) went on to show a clinical benefit in their confirmatory trials [39].
