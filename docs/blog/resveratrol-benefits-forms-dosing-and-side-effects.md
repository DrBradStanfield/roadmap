Brad says taking resveratrol supplements isn't a good strategy: there is no good evidence that it helps people live longer, but there is evidence of harm ([Brad, 15 May 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-incredibly-popular-supplement-damages-your-blood-vessels)). He adds that there is some clinical evidence that resveratrol can reduce testosterone levels, and that lower testosterone is linked to a higher risk of death, especially in older men ([Brad, 15 May 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-incredibly-popular-supplement-damages-your-blood-vessels)). He leaves resveratrol and pterostilbene out of his own plan until there is more compelling research (Brad's current guidance, 31 Jul 2026).

## Key safety points

- Resveratrol slows platelet clumping in lab and animal studies and blocks CYP2C9, the enzyme that clears warfarin, so it may add to the effect of warfarin, clopidogrel and other blood thinners. If you take one, check with your doctor first [71][82][83].
- At 1 g a day it inhibited the drug-clearing enzymes CYP3A4, CYP2D6 and CYP2C9, which could change how other medicines work. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting [69].
- Do not take it in pregnancy: in pregnant monkeys it caused unexplained changes in the developing fetal pancreas [68].
- It can act as a phytoestrogen, so if you have an estrogen-sensitive condition, including some cancers, ask your doctor first [47].
- High doses may raise LDL cholesterol. 1,000 mg a day raised total and LDL cholesterol, and a trial group on 350 mg twice daily was stopped when LDL rose [30][31].
- A trial of SRT501, a high-absorption form, was halted after 5 of 24 subjects developed kidney damage [6]. It enrolled people with multiple myeloma, so take particular care if you have multiple myeloma or kidney disease [86][87].
- Doses of 2.5 to 5 g a day caused mild to moderate gut symptoms, and nausea and diarrhea were the most common side effects in a year-long trial [41][57].

## 1. Overview

Resveratrol is a plant compound found in grapes, red wine, peanuts and peanut products [14]. It also occurs in the root of Japanese knotweed (Polygonum cuspidatum) [2].

An early study claimed that resveratrol extended the lifespan of yeast by 70% [3][4]. In 2006, David Sinclair published a study in Nature showing resveratrol extended lifespan in mice on a high-fat diet [5][6]. These findings triggered intense scientific interest and a supplement boom.

However, the initial excitement has been tempered by subsequent research. Clinical trials in humans have produced mixed and often disappointing results for many of the benefits suggested by animal and laboratory studies [19][59]. Resveratrol's oral bioavailability is extremely low — less than 1% to approximately 5% of the parent compound reaches systemic circulation due to extensive first-pass metabolism in the gut and liver [7][8]. Although at least 70% of a dose is absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, it is rapidly converted to glucuronide and sulfate conjugates, with the parent compound having a plasma half-life of only 9-14 minutes in humans [7][9][10].

The compound remains widely researched, with many clinical trials underway, but definitive proof of major clinical benefit in humans remains elusive for most claimed applications.

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Isomeric Forms

Resveratrol occurs as two isomers, trans-resveratrol and cis-resveratrol, which have the same atoms in a different arrangement [23].

- **Trans-resveratrol** is the form used in many of the human trials described below [38][39][49].
- **Cis-resveratrol** scavenged free radicals as well as the trans form in a lab study but bound copper less strongly [64].

Both isomers also occur bound to glucose, as trans-piceid and cis-piceid (resveratrol glucosides) [23]. In grapes and grape juice, piceid is the predominant form, often present at concentrations 4- to 10-fold higher than free resveratrol [12]. Upon ingestion, piceid undergoes hydrolysis in the gastrointestinal tract, primarily by gut microbiota such as Bifidobacterium infantis, converting it to free resveratrol for absorption [12].

### Sources of Supplemental Resveratrol

Supplement makers use several plant sources:

- **Japanese knotweed (Polygonum cuspidatum) root extract:** Knotweed root contains resveratrol [2].
- **Red grape skin extract:** Contains resveratrol along with other grape polyphenols.
- **Grape seed extract (GSE):** Contains resveratrol plus proanthocyanidins.
- **Grape pomace extract (GPE):** Made from pressed grape skins, seeds, and stems.
- **Red wine extract:** Red wine contains resveratrol [13].

### Bioavailability Challenges

Resveratrol faces severe bioavailability limitations that are central to understanding its clinical profile [7][8]:

**Absorption:** Resveratrol is rapidly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract, with studies indicating at least 70% of the dose is absorbed via passive diffusion in the intestine [7].

**First-pass metabolism:** Despite high absorption, the oral bioavailability of the parent compound is less than 1% to approximately 5%, due to extensive presystemic metabolism. Resveratrol undergoes rapid phase II conjugation (glucuronidation and sulfation) in the gut wall and liver. Over 90% of absorbed resveratrol is converted to metabolites within hours of oral administration [7][8][14].

**Key metabolites:** The primary metabolites include resveratrol-3-O-glucuronide (formed by UGT1A1 and UGT1A3), resveratrol-4'-O-glucuronide (formed by UGT1A9), and resveratrol-3-O-sulfate (formed by SULT1A1). Some of these metabolites retain partial bioactivity comparable to the parent compound in antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and estrogenic assays [14]. In addition, gut microbiota reduce resveratrol to dihydroresveratrol, and cytochrome P450 enzyme CYP1B1 converts it to piceatannol [14].

**Peak plasma concentration:** Reached within 30-60 minutes after ingestion [7].

**Half-life:** The parent resveratrol has a very short plasma half-life of approximately 9-14 minutes. However, its major metabolites persist longer, with half-lives extending to several hours [9][10].

**Protein binding:** Approximately 98% of circulating resveratrol is bound to plasma proteins such as albumin [15].

**Tissue distribution:** Higher concentrations accumulate in the liver, kidney, and heart compared to plasma levels. Resveratrol is lipophilic and theoretically capable of crossing the blood-brain barrier, but penetration into the central nervous system is limited by low systemic exposure and rapid biotransformation. Metabolites (resveratrol-3-glucuronide and resveratrol-3-sulfate) have been detected in cerebrospinal fluid following oral administration [15][16].

**Enterohepatic recirculation:** Conjugated metabolites undergo biliary excretion followed by reabsorption in the intestines, which extends overall systemic exposure. Elimination occurs predominantly through fecal excretion (up to 98% in some animal models), with urinary excretion accounting for a smaller portion as metabolites [9][10].

**Dose-dependent kinetics:** At higher doses, metabolic pathways become saturated, resulting in nonlinear increases in exposure [8][17].

**Interspecies differences:** Phase II conjugation occurs more slowly in rodents compared to humans, leading to higher plasma levels and prolonged half-lives of resveratrol in mice and rats. This difference can affect the translation of preclinical findings to human applications [14].

### Strategies to Improve Bioavailability

Several approaches have been explored to address resveratrol's poor bioavailability:

- **Micronized formulations (e.g., SRT501):** Micronizing allows more of the dose to be absorbed. Single doses of SRT501 gave plasma resveratrol levels 3.6-fold higher than those published for equal doses of standard resveratrol [18]. A trial of SRT501 was halted after 5 of 24 subjects developed kidney damage (see Safety section) [6]. It enrolled people with multiple myeloma [87].
- **Lipid-based carriers and nanoformulations:** Can significantly improve oral bioavailability by mitigating first-pass effects [8][17].
- **Co-ingestion with fats:** The food matrix affects absorption, with co-ingestion of fats enhancing bioavailability by improving solubility [8][17].

### Labeling Caution

Check the units on the label: 1 milligram (mg) is 1,000 micrograms (mcg), so 100 mcg is a thousandth of 100 mg.

### Related Compounds

**Pterostilbene:** A close chemical relative of resveratrol (its dimethylether analog). In rats, oral pterostilbene reached far higher blood levels than the same molar dose of resveratrol [8]. In 80 adults with high cholesterol, pterostilbene alone (125 mg twice daily, or 50 mg twice daily) for 6-8 weeks raised LDL cholesterol by 17.1 mg/dL, while the higher dose lowered systolic blood pressure by 7.8 mmHg and diastolic by 7.3 mmHg [21]. A safety analysis of the same trial found no harm to liver, kidney or glucose markers and judged pterostilbene generally safe up to 250 mg/day [22].

**Piceid:** The predominant glycosylated form of resveratrol in grape skins and wine. In red wines, total stilbene levels average around 4-5 mg/L, where piceid accounts for the majority, often exceeding free resveratrol by 5- to 20-fold. Upon ingestion, piceid is hydrolyzed to resveratrol by gut microbiota [12][23].

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Cardiovascular and Blood Pressure Effects

Resveratrol has been investigated for cardiovascular protection through its effects on endothelial function, blood pressure, and lipid profiles. However, results in humans have been largely disappointing despite promising animal data.

**Endothelial function — modest improvement:** Resveratrol enhances endothelial nitric oxide (NO) production by upregulating endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) and reducing oxidative stress, improving vasodilation [24]. In 34 people treated for metabolic syndrome, three months of a modified resveratrol product (Longevinex) improved flow-mediated dilation, a measure of artery function, and the gain faded three months after stopping. Blood pressure, insulin resistance, blood fats and inflammatory markers did not change [25].

**Blood pressure — inconsistent evidence:** A 2015 meta-analysis of 6 randomized trials (247 people) found no significant overall effect on systolic or diastolic blood pressure. In a subgroup taking 150 mg a day or more, systolic pressure fell (-11.90 mmHg, 95% CI -20.99 to -2.81 mmHg), but the analysis found no dose-response and no effect on diastolic pressure. The authors called for more high-quality trials [26]. Several individual trials found no blood pressure benefit:

- In 41 overweight adults aged 40-70, 150 mg/day of resveratrol for 6 months did not change blood pressure or body composition compared with placebo [27].
- In 192 people with type 2 diabetes, neither 40 mg nor 500 mg a day for 6 months changed blood pressure compared with placebo [28].
- In 24 obese men, 4 weeks of high-dose resveratrol did not change blood pressure, insulin sensitivity or the rate at which they burned fat [29].
- In 74 middle-aged men with metabolic syndrome, neither 150 mg nor 1,000 mg a day for 16 weeks had any effect on blood pressure, body composition or inflammation [30].

**Lipid effects — potentially harmful at higher doses:** Several studies have found that higher doses of resveratrol may worsen cholesterol levels:

- In the same trial in men with metabolic syndrome, 1,000 mg a day raised total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol and fructosamine (a blood sugar marker) compared with placebo; the 150 mg dose did not, and neither dose brought any benefit [30].
- In a 2-year trial in 120 healthy adults with an average age of 71.7, the group taking 350 mg of resveratrol twice daily with 100 U of TA-65 was stopped at month 5 because LDL cholesterol rose, probably because of the high-dose resveratrol [31].
- In rabbits fed a high-cholesterol diet, those given resveratrol developed more atherosclerosis in the aorta than those not given it [32].

### Metabolic Health: Blood Sugar, Insulin Resistance, and Obesity

**Calorie restriction mimicry:** In a 30-day crossover trial in 11 healthy obese men, 150 mg/day of resveratrol (resVida) lowered resting metabolic rate, liver fat, blood glucose, triglycerides, the liver enzyme ALT and markers of inflammation, and systolic blood pressure fell. The authors concluded that it mimicked the effects of calorie restriction [34].

**Blood sugar and insulin — mostly negative results in humans:**

- In the 6-month trial of 150 mg/day in 41 overweight adults, insulin sensitivity did not change, but HbA1c after treatment was lower with resveratrol than with placebo (35.8 vs 37.6 mmol/mol) [27].
- In 192 people with type 2 diabetes, neither 40 mg nor 500 mg a day for 6 months lowered CRP or improved fasting glucose, HbA1c or insulin, and total cholesterol and triglycerides rose slightly at 500 mg. People who had had diabetes for a shorter time saw a larger fall in CRP [28].
- The Danish metabolic syndrome study found no improvement in glucose measures at either 150 mg or 1,000 mg/day; the higher dose actually worsened fructosamine [30].

**Systematic review:** Some have suggested resveratrol might help with type 2 diabetes, but a Cochrane analysis found the current research does not support any such claims [35]. Other meta-analyses have reported small effects. One, which pooled 15 trials, found HbA1c 0.60% lower at 250 to 500 mg/day in people aged 45 to 59, but no HbA1c change in people over 60; its authors concluded there is not enough evidence to propose a therapeutic dose [72]. Another, of 17 trials in 871 people with type 2 diabetes, found HbA1c lower at three months (mean difference -0.41) [73].

**Obesity and body weight:** Resveratrol did not change weight or body composition in trials in people with type 2 diabetes [28] or overweight adults [27]. One memory trial, which gave resveratrol with quercetin, found less body fat than with placebo [37], but in its replication body fat rose in both groups [36].

### Cognitive Function and Brain Health

**Memory improvement in overweight older adults:** In 46 healthy overweight adults aged 50-75, a daily 200 mg of resveratrol plus 320 mg of quercetin for 26 weeks improved recall of words after 30 minutes compared with placebo. The combination also strengthened connections of the hippocampus, a memory region of the brain, and lowered HbA1c and body fat [37].

**Failed replication:** A later trial gave 60 adults aged 60-79 years the same 200 mg/day for 26 weeks, and verbal memory did not improve. Body fat, cholesterol and fasting glucose rose over time in both groups, partly more after resveratrol, while physical activity fell; the authors suggested these lifestyle changes may have confounded the results [36].

**Cognitive benefits in postmenopausal women:** In 129 postmenopausal women aged 45-85 years, a year of trans-resveratrol at 75 mg twice daily led to better overall cognition than placebo and slowed the decline in blood-flow responses in the brain during mental tasks [38].

**Cerebrovascular effects:** In a double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study, single oral doses of 250 mg and 500 mg trans-resveratrol increased cerebral blood flow during cognitive tasks in healthy adults, with dose-dependent enhancements in total hemoglobin concentrations and deoxyhemoglobin changes indicative of improved oxygen extraction [39]. Chronic low-dose supplementation (150 mg/day) has also demonstrated improvements in resting cerebral blood flow and cerebrovascular responsiveness in postmenopausal women [40]. In 16 young adults after one night of restricted sleep (4 hours in bed), a single 250 mg dose restored the blood flow response to a visual task in one brain artery (the posterior cerebral artery) but not in another (the middle cerebral artery) [74].

**Alzheimer's disease:** In a 52-week trial in 119 people with mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease, resveratrol started at 500 mg once daily and rose to 1,000 mg twice daily. Levels of the biomarker Abeta40 in blood and spinal fluid fell less than with placebo, but brain volume fell more with resveratrol. The most common side effects were nausea, diarrhea and weight loss. The authors said more studies are needed to interpret the biomarker changes [41].

### SIRT1 Activation and Longevity

Early lab work found that resveratrol activates SIRT1, the human member of the sirtuin enzyme family, which in yeast drives the longer lifespan seen with calorie restriction [3]. SIRT1 is an NAD+-dependent deacetylase that deacetylates peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1alpha), stimulating mitochondrial biogenesis. Resveratrol activates SIRT1 by lowering its Michaelis constant for both acetylated substrates and NAD+, enhancing deacetylation activity at low micromolar concentrations [42].

**Animal lifespan data:** Resveratrol improved health and survival in middle-aged mice on a high-calorie diet, with better insulin sensitivity and motor function [5]. An earlier study claimed a 70% lifespan extension in yeast [3][4]. In a short-lived fish, resveratrol added to food from early adulthood caused a dose-dependent increase in median and maximum lifespan; the authors noted earlier reports of longer life in worms and fruit flies [75]. However, when the National Institute on Aging's Interventions Testing Program, which runs each test at three sites, studied resveratrol in mice, there was no effect on lifespan; the program confirmed that the resveratrol was absorbed into the bloodstream [6][35].

**Key controversies:** The claim that resveratrol directly activates SIRT1 has been challenged. Other labs found that the mechanism proposed for the yeast result was likely caused by the fluorescent dye used in the experiment [6]. A subsequent 2013 study provided additional evidence supporting SIRT1 activation [42], but the field remains contentious. A paper published in 2020 used CRISPR technology and found that resveratrol's ability to inhibit human cell proliferation was independent of SIRT1; its primary effect was low-level replicative stress [44].

**Human aging data:** In the trial in older adults described above, the resveratrol and TA-65 group stopped at month 5, and none of the treatments significantly changed proBNPage, an estimate of biological age based on the heart-strain marker NT-proBNP [31]. There is no good evidence that resveratrol helps people live longer [35].

**Hormetic dose-response:** Resveratrol's pharmacodynamic effects often follow a hormetic dose-response curve, where low doses (typically 1-10 micromolar in vitro) elicit beneficial stimulatory responses through adaptive stress pathways, while high doses (>50 micromolar) may inhibit these effects or induce toxicity by overwhelming cellular defenses. This biphasic pattern is observed across models affecting cell survival and antioxidant enzyme induction [45].

### Bone Health

One year-long trial in postmenopausal women (75 mg twice daily) reported small gains in bone density at the lumbar spine (+0.016 ± 0.003 g/cm2) and femoral neck (+0.005 ± 0.002 g/cm2) and a lower estimated fracture probability. Its authors said resveratrol "has the potential to slow bone loss" [47].

A 2021 meta-analysis of 10 randomized trials with 698 participants found that resveratrol did not change bone mineral density at the spine, hip or whole body, or blood markers of bone turnover. Results were similar across doses, trial lengths and health conditions [46].

### Menopause Symptoms

In 125 healthy postmenopausal women in the same research program, 75 mg of resveratrol twice daily reduced overall pain scores and improved somatic menopausal symptoms and general well-being compared with placebo [48].

### Migraine

Resveratrol is a vasoactive phytoestrogen, and women with hormonal migraine have poorer blood vessel function in the brain than women without it. In 62 women with hormonal migraine (mean age 37.5), three months of 75 mg of resveratrol or placebo twice daily did not change the number of menstrual migraine days, migraine-related disability or quality of life compared with placebo [49].

### Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD)

Evidence for AMD is very preliminary. In retinal cells from aged donor eyes, resveratrol suppressed the release of VEGF, a growth factor whose overproduction worsens AMD, and the authors suggested it may be useful as a nutritional aid [50]. Anti-VEGF eye injections such as Lucentis, Avastin and Eylea work by blocking it [52].

A few case reports describe people with AMD whose retinal structure and vision improved while taking a resveratrol-based supplement (Longevinex combines a low dose of resveratrol with red wine solids, vitamin D3 and inositol hexaphosphate), with effects lasting up to two to three years [51][52][53]. In 34 young healthy adults, one dose of the same product thickened the choroid (the blood vessel layer behind the retina) within 1 hour compared with placebo, which the authors put down to widened blood vessels [54].

These were case reports without a placebo group [51][52][53].

### Anti-Viral Effects and COVID-19

In laboratory studies, resveratrol blocks viral replication, protein synthesis and gene expression [55]. In a trial in 100 outpatients with mild COVID-19, hospitalization (2% vs 6%), COVID-related ER visits (8% vs 14%) and pneumonia (8% vs 16%) were less common with resveratrol than with placebo, but the trial was too small and the results were statistically similar. Both groups also received a single dose of vitamin D3 [56].

### Cancer

Most cancer research on resveratrol has been in cells and animals, and confirmation in humans is very limited [59]. In an early study, resveratrol showed cancer-preventive activity in lab tests of three major stages of cancer development and slowed tumor growth in a mouse skin cancer model [76].

In healthy volunteers, daily doses up to 5 g for 29 days were judged safe, although 2.5 g and 5 g caused mild to moderate gastrointestinal symptoms [57]. Resveratrol has shown no standalone efficacy and has no regulatory approval for cancer treatment [58]. Because resveratrol can act as a phytoestrogen [47], women with an estrogen-sensitive cancer should ask their doctor before taking it.

### Exercise Performance

Two studies suggest resveratrol may blunt some positive effects of exercise training:

**In older inactive men:** 27 healthy, physically inactive men with an average age of 65 took 250 mg/day of trans-resveratrol or placebo during 8 weeks of high-intensity exercise training. Maximal oxygen uptake rose 45% more in the placebo group, and only the placebo group saw mean arterial pressure fall (by 4.8 mmHg). Resveratrol also cancelled the training gains in LDL cholesterol, the total-to-HDL cholesterol ratio and triglycerides [60].

**In young men doing HIIT:** 16 recreationally active men aged about 22 did 4 weeks of high-intensity interval training, 3 days a week, with 150 mg/day of resveratrol or placebo. Peak aerobic power rose only in the placebo group, and the training-induced rise in PGC-1alpha, SIRT1 and SOD2 gene expression was smaller with resveratrol. The authors concluded that resveratrol may alter the normal response to this training [61].

### Liver Health (NAFLD)

In a 30-day study in obese men, 150 mg/day of trans-resveratrol lowered liver fat and the liver enzyme ALT [34]. In a placebo-controlled trial in overweight or obese men with fatty liver disease, 10 took 3,000 mg of resveratrol a day for 8 weeks and 10 took a placebo; resveratrol did not reduce insulin resistance, liver fat or abdominal fat, and liver enzymes (ALT and AST) rose significantly until week 6 compared with placebo [62].

### Skin Aging

The sources checked for this review hold no trials of resveratrol for skin aging.

### Erectile Dysfunction

One small pilot crossover trial in 20 men with sexual dysfunction, who kept using PDE5 inhibitor drugs, tested a drink combining resveratrol (300 mg/day) with testofen (600 mg/day), L-citrulline (800 mg/day) and caffeine (40 mg/day). After 14 days, erection, desire, orgasm and overall satisfaction scores improved compared with placebo [77]. Because the drink had four ingredients, the trial cannot show what resveratrol did on its own.

### Antimicrobial Effects

The sources checked for this review hold no human trials of resveratrol against bacterial infections. Its anti-viral effects in laboratory studies are covered above.

## 4. Recommended Dosing

Confirmation of benefit in human trials is still very limited, and the safety of long-term high doses is unresolved, especially in people taking medicines [59]. There is no official RDA, adequate intake, or tolerable upper intake level for resveratrol. Brad does not recommend taking resveratrol (see Current view); the doses below describe the research, not a recommendation.

### Doses Used in Research

| Clinical Context | Dose | Duration | Outcome |

| Glucose metabolism + memory in overweight elderly | 200 mg/day (with 320 mg quercetin) | 26 weeks | Improved memory and glucose metabolism (not replicated) [37][36] |
| Cognitive function in postmenopausal women | 150 mg/day (75 mg twice daily) | 12 months | Modest cognitive improvement [38] |

### Practical Considerations

- **Evidence suggests lower doses (150-200 mg/day) may have metabolic activity** [34], while avoiding the cholesterol-raising effects seen at higher doses (more than 500 mg/day) [30][31].
- **Higher doses (more than 500 mg/day) have been associated with increased total and LDL cholesterol** in multiple studies [30][31] and should be used with caution.
- **Very high doses (2,500-5,000 mg/day) are associated with gastrointestinal side effects** including nausea, loose stools, and diarrhea [57][62].
- **Resveratrol may interfere with exercise adaptations,** so individuals using resveratrol during intensive training should be aware of this potential effect [60][61].
- **Most human trials described here used trans-resveratrol** [38][39][49].
- **Co-administration with food containing fat** may improve absorption [8][17].
- **Splitting doses** across the day (e.g., twice daily with meals) may help maintain more consistent plasma levels given the very short half-life of the parent compound.

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Common Side Effects

At 2.5 or 5 g a day, resveratrol caused mild to moderate gastrointestinal symptoms in healthy volunteers [57]. Nausea and diarrhea were the most common side effects in a year-long Alzheimer's trial in which the dose rose to 1,000 mg twice daily [41].

### Cholesterol Elevation

High doses of resveratrol may raise total and LDL cholesterol [30][31]. Several trials found this:

- 1,000 mg/day increased total and LDL cholesterol and worsened fructosamine in men with metabolic syndrome [30].
- 350 mg twice daily (with TA-65) raised LDL cholesterol in healthy older adults, and that trial group was stopped at month 5 [31].
- Pterostilbene, a close relative, raised LDL cholesterol (100-250 mg/day) in adults with high cholesterol [21].

### Estrogenic Activity

Resveratrol can act as a phytoestrogen, a plant compound with estrogen-like effects [14][47]. If you have an estrogen-sensitive condition, including some cancers, ask your doctor before taking resveratrol.

### Exercise Blunting

Resveratrol may blunt some positive effects of exercise training by scavenging reactive oxygen species that serve as signaling molecules for exercise adaptations. Two studies in men found reduced improvements in blood pressure, cholesterol, maximal oxygen uptake, and skeletal muscle gene expression when resveratrol was taken during exercise training [60][61].

There is also some clinical evidence that resveratrol can reduce testosterone levels, from a trial in women with polycystic ovary syndrome [35][63]. In middle-aged men with metabolic syndrome, 1,000 mg/day for 4 months lowered the androgen precursors DHEA (by 41%) and DHEAS (by 50%), but testosterone, free testosterone, DHT, PSA and prostate size did not change [78].

### Kidney Safety Concern (SRT501)

SRT501 is a micronized form of resveratrol that reaches higher blood levels than standard resveratrol [18]. A trial of SRT501 was halted after 5 of 24 subjects developed kidney damage [6]. It tested SRT501, alone or with the cancer drug bortezomib, in people with multiple myeloma [86][87]. Across other trials of resveratrol, a 2025 meta-analysis of 31 studies with 2,299 participants found blood urea nitrogen 1.20 mg/dL lower than with placebo; creatinine and GFR improved only in people without diabetes. The authors concluded that resveratrol might improve these kidney markers [79].

### Iron and Copper Effects

Animal studies give mixed signals on iron. In iron-overloaded mice, resveratrol reduced iron in the liver and lowered liver ferritin [66], while very high doses caused mild anemia in male mice [65]. In a lab study, resveratrol bound copper strongly but did not bind iron [64]; whether this affects copper levels in people is not known.

### Thyroid Effects

Resveratrol may affect the thyroid. In rats given resveratrol by injection at 25 mg/kg for 60 days, TSH (thyroid-stimulating hormone) rose and the thyroid grew larger, although the rats showed no signs of an underactive thyroid. The authors concluded that resveratrol may act as a thyroid disruptor and urged caution in its use [67]. If you have thyroid disease or take thyroid medicine, ask your doctor before using resveratrol.

### Contraindications

- **Pregnancy:** Do not use (animal data). In pregnant monkeys, resveratrol caused an unexplained change in the developing fetal pancreas, and the authors strongly caution against its use in pregnancy [68]. If you are breastfeeding or trying to conceive, ask your doctor first.
- **Children:** Avoid: not studied in children; high doses lowered the growth factor IGF-I in adults [57].
- **Blood-thinning medications:** Consult a physician before use (see Drug Interactions).
- **Estrogen-sensitive cancers or conditions:** Consult a physician before use.
- **Multiple myeloma or kidney disease:** Take particular care, given the SRT501 trial in multiple myeloma halted for kidney damage [6][87].

### Long-Term Safety

Short trials found resveratrol generally well tolerated [19], but its long-term safety, especially at high doses and in people taking medicines, has not been settled [33][59]. In healthy volunteers, single doses up to 5 g caused no serious adverse events [9], and daily doses up to 5 g for 29 days were judged safe, although 2.5 g and 5 g caused mild to moderate gastrointestinal symptoms [57]. A 2024 systematic review of 10 trials in older adults found that no study reported significant adverse events, but high doses raised some markers of heart disease risk, and the best dose, long-term effects and interactions with medicines still need study [80]. LiverTox, the NIDDK database on drug-induced liver injury, says resveratrol has been linked to rare rises in liver enzymes during use but has not been convincingly linked to clinically apparent liver injury [81].

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Cytochrome P450 Inhibition

In 42 healthy volunteers, 1 g of resveratrol daily for 4 weeks inhibited the drug-metabolizing enzymes CYP3A4, CYP2D6 and CYP2C9 and induced CYP1A2. The authors warned that such doses could lead to more adverse drug reactions or change how well drugs work [69]:

| Drug/Class | Enzyme Affected | Clinical Concern |
|---|---|---|
| Statins and other heart medicines | CYP3A4 and other drug-metabolizing enzymes | Theoretical: 1 g a day inhibited enzymes that clear many medicines, but the study did not test statins [69]. Check with your doctor or pharmacist before combining |
| Warfarin (S-warfarin) | CYP2C9 | Resveratrol may strengthen the effect of warfarin; if you take warfarin, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting. In lab tests, resveratrol inhibited CYP2C9's breakdown of warfarin [82] |
| Tacrolimus | CYP3A4 | Theoretical, inferred from resveratrol's CYP3A4 inhibition; the cited study did not test tacrolimus [69]. Tacrolimus levels in transplant patients could rise, so drug-level monitoring may be needed |

Reviews note that the safety of long-term high doses is unresolved, especially for people taking medicines [33][59]. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

### Antiplatelet and Anticoagulant Effects

In lab studies, resveratrol inactivates cyclooxygenase-1 (COX-1), the enzyme aspirin blocks to reduce platelet clumping [71]. Resveratrol inhibited platelet aggregation in human platelets in the lab and in rabbits [83]. In a trial of 40 people after a heart attack, platelet aggregation rose over 3 months with placebo but not with 10 mg/day of resveratrol [84]. This creates additive bleeding risk when combined with:

- **Warfarin:** May enhance anticoagulant effects through both pharmacokinetic interaction (CYP2C9 inhibition, which could raise warfarin levels) and pharmacodynamic interaction (antiplatelet activity) [71][82]
- **Clopidogrel and other antiplatelet agents:** Potential synergistic inhibition of platelet aggregation, increasing bruising and hemorrhage risk [71]

If you take a blood thinner, check with your doctor before starting resveratrol; they may want to check your INR or watch for bleeding.

### Noted Combinations

Several trials tested resveratrol in combinations: with TA-65 in older adults [31], and in Longevinex, which adds red wine solids, vitamin D3 and inositol hexaphosphate [53].

## 7. Dietary Sources

Resveratrol occurs naturally in several foods and beverages, though concentrations are low relative to supplement doses.

### Content in Foods and Beverages

| Food/Beverage | Resveratrol Content | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Red grape juice | Average 0.50 mg/L trans-resveratrol and 3.38 mg/L trans-piceid | Piceid, the glucose-bound form, was the main component [23] |
| White grape juice | Average 0.05 mg/L trans-resveratrol | Far less than red grape juice [23] |
| Red wine | Total resveratrol similar to grape juice in Spanish wines [23] | Reviewed in [13] |
| Peanuts and peanut products | Not stated | A major dietary source [14] |

### Dietary Intake

Food sources supply far less resveratrol than the supplement doses used in trials. Red grape juice averaged 0.50 mg/L of trans-resveratrol [23].

### Red Wine Context

The cardiovascular benefits of moderate alcohol consumption are likely attributable to the combined effects of ethanol and hundreds of polyphenolic compounds in wine rather than resveratrol alone. The "French paradox" is the observation that France had low death rates from coronary heart disease despite a high intake of saturated fat. A 1992 paper suggested this may be due in part to high wine consumption, and pointed to alcohol's effect on platelets as one explanation [85].

### Processing Effects on Food Content

Grape juice carries most of its resveratrol as piceid rather than free resveratrol [23].

### Japanese Knotweed

Japanese knotweed (Polygonum cuspidatum / Fallopia japonica) root, known as Ko-jo-kon, contains resveratrol [2].
