## Current evidence position

Brad has not made a video on this topic; this is a summary of the evidence, not his view. Most ginseng trials are small and short: Asian ginseng alone may slightly ease general fatigue, and a 2022 review of 20 trials found it improved fasting blood glucose in prediabetes and diabetes, though research on blood sugar control is overall inconclusive and conflicting [2][9][11]. In the largest trial, 2,000 mg a day of Wisconsin American ginseng for 8 weeks improved cancer-related fatigue more than placebo [5], and a Cochrane review found ginseng appears to have a trivial effect on erectile function scores [36]. Short-term use of Asian ginseng by mouth, up to 6 months, appears to be safe for most people, and insomnia is its most common side effect [2]. If you take warfarin or another blood thinner, have diabetes, or are pregnant or breastfeeding, talk to your doctor first: American ginseng weakened warfarin's effect in one small trial, and ginseng types can lower or raise blood sugar [2][34][37].

## Key safety points

- American ginseng reduced warfarin's effect in a small trial, another trial found no effect, and Asian ginseng may interfere with blood clotting. If you take warfarin or another blood thinner, check with your doctor first [2][37][38].
- Ginseng may lower or raise blood sugar, depending on the type. If you have diabetes or take glucose-lowering medicines, talk to your doctor first [2][34].
- Panax ginseng changed how the body handled a CYP3A test drug, and a case report linked ginseng to liver injury with imatinib. Ask your doctor or pharmacist if you take medicines broken down by CYP3A4 [27][28].
- Asian ginseng may be unsafe in pregnancy, and little is known about breastfeeding [2].
- Asian ginseng may worsen autoimmune disorders, and in lab studies ginseng made breast cancer cells grow, though higher concentrations slowed their growth in one study. With either condition, ask your doctor first [2][39][40].
- Insomnia is Asian ginseng's most common side effect. Severe allergic reactions are uncommon; for swelling of the face or throat or trouble breathing, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) [2].

## 1. Overview

Ginseng supplements are usually made from the root of Panax plants [2]. Asian ginseng is native to Korea, northeastern China and far-eastern Siberia, and traditional Chinese medicine has used it as a calming agent and as an adaptogen, a herb thought to help the body resist physical and mental stress [2].

The two most important species in clinical research are:

- **Asian ginseng (Panax ginseng C.A. Meyer)**: also called Chinese or Korean ginseng [2]. "White ginseng" and "red ginseng" are Asian ginseng prepared in two ways: white ginseng is dried, and red ginseng is steamed [2][31]. The two differ in chemical makeup, but evidence that this changes their effects in people is still needed [31].

- **American ginseng (Panax quinquefolius L.)**: native to North America. Wisconsin-grown American ginseng was tested in the largest trial for cancer-related fatigue [5], and American ginseng extract has been tested for blood sugar control [6].

**Siberian ginseng (Eleutherococcus senticosus)** is a different plant sold under a similar name. In a test of eight ginseng types, it raised blood sugar after a glucose drink [34]. This article covers Panax ginseng and Panax quinquefolius.

### Ginsenosides: The Active Compounds

Ginseng contains many substances; those thought to matter most are called **ginsenosides** (or panaxosides) [2].

Different ginseng types can push blood sugar in different directions. In a crossover test of eight ginseng types in 12 healthy people, some tended to lower blood sugar after a glucose drink and others raised it; the ratio between two ginsenoside groups explained only a small part of the difference [34]. A batch of American ginseng with a different ginsenoside profile failed to repeat the blood sugar effect of earlier batches [35].

### State of the Evidence

Research on Asian ginseng has grown, but most clinical trials have been small, with fewer than 200 participants, and shorter than 3 months [2]. A review of registered and published ginseng trials found most were single-centre [3]. Larger multicentre trials that last longer than 3 months are needed [2]. The research is suggestive for fatigue, blood sugar and sexual function, but it does not yet support firm recommendations for most conditions.

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

Ginseng supplements come in many forms. The species, the processing method and the ginsenoside content can all change what a product does.

### Types of Ginseng

| Type | Species | Processing | Key Features |
|------|---------|------------|--------------|
| White Asian Ginseng | Panax ginseng | Dried root | Traditional preparation [2][31]. |
| Red Asian (Korean) Ginseng | Panax ginseng | Steamed, then dried | Different chemical makeup from white ginseng [31]. The form used in most sexual function trials below [12][13][14]. |
| American Ginseng | Panax quinquefolius | Dried root or extract | Tested for cancer-related fatigue and blood sugar control [5][6]. |
| Hydrolyzed Ginseng | Panax ginseng | Hydrolyzed extract | Tested in one small blood sugar trial [7]. |
| Siberian "Ginseng" (Eleuthero) | Eleutherococcus senticosus | Various | A different plant. Raised blood sugar after a glucose drink in one test [34]. |

### Product Forms

Ginseng is available as:
- **Root powder**: the dried, ground root, as used in the cold hypersensitivity trial [24].
- **Standardized extract**: a concentrated preparation with a stated ginsenoside content, such as G115 [17].
- **Liquid extracts and tinctures**: less common in research. Strength varies by product.
- **Liquid pouches**: used in some Korean red ginseng products, such as the extract in the ADHD trial [10].

### Ginsenoside Content Standards

For independent product testing, see consumerlab.com or Labdoor.

### Key Bioavailability Considerations

- **Batches differ.** One batch of American ginseng with lower total ginsenosides did not lower blood sugar after a 75 g glucose drink, unlike batches the same research group had tested before [35].

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Fatigue and Energy

Many people take ginseng for energy. The trials give it modest support for fatigue.

**General fatigue in healthy adults:** In a trial in 46 healthy middle-aged Japanese adults, 960 mg of Korean red ginseng extract for 3 weeks lowered fatigue scores more than placebo. Blood cortisol and lactic acid did not differ between groups, and adverse events were no more common than with placebo [4].

**Systematic review of fatigue:** A 2023 review of 19 studies (2,413 participants) suggested that Asian ginseng alone may slightly ease general fatigue, and that some ginseng herbal formulas with other ingredients may help chronic fatigue, though ginseng alone did not [2]. Pooled across all trials, ginseng did not significantly reduce fatigue severity, and effect sizes were small [9]. Not all research agrees on ginseng for fatigue [2].

**Cancer-related fatigue:** The largest trial randomized 364 fatigued cancer survivors from 40 institutions to 2,000 mg of Wisconsin American ginseng a day or placebo for 8 weeks [5]. At 4 weeks, the primary endpoint, the difference was not significant (P = .07). At 8 weeks, fatigue scores improved by 20 points with ginseng against 10.3 with placebo (P = .003). People in active cancer treatment benefited more than those who had finished treatment, and side effects did not differ between groups. The trial followed earlier pilot data [5].

**Athletic performance:** Most research shows that Asian ginseng does not improve athletic performance [2].

### Blood Sugar Control

In diabetes and prediabetes, ginseng may lower blood sugar, but results are inconsistent, and some ginseng types raised blood sugar in a test in healthy people [2][11][34].

**American ginseng and type 2 diabetes:** In a crossover trial in 24 adults with type 2 diabetes (average age 64) who kept their usual treatment, 1 g of American ginseng extract with each meal (3 g a day) for 8 weeks lowered, against placebo, HbA1c (-0.29%), systolic blood pressure (-5.6 mmHg) and fasting blood glucose (-0.71 mmol/L) [6].

**Hydrolyzed ginseng extract and impaired fasting glucose:** In an 8-week trial in 23 Korean adults with impaired fasting glucose, 960 mg a day of hydrolyzed ginseng extract lowered fasting and post-meal glucose more than placebo, with no clinically significant change in safety measures [7].

**Red Korean ginseng and type 2 diabetes:** In a randomized trial with a placebo group in patients with type 2 diabetes or impaired glucose regulation, the red ginseng group's fasting blood glucose fell from 6.44 to 6.19 mmol/L, 2-hour glucose from 11.96 to 10.48 mmol/L and HbA1c from 6.27 to 6.12%; no participant had adverse effects [8]. Compared with placebo, 2-hour glucose and HbA1c fell more with red ginseng; fasting glucose did not differ between groups [8].

**Meta-analysis:** A 2022 review of 20 studies (1,295 participants) found that Asian ginseng improved many cardiometabolic factors in people with prediabetes and diabetes, including fasting blood glucose, total cholesterol and certain inflammatory markers. Research on blood sugar control is still overall inconclusive and conflicting [2][11].

**Critical caution:** In a crossover test of eight ginseng types taken before a glucose drink, Asian ginseng and Siberian ginseng raised blood sugar compared with placebo, while American ginseng tended to lower it [34]. The authors linked part of the difference to the ginsenoside profile [34]. Because results with ginseng have been hard to reproduce, its use in diabetes calls for caution [26]. If you have diabetes, talk to your doctor before using ginseng [2].

### Sexual Function

Two areas of sexual function have been studied: erectile dysfunction in men and sexual dysfunction in women.

**Erectile dysfunction (men):** A Cochrane review of nine placebo-controlled trials found that ginseng appears to have a trivial effect on erectile function scores (mean difference 3.52, 95% CI 1.79 to 5.25; low certainty) and may improve men's self-reported ability to have intercourse (RR 2.55, 95% CI 1.76 to 3.69) [36]. In an older trial, 90 men, 30 per group, took Korean red ginseng, placebo or the antidepressant trazodone; overall efficacy was 60% with ginseng against 30% with placebo or trazodone [12].

In a trial in 60 men with mild or mild to moderate erectile dysfunction, 1,000 mg of Korean red ginseng three times daily raised erectile function scores, and 20 men (66.6%) on ginseng reported better erections; the placebo group did not change. Testosterone did not differ between groups [13].

Some research shows that oral Asian ginseng seems to improve sexual function in erectile dysfunction [2]. In a 12-week trial in 88 men with lower urinary tract symptoms, red ginseng oil improved urinary symptom scores and total erectile function scores, without changing testosterone [32].

**Sexual function (women):** In a double-blind crossover trial, 32 menopausal women took three 1 g capsules of Korean red ginseng a day or placebo, and 28 completed it (average age 51.2) [14]. Ginseng improved the sexual arousal score. There were no severe adverse events, but two women had vaginal bleeding while taking ginseng [14].

### Cognitive Function and Memory

The evidence for ginseng and thinking is mixed.

**Younger adults:** In a trial in 61 healthy young adults, an American ginseng extract (Cereboost) improved working memory and attention in the hours after a dose, more so after 2 weeks of daily use, and 2 weeks of use also eased mental fatigue [16].

**Middle-aged and older adults (possible benefit):** A small amount of research has suggested that Asian ginseng may improve abstract thinking, attention, arithmetic skills and reaction time in middle-aged adults but not in young adults. Any benefit on memory has appeared to depend on adding ginkgo [2].

**Synthesis:** Small trials point to possible short-term effects on attention, but the evidence is too preliminary to recommend ginseng for thinking or memory [2][16].

### Immune Function and Respiratory Infections

Whether ginseng prevents colds or flu is uncertain.

**American ginseng extract (Cold-FX):** COLD-fX is a proprietary American ginseng root extract rich in polysaccharides [19]. In a 4-month trial in 43 adults aged 65 or older, two 200 mg capsules a day made no difference at first; in the last 2 months, 32% on COLD-fX reported a respiratory illness against 62% on placebo, and symptoms were shorter (5.6 days against 12.6 days) [19].

**Asian ginseng and flu vaccination:** In a trial in 227 people in Milan, 100 mg a day of a standardized ginseng extract (Ginsana G115) for 12 weeks, with a flu vaccine at week 4, was followed by 15 cases of flu or common cold between weeks 4 and 12, against 42 with placebo. Antibody levels and natural killer cell activity were also higher with ginseng [17].

**Korean red ginseng and immune cells:** In an 8-week trial in 100 healthy adults, taking a 2 g Korean red ginseng tablet each day raised T cells, B cells and white blood cell counts compared with placebo [18].

**NCCIH assessment:** A very small amount of preliminary research suggests that Asian ginseng extract may help reduce the risk of developing the flu but not the severity or duration of symptoms [2].

**Synthesis:** Some trials show changes in immune cell counts, but that has not reliably meant fewer infections. The evidence is too thin to recommend ginseng to prevent respiratory infections [2].

### ADHD (Attention Deficit-Hyperactivity Disorder)

In an 8-week placebo-controlled trial in children aged 6-15 years with ADHD symptoms (33 on ginseng, 37 on placebo), one 1 g pouch of Korean red ginseng extract twice a day lowered inattention and hyperactivity scores more than placebo (inattention -2.25 vs. -1.24, p=0.048; hyperactivity -1.53 vs. -0.61, p=0.047) [10]. A brain wave measure linked to ADHD (the theta/beta ratio) also fell more with ginseng, but salivary cortisol and DHEA did not change, and no serious adverse events occurred [10].

An open 4-week study with no placebo group gave 36 children a combination of American ginseng extract (200 mg) and Ginkgo biloba extract (50 mg) twice a day, and parents reported fewer ADHD symptoms. The combination and the open design mean the result cannot be credited to ginseng [25].

These are small, early studies. Ginseng should not replace established ADHD treatment.

### Metabolic Syndrome and Hormonal Effects

In a 4-week trial in 62 men with metabolic syndrome, 3.0 g a day of red ginseng raised total testosterone and IGF-1 compared with placebo, and diastolic blood pressure and cortisol fell in the ginseng group [22].

One short trial does not make ginseng a testosterone booster. In two erectile dysfunction trials, testosterone did not differ from placebo [13][32].

### Cold Hypersensitivity (Raynaud's Phenomenon)

In an 8-week trial in 80 Korean women with cold hands and feet, six 500-mg capsules of Korean red ginseng powder twice daily raised skin temperature in the hands and feet and eased symptom scores more than placebo, with no serious adverse events [24].

This single trial needs repeating.

### General Wellbeing

There is interest in Asian ginseng for aging, asthma, anxiety and other conditions, but there is not enough reliable information to say whether it helps [2].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

Doses vary with the species and form. The doses below are the ones trials used; none is an established dose.

### General Dosing Guidelines

No official dose exists for ginseng. Match the form and dose to the trial behind the use you are considering, listed below.

### Dosing by Indication (From Clinical Trials)

**Cancer-related fatigue:** 2,000 mg Wisconsin American ginseng daily for 8 weeks [5].

**Blood sugar control (type 2 diabetes):** 1 g American ginseng extract with each meal (3 g a day) for 8 weeks [6]. In impaired fasting glucose, 960 mg a day of hydrolyzed ginseng extract for 8 weeks [7].

**Erectile dysfunction:** 1,000 mg Korean red ginseng three times daily [13].

**ADHD in children:** 1 g Korean red ginseng extract twice daily, in one 8-week trial [10].

**Cold hypersensitivity:** six 500-mg capsules of Korean red ginseng powder twice daily [24].

**Respiratory infection prevention:** two 200 mg capsules of COLD-fX daily for 4 months [19]. Or 100 mg of G115 extract daily for 12 weeks, with a flu vaccine at week 4 [17].

**General fatigue:** 960 mg Korean red ginseng extract for 3 weeks [4].

**Immune enhancement:** a 2 g Korean red ginseng tablet each day for 8 weeks [18].

### Practical Dosing Notes

Most Asian ginseng trials have been shorter than 3 months, so less is known about longer use [2].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Safety Profile

Taken by mouth for up to 6 months in recommended amounts, Asian ginseng appears to be safe for most people [2]. In a Cochrane review, ginseng may have little to no effect on adverse events compared with placebo [36], and in a fatigue review it did not increase adverse events [9].

### Common Side Effects

Insomnia (trouble sleeping) is Asian ginseng's most common side effect [2][17]. In a systematic review of Panax ginseng, adverse events with ginseng alone were about as common as with placebo, and the most common were [43]:
- Headache
- Sleep problems
- Stomach and gut upsets

### Uncommon but Reported Side Effects

- Severe rash [2]
- Liver damage [2]
- Severe allergic reactions [2]. For swelling of the face, lips or throat, or trouble breathing, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).
- Vaginal bleeding, in two menopausal women in one trial [14]

An early uncontrolled report on long-term ginseng users described what it called a ginseng abuse syndrome [44]. Reports of this kind cannot show that ginseng caused the symptoms.

### Blood Sugar Effects — A Dual Concern

Some ginseng types lowered blood sugar in trials and others raised it [6][34], and some evidence suggests Asian ginseng might lower blood sugar [2]. This creates two risks for people with diabetes:
- If ginseng lowers blood sugar, diabetes medicines may need a lower dose to avoid hypoglycemia
- If ginseng raises blood sugar, diabetes control may worsen

If you have diabetes, talk to your doctor before using ginseng [2][26].

### Cancer and Estrogenic Effects

Lab studies disagree on whether ginseng acts like estrogen. In one, ginseng made breast cancer cells grow without estrogen-like activity [39]; in another, a methanol extract of American ginseng acted like estrogen at low concentrations, but a water extract did not, and at higher concentrations both extracts slowed breast cancer cell growth [40]. Because of these findings, people with breast cancer should not use ginseng without their oncologist's advice.

### Autoimmune Conditions

Asian ginseng may worsen autoimmune disorders [2]. If you have one, check with your doctor before using it.

### Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

Some studies suggest Asian ginseng may be unsafe when taken by mouth in pregnancy, and one of its chemicals has caused birth defects in animals [2]. Little is known about safety while breastfeeding. Some experts recommend against its use in pregnancy, while breastfeeding, and for infants and children [2].

### Bipolar Disorder

Case reports describe mania linked to ginseng [41] and a suspected interaction with the antidepressant phenelzine [42]. Case reports cannot prove cause, but people with bipolar disorder, or who take antidepressants, should check with their doctor first.

### Long-Term Safety

Questions have been raised about Asian ginseng's long-term safety [2]. The evidence of safety covers use up to 6 months; longer use is less studied [2].

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Warfarin (Coumadin)

Trials disagree on ginseng and warfarin. In a randomized trial in 20 healthy volunteers, American ginseng reduced warfarin's anticoagulant effect: peak INR fell compared with placebo (difference -0.19, 95% CI -0.36 to -0.07) [37]. In another trial in 12 healthy men, ginseng did not affect warfarin's levels or its effect on INR [38]. Asian ginseng may also interfere with blood clotting [2]. If you take warfarin or another blood thinner, check with your doctor before using ginseng.

### CYP3A4 Enzyme System

Ginseng's effect on CYP3A4, a liver and gut enzyme that breaks down many medicines, is unclear. In 12 healthy volunteers, Panax ginseng 500 mg twice daily for 28 days lowered blood levels of midazolam, a CYP3A test drug (AUC ratio 0.66), which suggests it speeds up CYP3A [28]. A case report described liver injury in a man who had taken imatinib for 7 years, after 3 months of drinking ginseng energy drinks; the authors proposed that ginseng slowed imatinib's breakdown through CYP3A4 [27]. Medicines broken down by CYP3A4 include many statins (atorvastatin, simvastatin), calcium channel blockers, immunosuppressants (cyclosporine, tacrolimus), some cancer drugs and benzodiazepines.

If you take a medicine broken down by CYP3A4, ask your doctor or pharmacist before using ginseng.

### Antidepressants

As noted above, case reports describe mania with ginseng and a suspected interaction with the antidepressant phenelzine [41][42].

### Antidiabetes Medications

Ginseng may strengthen or weaken the effect of diabetes medicines (see Sections 3 and 5). If you use insulin or other glucose-lowering medicines, monitor your blood sugar closely and talk to your doctor first [2][34].

### Blood Thinning and Clotting

Asian ginseng may interfere with blood clotting [2]. If you take an antiplatelet or anticoagulant medicine, check with your doctor first.

### Siberian Ginseng (Eleuthero) — Specific Statin Interaction

A 2019 case report described possible liver injury in a man aged 82, taking atorvastatin 80 mg, who had started a supplement combining Siberian ginseng with silymarin (milk thistle); his liver tests returned to normal within 2 months of stopping the supplement and atorvastatin [29]. In a lab study, Siberian ginseng extract partly blocked OATP-B, a transporter thought to help the gut absorb some drugs [30]. If you take a statin, check with your doctor before using any product sold as ginseng, including Siberian ginseng.

### General Precaution

Asian ginseng might interact with certain medicines. If you take any medicine, talk with your doctor before using ginseng or other herbal products [2].

## 7. Dietary Sources

Unlike minerals and vitamins, ginseng is not a nutrient found in common foods. It is an herbal product made from the root of Panax plants.

### Traditional Uses

- **Ginseng tea**: sliced or powdered root steeped in hot water, widely drunk in Korea, China and Japan.
- **Ginseng-containing foods**: some Asian dishes include ginseng root, such as samgyetang (Korean ginseng chicken soup).
- **Ginseng candy and extracts**: sold mainly in East Asian markets.
- **Energy drinks**: some contain ginseng.

### Source Quality Considerations

- Asian ginseng is native to Korea, northeastern China and far-eastern Siberia [2].
- Batches of the same species can differ in ginsenoside content and effect [35].

### Is Dietary Ginseng Sufficient?

Teas and soups do not state a ginsenoside dose, so they cannot be matched to trial doses. Trials used standardized extracts or measured amounts of root powder [17][24].
