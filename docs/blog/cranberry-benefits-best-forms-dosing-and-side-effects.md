## Current evidence position

Brad has not made a video on this topic; this is a summary of the evidence, not his view. A 2023 Cochrane review of 50 trials found that cranberry products probably reduce symptomatic, culture-verified UTIs in women with recurrent UTIs (RR 0.74, 95% CI 0.55 to 0.99), in children (RR 0.46, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.68) and in people prone to UTIs after a medical intervention (RR 0.47, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.61), but may give little or no benefit to elderly people in care, adults with bladder-emptying problems or pregnant women [6]. NIH notes that findings are inconsistent and that cranberry is not recommended as a treatment for an existing UTI in any population [4], and a Cochrane review of cranberry for treating UTIs found no trials that met its criteria [29]. Trials have used one 240 mL serving of cranberry beverage a day [13] or one capsule of 500 mg a day of whole cranberry powder [15], and a 2024 meta-analysis found lower UTI risk only when daily PAC intake was at least 36 mg [21]. Evidence on whether cranberry interacts with warfarin conflicts, so NIH advises anyone taking warfarin or other medicines to talk with their health care provider first [4].

## Key safety points

- Evidence on cranberry and warfarin conflicts [4]. One review rated it a major-severity interaction [58]; another found no interaction at moderate juice intakes, said reports suggest one needs very large amounts, and noted that most cases involved people with serious illness or other medicines [59]. If you take warfarin, talk to your doctor first.
- Cranberry does not treat an existing UTI [4][29]. See a doctor the same day for urinary symptoms with fever, back or side pain, or vomiting, or any UTI symptoms in pregnancy; if you feel very unwell, confused or faint, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department.
- A kidney transplant patient had low tacrolimus levels while taking cranberry extract, and they returned to range when she stopped it [64]. If you take tacrolimus, ask your transplant team first.
- Cranberry tablets raised urinary oxalate in small studies, and 1 L a day of juice raised calcium oxalate saturation, though another juice study found the opposite [52][53][54][55]. If you have had kidney stones, ask your doctor before taking cranberry supplements.
- Some studies suggest food amounts are safe in pregnancy and breastfeeding, but the evidence for larger amounts is not conclusive [4]. Ask your doctor first.
- Very large amounts can cause stomach upset and diarrhea, especially in young children [4].

## 1. Overview

Cranberry (*Vaccinium macrocarpon*) is an evergreen shrub of wet habitats in north-eastern and north-central North America [4]. Its proanthocyanidins (PACs) contain A-type linkages. In laboratory tests, A-type PACs from cranberry juice cocktail blocked adhesion of uropathogenic *E. coli*, B-type PACs from grape showed only minor activity, and B-type PACs from apple juice, green tea and dark chocolate showed none; urine anti-adhesion activity appeared only after the cranberry drink [3].

Native Americans and later European settlers used cranberry for dyes, food and medicine [4]. Today it is mostly promoted to prevent repeat urinary tract infections (UTIs). Part of the reason is interest in non-antibiotic prevention as antibiotic resistance grows [4].

Since 2020 the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA) has let makers claim on labels that "limited" evidence suggests daily cranberry supplements may lower recurrent UTI risk in healthy women; for juice beverages the evidence must be called "limited and inconsistent" [4][5]. The 2023 Cochrane review of 50 trials with 8,857 participants found that cranberry products reduced the risk of symptomatic, culture-verified UTIs (RR 0.70, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.84; moderate certainty), with clearer benefit in women with recurrent UTIs, children and people susceptible after a medical intervention [6].

Cranberry has also been studied for *Helicobacter pylori* infection, heart and metabolic health, memory, skin and men's urinary symptoms, but research outside UTIs is limited [4][7][8][9].

Cranberry comes as juice, juice cocktail, capsules or tablets made from juice extract or whole-fruit powder, chews and sweetened dried cranberries. Processing cranberries into products such as tablets or capsules can lower their PAC content, which can reduce a product's potential effect [4].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Understanding PAC Measurement Methods

PAC content is usually measured with the DMAC assay, and the result depends on the reference standard. A cranberry-derived standard gave values 2.2 times higher than the procyanidin A2 standard for the same samples, so PAC figures measured different ways are not directly comparable [70].

### Soluble vs. Insoluble PACs

In a study of two commercial supplements, only fractions containing soluble PACs blocked bacterial adhesion. The juice-extract product held more soluble PACs than a whole-cranberry blend, which contained mainly insoluble PACs, and it produced higher urinary anti-adhesion activity when taken once daily for a week [11].

Mass spectrometry of cranberry PACs found that oligomers with one or more A-type bonds made up more than 91% of those from trimers to undecamers [12].

### Product Forms Comparison

PAC content varies widely between products. Not-from-concentrate juice held 23.0 mg of PACs per 100 mL, against 8.9 mg per 100 mL for juice from concentrate, and sweetened dried cranberries still held 64.2 mg per 100 g [18]. Commercially processed products held less polyphenol than fresh or home-prepared cranberries [18]. Capsules made from juice extract and capsules made from whole cranberry blends differ in soluble PAC content [11].

### Bioavailability and Duration of Effect

In a crossover pilot study of 20 people, a cranberry chew taken morning and evening raised urinary anti-adhesion activity against P-type *E. coli* above placebo at every collection point except 24 to 36 hours; activity against type 1 *E. coli* was higher at 3 to 6 and 6 to 9 hours [16].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Urinary Tract Infection (UTI) Prevention in Women

UTI prevention is the most studied use of cranberry, with systematic reviews and many randomised trials.

#### Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses

**Cochrane systematic review (2023):** The review included 50 studies with 8,857 participants. Overall, cranberry products reduced the risk of symptomatic, culture-verified UTIs (RR 0.70, 95% CI 0.58 to 0.84; moderate certainty). By group [6]:
- Women with recurrent UTIs: RR 0.74, 95% CI 0.55 to 0.99
- Children: RR 0.46, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.68
- People susceptible to UTIs after an intervention: RR 0.47, 95% CI 0.37 to 0.61
- Elderly people in institutions: little or no benefit (RR 0.93, 95% CI 0.67 to 1.30)
- Pregnant women: little or no benefit (RR 1.06, 95% CI 0.75 to 1.50)
- Adults with neuromuscular bladder dysfunction and incomplete bladder emptying: little or no benefit (RR 0.97, 95% CI 0.78 to 1.19)

The review could not tell whether juice and tablets, or different PAC doses, differ in effect, because the certainty of that evidence was very low [6].

**Wang et al. meta-analysis (2012):** Across 10 trials with 1,494 participants, cranberry users had fewer UTIs (RR 0.62, 95% CI 0.49 to 0.80). Effects seemed larger in women with recurrent UTIs (RR 0.53, 95% CI 0.33 to 0.83), in children (RR 0.33, 95% CI 0.16 to 0.69), in juice drinkers (RR 0.47, 95% CI 0.30 to 0.72) and in people using cranberry products more than twice daily (RR 0.58, 95% CI 0.40 to 0.84), though trials varied a lot [20].

**PAC dose-response analysis (2024):** A meta-analysis of 10 randomised trials found that a daily PAC intake of at least 36 mg lowered UTI risk (RR 0.82, 95% CI 0.69 to 0.98), while intakes below 36 mg showed no significant effect [21].

**Network meta-analysis (2024):** The analysis included 20 trials with 3,091 participants; in 18 of them, cranberry juice was linked to a 54% lower rate of UTIs than no treatment and a 27% lower rate than a placebo liquid, with moderate to low certainty; extra fluids alone also helped compared with no treatment [22].

#### Cranberry Juice Trials

**Maki et al. (2016):** Women with a recent UTI (mean age 40.9) drank one 240 mL serving of cranberry beverage or a placebo daily for 24 weeks. There were 39 clinical UTI episodes in the cranberry group and 67 in the placebo group (incidence rate ratio 0.61, 95% CI 0.41 to 0.91), though time to a culture-positive UTI did not differ [13].

#### Cranberry Capsule and Supplement Trials

**Stonehouse et al. (2025):** In 150 women with recurrent UTIs, one Pacran capsule of 500 mg a day of whole cranberry powder for 6 months lowered the risk of culture-confirmed UTI compared with placebo (adjusted RR 0.48, 95% CI 0.26 to 0.87) and delayed the first UTI (adjusted HR 0.36, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.74). Suspected UTIs based on symptoms alone did not differ, and no safety concerns were reported [15].

**Tsiakoulias et al. (2024):** In 160 women with recurrent UTIs (median age 40 years), a daily high-dose PAC product (Cysticlean 240 mg) for 12 months lowered the number of UTIs compared with placebo (incidence rate ratio 0.49) and reduced *E. coli* in vaginal and rectal swabs. The trial reported no adverse events [25].

**Foxman et al. (2015):** In 160 women having elective gynaecological surgery with urinary catheterisation, 2 cranberry juice capsules twice a day for 6 weeks after surgery cut the UTI rate from 30 of 80 (38%) on placebo to 15 of 80 (19%) (odds ratio 0.38, 95% CI 0.19 to 0.79). The benefit held after adjusting for self-catheterisation (odds ratio 0.42, 95% CI 0.18 to 0.94) [26]. NIH, however, notes that studies in women having gynaecological surgery have not found cranberry beneficial for UTI prevention [4].

**Juthani-Mehta et al. (2016):** In 185 women aged 65 or older in 21 nursing homes, 2 capsules a day (72 mg of PACs in total) for a year did not reduce bacteriuria plus pyuria compared with placebo (29.1% vs 29.0%). Symptomatic UTIs (10 vs 12 episodes) and hospitalisations (RR 0.67, 95% CI 0.32 to 1.40) did not differ significantly [19].

#### Cranberry Chews

**Liu et al. (2019):** In a crossover pilot study of 20 people, a cranberry chew taken morning and evening raised urinary anti-adhesion activity against *E. coli* compared with a placebo chew [16]. The study measured urine activity, not infections.

#### Dried Cranberries

**Burleigh et al. (2013):** Twenty women with recurrent UTIs ate one serving of sweetened dried cranberries daily for two weeks. More than half had no UTI in the next six months, and the mean six-month UTI rate fell, but there was no placebo arm, and results were compared with an earlier control group [17].

### UTI Prevention in Children

Cranberry products may reduce UTI recurrence in children, though nitrofurantoin may work better.

**Gkiourtzis et al. (2024):** A meta-analysis of 23 trials with 3,335 children with recurrent UTIs found that cranberry products and nitrofurantoin both lowered the odds of symptomatic UTI compared with controls, and nitrofurantoin may be the best option. No prophylaxis reduced kidney scarring [27].

**Afshar et al. (2012):** In a trial of 40 children aged 5 to 18, children with recurrent UTIs drank cranberry juice high in PACs or a juice with no PACs for a year. UTIs averaged 0.4 per child per year with high-PAC juice against 1.15 with the comparison juice, a 65% reduction [28].

The 2023 Cochrane review also found lower UTI risk in children (RR 0.46, 95% CI 0.32 to 0.68) [6].

### UTI Treatment

NIH says cranberry is not recommended as a treatment for an existing UTI in any population and should not replace proven treatment [4]. A 2023 Cochrane review of cranberry for treating UTIs found no randomised trials that met its criteria and no good-quality evidence that it works [29]. Cranberry is a prevention strategy, not a treatment.

### Overactive Bladder (OAB)

**Cho et al. (2021):** Women with overactive bladder took 500 mg of powdered dried cranberry or placebo daily for 24 weeks. Of 98 women randomised, 60 were in the per-protocol analysis, which showed fewer daily voids (-1.91) and urgency episodes (-2.81) with cranberry. Night-time voiding did not change, and the intention-to-treat analysis showed no significant difference [30].

### Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms and Prostate Health in Men

Several small studies have tested cranberry for lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS), benign prostatic hyperplasia (BPH), chronic prostatitis and prostate cancer, with mixed results.

#### LUTS Improvement

**Vidlar et al. (2016):** In 124 men over the age of 45 with LUTS, Flowens cranberry powder, 250 mg or 500 mg once a day, for 6 months cut symptom scores (International Prostate Symptom Score, IPSS) by 3.1 and 4.1 points, against 1.5 points on placebo [32].

#### BPH with Recurrent UTIs

**Ledda et al. (2016):** In a non-randomised pilot registry of 43 men aged over 65 years with BPH and recurrent UTIs, men who chose a daily cranberry extract capsule (Anthocran) for 60 consecutive days alongside standard care averaged 0.8 UTIs during the study period against 3.2 at entry, and did better than men on standard care alone [33].

#### Chronic Non-Bacterial Prostatitis and Elevated PSA

**Vidlar et al. (2010):** In 42 men with LUTS, raised PSA, a negative biopsy and chronic non-bacterial prostatitis, 1,500 mg of dried powdered cranberries each day for 6 months improved symptom scores, quality of life and urine flow, and lowered total PSA, compared with no treatment [34].

#### Prostate Cancer — PSA Reduction Before Surgery

**Student et al. (2016):** In 64 men with prostate cancer awaiting radical prostatectomy, 1,500 mg of cranberry fruit powder daily for a mean of 30 days lowered serum PSA by 22.5% in the cranberry group; prostate tissue markers did not change [35].

#### Prostate Cancer — Radiation Treatment

Results during radiation therapy for prostate cancer are mixed across three studies:

**Hamilton et al. (2015):** In 41 men in New Zealand, one capsule a day (72 mg PACs) during radiotherapy and for a further 2 weeks was linked to cystitis in 65% against 90% on placebo, a difference that missed significance (p = 0.058); pain or burning was significantly less common with cranberry [36].

**Herst et al. (2020):** In a larger trial of 101 men, cranberry capsules were not better than beetroot-containing placebo capsules and did worse for daytime frequency and bladder control [37].

**Bonetta et al. (2012):** In 370 men having radiotherapy, 16 of 184 (8.7%) taking an enteric-coated cranberry extract developed a lower urinary tract infection, against 45 of 186 (24.2%) controls, with less night-time voiding, urgency, frequency and painful urination [38].

### Helicobacter pylori Infection

*H. pylori* is a stomach bacterium linked to gastritis and peptic ulcers. Trials of cranberry juice, alone or with antibiotics, show modest and inconsistent effects.

**Evidence for cranberry with antibiotics:** In 177 people on one week of triple therapy, 250 mL of cranberry juice twice daily did not change the overall eradication rate (82.5% across arms), though women had higher eradication with cranberry (95.2%) than with placebo (86.8%, significance not reported) and significantly higher than the 80% on antibiotics alone (p = 0.03) [40]. In 200 people with peptic ulcer disease, adding 500 mg cranberry capsules, twice a day, to 14 days of triple therapy raised eradication from 74% to 89% [42]. Without antibiotics, two 250 mL servings of juice a day for 90 days led to negative breath tests in 14 of 97 people against 5 of 92 on placebo [39]. In a trial of 522 H. pylori-positive adults, only high-PAC juice twice daily (44 mg of PACs per 240 mL serving) for 8 weeks lowered the infection rate, while cranberry powder capsules did not [41].

**Gao et al. (2021):** In 171 H. pylori positive participants, 480 mL of cranberry beverage for 4 weeks raised total antioxidant capacity compared with placebo, and both the 480 mL and 240 mL doses reduced *Pseudomonas* in the gut [7].

### Cardiovascular and Metabolic Effects

Meta-analyses of cranberry and heart or metabolic markers disagree.

**Blood pressure, positive evidence:** A 2020 meta-analysis found that cranberry lowered systolic blood pressure and body mass index, with a larger systolic drop in studies with a mean participant age of 50 or more [43].

**Blood pressure, contradictory evidence:** A meta-analysis of blueberry and cranberry trials in people with cardiometabolic diseases found no significant effect on systolic (-0.81 mm Hg, 95% CI -2.26 to 0.63) or diastolic blood pressure, regardless of duration or age [44]. A 2026 meta-analysis of 12 trials found non-significant reductions of 1.31 mmHg in both systolic and diastolic pressure and concluded cranberry was not effective [69].

**Lipid profiles:** The 2020 meta-analysis found no significant change in total cholesterol, LDL, HDL or triglycerides overall, though HDL rose in subgroups with a mean age under 50 [43].

**Inflammation:** The same meta-analysis found no significant change in C-reactive protein [43].

**Blood sugar and diabetes:** The same meta-analysis found no significant change in fasting glucose, fasting insulin or insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) [43].

### Gut Microbiota

In the Gao trial, both cranberry doses reduced *Pseudomonas* in the gut, and the authors suggest the better antioxidant status may relate to these gut changes [7].

### Memory and Cognition

**Flanagan et al. (2022):** In a 12-week feasibility trial in 60 adults aged between 50 and 80 years, freeze-dried cranberry powder (equivalent to 1 small cup of cranberries a day) improved visual episodic memory compared with placebo and increased blood flow in some brain regions. LDL cholesterol fell, and BDNF did not differ between groups [8].

### Skin Health

**Christman et al. (2024):** In a crossover trial in 22 women, a cranberry drink rich in polyphenols, taken for 6 weeks, reduced UVB-induced skin redness and improved some measures of elasticity and smoothness compared with placebo, mainly in women over 40 years old [9].

### Anticancer Properties

Human evidence on cranberry and cancer is limited to the PSA studies above, which measured a blood marker rather than cancer outcomes [34][35].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### For UTI Prevention

No dose is established. Doses that helped in trials include:

**Cranberry juice:**
- One 240 mL serving of cranberry beverage a day for 24 weeks [13]
- In a meta-analysis, juice drinkers and people using cranberry products more than twice daily seemed to benefit more [20]

**Cranberry capsules/supplements:**
- One capsule of 500 mg a day of whole cranberry powder for 6 months [15]
- A daily high-dose PAC product (Cysticlean 240 mg) for 12 months [25]
- After gynaecological surgery, 2 cranberry juice capsules twice a day for 6 weeks [26], though NIH notes surgery studies have not found a benefit [4]
- A meta-analysis found benefit only with a daily PAC intake of at least 36 mg [21]

**For children:** Trials in children used cranberry juice or extract products [27][28]. Ask your child's doctor about dose.

### For H. pylori (Adjunct to Antibiotics)

Trials used 250 mL of cranberry juice twice daily with triple therapy [40], or 500 mg cranberry capsules, twice a day, with triple therapy [42]. High-PAC juice (44 mg of PACs per 240 mL serving) twice daily helped without antibiotics [41]. Cranberry does not replace antibiotic treatment.

### For Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms in Men

Trials used Flowens cranberry powder, 250 mg or 500 mg once a day [32], and 1,500 mg of dried powdered cranberries each day for chronic prostatitis with raised PSA [34].

### How to Read a Cranberry Supplement Label

When choosing a cranberry supplement, look for:

1. **PAC content per serving**: the 2024 dose-response analysis linked benefit to a daily PAC intake of at least 36 mg [21].
2. **Measurement method**: PAC figures depend on the assay standard, so numbers from different methods are not directly comparable [70].
3. **Source material**: a juice-extract supplement held more soluble PACs and gave higher urinary anti-adhesion activity than a whole-cranberry blend [11].
4. **Independent testing**: you can check product testing at consumerlab.com or Labdoor.

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Tolerability

NIH says cranberry taken by mouth is generally thought to be safe, but very large amounts can cause stomach upset and diarrhea, particularly in young children [4]. In the Cochrane review, gastrointestinal side effects probably did not differ between cranberry and placebo or no treatment (RR 1.33, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.77) [6]. In the surgery trial, gastrointestinal upset was similar on cranberry and placebo (56% vs 61%) [26].

### Kidney Stones (Oxalate Concerns)

Studies of cranberry and kidney stones conflict [59]:

- **Cranberry juice:** In 12 healthy people and 12 calcium oxalate stone formers, 1 L of juice a day for 7 days raised urinary calcium and oxalate and increased calcium oxalate saturation by 18%; the authors concluded it makes calcium oxalate and uric acid stones more likely but brushite stones less likely [52]. In 20 male students, 500 mL of juice diluted in water daily for 2 weeks lowered urinary oxalate and calcium oxalate supersaturation [53]
- **Cranberry supplements:** In 5 volunteers, cranberry tablets for 7 days raised urinary oxalate by an average of 43.4% [55]. In 15 people, cranberry tablets for 14 days raised urinary oxalate, more so in tablets with added vitamin C [54]
- **Practical guidance:** The authors of both tablet studies advised people at risk of kidney stones to avoid cranberry tablets [54][55]. If you have had kidney stones, ask your doctor before taking cranberry supplements

### Sugar and Calorie Content

If you have diabetes or are limiting sugar, check the label or choose a capsule.

### Bladder Irritation

During prostate radiotherapy, cranberry capsules did worse than a beetroot-containing placebo for daytime frequency and bladder control [37].

### Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

Some studies suggest cranberry is safe in amounts commonly found in food during pregnancy and breastfeeding, but the evidence is not conclusive for larger amounts [4]. The 2023 Cochrane review found little or no benefit for UTI prevention in pregnant women (RR 1.06, 95% CI 0.75 to 1.50) [6]. Talk to your doctor before using cranberry supplements while pregnant or breastfeeding.

## 6. Drug Interactions

If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

### Warfarin (Coumadin)

NIH says there is conflicting evidence on whether cranberry interacts with warfarin, and advises anyone taking warfarin or another medicine to talk with their health care provider before using cranberry [4].

Key nuances:
- A 2014 review of herb-warfarin interactions rated cranberry among herbs with a major-severity interaction [58]
- A 2021 safety review found no interactions with moderate juice intakes (240 to 480 mL); some reports suggest an interaction needs very large intakes (1 to 2 L of juice a day, or cranberry extracts at 3,000 mg/day), and most cases involved people with serious illness or other medicines [59]
- In a randomised trial of 30 patients on stable warfarin, 240 mL of cranberry juice once daily for 2 weeks did not change warfarin blood levels, and INR differed from placebo only on day 12 [67]
- In 10 men on stable warfarin, cranberry juice at 240 mL twice daily for 7 days did not change prothrombin time [68]
- A 2013 review notes that cranberry juice inhibits CYP enzymes in the laboratory, but controlled trials do not give strong support for an interaction with warfarin at normal juice intakes; large amounts of juice (about 1 to 2 L a day), or juice concentrate in supplements taken for more than 3 to 4 weeks, may alter warfarin's effect, and warfarin dose and effect may then need monitoring [61]

**Practical guidance:** If you take warfarin, talk to your doctor before starting cranberry products, keep intake moderate, and ask whether you need extra INR checks when you start or stop [59][61].

### Statins (Atorvastatin)

A 2014 case report described back pain in a man taking atorvastatin who drank cranberry juice [63].

### Immunosuppressants (Tacrolimus)

A 40-year-old kidney transplant patient taking cranberry extract capsules had low tacrolimus levels; raising the dose did little, and stopping the extract brought levels back to range [64].

### Summary of Known Drug Interactions

| Medication | Interaction | Risk Level | Recommendation |
|-----------|:-:|:-:|---------|
| Warfarin (Coumadin) | Conflicting evidence on raised INR | Rated major in one review; cases mostly at very large intakes | Talk to your doctor first [4][58][59] |
| Atorvastatin (Lipitor) | Back pain in one case report | Low (single case report) | Tell your doctor about any new pain [63] |
| Tacrolimus | Lower drug levels | Low (single case report) | Ask your transplant team first [64] |

### Nutritional Profile of Raw Cranberries (per 100g)

USDA FoodData Central lists the full nutrient profile of raw cranberries [65].

### Practical Notes on Cranberry Consumption

- **Processing reduces bioactive content:** Commercially processed cranberry products held less polyphenol than fresh or home-prepared ones, and anthocyanins broke down more easily than PACs [18]. Freeze-drying caused no detectable loss [18].
- **Juice type matters:** Not-from-concentrate juice held more than twice the PACs of juice from concentrate [18].
- **Added sugar:** Sweetened dried cranberries were low in anthocyanins (7.9 mg/100 g) but kept considerable PACs (64.2 mg/100 g) [18].
