## Current evidence position

Brad has not made a video on this topic; this is a summary of the evidence, not his view. The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements notes that only a few human weight-loss trials exist and all were of poor methodological quality; a 2011 meta-analysis of three of them found 2.47 kg more weight loss than placebo [4][52]. Later pooled analyses disagree: one of 16 trials found a small drop in BMI but no significant change in body weight, while a 2024 umbrella review of five meta-analyses estimated 1.22 kg more weight loss, though one of the meta-analyses it pooled later drew a published comment on calculation and data errors [56][57][69]. The trial that made the extract famous (Vinson, 2012) was retracted in 2014 after the US Federal Trade Commission charged that it was seriously flawed, so its results cannot be relied on [11][12][51][52]. NIH describes the extract as apparently well tolerated but not rigorously studied for safety, and its caffeine can cause side effects depending on the dose and whether it is combined with other stimulants [52].

## Key safety points

- If you are booked for a heart stress test with regadenoson, avoid caffeine for at least 12 hours beforehand, as its label advises; caffeine blunts the test [71].
- If you take theophylline or clozapine, ask your doctor first: caffeine and many medicines can change each other's blood levels, and caffeine raised clozapine levels in a small trial [41][70]. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.
- Combining caffeine with other stimulants can make its side effects worse [52].
- Safety in pregnancy is unknown and caffeine passes into breast milk, so ask your doctor first if you are pregnant or breastfeeding [41]. US paediatricians discourage caffeine for children and teenagers [52].
- Caffeine ranged from 0% to 17% across 54 commercial extracts [53]. Count it toward your daily caffeine; for healthy adults, up to 400 mg a day is not considered a safety concern [52].
- Reported side effects include headaches and urinary tract infections, and caffeine can cause poor sleep, nervousness, jitteriness and an upset stomach. Trials were short, so long-term safety is unknown [41][52].

## 1. Overview

Green coffee bean extract (GCE) is a dietary supplement made from the unroasted beans of *Coffea* species, mainly *Coffea arabica* and *Coffea canephora* (robusta). Green beans hold more chlorogenic acids (CGAs) than roasted beans, because roasting breaks many of them down [2][52]. CGAs are esters of caffeic acid and other hydroxycinnamic acids with quinic acid, and 5-O-caffeoylquinic acid (5-CQA) is the most abundant isomer [5][6].

Marketing for green coffee extracts sets a 50% chlorogenic acid content as the benchmark, but in a 2019 analysis of 54 commercial extracts only 28% met it [53]. Most extracts also contain caffeine: in the same analysis, caffeine ranged from 0% to 17% of the extract [53].

Green coffee bean extract is sold mainly for weight loss. Proposed mechanisms include slower glucose absorption from the gut, effects on glucose and fat metabolism, and antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity [5][6][52]. It has also been tested for effects on blood pressure, blood glucose and blood lipids [7][8].

Its popularity grew after Dr. Mehmet Oz promoted a trial of it on television [27]. That trial, a 2012 crossover study by Vinson and colleagues, was retracted in 2014 by two of its three authors, who said the sponsors could not vouch for the data [11][27][51][52]. The extract's maker, Applied Food Sciences, paid $3.5 million to settle Federal Trade Commission charges [12]. This history still shapes any honest reading of the evidence.

The rest of the evidence comes from a small number of short, small trials and several meta-analyses. Pooled results show small effects on weight and metabolic markers, and reviewers rate the trial quality as poor [4][52]. This article sets out that evidence.

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Standardized Extracts

Green coffee bean extract is sold as branded and generic extracts standardized to differing amounts of chlorogenic acids.

**GCA (Green Coffee Antioxidant)** (Applied Food Sciences): the extract tested in the retracted 2012 Vinson study and at the centre of the FTC enforcement action [11][12].

**Generic extracts**: Many manufacturers sell extracts with varying CGA content. Labels often overstate it. A 2019 laboratory analysis of 54 commercial extracts found an average labeled dose of 233 mg of CGAs, against an average measured dose of 157 mg [53].

### Supplement Forms

Green coffee bean extract is available as capsules, tablets, softgels, and powdered extracts.

### Key Chemical Components

Beyond chlorogenic acids, green coffee bean extract usually contains caffeine, which ranged from none to 17% of the extract in one analysis [53]. 5-CQA is the most abundant chlorogenic acid isomer [6].

### Bioavailability of Chlorogenic Acids

The body absorbs and transforms chlorogenic acids extensively. In a randomised trial in 11 healthy volunteers who drank coffee with three different CGA doses, researchers found 12 metabolites in plasma and 16 in urine, principally sulphates, and to a lesser extent glucuronides, of caffeic, ferulic, dihydrocaffeic and dihydroferulic acids [17]. Urinary metabolites collected for 24 hours accounted for 24%, 25% and 16% of the doses, and the highest dose showed a trend toward lower bioavailability [17].

Ferulic acid, a CGA metabolite, has been identified as a possible mediator of the blood pressure-lowering effect seen in rats, acting through muscarinic acetylcholine receptors [19].

### Quality Concerns

Label accuracy is the main documented quality concern. In the 2019 analysis of 54 commercial extracts, the average product held a little more than half its labeled CGA content, and only 28% reached the 50% CGA content that marketing treats as the standard [53]. Mould toxins have also been found in green coffee supplements [66][67].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Weight Loss

Weight loss is by far the most heavily promoted use of the extract. The evidence consists of a handful of small, short-term RCTs and several meta-analyses, and NIH describes all the human trials as poor in methodological quality [52].

#### Individual Clinical Trials

**Thom (2007)**: In a randomised, double-blind, 12-week trial in 30 overweight people, those drinking chlorogenic acid-enriched instant coffee lost an average of 5.4 kg, against 1.7 kg with normal instant coffee [54].

**Vinson et al. (2012), RETRACTED**: A 22-week crossover trial in 16 overweight adults tested 700 mg and 1,050 mg of GCA in six-week periods. It reported falls of 8.04 kg in body weight, 2.92 kg/m2 in BMI and 4.44% in body fat [11]. The study was retracted in 2014, after the FTC charged that it was so flawed that no reliable conclusions could be drawn from it [12]. The authors retracted it because the sponsors could not vouch for the data [27].

**Roshan et al. (2018)**: In adults with the metabolic syndrome, 400 mg of decaffeinated GCE twice daily for 8 weeks, with an energy-balanced diet, gave a weight change of -2.08 kg versus -0.92 kg with placebo, a difference the authors called marginally significant (P=0.05) [7].

#### Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses

**Onakpoya et al. (2011)**: The first systematic review and meta-analysis pooled 3 RCTs in overweight adults taking 180 or 200 mg a day for 4 to 12 weeks. Weight fell by 2.47 kg more than with placebo (95% CI -4.23 to -0.72). All included trials carried a high risk of bias, and heterogeneity was significant [4][52].

**Gorji et al. (2019)**: A dose-response meta-analysis of 16 RCTs from 13 articles found a small but significant fall in BMI (-0.403 kg/m2, 95% CI -0.800 to -0.005) but no significant change in body weight (-0.585 kg, 95% CI -1.498 to 0.329) or waist circumference. Trials in people with a baseline BMI of 25 kg/m2 or more showed greater falls in weight and BMI, and supplementation for less than 4 weeks had no effect. A corrigendum to the paper was published in 2020 [56].

**Yang et al. (2024)**: An umbrella review pooling 5 meta-analyses estimated a fall in body weight of 1.22 kg (95% CI -1.53 to -0.92), in BMI of 0.48 kg/m2 and in waist circumference of 0.55 cm. Doses of 600 mg a day or less and courses longer than 7 weeks were more likely to reduce weight [57]. One of the meta-analyses it pooled, Asbaghi 2020, later drew a published comment titled "Calculation and data errors require correcting" [68][69].

#### Summary of Weight Loss Evidence

The published, non-retracted evidence suggests that green coffee bean extract may give a modest extra weight loss, estimated at between 1.22 kg and 2.47 kg more than placebo in pooled analyses, while one analysis of 16 trials found no significant change in weight [4][56][57]. This effect is clinically small. Important limitations include:

- Most trials have been small and short, and NIH rates their methodological quality as poor [52]
- The most dramatic results came from the retracted Vinson study [11][51]
- The first meta-analysis found significant heterogeneity between trials [4]
- The trials in that meta-analysis lasted 4 to 12 weeks [52]

### Blood Pressure

Chlorogenic acids may lower blood pressure in part through the metabolite ferulic acid, which acted through muscarinic acetylcholine receptors in rats [19].

**Watanabe et al. (2006)**: In 28 people with mild hypertension, 140 mg a day of CGA from green coffee extract lowered systolic and diastolic blood pressure significantly, with no apparent side effects [8].

**Kozuma et al. (2005)**: In 117 men with mild hypertension given 46 mg, 93 mg or 185 mg of GCE daily, systolic blood pressure fell after 28 days by 5.6 mmHg on the highest dose, against 1.3 mmHg on placebo. Both systolic and diastolic pressure fell in a dose-related manner [55].

**Roshan et al. (2018)**: In adults with the metabolic syndrome, systolic blood pressure fell by 13.76 mmHg on GCE versus 6.56 mmHg on placebo in the 8-week trial [7].

**Animal data**: Single doses (180 to 720 mg/kg) and longer feeding (0.25 to 1% of the diet for 6 weeks) of GCE lowered blood pressure dose-dependently in spontaneously hypertensive rats, and ferulic acid acting through muscarinic acetylcholine receptors was a candidate mechanism [19].

**Summary**: Two meta-analyses found small falls in blood pressure. One, of 9 studies, found systolic pressure fell by 3.093 mmHg and diastolic by 2.170 mmHg [58]. A 2024 analysis of 10 studies with 563 participants found falls of 2.95 mmHg systolic and 2.15 mmHg diastolic, a larger effect in people with high blood pressure, no effect in women, and no change in heart rate [59]. The trials are small and short, and the evidence does not support using the extract in place of blood pressure treatment.

### Blood Sugar and Diabetes

Lab and animal studies suggest chlorogenic acids may affect glucose and lipid metabolism [6], and NIH notes that the extract may work partly by reducing glucose absorption in the gut [52].

**Thom (2007)**: In 12 healthy volunteers, instant coffee enriched with chlorogenic acid reduced glucose absorption by 6.9% compared with control [54].

**Roshan et al. (2018)**: In adults with the metabolic syndrome, GCE lowered fasting blood glucose and insulin resistance (HOMA-IR) compared with placebo in the 8-week trial, but did not change HbA1c [7].

**Chen et al. (2020)**: A meta-analysis of 10 trials found a small fall in fasting blood glucose (-1.791 mg/dl, 95% CI -3.404 to -0.177) and no significant overall change in insulin [60].

**Asbaghi et al. (2020)**: A meta-analysis of 14 trials with 766 participants found falls in fasting glucose (-2.35 mg/dL) and insulin (-0.63 μU/L) [61].

**Summary**: There is preliminary evidence that green coffee bean extract may slightly lower fasting blood glucose [60][61]. The one metabolic syndrome trial found no change in HbA1c [7]. The trials are small and short, and falls this small may not matter clinically. Green coffee bean extract should NOT be used as a substitute for established diabetes treatments.

### Lipid Profile

Several studies have examined the effects of GCE on blood lipids, including cholesterol and triglycerides.

**Meta-analysis data**: A 2020 meta-analysis of 17 effect sizes found falls in total cholesterol (-4.51 mg/dL) and LDL cholesterol (-4.38 mg/dL), a rise in HDL cholesterol (2.63 mg/dL) and no significant change in triglycerides [62]. Another 2020 meta-analysis found a significant fall only in total cholesterol overall (-4.51 mg/dL), with falls in other lipids in some subgroups [61].

**Trial data**: The 8-week metabolic syndrome trial found no difference in lipid profile between GCE and placebo [7].

**Animal data**: In ApoE-/- mice, an atherosclerosis model, GCE did NOT reduce plasma lipids or atherosclerotic lesions despite improvements in other metabolic markers [65].

**Summary**: The lipid-lowering effects of GCE appear modest at best. Falls of 4.51 mg/dL in total cholesterol and 4.38 mg/dL in LDL cholesterol are small and of questionable clinical significance [62].

### Antioxidant and Anti-Inflammatory Effects

Reviews of lab and animal work describe antioxidant effects of chlorogenic acids that may limit oxidative stress, and anti-inflammatory effects through several metabolic pathways [5][6].

In people, a meta-analysis of 8 trials with 330 participants found that GCE lowered the inflammatory marker TNF-alpha but did not change the other inflammatory or liver markers measured [63].

**Important caveat**: The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) concluded in 2011 that evidence does NOT substantiate claims for chlorogenic acids from coffee regarding protection of DNA, proteins, or lipids from oxidative damage, or contributions to maintenance of normal blood glucose concentrations [40].

### Body Composition

Beyond total weight loss, some studies have examined effects on body fat and waist size.

**Waist circumference**: The 2024 umbrella review estimated a fall of 0.55 cm (95% CI -0.80 to -0.31) [57], and in the metabolic syndrome trial waist circumference fell by 2.40 cm on GCE versus 0.66 cm on placebo [7].

**The retracted Vinson study** reported 4.44% body fat decrease, but these data cannot be relied upon [11][51].

### Gut Microbiome

One animal study provides preliminary evidence. In ApoE-/- mice fed an atherogenic diet for 14 weeks, GCE equivalent to 220 mg/kg of CGA shifted gut microbiota and improved fasting glucose and insulin resistance [65]. A single preclinical study cannot be extrapolated to humans.

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Clinical Trial Dosages

Trials have not settled on an ideal dose, and the doses tested have ranged widely:

| Study | Daily Dose | Duration | Extract Type |
|-------|-----------|----------|-------------|
| Onakpoya meta-analysis (2011) | 180 or 200 mg | 4 to 12 weeks | GCE [52] |
| Kozuma (2005) | 46 mg, 93 mg or 185 mg | 28 days | GCE [55] |
| Vinson (2012, retracted) | 700 mg or 1,050 mg | 6-week periods | GCA [11] |
| Roshan (2018) | 400 mg twice daily | 8 weeks | Decaffeinated GCE [7] |

### Practical Dosing Guidance

Based on the available (limited) evidence:

- **Dose range in trials**: Trials in one meta-analysis used 50 mg to 1,200 mg a day for 8 to 12 weeks [63]
- **Dose and effect**: A 2024 umbrella review found weight loss more likely with 600 mg a day or less and courses longer than 7 weeks [57]
- **CGA content**: Labels often overstate chlorogenic acid content [53]
- **Caffeine consideration**: Caffeine content varies from none to 17% of the extract, so people sensitive to caffeine should check the label or choose a decaffeinated extract [52][53]

### Duration

The trials in the first meta-analysis lasted 4 to 12 weeks [52], and the trials in a liver-marker meta-analysis 8 to 12 weeks [63]. There are no long-term safety or efficacy data. The clinical value of a small, short-term weight loss, with no evidence that it lasts, is questionable.

### Important Context

Green coffee bean extract is NOT a substitute for dietary modification and physical activity for weight management. The small effects seen in short-term trials are far smaller than what lifestyle changes or weight-loss medicines can achieve.

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Tolerability

NIH describes green coffee bean extract as apparently well tolerated, but its safety has not been rigorously studied. Reported adverse effects include headaches and urinary tract infections [52]. Two blood pressure trials reported no adverse effects [8][55]. A meta-analysis of 8 trials using 50 mg to 1,200 mg a day for 8 to 12 weeks found no significant change in liver enzymes [63].

In a Slovenian case series, four people had liver injury after taking different fat burners; the products contained ingredients such as green tea extract, garcinia, green coffee beans or spirulina, and one person needed a liver transplant [72]. The authors blamed the fat burners, but the series cannot show which ingredient caused the harm. If your skin or eyes turn yellow or your urine turns dark, stop it and see a doctor promptly.

### Caffeine-Related Side Effects

The main safety concern is the caffeine in extracts that are not decaffeinated; caffeine content ranged from none to 17% of the extract in one analysis [53]. Caffeine-sensitive individuals may experience [41][52]:

- Trouble sleeping
- Nervousness, restlessness or jitteriness
- Shakiness
- Nausea or an upset stomach
- A greater need to urinate
- A faster heart rate and higher blood pressure

### Preclinical Safety Data

In Wistar rats, a single oral dose of 2,000 mg/kg of a decaffeinated extract with 50% chlorogenic acids caused no signs of toxicity or deaths during 14 days of observation. Daily doses of 250, 500, and 1,000 mg/kg for 90 days caused no organ toxicity, and the changes seen in some blood and organ-weight measures were judged toxicologically insignificant [64].

### Long-Term Safety

Human safety data are limited to short-term use (12 weeks or less) [52][63]. Long-term effects are unknown. This is a significant gap in the evidence.

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy and breastfeeding**: It is not known whether green coffee affects pregnancy. Its caffeine passes into breast milk, and large amounts can make breastfed babies fussy or disturb their sleep. Ask your doctor first if you are pregnant or breastfeeding [41].

**Children**: The American Academy of Pediatrics discourages children and teenagers from having caffeine and other stimulants [52].

**Caffeine-sensitive individuals**: Should use extracts that contain caffeine with caution [41].

### Contamination Risks

Mould toxins are the main documented contaminant. A 2013 analysis of 50 supplements containing green coffee bean extract found ochratoxin A in 36% of them [66]. A 2025 analysis found mycotoxins in all 16 supplements tested; it judged the risk low, except that chronic aflatoxin exposure in adults may lead to potential health concerns [67]. Label accuracy is a separate problem: the average extract in a 2019 analysis held a little more than half its labeled CGA content [53].

## 6. Drug Interactions

Most of the extract's known interactions come from its caffeine. Many medicines can change caffeine levels in the blood, and caffeine can change theirs; research on interactions with green coffee itself is incomplete [41].

### Medications That May Interact with Green Coffee Extract

| Drug/Drug Class | Interaction | Clinical Concern |
|----------------|------------|-----------------|
| **Stimulants** (bitter orange, ephedrine) | Additive stimulant effects | Worse caffeine side effects [52] |
| **Clozapine** | Caffeine slowed clozapine breakdown in a small trial | Higher clozapine levels [70] |
| **Regadenoson** (heart stress tests) | Caffeine blocks the adenosine receptors the drug acts on | Weaker test; avoid caffeine for at least 12 hours beforehand [71] |
| **Theophylline** | Caffeine and theophylline can change each other's blood levels | Ask your doctor before combining them [41] |

### Conditions Requiring Caution

The following conditions warrant medical consultation before using green coffee bean extract [41]:

- **Anxiety**
- **Heart rhythm problems or heart disease**: Caffeine may raise heart rate and blood pressure or cause an abnormal heart rhythm

### Caffeine Accumulation

People who already get caffeine from coffee, tea, energy drinks or other supplements should count the caffeine in the extract too. For healthy adults, the FDA and EFSA state that up to 400 mg a day does not pose safety concerns. Decaffeinated forms are available [52].

## 7. Dietary Sources

### Green Coffee vs. Roasted Coffee

Green and roasted coffee beans come from the same plant, but roasting changes their chemistry. Green beans have higher levels of chlorogenic acids than roasted beans [2][52].

### Chlorogenic Acid Content in Coffee Beverages

Brewed coffee is still a dietary source of chlorogenic acids, and coffee drinks contain different mixtures of CGA isomers [5].

### Other Dietary Sources of Chlorogenic Acids

Chlorogenic acids are not unique to coffee. Tea, wine, herbal infusions and some fruit juices also contain them [5].

### Practical Implication

Regular coffee consumption provides chlorogenic acids alongside other bioactive compounds. No trial has tested whether an extract adds anything for people who already drink coffee regularly.

## 8. Regulatory Status and the FTC Enforcement History

### United States

In the US, green coffee bean extract is regulated as a dietary supplement under the Dietary Supplement Health and Education Act (DSHEA) of 1994, which exempts such products from pre-market FDA approval for safety or efficacy [45]. The FDA has not reviewed green coffee extracts for safety and effectiveness, and they cannot be marketed to treat or cure a disease [41].

The 2014 FTC settlement with Applied Food Sciences, a $3.5 million payment over weight-loss claims based on the retracted Vinson study, remains a landmark case in supplement enforcement. The order barred the company from misrepresenting any study and required at least two adequate and well-controlled human clinical tests to support future weight-loss claims [12]. In 2015 promoter Lindsey Duncan and his companies agreed to pay $9 million for consumer redress [73]. In 2016 a court entered a $30 million judgment against the ringleader of the Pure Green Coffee operation, which the FTC had sued in 2014 [13][74]. In 2025 the FTC sent more than $905,000 in refunds to 39,977 people who bought Pure Green Coffee [9].

### European Union

In the EU, green coffee bean extract is permitted in food supplements but lacks authorization for health claims under Regulation (EC) No 1924/2006. EFSA concluded in 2011 that evidence does not substantiate claims for chlorogenic acids regarding oxidative protection or blood glucose maintenance [40].

### The Retracted Study: A Detailed Account

Given the centrality of the retracted Vinson et al. (2012) study and subsequent FTC enforcement to the story of this supplement, a detailed account is warranted.

**The Study**: Published in *Diabetes, Metabolic Syndrome and Obesity*, the study was described as a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial in 16 overweight adults over 22 weeks. It reported a fall in body weight of 8.04 kg on GCA, the extract sold by Applied Food Sciences, which also paid for the study, alongside falls in BMI and body fat [11][12].

**The Investigation**: The US Federal Trade Commission's 2014 complaint alleged the study was so "hopelessly flawed" that no reliable conclusions could be drawn from it. The FTC charged that [12][27]:

- Applied Food Sciences paid researchers in India to run the trial in 2010
- The lead investigator repeatedly altered subjects' weights and other key measurements
- The lead investigator changed the length of the trial
- The lead investigator misstated which subjects were taking placebo or GCA

The company settled the charges [12].

**The Retraction**: In October 2014, authors Vinson and Burnham retracted the paper, saying the sponsors could not vouch for the data [27][51].

**The Consequences**:

- Applied Food Sciences settled with the FTC for $3.5 million [12][13]
- Promoter Lindsey Duncan settled for $9 million in consumer redress [73]
- A 2016 court judgment of $30 million was entered against the ringleader of the Pure Green Coffee operation [74]
- In 2025 the FTC sent more than $905,000 in refunds to 39,977 consumers [9]

This episode is one of the most prominent examples of how industry-funded research, media promotion, and regulatory gaps in the dietary supplement industry can combine to mislead consumers. It reinforced the importance of scrutinizing supplement claims, particularly for weight loss products, and highlighted systemic issues in supplement marketing where preliminary or sponsored research is extrapolated into definitive health benefits.
