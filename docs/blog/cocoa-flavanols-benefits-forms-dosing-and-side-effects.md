## Current view (reviewed 26 October 2026)

Cocoa flavanols were tested in COSMOS, a large trial of more than 21,000 older adults, the same trial that tested a daily multivitamin (Brad's current guidance, 9 Oct 2026). The cocoa extract did not significantly change the trial's main outcome (Brad's current guidance, 9 Oct 2026). Some secondary results were encouraging, and Brad is watching the long-term follow-up closely (Brad's current guidance, 9 Oct 2026). For now, Brad sees no clear benefit he could stand behind (Brad's current guidance, 9 Oct 2026).

## Key safety points

- In 20 people with heart disease taking aspirin and clopidogrel, 30 g a day of dark chocolate for a week strengthened clopidogrel's effect on platelets [27]. If you take clopidogrel or another antiplatelet medicine, tell your doctor before using cocoa extract or eating dark chocolate daily.
- Cocoa products can lower blood pressure slightly [79]. If you take blood pressure medicines, check with your doctor first.
- Theobromine, a stimulant in cocoa, raised blood sugar and insulin after meals at 500 mg a day [19]. If you take diabetes medicines, check your blood sugar and tell your doctor.
- Chocolate caused a short rise in urine oxalate in a small study [77]. If you have had calcium oxalate kidney stones, ask your doctor before eating cocoa or chocolate regularly.
- Dark chocolate labelled dairy free can still contain milk [84]. If you have a milk allergy, take care.
- Cocoa and chocolate sold in the US can contain cadmium and lead [80]. A 2026 assessment judged harm unlikely at typical intakes but possible from lead at high intakes [81].
- Trials found cocoa flavanols well tolerated; stomach upset and nausea were uncommon [7][79].

## 1. Overview

Cocoa flavanols are plant compounds found in cacao beans, the raw material for cocoa powder and chocolate. They belong to the flavonoid family and occur as single units (catechin and epicatechin) or as linked chains called proanthocyanidins [72]. Do not confuse them with flavonols, a separate group that includes quercetin and acts differently.

Processing changes how much flavanol a product holds. "Dutching", or treating cocoa with alkali, darkens the powder and softens its bitterness. In a survey of commercial cocoa powders, natural powders averaged 34.6 mg per gram of powder, lightly alkalized powders 13.8 mg, medium 7.8 mg and heavily alkalized 3.9 mg [3].

Interest began with population studies. The Kuna people of Panama drink large amounts of flavanol-rich cocoa and have very low blood pressure [2]. In the Zutphen Elderly Study of 470 men, those in the highest third of cocoa intake had a relative risk of 0.50 (95% CI 0.32 to 0.78) for cardiovascular death compared with the lowest third [4]. Studies like these cannot show cause and effect. The largest trial, COSMOS, randomized 21,442 older US adults to 500 mg of cocoa flavanols a day, including 80 mg of epicatechin, or placebo. Over a median of 3.6 years, the extract did not significantly reduce total cardiovascular events, its main outcome, though cardiovascular deaths, a secondary outcome, were lower [5].

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) concluded in 2012 that cocoa flavanols help arteries widen normally (endothelium-dependent vasodilation), which contributes to normal blood flow, and set 200 mg a day as the amount needed [6].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Sources of Cocoa Flavanols

Cocoa extract capsules and powders give a set dose with few calories; COSMOS used one [5]. Natural (non-alkalized) cocoa powder holds far more flavanol than Dutched powder [3]. Dark chocolate carries the calories of cocoa butter and sugar. White chocolate is made from cocoa butter [32] and has no cocoa solids. Section 7 lists measured amounts.

### Key Compounds

The main flavanols in cocoa are catechin and epicatechin, plus chains such as procyanidin B2, procyanidin C1 and cinnamtannin A2 [72]. The COSMOS extract supplied 500 mg of flavanols a day, including 80 mg of epicatechin [5].

### Factors Affecting Bioavailability

**Dutching (alkaline processing):** Flavanol content fell in a straight line as alkalization increased [3].

**Interaction with polyphenol oxidase-containing foods:** Some fruits contain polyphenol oxidase (PPO), an enzyme that breaks down flavanols. In 8 healthy men, flavanols blended into a banana smoothie gave peak blood levels of flavanol metabolites 84% lower than the same flavanols in a capsule. A mixed berry smoothie, low in PPO, gave levels similar to the capsule. In a second study of 11 people, blood levels still fell when the flavanols were swallowed with a banana drink they had not been mixed into [10].

**Caffeine and theobromine content:** Cocoa contains caffeine and theobromine, both stimulants of the methylxanthine family; theobromine is present in larger amounts [57]. These compounds have their own effects (see Sections 3 and 5).

### Standardized Cocoa Extract vs. Dark Chocolate

Most trials, including COSMOS, used standardized cocoa extracts with a known flavanol dose [5]. Cocoa powders vary widely in flavanol content [3], and the label rarely gives it. A standardized extract avoids the calories, fat and sugar of chocolate.

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Cardiovascular Disease and Mortality

**COSMOS trial:** COSMOS randomized 21,442 US adults (women aged 65 and over, men aged 60 and over) without major heart disease to cocoa extract (500 mg flavanols, including 80 mg epicatechin, a day) or placebo. Over a median of 3.6 years, 410 people taking cocoa extract and 456 taking placebo had a cardiovascular event (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.78 to 1.02), so the main outcome was not significantly reduced. Among secondary outcomes, the hazard ratio for cardiovascular death was 0.73 (95% CI 0.54 to 0.98), which the authors describe as a 27% reduction. Hazard ratios were 0.87 (95% CI 0.66 to 1.16) for heart attack, 0.91 (95% CI 0.70 to 1.17) for stroke and 0.89 (95% CI 0.77 to 1.03) for death from any cause. An analysis limited to people who kept taking their capsules found fewer total cardiovascular events (HR 0.85, 95% CI 0.72 to 0.99). The trial found no safety concerns, and the authors call for more research [5].

**Inflammatory mechanism:** In blood samples from 598 COSMOS participants taken at baseline, year 1 and year 2, cocoa extract lowered high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hsCRP), a marker of inflammation, compared with placebo: the yearly percentage change differed by -8.4% (95% CI -14.1% to -2.3%). It raised interferon-gamma, with a 6.8% (95% CI 1.5% to 12.2%) difference in yearly percentage change, a finding the authors say needs more study, and did not significantly change the other markers [12].

### Blood Pressure

**Short-term trials:** A Cochrane review of 35 trials in 1,804 mainly healthy adults found that flavanol-rich cocoa products lowered systolic and diastolic blood pressure by 1.76 mmHg each over 2 to 18 weeks. Systolic pressure fell by 4 mmHg in people with high blood pressure, with no significant change in people with normal pressure, though this subgroup difference was of borderline significance and needs confirming. The authors rated the evidence moderate quality [79].

**Short-term effects in healthy adults:** In 100 healthy adults aged 35 to 60, a drink with 450 mg of cocoa flavanols taken twice a day for one month lowered systolic blood pressure by 4.4 mmHg and diastolic by 3.9 mmHg compared with a flavanol-free drink [11].

**In people without hypertension:** In 8,905 COSMOS participants free of hypertension at the start (mean age 71.1 years), cocoa extract made no difference to new hypertension over a median of 3.4 years (HR 0.96, 95% CI 0.88 to 1.05). In people whose systolic pressure started below 120 mmHg, it reduced new hypertension (HR 0.76, 95% CI 0.64 to 0.90), which the authors describe as a 24% lower risk, an effect that appeared at year 2. There was no benefit for those starting at 120 to 139 mmHg (HR 1.05, 95% CI 0.93 to 1.18) [13].

**Response varies between people:** In 11 healthy young adults with normal blood pressure, 862 mg of cocoa flavanols lowered 12-hour systolic pressure by 1.4 mmHg. The effect peaked during the first 3 hours and again 8 hours after intake, and responses varied widely between people and from day to day [14].

**In mild hypertension:** In a crossover trial in 22 mildly hypertensive adults aged 33 to 64, eating 49 g of dark chocolate daily for 8 weeks left blood pressure and arterial stiffness unchanged [16].

**Synthesis:** Cocoa flavanols may lower blood pressure by a small amount in short trials [79]. In COSMOS, cocoa extract did not prevent hypertension overall [13].

### Cholesterol and Lipid Profile

**Short-term improvements in healthy adults:** In the same trial of 100 healthy adults taking 450 mg of cocoa flavanols twice daily for one month, total cholesterol fell by 0.20 mmol/L and LDL cholesterol by 0.17 mmol/L, while HDL cholesterol rose by 0.10 mmol/L [11].

**Dose-dependent effects:** In 48 healthy adults given 1, 2 or 4 g of flavanol-rich cocoa a day for 4 weeks, the 4 g group (about 220 mg flavanols) had lower total cholesterol, LDL, oxidized LDL and triglycerides, and higher HDL, compared with baseline [17].

**HDL improvements in women:** In 24 young women, 12.7 g of natural cocoa a day for 4 weeks was linked with an 18% rise in HDL cholesterol and a 60% fall in endothelial microparticles, a marker of blood vessel damage, with further changes in women with obesity [18].

**Theobromine contribution:** In 44 healthy adults with low HDL, 500 mg of theobromine a day for 4 weeks lowered LDL cholesterol by 0.21 mmol/L [19].

**Limitation:** A meta-analysis of 19 trials found that cocoa flavanols lowered triglycerides by 0.10 mmol/L and raised HDL by 0.06 mmol/L, but the trials lasted 2 to 52 weeks, and the authors call for large long-term trials [28].

### Vascular Function and Blood Flow

**Endothelial function:** The trial of 100 healthy adults also found that cocoa flavanols raised flow-mediated dilation, a measure of how well arteries widen, by 1.2% over control [11]. EFSA's health claim on blood flow requires 200 mg a day [6].

**Peripheral artery disease:** In 20 people with peripheral artery disease (PAD), 40 g of dark chocolate (over 85% cocoa) raised maximal walking distance by 11% and walking time by 15% two hours later; milk chocolate did nothing [20]. In a six-month pilot trial in 44 people with PAD, a daily cocoa drink with 15 g of cocoa and 75 mg of epicatechin improved 6-minute walk distance by 42.6 m compared with placebo when measured 2.5 hours after a drink. Measured 24 hours after a drink, the gain of 18.0 m was not significant [21].

**Nitric oxide mechanism:** Cocoa flavanols increase nitric oxide production in healthy people, which may explain some of their effects on blood vessels [2].

### Atrial Fibrillation

**Observational evidence:** In a Danish study of 55,502 adults followed for a median of 13.5 years, people eating chocolate 1 to 3 times a month had a lower rate of atrial fibrillation than those eating it less than once a month (HR 0.90, 95% CI 0.82 to 0.98). The figures were HR 0.83 (95% CI 0.74 to 0.92) for once a week and HR 0.80 (95% CI 0.71 to 0.91) for 2 to 6 times a week. The authors note that other factors could explain the link [22].

**COSMOS trial:** A COSMOS report on cocoa flavanols and atrial fibrillation was published in 2025 [23].

### Heart Failure

A meta-analysis of 5 prospective studies with 106,109 people found that eating chocolate less than 7 times a week was linked with lower heart failure risk than eating none (HR 0.86, 95% CI 0.82 to 0.91). Eating it 7 or more times a week was not (HR 0.94, 95% CI 0.80 to 1.09) [24].

### Blood-Thinning and Clotting Effects

Cocoa flavanols have short-lived effects on platelets, but COSMOS found no reduction in blood clots.

**Acute platelet effects:** In 18 healthy men, 50 g of 90% cocoa chocolate lengthened one measure of platelet clotting time four hours later, linked with blood levels of flavanol breakdown products [25].

**No long-term antithrombotic benefit:** In COSMOS, cocoa extract did not significantly reduce venous blood clots over a median of 3.5 years (HR 0.88, 95% CI 0.72 to 1.08), with similar results for deep vein thrombosis and pulmonary embolism [26]. In a 12-week trial in healthy adults, up to 2000 mg of cocoa flavanols a day did not change platelet function [7].

**Interaction with antiplatelet medications:** In 20 people with stable coronary artery disease taking aspirin 81 mg and clopidogrel 75 mg a day, 30 g a day of 65% dark chocolate for one week strengthened the effect of clopidogrel but not aspirin: platelet reactivity on clopidogrel fell by 11.9% (95% CI 5.7% to 18.0%) [27]. See Section 6.

### Blood Sugar, Insulin Resistance, and Diabetes

Results depend on the dose and the people studied.

**Insulin sensitivity:** A meta-analysis of 19 trials with 1,131 people found that cocoa flavanols improved insulin sensitivity, lowering fasting insulin by 2.33 μIU/mL and HOMA-IR (an insulin resistance score) by 0.93 [28].

**No benefit in insulin-resistant women:** In 32 premenopausal women with overweight or obesity and insulin resistance, a cocoa drink with 609 mg of flavanols, taken twice a day for 4 weeks, worked no better on insulin sensitivity than a drink with 13 mg [29].

**No additional benefit when diabetes is already treated:** In 42 people with type 2 diabetes and hypertension on stable treatment, 2.5 g of cocoa rich in flavanols each day for 12 weeks did not change blood pressure, blood sugar or blood fats [30].

**No reduction in diabetes risk (COSMOS):** In 18,381 COSMOS participants without diabetes, cocoa extract did not lower the rate of new type 2 diabetes over a median of 3.5 years (HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.91 to 1.20) [31].

**Dark chocolate and appetite regulation:** In 14 healthy postmenopausal women, people later ate 1,355 kJ after dark chocolate, against 1,693 and 1,842 kJ after milk and white (each dose 2,099 kJ). Blood sugar and insulin rose more after milk and white chocolate [32].

**Modest body composition changes:** In 132 postmenopausal women, adding 10 g of 99% cocoa chocolate a day for 6 months reduced body fat mass by 0.63 kg compared with no chocolate, without changing weight [33].

**Theobromine caution for blood sugar:** In the 4-week trial of 500 mg of theobromine a day, blood sugar and insulin rose more after a meal than on placebo [19].

### Memory and Cognition

The largest trials found no long-term cognitive benefit, with some small short-term effects under demanding conditions.

**Long-term effects: no benefit in healthy older adults:**

In COSMOS-Mind, 2,262 older adults (mean age 73) took cocoa extract (500 mg flavanols a day) or placebo for three years. Cocoa extract had no effect on global cognition [34]. A later analysis found that it did not reduce new cases of MCI (mild cognitive impairment) or dementia, though the study had low statistical power for this [35].

In the CANN trial, 259 older adults with memory complaints took DHA-rich fish oil plus flavanol-rich dark chocolate (500 mg flavanols a day) or placebo for a year. The combination did not improve cognition or brain structure, and executive function declined in the combined group [36]. The trial tested the two together, so it cannot show the effect of cocoa alone.

In a trial of 100 healthy older adults aged 65 to 75, 50 g of dark chocolate with 410 mg of flavanols a day for eight weeks gave no more cognitive benefit than chocolate with 86 mg [37].

In 140 postmenopausal women, 10 g of 99% cocoa chocolate a day for six months slightly improved one timed test of mental flexibility and speed; attention, verbal memory and working memory did not change [76].

**Short-term effects: limited and inconsistent:**

In 40 young adults, 250 mg of flavanols improved self-reported mental fatigue and one arithmetic task on the day of intake, with no other effects [38].

In healthy young adults, flavanols led to faster brain oxygen responses and better performance only on the most demanding cognitive task [39].

In 18 middle-aged adults, chocolate with 635.0 mg of cacao polyphenols kept accuracy steady across a second demanding task, while accuracy fell after chocolate with 211.7 mg [40].

In 36 healthy adults, drinks with 415 or 623 mg of flavanols did not improve cognitive control or response inhibition [41].

**Synthesis:** The evidence does not support cocoa flavanols as a cognitive enhancer for healthy older adults. A 2017 review of smaller studies called the research preliminary [42]. In COSMOS-Mind, cocoa extract did not benefit cognition over 3 years [34].

### Mood and Stress

**Cortisol reduction:** In 26 adults, 25 g a day of dark chocolate with 500 mg of flavonoids for four weeks lowered salivary cortisol, but mood scores did not differ from the control chocolate [43].

**Negative mood reduction:** In healthy adults aged 20 to 30, 30 g a day of 85% dark chocolate for three weeks reduced negative mood, while 70% chocolate did not. The 85% chocolate also increased gut bacterial diversity [44].

**Middle-aged women:** In 60 women aged 40 to 60 with fatigue, a cacao drink with 240 mg of flavanols a day for 8 weeks lowered negative mood scores and raised vigour compared with placebo, but did not change fatigue scale scores [8].

**Epidemiological association with depression:** In a US survey of 13,626 adults, people who reported eating dark chocolate had lower odds of clinically relevant depressive symptoms (OR 0.30, 95% CI 0.21 to 0.72). A survey like this cannot show which came first [45].

### Exercise and Physical Performance

**In young adults:** In 9 moderately trained men, 40 g of dark chocolate a day for two weeks improved a 2-minute cycling time trial compared with white chocolate. The authors suggest dark chocolate "may be an effective ergogenic aid for short-duration moderate intensity exercise" [46].

**In older adults:** In a two-phase trial (60 adults aged 55 to 70, then 74 aged 65 to 90), a flavonoid-rich cocoa drink once a day for up to 12 weeks improved physical performance, frailty measures and markers of oxidative stress compared with low-flavonoid cocoa [47]. In a placebo-controlled trial in 68 healthy adults aged 55 to 79, 500 mg of flavanols twice a day for 30 days raised peak oxygen uptake by 190 ml per minute and lowered resting systolic pressure by 5.4 mmHg [48].

### Skin

**Wrinkle reduction (modest):** In a 12-week trial in people with aged skin, cacao powder improved skin hydration and reduced wrinkles; lab tests found it raised collagen production in skin cells [49].

**Skin elasticity:** In Korean women aged 43 to 86 with sun-damaged skin, a cocoa drink with 320 mg of flavanols a day for 24 weeks reduced wrinkle roughness by 8.7 percentage points more than placebo and improved elasticity, with no change in skin hydration [50].

### Vision

**Conflicting short-term evidence:** In 30 young adults, contrast sensitivity was higher about 2 hours after a dark chocolate bar than after a milk chocolate bar, and visual acuity was slightly better [51]. A double-blind trial in 22 people found no change in retinal blood flow or vision 2 hours after 20 g of dark chocolate with 400 mg of flavanols, compared with milk chocolate [52].

**No long-term protection against macular degeneration:** COSMOS found no overall effect of cocoa extract on age-related macular degeneration (AMD) over a median of 3.6 years (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.71 to 1.08). The risk was lower in the first 2 years (HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.59 to 1.01), with no benefit after that (HR 1.06, 95% CI 0.76 to 1.50) [53].

### Cancer

**No demonstrated benefit:** In the Women's Health Initiative (114,281 women followed for a mean of 14.8 years), chocolate candy intake was not linked with total invasive cancer or breast cancer. Women who ate a 1 oz serving at least 1.5 times a week had a higher risk of colorectal cancer than those eating it less than once a month (HR 1.18, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.35), which the authors suggest may reflect the extra weight linked with eating chocolate often [54]. Chocolate is not a cancer-prevention food.

### Intestinal Health

Cocoa flavanols may act as a prebiotic, feeding helpful gut bacteria. In pigs fed cocoa powder for 27 days, Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium species increased [55]. In a crossover trial in 22 healthy adults, a drink with 494 mg of flavanols a day for 4 weeks increased bifidobacteria and lactobacilli and lowered clostridia, compared with a drink with 23 mg [56].

### Caffeine and Theobromine Effects

Caffeine and theobromine block adenosine receptors and can improve mood and alertness [57]. In two European cohorts (509 and 1,160 people), higher blood theobromine was linked with slower ageing on DNA markers, a link not explained by other cocoa or coffee compounds [58]. A link like this does not show that theobromine slows ageing.

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Clinical Trial Doses

COSMOS used 500 mg of flavanols a day, 80 mg of it epicatechin, taken once a day for a median of 3.6 years [5]. Across 19 trials in one meta-analysis, doses ranged from 166 to 2110 mg a day [28].

### Practical Dosing by Indication

No dose has been shown to prevent heart disease or any other condition. These are the doses trials used.

**Cardiovascular risk reduction:** COSMOS used 500 mg a day and did not significantly reduce total cardiovascular events [5]. EFSA's blood flow claim is based on 200 mg a day [6].

**Blood lipid support:** 4 g of cocoa with about 220 mg of flavanols a day [17], or 450 mg of flavanols twice daily [11].

**Blood sugar and insulin sensitivity:** No reliable dose. About 1.2 g of flavanols a day left insulin sensitivity unchanged in women with insulin resistance [29].

**Physical performance in older adults:** 500 mg twice a day for 30 days [48].

### How to Get Cocoa Flavanols

**Cocoa extract supplements:** The most consistent dose, without the calories, sugar or fat of chocolate. COSMOS used a standardized extract [5].

**Non-Dutched cocoa powder:** Natural powder holds the most flavanol; avoid powders labelled "processed with alkali" [3].

**High-flavanol dark chocolate:** One trial compared a high-flavanol (410 mg) and a low-flavanol (86 mg) dark chocolate [37]; chocolate adds calories and fat.

### Important Dosing Considerations

- **Do not mix with banana or other high-PPO fruit.** A banana smoothie cut peak flavanol levels by 84% [10].
- **No effective dose is established** for any condition. Trials have used 166 to 2110 mg a day [28].
- **The EFSA claim** is based on 200 mg of flavanols a day. EFSA estimated that 2.5 g of cocoa powder or 10 g of dark chocolate, both high in flavanols, could provide this [6].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Safety

In a 12-week trial in healthy adults, up to 2000 mg of cocoa flavanols a day was well tolerated; blood pressure and platelet function did not change significantly [7]. COSMOS found no safety concerns [5].

### Gastrointestinal Effects

In the Cochrane review of cocoa and blood pressure, stomach complaints and nausea were reported by 1% of people taking cocoa and 0.4% of people in control groups [79].

### Caffeine and Theobromine Side Effects

Cocoa contains caffeine and theobromine [57].

In 80 healthy adults, theobromine had little effect at 250 mg but worsened mood at higher doses and raised heart rate as the dose rose to 1,000 mg [60]. In a 4-week trial, 500 mg of theobromine a day raised blood sugar and insulin after meals [19].

### Heavy Metals

Cocoa and chocolate can contain cadmium and lead from the environment. In US retail products, cadmium ranged from 0.004 to 3.15 mg/kg and lead from undetectable to 0.38 mg/kg; both rose with the percentage of cocoa solids, and cadmium was higher in products said to come from Latin America [80]. A 2026 risk assessment of US chocolate bars judged harm from heavy metals unlikely at typical intakes [81]. Under high-end intake assumptions, the same assessment found possible risks from lead and arsenic, though arsenic was measured in only two samples [81].

### Kidney Stones

Chocolate is rich in oxalic acid [77]. In 6 men, 50 g and 100 g of chocolate raised urine oxalate 2 to 4 hours later, to 235% and 289% of fasting levels; the authors judged this short rise an important factor in calcium oxalate stones in people at risk of them [77]. A 2025 study of 7 people found no significant rise in 24-hour urine oxalate after 100 g of dark chocolate [78]. If you have had kidney stones, ask your doctor whether to limit cocoa and chocolate.

### Acne

In a small double-blind trial, 13 men aged 18 to 35 with a history of acne swallowed capsules of unsweetened cocoa. Acne lesions increased at day 4 and day 7, and more cocoa was weakly linked with more lesions [62].

### Migraine

Chocolate has long been blamed as a migraine trigger. A 2014 review found that in double-blind studies, chocolate was no more likely than placebo to trigger a headache in people with migraine, and concluded that advice to avoid chocolate lacks a reliable scientific basis [65]. In rats, cocoa extract and a cocoa-rich diet reduced inflammatory signals in the trigeminal nerve, which is involved in migraine [66][67].

### Allergies and Contaminants

**Nickel:** A Belgian survey found high nickel levels in chocolate and chocolate spreads [82]. In a related exposure study, dark chocolate supplied 15% of adults' nickel intake from the foods studied, and the authors advise people sensitised to nickel to limit high-nickel foods [83]. Whether dietary nickel triggers skin reactions in nickel-allergic people is still debated [68]. In a human study, milk, coffee, tea, orange juice and vitamin C each reduced the absorption of a nickel dose [69].

**Milk in dark chocolate:** In a US survey of 88 dark chocolate bars, 15% of those labelled dairy free or lactose free contained milk, all at more than 1,000 ppm. The authors advise people with food allergies to be cautious when buying dark chocolate [84].

**Ochratoxin A:** All 32 cocoa powders and 28 chocolates tested in Canada contained this fungal toxin. Six powders and one chocolate were above limits the European Union had previously considered [70].

### Toxicity to Pets

Theobromine is safe for people at normal intakes but not for other mammals, pets included [57]. Keep chocolate away from dogs and cats.

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Antiplatelet and Anticoagulant Medications

Dark chocolate may strengthen the effect of clopidogrel. In 20 heart patients taking clopidogrel and aspirin, 30 g of 65% dark chocolate a day for one week reduced platelet reactivity on clopidogrel by 11.9%, with no effect on aspirin [27]. If you take clopidogrel or another antiplatelet medicine, tell your doctor about regular cocoa or dark chocolate. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

### Caffeine-Sensitive Medications

Cocoa contains caffeine and theobromine [57]. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

### Blood Pressure Medications

Cocoa flavanols can lower blood pressure slightly [79], which could add to the effect of blood pressure medicines. If you take blood pressure medicines, check with your doctor first.

### Blood Sugar Medications

Cocoa flavanols may improve insulin sensitivity [28], which could add to diabetes medicines. Theobromine raised blood sugar after meals [19]. Monitor blood sugar if combining regular cocoa supplementation with diabetes medications.

### Practical Tips for Maximizing Flavanol Intake

- **Choose natural (non-Dutched) cocoa powder.** Look for products NOT labeled "Processed with alkali" [3].
- **Do not blend cocoa with banana.** Banana is high in PPO, which can lower flavanol absorption [10].
- **Berry smoothies are fine.** Flavanol levels after a mixed berry smoothie matched those after a capsule [10].
- **For a known dose, choose a standardized cocoa extract** rather than food-grade cocoa products [5].
