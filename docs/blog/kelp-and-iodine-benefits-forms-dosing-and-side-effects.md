## Key safety points

- Amiodarone is 37.3% iodine by weight and can cause hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism, and lithium taken with iodide may produce hypothyroidism [78][79]. High-dose iodine with antithyroid medicines such as methimazole could cause hypothyroidism [1]. If you take any of these, check with your doctor before adding iodine or kelp.
- Too much iodine can cause hypothyroidism, hyperthyroidism, goiter and thyroiditis [1][53]. People with thyroid disease, such as Hashimoto's disease, Graves' disease or nontoxic thyroid nodules, may react at intakes safe for others [1][49][74].
- Potassium iodide with ACE inhibitors or potassium-sparing diuretics can raise blood potassium (hyperkalemia) [1].
- In pregnancy, the American Thyroid Association recommends 150 mcg/day of iodine as potassium iodide [1]. Supplements of 200 mcg a day or more were linked to raised TSH in one study [63], and maternal intake of 12.5 mg a day likely caused congenital hypothyroidism in three infants [76].
- The American Thyroid Association advises against supplements with over 500 mcg of iodine a day [74]. The adult upper limit (UL) is 1,100 mcg from all sources, and 900 mcg for ages 14-18 years [1].
- Kelp's iodine content varies widely, and some kelp contains inorganic arsenic [8][9]. One woman had possible arsenic poisoning from kelp [75].
- On a blood thinner such as warfarin, ask your doctor or pharmacist before taking kelp; its fucoidans were anticoagulant in an experimental study [46].

## 1. Overview

Iodine is a trace element that is an essential component of the thyroid hormones thyroxine (T4) and triiodothyronine (T3) [1][2]. These hormones regulate many critical biochemical reactions, including protein synthesis and enzymatic activity, and are the principal determinants of metabolic rate throughout the body [1][2]. Thyroid hormones are also required for proper skeletal and central nervous system development in fetuses and infants [1].

Thyroid function is primarily regulated by thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH), secreted by the pituitary gland. TSH controls thyroid hormone production and secretion, thereby maintaining the balance between hypothyroidism and hyperthyroidism [1]. TSH secretion stimulates thyroidal uptake of iodine and promotes the synthesis and release of T3 and T4. In the absence of sufficient iodine, TSH levels remain elevated, leading to goiter, an enlargement of the thyroid gland that reflects the body's attempt to trap more iodine from the circulation [1][2]. Beyond thyroid function, iodine appears to play a role in immune response and may have beneficial effects on mammary dysplasia and fibrocystic breast disease [2].

The iodine-replete healthy adult has approximately 15-20 mg of iodine in the body, 70%-80% of which is contained in the thyroid gland [1]. Iodine in food and supplements is present in several chemical forms: sodium and potassium salts, inorganic iodine (I2), iodate, and iodide (the reduced form). Iodide is quickly and almost completely absorbed in the stomach and duodenum [1][2]. Iodate is reduced in the gastrointestinal tract and absorbed as iodide. When iodide enters the circulation, the thyroid gland concentrates it for hormone synthesis, and most of the remaining amount is excreted in the urine [2].

Kelp is the name for the large brown seaweeds of the order Laminariales, which are the strongest iodine accumulators among living organisms [7]. US food rules define kelp as the dried, ground product of *Macrocystis pyrifera*, *Laminaria digitata*, *Laminaria saccharina* and *Laminaria cloustoni* [77]. *Laminaria digitata* can be very high in iodine [9], and some samples of it contain significant amounts of inorganic arsenic, the most toxic form [8].

Seaweeds are common in Asian cooking and are now part of international cuisine [9]. Their iodine content varies enormously: a survey of 12 species found from 16 mcg/g in nori to over 8,165 mcg/g in one sample of kelp granules (a salt substitute) made from *Laminaria digitata* [9].

Iodine deficiency remains the most common cause of preventable intellectual disability worldwide [1][4]. While the general US population has adequate iodine intake thanks to salt iodization programs introduced in the 1920s, subgroups remain at risk, particularly pregnant women, vegans, people who avoid dairy and seafood, and those who skip iodized salt [1][2][11].

Median urinary iodine concentrations of 100-199 mcg/L in children and non-pregnant adults, 150-249 mcg/L in pregnant women, and >100 mcg/L in lactating women indicate adequate iodine status [1][12]. Values below 100 mcg/L in non-pregnant individuals indicate insufficient intake, though severe deficiency is not classified until levels fall below 20 mcg/L.

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Potassium Iodide

Potassium iodide (KI) and sodium iodide are the usual forms of iodine in dietary supplements [1]. A small study found that potassium iodide is almost completely absorbed (96.4%) [1][13]. Many multivitamin/multimineral supplements contain iodine, often at a dose of 150 mcg [1][14]. The American Thyroid Association names potassium iodide in its advice for pregnant and lactating women, and FDA has approved potassium iodide to block the thyroid in radiation emergencies [1][15][16].

### Sodium Iodide

Similar to potassium iodide in terms of bioavailability. Used less frequently in dietary supplements than potassium iodide. Both forms provide iodide ions that are rapidly absorbed in the gastrointestinal tract [2].

### Kelp-Derived Iodine

Kelp supplements are an alternative iodine source, but iodine from seaweed may be less bioavailable than from potassium iodide. In a study of healthy non-pregnant women in the UK who ate little dairy or seafood, 33% of the iodine in an *Ascophyllum nodosum* seaweed was bioavailable, against 59% for potassium iodide [17]. A study in Marseille and Brussels of two other seaweeds, *Laminaria hyperborea* and *Gracilaria verrucosa*, found higher figures, from 61.5% to 101% depending on the seaweed and the group tested [13].

Iodine content also varies widely between seaweeds, between harvests and with storage and cooking [9], which makes dosing less consistent than with potassium iodide.

### Molecular Iodine (I2)

Molecular (elemental) iodine has been studied in the context of fibrocystic breast disease at doses of about 1,500 to 6,000 mcg/day [1]. This form is not commonly found in standard supplements, but in one trial it reduced breast pain, tenderness and nodularity at the higher doses [1][37].

### Iodized Salt

In the United States, iodized table salt contains approximately 45 mcg iodine per gram of salt (roughly 78 mcg per 1/4 teaspoon) [1][2][20]. Measured samples average 47.5-50.7 mcg iodine/g salt [1][20]. However, most salt intake in the US comes from processed foods, and food manufacturers almost always use non-iodized salt [1][2]. Specialty salts (sea salt, kosher salt, Himalayan salt, and fleur de sel) are typically not iodized and provide virtually no iodine [1][2].

### Iodate vs. Iodide in Foods

Iodized salt in the US uses potassium iodide or cuprous iodide [1][21]. The WHO recommends potassium iodate for salt iodization because it is more stable, particularly in warm, damp or tropical climates [1][12].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Preventing and Treating Iodine Deficiency

Iodine supplementation effectively prevents and treats iodine deficiency, which causes hypothyroidism, goiter, impaired mental function, and, during pregnancy, severe developmental consequences including cretinism [1][2][10].

**Case report of kelp treating deficiency:** A 9-year-old American child developed severe hypothyroidism from iodine deficiency caused by a restrictive diet for eosinophilic esophagitis. A kelp supplement quickly corrected both [24].

**Seaweed for mild deficiency:** In the same UK study of women with low iodine intakes, 0.5 g of *Ascophyllum nodosum* seaweed a day for 2 weeks raised median urinary iodine from 78 to 140 mcg/L [17]. TSH also rose, from 1.5 to 2.1 mIU/L, and two women had TSH above the normal range with normal free thyroxine [17].

**Impact of deficiency on thyroid:** If iodine intake falls below approximately 10-20 mcg/day, hypothyroidism occurs, frequently accompanied by goiter [1][2]. Under normal conditions, TSH secretion increases when iodine intake drops below about 100 mcg/day, stimulating thyroidal iodine uptake from the blood [1]. However, very low iodine intakes can reduce thyroid hormone production even in the presence of elevated TSH.

### Fetal and Infant Development

Iodine sufficiency during pregnancy is critically important for proper fetal development. During early pregnancy, when the fetal thyroid gland is still developing, the fetus depends entirely on maternal T4, and therefore on maternal iodine intake [1]. Production of T4 increases by approximately 50% during pregnancy, requiring a concomitant increase in maternal iodine intake [1].

**Meta-analysis of IQ effects:** A meta-analysis of 6,180 mother-child pairs from three birth cohorts in the Netherlands, Spain, and the United Kingdom found a curved link between maternal iodine status and child verbal IQ at 1.5-8 years of age, but not nonverbal IQ, and only for iodine measured up to 14 weeks of pregnancy [27]. A low maternal iodine level (a urinary iodine-to-creatinine ratio below 150 mcg/g) was not significantly linked to lower verbal IQ (-0.6 point; 95% CI -1.3 to 0.1) [27].

**Severe deficiency consequences:** In pregnant women, severe iodine deficiency can badly impair the brain development and growth of the fetus, and can cause miscarriage and stillbirth [1]. Chronic severe deficiency in utero causes cretinism, characterized by intellectual disability, deaf mutism, motor spasticity, stunted growth, delayed sexual maturation, and other neurological abnormalities [1][26].

**ADHD risk:** In a small Italian cohort, ADHD was diagnosed in 11 of 16 children of mothers from a moderately iodine-deficient area and in none of 11 children from a marginally iodine-sufficient area [28].

**Supplementation trial results:** However, two randomized clinical trials providing iodine from early pregnancy to delivery (150 or 200 mcg/day as potassium iodide) found no statistically significant effect on child cognitive, language, or motor scores assessed at ages 1.5-6 years [1][29][30][31]. The authors of a 2020 meta-analysis concluded that good-quality evidence for supplementation in areas of mild-to-moderate deficiency is insufficient, and called for trials in moderately deficient women [31].

**Breastfeeding:** Breast milk contains iodine, though concentrations vary based on maternal iodine levels. A study of 57 healthy lactating women from the Boston area found a median breast milk iodine content of 155 mcg/L, and calculated that 47% of the women may have been providing their infants insufficient iodine through breast milk alone [32].

**Professional recommendations:** The American Thyroid Association recommends that women who are planning a pregnancy, currently pregnant, or lactating supplement with 150 mcg/day iodine as potassium iodide [1][15]. The American Academy of Pediatrics similarly recommends at least 150 mcg iodine supplementation plus use of iodized salt for pregnant and lactating women [1][33].

### Cognitive Function in Children

Chronic moderate to severe iodine deficiency in children reduces IQ by approximately 12-13.5 points [1]. A 2004 Cochrane Review concluded that iodine supplementation in children living in areas of deficiency appears to positively affect both physical and mental development and decrease mortality, although results were not always significant [1][34]. In 1,221 schoolchildren in southern Europe, IQ was significantly higher in those with urinary iodine above 100 mcg/L [72].

**Randomized trial in mildly deficient children:** In a 2009 RCT, 184 children aged 10-13 years in New Zealand with a median urinary iodine concentration of 63 mcg/L received either iodine supplements (150 mcg/day) or placebo for 28 weeks [35]. Iodine supplementation improved urinary iodine status (median 145 mcg/L post-treatment) and significantly improved measures of perceptual reasoning and overall cognitive score compared to placebo. These findings suggest that correcting mild deficiency could improve certain components of cognition [1].

### Fibrocystic Breast Disease

Fibrocystic breast disease is a benign condition characterized by lumpy, painful breasts and palpable fibrosis, commonly affecting women of reproductive age [1][19]. Breast tissue has a high concentration of iodine, especially during pregnancy and lactation [1].

**Double-blind RCT (n=56):** Women with fibrocystic breast disease received daily iodine supplements (70-90 mcg I2/kg body weight) or placebo for 6 months. At completion, 65% of women receiving iodine reported decreased pain compared to 33% in the placebo group [1][19].

**Dose-response RCT (n=111):** Women aged 18-50 years with fibrosis and a history of breast pain were randomized to 0, 1,500, 3,000, or 6,000 mcg of molecular iodine (I2) per day [1]. The trial lasted 6 months [37]. After 5 months, women receiving 3,000 or 6,000 mcg had a significant decrease in breast pain, tenderness, and nodularity compared to placebo or 1,500 mcg. The study also showed a dose-dependent reduction in self-assessed pain. None of the doses was associated with major adverse events or changes in thyroid function [1][37].

**Important caveat:** The doses used in these studies (1,500-6,000 mcg/day) are several times higher than the UL of 1,100 mcg for adults. Such doses should only be used under the guidance of a physician [1][2][37].

### Thyroid Cancer Prevention (Radiation Emergencies)

Nuclear accidents can release radioactive iodine (I-131) into the environment, increasing the risk of thyroid cancer in exposed individuals, especially children [16][38]. People with iodine deficiency have higher thyroidal uptake of radioactive iodine and therefore a particularly high risk. FDA has approved potassium iodide to block the thyroid in these emergencies: pharmacological doses (16-130 mg depending on age) saturate the thyroid and prevent radioactive iodine uptake [1][16][38].

**Chernobyl evidence:** Potassium iodide was widely distributed in Poland following the 1986 Chernobyl accident, and childhood thyroid cancer rates did not substantially increase in subsequent years [1][39]. In contrast, in Belarus and Ukraine, where iodide prophylaxis was not used and many children were mildly iodine-deficient, thyroid cancer incidence in children and adolescents sharply increased [1][16].

### Energy and Weight Loss

Kelp supplements are sometimes sold to boost energy or help weight loss. The human evidence is limited to small trials of seaweed fibre, seaweed-enriched bread and kelp powder [40][41][42][43].

**Alginate and appetite:** Alginate is a gel-forming fibre made from seaweed. In a 7-day crossover trial in 68 adults, a strong-gelling sodium alginate formulation taken before meals cut daily energy intake by 134.8 kcal (7%) [40]. In a small imaging study, a strong-gelling alginate meal increased fullness and reduced hunger [41]. These trials used purified sodium alginate, not dried kelp [40][41].

**Bread enrichment study:** In a pilot crossover trial in 12 healthy overweight men, a breakfast with bread containing 4% *Ascophyllum nodosum* cut how much the men ate at a test meal 4 hours later by 16.4%, against control bread. Blood glucose and cholesterol responses did not differ significantly [42].

**Body fat RCT:** In a randomized, double-blind trial in 50 overweight Japanese adults, 6 g of iodine-reduced kelp powder daily for 8 weeks lowered body fat percentage in men but not in women [43]. The powder still supplied 1.03 mg/day of iodine, and thyroid hormone levels did not rise in these adults, who already had a relatively high iodine intake from seaweed [43]. These results are preliminary and require replication.

### Interference Compounds and Iodine Absorption

Iodine deficiency may make people more vulnerable to the effects of some pollutants, such as nitrate, thiocyanate and perchlorate [33].

Goitrogens, substances in foods that interfere with thyroidal iodine uptake, are found in soy, cassava, and cruciferous vegetables (cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower) [1][2]. Iron and vitamin A deficiencies may also be goitrogenic [1][45]. These are primarily a concern for people living in iodine-deficient regions; for those with adequate iodine intake eating a varied diet, reasonable consumption of goitrogen-containing foods is not a concern [1][2].

### Other Kelp Bioactive Compounds

Kelp contains several bioactive compounds beyond iodine that may have health implications, though clinical evidence in humans remains limited:

**Fucoidans:** Sulfated polysaccharides found in brown seaweeds. Fucoidans from *Laminaria japonica* showed antithrombotic and anticoagulant activity in an experimental thrombosis model [46], which raises a possible bleeding concern (see Drug Interactions section).

**Alginates:** Gel-forming soluble fibres from seaweed cell walls, studied for appetite (see Energy and Weight Loss) [40][41].

### American Thyroid Association Recommendation

In 2015, the American Thyroid Association's Public Health Committee issued a statement on the potential risks of excess iodine ingestion and exposure [74]. It advises against iodine and kelp supplements whose daily dose of iodine exceeds 500 mcg, for children and adults and in pregnancy and breastfeeding, and notes that many iodine and kelp supplements contain up to a hundred times the daily upper limit [74]. Infants, older adults, pregnant and breastfeeding women, and people with thyroid disease such as Hashimoto's disease, Graves' disease or nontoxic thyroid nodules are more susceptible to excess iodine [74]. Fetuses and newborns may also be at higher risk of iodine-induced thyroid dysfunction [49].

### FDA Limit for Kelp Supplements

US food rules let kelp be added to food as an iodine source only if a day's use supplies at most 225 mcg of iodine for anyone 4 or more years of age, and at most 300 mcg in pregnancy or breastfeeding [77].

### Practical Dosing Guidance

**General adult supplementation:** The adult RDA is 150 mcg a day from all sources, and many multivitamins supply 150 mcg [1][14]. Kelp gives a less predictable dose: in one study, 33% of the iodine in a seaweed was bioavailable, compared with 59% from potassium iodide [17], and iodine content varies widely between seaweeds [9].

**Pregnancy and lactation:** 220-290 mcg/day total from diet and supplements [1]. The ATA recommends 150 mcg/day supplementation as potassium iodide for pregnant and lactating women [1][15]. Kelp is a less predictable source, because its iodine content varies widely and some kelp contains arsenic [8][9][75].

**Children ages 1-8:** 90 mcg/day. Children ages 9-13: 120 mcg/day. Adolescents ages 14+: 150 mcg/day. Most children in the US meet these requirements through diet [1][2].

**People on low-salt diets:** Those who avoid iodized salt (including users of Himalayan salt, sea salt, or kosher salt) may be at particular risk for inadequate intake and should ensure iodine from other dietary sources or supplements [1][2].

**Vegans and plant-based dieters:** Seafood, eggs, milk, and milk products are among the best dietary iodine sources. People who consume none or minimal amounts of these foods may not obtain sufficient iodine and may benefit from supplementation [1][50].

### How to Assess Iodine Status

Urinary iodine is the standard measure. Median urinary iodine concentrations above 100 mcg/L in non-pregnant adults indicate adequacy [1][12]. However, spot urine samples are suitable only for population-level assessment, not individual diagnosis; multiple 24-hour collections or repeat spot samples are needed for reliable individual assessment [1][51][52]. TSH may stay within the normal range when iodine intake is low, so it is not a sensitive indicator of deficiency [1].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Iodine-Induced Thyroid Dysfunction

The most significant risk of iodine supplementation, whether from kelp or potassium iodide, is disruption of thyroid function. Both hypo- and hyperthyroidism can result from excessive iodine intake [1][2][53].

**Kelp raised TSH in a trial:** In a 4-week double-blind trial in 36 healthy adults, kelp capsules raised TSH at both doses tested [70]. Thyroid values returned to baseline 2 weeks after stopping, except TSH in the high-dose group, which was significantly decreased [70].

**Hyperthyroidism from kelp (iodine-induced thyrotoxicosis):** A 1998 case report described transient hyperthyroidism in a patient taking dietary supplements that contained kelp [71]. In a 2006 case, a woman aged 39 who had a multinodular goiter developed iodine-induced hyperthyroidism after 4 weeks of a herbal tea containing kelp; the authors advise people with thyroid disease to avoid iodine-containing alternative remedies [80].

**Congenital hypothyroidism:** In three infants with congenital hypothyroidism, the evidence pointed to excess maternal iodine intake of 12.5 mg a day as the cause [76].

**Thyroid inflammation and cancer:** High iodine intakes can cause thyroid gland inflammation (thyroiditis) and studies have shown associations with thyroid papillary cancer [1][2][53]. A 2015 review concluded that iodine deficiency is a risk factor for thyroid cancer, particularly the follicular type [73].

**Population susceptibility:** Responses to excess iodine vary. People with autoimmune thyroid disease and those with iodine deficiency may experience adverse effects at iodine intakes considered safe for the general population [1][2]. Pre-existing thyroid disease (such as Hashimoto's disease, Graves' disease or nontoxic thyroid nodules), older age, and being a fetus or newborn may also raise the risk [49][74].

### Arsenic Contamination in Kelp

Kelp can contain arsenic. In a survey of 10 New England seaweed species, several samples of *Laminaria digitata* contained significant inorganic arsenic (2.8-20 mg/kg), the most toxic form [8]. In a 2007 case report, a woman aged 54 who took daily kelp supplements had hair loss, memory loss, rash, fatigue, nausea and vomiting, with raised urine arsenic; her symptoms resolved within weeks of stopping [75]. Eight of nine kelp samples the authors then bought from health food stores had arsenic above the FDA tolerance level of 0.5 to 2 ppm set for certain foods [75].

### Skin Reactions

Excess iodine has caused sensitivity reactions in some people [53].

**Case report (acne):** A 1976 letter described an acne-like eruption caused by a kelp diet [58].

### Acute Iodine Poisoning

Very large doses of iodine (usually many grams) can cause burning of the mouth, throat, and stomach; fever; abdominal pain; nausea; vomiting; diarrhea; weak pulse; and coma [1][2]. Acute poisoning is rare. If someone has swallowed a very large amount of iodine, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).

### Autoimmune Reaction from Kelp

A 1992 case report linked kelp supplements with autoimmune thrombocytopenia (a low platelet count) and severe dyserythropoiesis, a disorder of red cell production [62].

### Safety in Pregnancy

**Recommended approach:** The American Thyroid Association advises 150 mcg/day of supplemental iodine as potassium iodide [1][15]. Kelp is not the form it names: its iodine content varies widely and some kelp contains arsenic [8][9][75].

**Risk of oversupplementation in pregnancy:** A cross-sectional study of pregnant women in Spain found a significantly increased risk of elevated TSH (>3 microU/mL) in women taking iodine supplements at doses of 200 mcg/day or more compared to those taking less than 100 mcg/day [63]. This suggests that even moderately high supplemental iodine doses during pregnancy can induce thyroid dysfunction in some women.

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Antithyroid Medications

Antithyroid medicines such as methimazole (Tapazole) treat hyperthyroidism. Taking high doses of iodine with them can have an additive effect and could cause hypothyroidism [1][64].

### Blood Thinners / Anticoagulants

Fucoidans from kelp showed anticoagulant and antithrombotic activity in an experimental thrombosis model [46]. If you take a blood thinner such as warfarin, heparin, aspirin or clopidogrel, check with your doctor or pharmacist before taking kelp. This concern applies to kelp, which contains fucoidans, not to potassium iodide.

### ACE Inhibitors

Taking potassium iodide with ACE inhibitors (benazepril/Lotensin, lisinopril/Prinivil/Zestril, fosinopril/Monopril) can increase the risk of hyperkalemia (elevated blood potassium) [1][64].

### Potassium-Sparing Diuretics

Taking potassium iodide with potassium-sparing diuretics (spironolactone/Aldactone, amiloride/Midamor) can increase the risk of hyperkalemia [1][64].

### Levothyroxine and Other Thyroid Medications

People with pre-existing thyroid disease, such as Hashimoto's disease, Graves' disease or nontoxic thyroid nodules, may be at higher risk of thyroid problems from extra iodine [49][74]. If you take levothyroxine or another thyroid medicine, check with your doctor before starting iodine or kelp supplements.

### Amiodarone

Amiodarone, a heart-rhythm medicine, is 37.3% iodine by weight and can cause hypothyroidism or hyperthyroidism [78]. If you take amiodarone, check with your doctor before adding iodine or kelp supplements.

### Lithium

Lithium taken with iodide preparations may produce hypothyroidism, and the lithium label advises monitoring for its signs [79].

## 7. Dietary Sources

Seaweed (kelp, nori, kombu, wakame) is one of the best food sources of iodine, though the iodine content is highly variable: commercially available seaweeds in whole or sheet form range from 16 mcg/g to 2,984 mcg/g [1]. Other good sources include fish, seafood, dairy products, eggs, and bread made with iodate dough conditioners [1].

### Key Notes on Dietary Iodine

- **Dairy products** contain iodine, but amounts vary widely (38-160 mcg per cup of nonfat milk) depending on whether cows received iodine feed supplements and whether iodophor sanitizing agents were used [1][2][20].
- **Bread** can be a significant source if manufacturers use iodate dough conditioners. Approximately 20% of bread products in the US list iodate conditioners; those made without these conditioners contain very little iodine [1].
- **Plant-based milk substitutes** (soy, almond) contain relatively small amounts of iodine [1][2].
- **Fruits and vegetables** are generally poor iodine sources, and their content varies based on soil iodine levels, fertilizer use, and irrigation practices [1][2].
- **Seaweed iodine content** varies enormously by species: *Laminaria digitata* has among the highest concentrations, while nori (*Porphyra*) tends to have lower, more moderate levels [9].

### Who Tends to Run Low on Iodine

**People who skip iodized salt:** About 88% of households worldwide use iodized salt, but iodine insufficiency persists in Southeast Asia, sub-Saharan Africa, and Eastern Europe [1][66][67].

**Pregnant women:** Many pregnant women in the US consume insufficient iodine. NHANES data from 2007-2014 showed a median urinary iodine concentration of 144 mcg/L in pregnant women, below the 150 mcg/L threshold for adequacy [1][11]. Pregnant women who consumed no dairy products had median urinary iodine of only 100 mcg/L compared to 163 mcg/L among dairy consumers [1][69].

**Vegans and people avoiding dairy, seafood, and eggs:** These are among the richest dietary iodine sources [1][50].

**People in iodine-deficient soil regions:** Mountainous areas (Himalayas, Alps, Andes) and flood-prone river valleys (South and Southeast Asia) have iodine-deficient soils that produce low-iodine crops [1].

**People with marginal iodine status who consume goitrogens:** Soy, cassava, and cruciferous vegetables contain goitrogens that can exacerbate deficiency [1][2][45].
