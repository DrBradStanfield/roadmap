## Current evidence position

Brad has not made a video on this topic; this is a summary of the evidence, not his view. Regular licorice contains glycyrrhizin, which stops the kidney from inactivating cortisol, so large or long-term intake can cause high blood pressure, low potassium and, in some cases, life-threatening heart rhythm problems [47][62]. In a 2024 randomized crossover trial in healthy young adults, licorice providing 100 mg glycyrrhizic acid a day for 2 weeks raised home systolic blood pressure by 3.1 mm Hg, and the authors suggested the safe limit may need to be reconsidered [19]. DGL (deglycyrrhizinated licorice) has most of the glycyrrhizin removed; in a 2025 trial sponsored by the maker of one DGL extract (GutGard, 150 mg a day), heartburn and regurgitation eased sooner than with placebo, while older ulcer trials gave mixed results [22][32][60]. People with hypertension, heart or kidney disease, those who are pregnant, and anyone taking diuretics, digoxin, corticosteroids or blood pressure medicines should talk to their doctor before using regular licorice [4][61][62].

## Key safety points

- Licorice can lower potassium. With thiazide or loop diuretics the risk of severe low potassium rises, and low potassium from licorice has caused digoxin toxicity [4][62][66].
- Licorice was reported to strengthen the effect of warfarin, and glycyrrhizin directly inhibits two blood clotting factors [4][48]. If you take warfarin or another anticoagulant, ask your doctor first.
- Corticosteroids such as prednisone add to licorice's effect, and blood pressure medicines may hide the rise in blood pressure it causes [62][67].
- Heavy use can cause dangerous heart rhythms, paralysis and death [50][62]. If you have used licorice and get palpitations, fainting, chest pain or sudden weakness, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department.
- Avoid regular licorice in pregnancy: heavy intake was linked to more than twice the risk of preterm birth [61]. Those with hypertension, heart disease, or kidney or liver failure are more sensitive [62][64].
- The EU upper limit for glycyrrhizin is 100 mg/day, but a 2024 trial raised blood pressure at that dose [4][19].
- The DGL extract GutGard was well tolerated in short trials, with no serious adverse events reported [22][23].

## 1. Overview

Licorice is the root of *Glycyrrhiza glabra*, a plant in the Fabaceae (pea) family that has been used in medicine since ancient times [3]. Its compounds include glycyrrhizin, glycyrrhetinic acid and isoflavones. Lab experiments with licorice extracts show antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, antiviral and antioxidant effects, and a few toxicology studies have raised concerns [3].

The main active compound is glycyrrhizin (glycyrrhizic acid), which gives licorice its sweet taste. In the intestine it is broken down to glycyrrhetinic acid, the pharmacologically active form [47][64].

Glycyrrhetinic acid blocks the enzyme 11-beta-hydroxysteroid dehydrogenase type 2 (11beta-HSD2), which normally turns active cortisol into inactive cortisone. Cortisol then acts on the same kidney receptor as aldosterone. The result is sodium and water retention, potassium loss, higher blood pressure and a suppressed renin-aldosterone system, a state called pseudohyperaldosteronism or apparent mineralocorticoid excess [47][49][64]. The block on the enzyme is reversible, but the body's compensating changes may last several months [47].

In 1946 the Dutch physician Revers reported that a licorice preparation relieved stomach ulcers; when the preparation was changed, patients developed oedema, headache and breathlessness on exertion [50]. Deglycyrrhizinated licorice (DGL) was later made to avoid these side effects by removing glycyrrhizin [4]. Removal is not complete: GutGard, the DGL extract in recent trials, is standardized to no more than 3.0% glycyrrhizin [22].

Licorice also contains flavonoids, including glabridin, liquiritigenin and isoliquiritigenin [22]. In lab studies, glabridin has antioxidant, anti-inflammatory and estrogen-like effects [13]. Caved-S, a DGL product, was tested against placebo and ulcer drugs in the 1970s and 1980s [32][60]. DGL is sold as capsules, lozenges, wafers and liquid [4].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Regular Licorice Root

Regular licorice keeps its glycyrrhizin. It is found in supplements, teas, candy, drinks, breath fresheners, tobacco and some laxatives [69]. Examples:

- **Licorice root extract:** Glycyrrhizin content depends on the product. One liver supplement gave a daily 1,000 mg dose of an extract of licorice root standardized to at least 20% glycyrrhizic acid [49].
- **Licorice tea:** A 2025 case report cites an average of around 31.5 mg of glycyrrhetinic acid in a 250 mL cup [51]. Homemade root drinks have not been measured but have caused harm [52].
- **Licorice candy:** In the US, the FDA allows up to 3.1% glycyrrhizin in soft candy [4]. Candy providing 100 mg glycyrrhizic acid a day raised blood pressure in a 2024 trial [19].

### Deglycyrrhizinated Licorice (DGL)

DGL products have had most of the glycyrrhizin removed [4]. Key forms include:

- **Chewable tablets:** Caved-S, a DGL tablet, was the product in the older ulcer trials, taken as 760 mg three times daily or six tablets a day [32][60].
- **Powder:** Sold for mixing into drinks.
- **Lozenges:** Sold as a DGL form [4]. A DGL mouthwash was tested in a small study of mouth ulcers [39].
- **Capsules:** Also sold [4].

### Standardized Extracts

Some DGL products are standardized to flavonoid content rather than simply listing total DGL weight:

- **GutGard** (by Natural Remedies Private Limited): A flavonoid-rich DGL extract standardized to at least 3.5% glabridin and at least 10% total flavonoids, with no more than 3.0% glycyrrhizin [22]. It is the extract used in the recent reflux, dyspepsia and *H. pylori* trials, at 150 mg a day, given as 75 mg twice daily in the dyspepsia trial [22][23][37].
- **Non-standardized DGL:** Products that list only a total DGL weight have not been tested in these trials, so the GutGard results may not apply to them.

### Production and Quality

Manufacturers remove glycyrrhizin from licorice extract to make DGL, but removal is incomplete; GutGard, for example, allows up to 3.0% [22].

GutGard's trial report states that it meets US Pharmacopeia and Ayurvedic Pharmacopoeia of India standards for pesticides, heavy metals, microbes and aflatoxins [22].

### Residual Glycyrrhizin in DGL Products

DGL is not glycyrrhizin-free; the GutGard specification allows up to 3.0% [22]. People vary widely in how sensitive they are to glycyrrhizin [47], so taking several DGL servings a day for months could add up.

### Bioavailability of Active Compounds

Glycyrrhizin is absorbed less well when taken as licorice than as the pure compound [5]. Its metabolites bind strongly to albumin in the blood and leave the body mainly through bile [63]. In cell studies, GutGard and glabridin reduced the release of inflammatory prostaglandins, thromboxane and leukotrienes, while glycyrrhizin itself had no effect [27]. In rats, DGL increased the number of mucus-secreting cells in the stomach lining [34].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Gastroesophageal Reflux Disease (GERD) and Heartburn

Reflux has the most recent trial evidence for DGL. One proposed mechanism is more mucus in the stomach lining, seen with DGL in rats [34].

**2025 Phase III RCT (GutGard):** A single-centre, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial enrolled 200 adults with reflux symptoms and gave GutGard 150 mg a day or placebo for 28 days [22]. The GutGard group reported better quality of life at the end of treatment and earlier relief of heartburn and regurgitation, within 2 weeks [22]. There were no serious adverse events [22]. The trial was sponsored by the extract's maker, Natural Remedies Private Limited [22].

**Synthesis:** DGL may ease heartburn and regurgitation, based mainly on one industry-sponsored 4-week trial of GutGard [22]. Whether other DGL products work the same way is unknown.

### Peptic and Duodenal Ulcers

Licorice was first used for ulcers in the 1940s, when Revers reported benefit but also fluid retention and other side effects [50]. DGL was developed to avoid those effects [4].

**Gastric ulcer trial:** In a 1982 single-blind trial of 100 patients with benign gastric ulcer, Caved-S (six tablets a day) healed about as many ulcers as cimetidine (1 g a day), approximately 63% at six weeks and 91% at 12 weeks [60]. Cimetidine relieved night pain better in the first two weeks [60].

**Negative trial:** In a 1973 double-blind crossover trial in chronic gastric ulcer, DGL (Caved-S) 760 mg three times daily showed no healing effect compared with placebo in two 4-week periods; 38 patients took part in the first period, and no side effects were seen [32].

**1969 gastric ulcer trial:** A 1969 controlled trial in *Gut* also tested DGL in gastric ulcer [33].

**Mechanism:** In rats, DGL increased mucus-secreting cells in the stomach glands [34]. A water extract of licorice (not DGL) protected rat stomachs against drug-induced ulcers about as well as famotidine [14].

**Historical context:** DGL is not a substitute for standard ulcer treatment, which today targets stomach acid and, where present, *H. pylori* infection.

**Synthesis:** Ulcer trials from the 1960s to the 1980s give mixed results: DGL matched cimetidine in one trial and did no better than placebo in another [32][60]. The trials are old and mostly small.

### Helicobacter pylori

*H. pylori* is one of the causes of peptic ulcer [36]. In lab studies, GutGard and its main flavonoid, glabridin, inhibited *H. pylori*, possibly by blocking bacterial protein synthesis and two bacterial enzymes; glycyrrhizin had no activity [36].

**2013 RCT (GutGard):** In a double-blind, placebo-controlled trial, adults with *H. pylori* infection took 150 mg of GutGard (55 people) or placebo (52 people) once daily for 60 days [37]. At day 60, the stool antigen test was negative in 56% of the GutGard group and 4% of the placebo group, and the breath test was negative in 48% of the GutGard group [37].

**In vitro evidence:** A water extract of licorice and its polysaccharides stopped *H. pylori* from sticking to human stomach tissue [35]. GutGard itself did not block adhesion to stomach cells at the concentrations tested [36].

**Practical note:** DGL is not a replacement for standard *H. pylori* antibiotic therapy. The 2013 trial tested GutGard alone, not added to antibiotics, so its value as an add-on is unknown [37].

### Gastritis and Dyspepsia

Functional dyspepsia is ongoing upper abdominal pain or discomfort without an organic cause [28]. Most of the DGL evidence in this area is for functional dyspepsia, not gastritis.

**Functional dyspepsia RCT (GutGard):** A randomized, double-blind trial gave patients with functional dyspepsia GutGard 75 mg twice daily or placebo for 30 days [23]. GutGard lowered total symptom scores at day 15 and day 30, improved a dyspepsia quality-of-life index, and was well tolerated [23].

The trial used GutGard; the results may not apply to other DGL products.

### Oral Health: Canker Sores (Aphthous Ulcers) and Dental Caries

**Canker sores:** In a small 1989 study, 20 patients with mouth ulcers used a DGL mouthwash; 15 improved by 50 to 75% within one day, and their ulcers healed completely by the third day [39].

**Dental health:** In a lab study, licorice polysaccharides strongly blocked *Porphyromonas gingivalis*, a gum-disease bacterium, from sticking to tissue [35]. This is lab evidence only.

**Sore throat:** DGL lozenges are sold [4]; their effect on sore throat has not been shown in trials.

### Adrenal Insufficiency

Because glycyrrhizin slows the breakdown of cortisol, some people with Addison's disease have used licorice [42][47].

**Clinical evidence:** In a 2011 study of 17 patients with Addison's disease on cortisone acetate, 3 days of licorice increased the cortisol available after each dose, and the authors warned patients and doctors about this interaction [41]. A 1973 case report described a patient with Addison's disease who self-medicated with licorice [42].

**Important distinction:** This applies to regular licorice, not DGL. DGL has most of its glycyrrhizin removed, so it would not be expected to affect cortisol much.

**Synthesis:** The evidence is one small study and a case report. Adrenal insufficiency is treated with hormone replacement; licorice changes how much cortisol those medicines deliver, so do not add it without your doctor [41].

### Fatigue and Chronic Fatigue Syndrome

A 1995 letter in the *New Zealand Medical Journal* discussed licorice for chronic fatigue syndrome [43]. It was a letter, not a trial.

Licorice raised blood pressure even in healthy young adults [19], so trying it for fatigue carries real risk.

### Stress, Anxiety, and Depression

A 2023 review of licorice flavonoids for depression describes effects on stress hormones, brain signalling and nerve cells, and calls them a theoretical foundation for antidepressant drug development [44]. Licorice has not been shown to treat stress, anxiety or depression in people.

### Skin Health (Topical Applications)

**Atopic dermatitis:** In a double-blind trial with 30 patients in each group, a 2% licorice gel reduced redness, swelling and itching more than a 1% gel over two weeks [45]. This was a skin gel, not oral DGL.

**Glabridin's skin effects:** Glabridin has been studied for skin whitening and anti-inflammatory effects [13]. These topical uses are separate from oral DGL.

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### DGL (Deglycyrrhizinated Licorice)

DGL doses come from a few trials, mostly of GutGard:

| Indication | Dose | Frequency | Duration | Form | Notes |
|-----------|------|-----------|----------|------|-------|
| GERD / heartburn | 150 mg/day standardized extract (GutGard) | Split not stated | 28 days | Extract | 2025 trial sponsored by the maker [22] |
| Functional dyspepsia | 75 mg standardized extract (GutGard) | Twice daily | 30 days | Extract | Symptoms improved vs placebo [23] |
| Gastric ulcers | 760 mg DGL (Caved-S) | Three times daily | 4 weeks | Tablets | No healing effect vs placebo [32] |
| Gastric ulcers | Caved-S, six tablets | Daily | 6 to 12 weeks | Tablets | Similar healing to cimetidine [60] |
| H. pylori | 150 mg standardized extract (GutGard) | Once daily | 60 days | Extract | Tested alone, not with antibiotics [37] |
| Canker sores | DGL mouthwash | Not stated | Until healed (by day three in most) | Mouthwash | Small study [39] |

**Key dosing principles:**
- The DGL doses tested against placebo are GutGard 150 mg a day (faster reflux relief) and Caved-S 760 mg three times daily (no gastric ulcer healing effect) [22][32].
- The longest DGL data come from a gastric ulcer trial with healing checked at 6 and 12 weeks, followed by a year of maintenance on a lower Caved-S dose [60].

### Regular Licorice (Glycyrrhizin-Containing)

Use regular licorice with great caution, because glycyrrhizin can raise blood pressure and lower potassium [62].

**European Commission Scientific Committee on Food recommendation:** In April 2003 the Scientific Committee on Food confirmed an upper limit for glycyrrhizin of 100 mg/day, based on human volunteer studies [4][62]. Sensitivity varies with age, other medicines, high blood pressure, and kidney or liver failure [62]. Constipation and low blood albumin also raise exposure [63].

**More conservative limit:** A 1993 review treated 100 mg glycyrrhizic acid a day as the lowest dose that causes harm and, applying a safety factor of 10, proposed a daily intake of 10 mg as safe for most healthy adults [47].

**FDA limits:** The US FDA lists licorice as generally recognized as safe in foods within set maximum levels, such as 3.1% glycyrrhizin in soft candy [4].

**Blood pressure effects in healthy young adults:** In a 2024 randomized crossover trial, 28 healthy volunteers with a median age of 24.0 ate licorice containing 100 mg glycyrrhizic acid a day for 2 weeks [19]. Home systolic blood pressure rose by 3.1 mm Hg, compared with -0.3 mm Hg on the control product, and renin and aldosterone fell [19]. In a 2014 study, 290 to 370 mg of glycyrrhizin a day for two weeks raised blood pressure by 7/4 mm Hg [65].

### How to Read a Licorice/DGL Supplement Label

- **"DGL" or "Deglycyrrhizinated licorice":** Most glycyrrhizin removed, not all; GutGard allows up to 3.0% [22].
- **"Licorice root" or "Glycyrrhiza glabra root":** Assume full glycyrrhizin content unless the label says deglycyrrhizinated.
- **"Standardized to X% glabridin" or "X% total flavonoids":** GutGard is standardized to at least 3.5% glabridin and at least 10% total flavonoids [22].
- **Glycyrrhizin content:** If a label gives glycyrrhizin per serving, compare the daily total with the 100 mg upper limit; a 2024 trial raised blood pressure at that dose [19][62].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Regular Licorice (Glycyrrhizin-Containing)

Regular licorice can cause harm at doses reached through supplements, candy or tea [49][50][51].

**Mechanism of harm:** Glycyrrhetinic acid blocks 11beta-HSD2, so cortisol activates the aldosterone receptor in the kidney. This causes sodium and fluid retention, potassium loss and higher blood pressure [47][62].

**Reported adverse effects of glycyrrhizin include:**
- Loss of potassium (hypokalemia)
- Fluid retention (oedema)
- Increased blood pressure (hypertension)
- Abnormal heart rhythms (arrhythmias)
- Muscle paralysis and muscle breakdown (rhabdomyolysis)
- Metabolic alkalosis
- Reduced consciousness or coma [47][49][62]

In a 2023 review of 104 case reports, 84 people needed hospital admission [62]. If you have used licorice and develop palpitations, fainting, chest pain, a severe headache or sudden muscle weakness, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department.

**Case reports illustrating severity:**

*Brain bleed after a licorice supplement:* A 68-year-old patient with no past medical history had a bleed in the brain, with slurred speech, weakness on one side and a blood pressure of 219/123, after taking a Chinese herbal supplement every day for 2 weeks for indigestion; its label listed licorice root at 800 mg [48]. The authors noted that glycyrrhizin also inhibits blood clotting factors [48].

*Acquired mineralocorticoid excess syndrome:* A 65-year-old patient had a blood pressure of 197/89 mmHg, with metabolic alkalosis, agitation, anxiety, tremor and a low potassium level, after 4 months on a liver supplement. It gave her a daily 1,000 mg dose of an extract of licorice root standardized to at least 20% glycyrrhizic acid. Her blood pressure, symptoms and blood tests returned to normal within 2 weeks of stopping it [49].

*Fatal cardiac arrest from licorice candy:* A 54-year-old patient had a cardiac arrest from ventricular fibrillation, with a blood potassium level of 2.0 mmol per liter, 3 weeks after switching to licorice-flavored soft candy containing glycyrrhizic acid; he ate one or two large packages a day, and he died [50].

*Severe hypokalemia from licorice tea:* A 2025 US case report described a woman with type 2 diabetes and cancer who drank about five licorice tea bags a day; routine tests showed a potassium level of 2.0 mEq/L (reference 3.5-5.3 mEq/L), and she was also taking an SGLT2 inhibitor [51]. An 84-year-old patient with long-standing hypertension had a hypertensive emergency, with headache, photophobia, chest pain, fatigue, mild pulmonary oedema and a potassium level of 2.5 (reference range 3.5-5.0) mmol/L; for the 2 weeks before, he had been having 1 to 2 glasses a day of a homemade licorice root drink, and his medicines included hydrochlorothiazide and furosemide [52]. A 57-year-old patient with no heart disease came to the emergency department with atrial fibrillation and a potassium level of 2.0 mmol/L after drinking licorice root syrup [53]. A 45-year-old patient drinking up to six cups of licorice tea a day had high blood pressure and mild low potassium; both resolved within 2 weeks of stopping [18].

**Licorice candy safety concerns:** A 1993 review estimated that a regular 100 mg glycyrrhizic acid a day, enough to harm the most sensitive people, corresponds to 50 g of licorice sweets at 0.2% glycyrrhizic acid, and that most people who take 400 mg a day have adverse effects [47].

### DGL Safety Profile

DGL appears well tolerated in trials [22][23][32].

**Reported adverse reactions:** In the 2025 reflux trial there were no serious adverse events [22]. In the 1973 ulcer trial, no side effects were seen [32]. The dyspepsia trial found GutGard generally safe and well tolerated [23].

**Residual glycyrrhizin concern:** DGL still contains some glycyrrhizin, up to 3.0% in GutGard [22]. Several servings a day for months could add up, especially in sensitive people [47].

**Long-term safety:** The longest DGL data come from a year of maintenance treatment with Caved-S in a gastric ulcer trial [60]. Longer use has little trial data.

### Populations Who Should Avoid All Forms of Licorice (Including DGL)

These groups should avoid regular licorice or ask their doctor before using any licorice product. Most of the evidence concerns regular licorice; DGL contains much less glycyrrhizin [22][62]:

- **People with hypertension:** glycyrrhizin raises blood pressure, and blood pressure medicines may hide the rise [19][62]
- **People with heart disease or heart failure:** excess licorice can cause life-threatening complications, especially in people with cardiovascular disease [64]
- **People with kidney or liver disease:** chronic kidney and liver failure add to the variation in sensitivity [62]
- **Pregnant women:** heavy glycyrrhizin intake (500 mg/week and above) was linked to more than twice the risk of preterm birth, and for early preterm birth the odds ratio was 3.07 (95% confidence interval: 1.17, 8.05) [61]
- **Nursing women:** ask your doctor before using licorice while breastfeeding
- **People with hormone-sensitive conditions** (e.g., breast cancer, endometriosis): licorice has estrogen-like activity, so ask your doctor first [13][62]
- **Older adults:** 11beta-HSD2 activity falls with age, which raises the risk [63]

### How Quickly Effects Appear and Resolve

Blood pressure rose within 2 weeks at 100 mg glycyrrhizic acid a day [19]. In case reports, problems appeared after 2 weeks to 4 months of daily use [48][49][52]. After stopping, symptoms usually settled within about 2 weeks [18][49]. Low potassium may take 1 to 2 weeks to resolve [50], and the body's hormone balance may take several months to recover [47]. In a 2023 review, the outlook was generally good, with symptoms resolving completely in 30 days [62].

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Regular Licorice (Glycyrrhizin-Containing) — Significant Interactions

| Drug/Drug Class | Interaction | Clinical Significance |
|----------------|-------------|----------------------|
| **Corticosteroids** (prednisone, hydrocortisone) | Steroids add to licorice's block on cortisol breakdown; licorice raised cortisol levels in people taking cortisone acetate | Avoid combining, or ask your doctor first [41][62] |
| **Diuretics** (furosemide, thiazides) | Thiazide and loop diuretics add to potassium loss | Higher risk of severe low potassium [62][63] |
| **Digoxin** | Low potassium from licorice can cause digoxin toxicity | Reported case of digoxin toxicity with heart failure [4][66] |
| **Antihypertensives** | Licorice raises blood pressure; these drugs may hide the rise | Low potassium reported with licorice plus antihypertensives [62][67] |
| **Warfarin and anticoagulants** | One study found licorice strengthened warfarin's effect; glycyrrhizin directly inhibits clotting factors Xa and thrombin | Ask your doctor before combining [4][48] |
| **Oral contraceptives** | A 2023 review advises avoiding glycyrrhetinic acid with oral contraceptives | Avoid combining [62] |
| **Spironolactone** | Potassium-sparing drugs such as spironolactone may prevent licorice-induced low potassium | Spironolactone is used to treat licorice toxicity [51][62] |
| **SGLT2 inhibitors** | Severe low potassium from licorice tea in a woman taking an SGLT2 inhibitor | The combination made the diagnosis harder [51] |
| **Laxatives** | Some herbal laxatives contain licorice; diarrhoea adds to potassium loss | Pseudohyperaldosteronism and digoxin toxicity reported [4][66][68] |

If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

### DGL — Minimal Interactions

DGL contains much less glycyrrhizin than regular licorice, so these interactions should be smaller [4][22]. DGL is not glycyrrhizin-free, though, so people taking several potassium-lowering medicines, or digoxin, should still ask their doctor first [22].

**Important caveat:** If you take digoxin, diuretics or blood pressure medicines, take extra care even with DGL, especially at higher doses or with long-term use.

### Important Distinctions

- **Licorice root in herbal teas** can be a hidden source of glycyrrhizin; two case reports involved herbal teas [18][51]. Check ingredient lists for *Glycyrrhiza glabra* or "licorice root" if you are avoiding glycyrrhizin.
- **DGL** has most, but not all, of its glycyrrhizin removed [22].

### Geographic and Cultural Context

In traditional Chinese medicine, Chinese licorice root (*Glycyrrhiza uralensis*, "gan zao") is used for its presumed anti-ulcer, anti-inflammatory, antiviral, antibacterial and expectorant properties [48].

### Sustainability Considerations

Industry demand for licorice and other traditional medicinal plants, for use in foods, supplements, cosmetics and medicines, is growing [3].
