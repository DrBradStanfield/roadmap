## Key safety points

- If you take warfarin or another blood thinner, check with your doctor first. Fish oil might raise the INR with warfarin, and prescription labels advise periodic INR checks [1]; in a 4 g a day prescription trial serious bleeding was 2.7% vs 2.1% on placebo (P=0.06) [2].
- After a transplant or during chemotherapy, ask your specialist first: high-dose omega-3 raised cyclosporine levels [3], and a fatty acid in fish oil blocked chemotherapy in mice; the researchers suggest avoiding it on the days around treatment [4].
- Fish oil may raise the risk of atrial fibrillation: in trials, about 49% higher above 1 g a day and about 12% higher at 1 g a day or less [5]. The FDA says supplement labels should not recommend a daily intake of EPA and DHA higher than 2 g [1].
- If you are allergic to fish or shellfish, check with your doctor before taking fish or krill oil [6][7].
- Omega-3 use in early pregnancy was linked to more postpartum hemorrhage, which the authors say gives some support to stopping it in late pregnancy [8].
- One study linked high blood omega-3 levels with more prostate cancer, but pooled studies found no consistent link [9][10].
- Prescription 4 g a day doses are drugs used under a doctor's care [11]. Cod liver oil adds vitamin A, which may harm the liver in excess [1][12]; check your total with your doctor or pharmacist.
- Side effects are usually mild: unpleasant taste, bad breath, heartburn, nausea, stomach discomfort and diarrhea [1].

## 1. Overview

Omega-3 fatty acids are a family of polyunsaturated fats. The three that matter most are alpha-linolenic acid (ALA), from plants, and eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA), from fish and algae [1]. The body can convert ALA to EPA and then DHA, but conversion is very limited, reported at less than 15%, so eating EPA and DHA directly is the only practical way to raise them [1].

EPA and DHA are part of the membranes of every cell, and DHA is especially high in the retina, brain and sperm [1]. They are also used to make eicosanoids, signalling molecules; those made from omega-6 fats are generally stronger drivers of inflammation than those made from omega-3s [1]. Like other fats, omega-3s are absorbed with an efficiency of about 95% [1].

The omega-3 index is the share of EPA plus DHA in red blood cell membranes. It reflects intake over about the previous 120 days [1]. In 10 cohort studies, moving from an index of 4% to 8% was estimated to cut the risk of fatal coronary heart disease by about 30%, and the authors proposed under 4% and over 8% as targets [13]. In Western populations that eat little fish, EPA and DHA make up about 3% to 5% of red cell fatty acids, about half the level in Japan [1].

For adults, the Adequate Intake for omega-3 is 1.6 g a day for men and 1.1 g for women, as ALA; no intake has been set for EPA or DHA [1]. The American Heart Association advises one to two seafood meals a week [1].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Chemical Forms

Supplements carry omega-3s in several chemical forms: natural triglycerides, free fatty acids, ethyl esters, re-esterified triglycerides and phospholipids [1].

**Natural Triglycerides:** The form in which EPA and DHA occur in fish oil. A typical fish oil supplement gives about 1,000 mg of oil, of which 180 mg is EPA and 120 mg is DHA [1].

**Ethyl Esters:** Made from natural triglycerides by replacing the glycerol molecule with ethanol [1]. Prescription icosapent ethyl contains at least 96% EPA ethyl ester and no DHA [14].

**Re-esterified Triglycerides:** Made by converting ethyl esters back into triglycerides. In a 2-week study, the bioavailability of EPA+DHA from re-esterified triglycerides was 124% of that from natural fish oil, while ethyl esters reached 73% [15].

**Free Fatty Acids:** A free fatty acid prescription product lowered triglycerides at 2 to 4 g a day [16]. Free fatty acids are absorbed somewhat better than ethyl esters [1].

**Monoglycerides:** A "pre-digested" form. In a crossover trial, a single 3 g dose of omega-3s as monoglycerides gave peak blood EPA and DHA levels 3 and 2.5 times higher than ethyl esters [17]. Another study found the monoglyceride form better absorbed than ethyl esters at high doses, about as well as free fatty acids, and similar to triglycerides at supplement doses on a low-fat diet [18].

**Phospholipids:** The form in krill oil. Some studies suggest omega-3s in this form are somewhat better absorbed than those in fish oil, while others do not [1]. In a network meta-analysis of 64 trials, krill oil and fish oil had the same effects on blood lipids [19].

### Key Absorption Principles

**Taking fish oil with a fatty meal improves absorption, especially of ethyl esters.** In one study, a high-fat meal raised absorption of EPA and DHA from ethyl esters about three-fold [20].

**Form summary:** Re-esterified triglycerides, natural triglycerides and free fatty acids have somewhat higher bioavailability than ethyl esters, but all forms significantly raise blood EPA and DHA [1].

Quality varies. Of 32 fish oil supplements on sale in New Zealand in 2015, only 3 contained at least the EPA and DHA on the label, and most exceeded limits for oxidation [21]. In a 7-week trial, oxidised fish oil lacked the favourable effects on lipoprotein particles that fresh oil had [22]. Choose a product that an independent lab has tested.

### DPA: The "Other" Omega-3

Few trials have tested DPA supplements. In a 7-day crossover trial in women, 8 g of pure DPA raised blood DPA and also EPA and DHA in some blood fats, and the authors suggest DPA may act as a reservoir of the main long-chain omega-3s [23].

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Cardiovascular Disease and Stroke

#### Overall Evidence

Fish oil supplements lower triglycerides and might reduce the risk of some cardiovascular outcomes, especially in people who eat little omega-3 [1]. Evidence of protection is stronger for people with existing coronary heart disease than for healthy people [1]. The large trials and meta-analyses disagree, partly because of dose, the form of omega-3, the placebo oil and background statin use [1].

#### Key Clinical Trials

**VITAL study (n=25,871, 5.3 years):** In older US adults, 1 g a day (460 mg EPA and 380 mg DHA) did not reduce major cardiovascular events (hazard ratio 0.92) or cancer. Total myocardial infarction, a secondary outcome, was 28% lower (hazard ratio 0.72) [24].

**ASCEND study (n=15,480, 7.4 years):** In adults with diabetes but no known cardiovascular disease, 1 g a day did not reduce serious vascular events: 8.9% vs 9.2% with olive oil placebo [25].

**REDUCE-IT (n=8,179, 4.9 years):** In statin users with raised triglycerides and either cardiovascular disease or diabetes with other risk factors, 4 g a day of icosapent ethyl cut first cardiovascular events by 25% (17.2% vs 22.0%; hazard ratio 0.75) and cardiovascular death (4.3% vs 5.2%) [2]. The placebo was mineral oil.

**EVAPORATE plaque study (n=80, 18 months):** In statin users with high triglycerides, icosapent ethyl reduced low-attenuation plaque by 17%, while it more than doubled (+109%) with placebo [26].

**STRENGTH (n=13,078):** 4 g a day of EPA+DHA in carboxylic acid form did not reduce cardiovascular events compared with corn oil in high-risk statin users (12.0% vs 12.2%), and the trial was stopped early. Gastrointestinal side effects were more common (24.7% vs 14.7%) [27]. A post hoc analysis found 28% fewer major events among Asian participants (hazard ratio 0.72), a finding the authors call hypothesis-generating [28].

**Mineral oil placebo controversy:** The conflicting results of REDUCE-IT and STRENGTH may partly reflect their placebo oils, mineral oil in REDUCE-IT and corn oil in STRENGTH [29]. In STRENGTH, people with the highest blood EPA levels had no lower risk than those on corn oil [30].

**End-stage kidney disease (n=1,228, 3.5 years):** In adults on hemodialysis, 4 g a day of fish oil reduced serious cardiovascular events by 43% compared with corn oil (hazard ratio 0.57) [31].

**Japan EPA Lipid Intervention Study (n=18,645, 4.6 years):** In an open-label trial in people with high cholesterol, 1,800 mg of EPA daily with a statin reduced major coronary events by 19% compared with a statin alone [32]. RESPECT-EPA, another open-label Japanese trial of EPA in stable coronary disease, missed statistical significance for its main outcome (9.1% vs 12.6%; P=0.055) [33].

**Meta-analysis (127,477 participants, 13 trials):** A 2019 review found that marine omega-3 supplements lowered the risk of myocardial infarction (rate ratio 0.92), coronary heart disease death and total cardiovascular disease, in a dose-related way, but did not lower stroke [34].

**Cochrane Review (162,796 participants, 86 RCTs):** A 2020 review found little or no effect on all-cause death, cardiovascular events (risk ratio 0.96) or stroke, a possible small reduction in coronary heart disease deaths and events, and a fall in triglycerides of about 15% [35].

**Mayo Clinic meta-analysis (135,267 participants, 40 studies):** Omega-3 supplements lowered the risk of myocardial infarction by 13% (risk ratio 0.87, high certainty), fatal myocardial infarction by 35% (0.65) and coronary heart disease events (0.90). The composite of all cardiovascular disease events was not significantly lower (risk ratio 0.95, 0.90 to 1.00) [36].

#### Heart Failure

In a VITAL substudy, omega-3 lowered first hospital admission for heart failure in people with type 2 diabetes (hazard ratio 0.69) but not in those without it (1.09) [37].

#### Post-Heart Attack Recovery

In the OMEGA-REMODEL trial, 6 months of high-dose omega-3 after a heart attack (n=180, vs 178 on placebo) reduced left ventricular systolic volume by 5.8% and scarring outside the heart attack by 5.6% on MRI [38]. In OMEMI, omega-3 in older people after a heart attack did not reduce cardiovascular events (21.4% vs 20.0%) [39].

#### Statins and Fish Oil

REDUCE-IT, STRENGTH and JELIS all gave omega-3 on top of a statin, with the mixed results above [2][27][32]. The American Heart Association says 4 g a day of prescription omega-3 lowers triglycerides on its own or added to other lipid-lowering drugs [11].

#### Summary of Cardiovascular Evidence

- In people at usual risk, fish oil supplements did not lower major cardiovascular events in VITAL or ASCEND [24][25]
- Meta-analyses disagree: two found fewer heart attacks [36][34], and the Cochrane review found little or no effect on cardiovascular events [35]
- High-dose prescription omega-3 (4 g a day) lowers triglycerides, and icosapent ethyl cut events in REDUCE-IT [11][2]
- The American Heart Association does not recommend omega-3 supplements for people without high cardiovascular risk [1]
- Brad reads VITAL and the Mayo Clinic meta-analysis as showing real benefits and finds the evidence of heart protection compelling, but he avoids the higher doses linked to atrial fibrillation [5]

### Triglyceride Reduction

According to an American Heart Association advisory, prescription omega-3s at 4 g a day (EPA+DHA or EPA alone) lower triglycerides by 30% or more in very high triglycerides. EPA+DHA products raised LDL cholesterol at that dose, while EPA alone did not [11].

A free fatty acid prescription product lowered triglycerides by 25.9% to 30.9% at 2 to 4 g a day, compared with 4.3% for olive oil [16]. Icosapent ethyl at 4 g a day lowered triglycerides by a placebo-corrected 33.1% in very high triglycerides without raising LDL cholesterol [14].

At lower doses the effect is smaller. An earlier analysis of 58 trials found that each 1 g a day of long-chain omega-3 lowered triglycerides by 5.9 mg/dL, more in people with higher levels [1]. A 2023 meta-analysis of 90 trials found that triglycerides and non-HDL cholesterol fell almost in a straight line with dose [40].

### Depression and Anxiety

#### Depression Treatment in Adults

Omega-3 may help as an add-on to antidepressants. In a meta-analysis, adjunctive omega-3 (mainly EPA) had a moderate to strong effect over placebo [41]. An expert guideline suggests 1 to 2 g a day of net EPA, from pure EPA or a mix with at least twice as much EPA as DHA, for major depression [42]. A 2015 Cochrane review found insufficient evidence for omega-3 in major depression in adults [1].

#### Depression Prevention

In VITAL, 1 g a day for 5.3 years did not prevent depression in 18,353 adults; depression was slightly more common with omega-3 (hazard ratio 1.13) [43].

#### Depression in Children and Adolescents

In a trial of 257 children and teens with major depression, omega-3 added to psychotherapy did no better than placebo over 36 weeks [44].

#### Anxiety

An analysis of 19 trials found fish oil was linked to a modest reduction in anxiety, more so in people with a clinical diagnosis, and only at 2,000 mg a day or more [45].

#### Stress

In 67 adults without high blood pressure, 8 weeks of fish oil blunted heart rate and nerve responses to mental stress, but not blood pressure responses [46]. In adults with chronic work stress, 2.2 g a day of EPA for 12 weeks did not reduce perceived stress [47].

### Cognitive Function and Alzheimer's Disease

#### Age-Related Cognitive Decline

Trials in older adults without cognitive problems have mostly found no benefit [1]. In AREDS2, 1 g a day for 5 years did not affect cognitive function [48], and in MAPT, 3 years of omega-3 did not slow cognitive decline [49]. A meta-analysis of 38 trials (49,757 people) found little or no effect on new dementia or cognitive impairment [50]. In a 2026 trial, 2 g a day of DHA for 2 years in older adults with low omega-3 did not change brain volume or cognition, though 38% dropped out [51].

An observational study from the ADNI cohort, published in 2026, linked omega-3 supplement use with faster cognitive decline [52]. It was not a trial. Brad notes it measured neither oil oxidation nor B-vitamin status [53].

The World Health Organization's 2026 guideline on reducing the risk of cognitive decline and dementia recommends against omega-3 supplements for that purpose. It is a strong recommendation with moderate-certainty evidence, and it applies only to people without a diagnosed deficiency [54]. WHO's stated reason is "unexpected harmful effects that outweigh any potential benefits" [54]. The guideline says the effects of supplements were not reassessed in the 2026 update [54].

Omega-3 may matter alongside B vitamins. In VITACOG, B vitamins slowed brain shrinkage by 40% only in people with high omega-3 levels at the start [55]. In the Dutch FACIT trial, folic acid's cognitive benefit was larger in people with low omega-3 levels [56]. Omega-3 was measured, not randomised, in both trials.

#### Mild Cognitive Impairment

In mild cognitive impairment, omega-3s may improve some aspects of cognition, such as attention, processing speed and immediate recall, but this needs confirming in more trials [1].

#### Alzheimer's Disease

Fish oil on its own has not been shown to treat Alzheimer's disease. In people with mild to moderate Alzheimer's, 2 g a day of DHA from algae for 18 months did not slow decline [57]. In the OmegAD trial, people whose homocysteine was below 11.7 micromol/L, a sign of good B-vitamin status, did better on omega-3: cognitive scores rose 7.1% and a dementia rating scale improved 22.3% compared with placebo. This was a later subgroup analysis, and it suggests good B-vitamin status may be needed for omega-3 to help [58].

### Rheumatoid Arthritis and Inflammatory Conditions

#### Rheumatoid Arthritis

In a meta-analysis of marine oil trials, pain fell a little in rheumatoid arthritis (22 trials), with moderate-quality evidence [59]. Omega-3s may help as an add-on to standard treatment, and in some trials people needed fewer anti-inflammatory drugs [1]. In VITAL, 1 g a day lowered autoimmune disease by 15%, a difference that was not statistically significant (hazard ratio 0.85) [60].

#### Osteoarthritis

Marine oils did not reduce osteoarthritis pain in the same analysis [59]. In a 2024 trial, 2 g a day of krill oil for 24 weeks did not improve knee pain [61]. A 2025 meta-analysis of 5 krill oil trials (730 people) found benefits on the WOMAC knee scores for pain (standardized mean difference -0.60), stiffness and function, but no improvement on a visual analogue pain scale [62].

#### Gout

Eating two or more servings of omega-3-rich fish was linked to fewer gout flares, but omega-3 supplements were not [63].

#### Ulcerative Colitis and Crohn's Disease

A review of 19 trials found no support for omega-3 supplements to treat inflammatory bowel disease, and a Cochrane review concluded they are probably not effective for keeping Crohn's disease in remission [1].

### Cancer

#### Prevention

In VITAL, fish oil did not reduce the overall risk of cancer [24], or of breast, prostate or colorectal cancer [1]. In DO-HEALTH, 1 g a day in adults aged 70 and over gave a hazard ratio for invasive cancer of 0.70, a difference that was not statistically significant, with 81 cancers in total [64].

Evidence for prostate cancer is mixed. Men in the top quarter of blood long-chain omega-3 levels in the SELECT study had a higher risk of low-grade (hazard ratio 1.44) and high-grade (1.71) prostate cancer [9]. This is an association. Fish oil use was not linked to prostate cancer in a cohort of 35,239 men [65], and a meta-analysis of diet and blood-level studies found no link with total prostate cancer [10]. NIH finds no consistent relationship between omega-3 and prostate cancer [1]. In men on active surveillance, a high omega-3 diet with fish oil for a year lowered a marker of tumour cell division (Ki-67), but not grade or PSA; four men stopped because of side effects from the fish oil [66].

#### During Chemotherapy

Fish oil, and herring and mackerel, contain a fatty acid, 16:4(n-3), that made tumours resistant to chemotherapy in mice. The researchers suggest that fish oil and these fish may best be avoided on the days around chemotherapy [4]. If you are having chemotherapy, check with your oncologist before taking fish oil.

### Pregnancy and Infant Development

#### Preterm Birth

A Cochrane review of 70 trials (19,927 women) found omega-3 in pregnancy reduced preterm birth (risk ratio 0.89) and early preterm birth before 34 weeks (risk ratio 0.58) [67]. In a later Australian trial of 5,486 pregnancies, omega-3 did not reduce early preterm birth (2.2% vs 2.0%) [68].

A 2024 clinical practice guideline from an international expert panel advises women who could become pregnant to get at least 250 mg a day of DHA plus EPA and, in pregnancy, at least 100 to 200 mg a day more of DHA; women with low DHA intake should get about 600 to 1,000 mg a day, starting no later than about 20 weeks [69]. The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists advises 8 to 12 ounces of lower-mercury seafood a week in pregnancy [1].

#### Allergy and Asthma in Offspring

In a Danish trial, fish oil from 24 weeks of pregnancy reduced persistent wheeze or asthma in the children: 16.9% vs 23.7% with placebo [70]. In the same trial, fish oil did not change the overall risk of eczema by the time the children were 10 years old, but risk was higher in children of the 6% of mothers with the CC form of the COX1 gene (hazard ratio 5.77) and lower with the common TT form (0.70) [71]. A Cochrane review found limited evidence that omega-3 in pregnancy or breastfeeding reduces allergy in children [1].

#### Respiratory Infection in Infants

In a trial in Mexico, infants whose mothers took 400 mg of DHA a day in pregnancy had fewer colds at 1 month (odds ratio 0.76); the effects on other symptoms at 3 and 6 months were mixed [72].

#### Cognitive Effects

Taking DHA in pregnancy does not appear to improve a child's later cognition. In an Australian trial of 2,399 women, 800 mg a day of DHA did not improve cognitive or language scores at 18 months [73]. If you are pregnant, check with your doctor or midwife before taking fish oil supplements.

#### Fertility

In a small trial in 34 women with infertility linked to polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS), omega-3 (3 × 600 mg a day) during ovulation induction gave more clinical pregnancies in overweight or obese women: 29.6% of treatment cycles vs 5.3% with placebo [74].

### Dry Eye

Clinical trials of omega-3 for dry eye have had mixed results, and the largest found no benefit [1]. In the DREAM trial, 3,000 mg a day of EPA plus DHA for 12 months was no better than olive oil: symptom scores fell 13.9 and 12.5 points [75]. In VITAL, 1 g a day did not prevent dry eye in 23,523 adults [1].

### Retinal Disease

People who eat more fatty fish have a lower risk of age-related macular degeneration in observational studies [1]. But adding 1 g of EPA and DHA to the AREDS formula did not slow progression to advanced disease [76], and in VITAL fish oil did not reduce it (hazard ratio 0.94) [77].

### Liver Disease (MASLD)

A meta-analysis of 22 trials (1,366 people) found omega-3 supplements reduced liver fat and lowered triglycerides in fatty liver disease [78]. But in a 2024 trial in 203 adults with MASLD, 1.89 g of DHA alone, taken daily for 6 months, did not significantly reduce liver fat compared with placebo [79]. Brad's 2026 post says the cleanest trials show no real effect on liver fat [80].

### Migraine Headache

In 182 adults with migraine, a diet that raised EPA and DHA cut total headache hours per day by 1.3 and headache days per month by 2.0 compared with a control diet. A diet that also cut the omega-6 fat linoleic acid did more: 1.7 fewer headache hours per day and 4.0 fewer headache days per month. Quality of life did not improve significantly [81].

### Muscle, Strength, and Falls

#### Older Adults

In 60 healthy older adults, 6 months of fish oil omega-3 increased thigh muscle volume by 3.6% compared with corn oil [82]. In DO-HEALTH, among 2,157 healthy, active adults aged 70 and over, 1 g a day of omega-3 over 3 years reduced total falls by 10%, which the authors call a possible modest benefit [83].

#### Younger Adults

In young women, 5 g a day of omega-3, started 4 weeks before 2 weeks of leg immobilization, limited the loss of muscle volume (14 vs 8% with sunflower oil and omega-3) [84]. In soccer players, 4 weeks of omega-3 during training did not improve strength, power or speed, though distance on an endurance running test improved [85].

### Insulin Sensitivity and Blood Sugar

In 68 overweight adults without diabetes, 2 g a day of DHA-rich fish oil for 12 weeks lowered fasting insulin and insulin resistance slightly [86]. A krill and salmon oil blend had the opposite effect in one trial (see Krill Oil and Insulin Sensitivity below) [87].

### Testosterone and Fertility

In the same trial, DHA-rich fish oil raised total testosterone in men but not women [88].

### Epilepsy

In a small crossover trial in 24 people with drug-resistant epilepsy, low-dose fish oil (1,080 mg EPA+DHA a day) reduced seizures by 33.6% compared with placebo; a higher dose did not [89].

### ADHD and Cognitive Function in Children

A meta-analysis of 10 studies in children with ADHD found no overall improvement in behaviour, though higher-quality studies showed some benefit [1]. In a 12-week trial in 92 young people with ADHD, 1.2 g a day of EPA improved focused attention, most in those with low EPA levels, but impulsivity improved less than on placebo [90]. An expert panel concluded that omega-3s may have a small benefit on behaviour in children with ADHD, but should not replace better-proven treatments [91].

### Psychosis and Schizophrenia

In NEURAPRO, 1.4 g a day of omega-3 did not prevent psychosis in 304 young people at ultra-high risk; the trial failed to repeat an earlier positive result [92].

### Other Conditions

A Cochrane review found omega-3 might improve lung function in cystic fibrosis, but not enough to recommend it routinely [1]. For other conditions, there is no good public evidence yet.

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### General Population

The American Heart Association recommends one to two seafood meals a week [1]. Brad takes about 1 g of omega-3 a day [53] and avoids higher doses because atrial fibrillation risk appears to rise with dose [5].

### Dosing by Indication

The doses below are those used in trials or advised by expert groups for each condition. Atrial fibrillation risk appears to rise with dose (see Atrial Fibrillation below), so a dose above the general range is a decision to make with your doctor [93].

**General cardiovascular support:** The AHA does not recommend omega-3 supplements for people without a high cardiovascular risk. For people with existing coronary heart disease, it recommends about 1 g/day of EPA plus DHA, preferably from oily fish, with supplements an option under a doctor's direction [1].

**Triglyceride reduction:** The AHA says prescription omega-3s at 4 g a day lower triglycerides, used alone or with other lipid-lowering drugs [11]. This is a prescription treatment, used under a doctor's care.

**Depression (adjunctive therapy):** An expert guideline suggests 1 to 2 g of net EPA a day, from pure EPA or a mix with at least twice as much EPA as DHA [42].

**Rheumatoid arthritis pain:** A meta-analysis of marine oil trials found a small reduction in pain in rheumatoid arthritis, with evidence of moderate quality [59].

**Pregnancy (preterm birth prevention):** The 2024 international guideline above advises at least 250 mg a day of DHA plus EPA, at least 100 to 200 mg a day more of DHA in pregnancy, and about 600 to 1,000 mg a day for women with low DHA intake, starting no later than about 20 weeks [69]. A register study gives some support to stopping omega-3 in late pregnancy because of postpartum hemorrhage risk [8].

**Cognitive function in older adults:** Trials have not shown a dose that prevents cognitive decline, and WHO advises against omega-3 supplements for that purpose [50][54].

**Migraine prevention:** The trial above used diets that raised EPA and DHA, with or without less linoleic acid [81].

**Epilepsy:** A small trial used 1,080 mg EPA+DHA a day; a higher dose was no better than placebo [89].

### How to Take

- **Take with a fatty meal** to improve absorption, especially of ethyl ester products: in one study a high-fat meal tripled absorption of EPA and DHA from fish oil ethyl esters [20].

### Omega-3 Index Testing

The omega-3 index (EPA+DHA as a percentage of red blood cell fatty acids) reflects intake over about 120 days [1]. Cohort data support under 4% and over 8% as targets [13]. Most clinicians do not test omega-3 status, and normal ranges have not been set [1].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Common Side Effects

Commonly reported side effects of omega-3 supplements are usually mild [1]. They include:
- Unpleasant taste and bad breath
- Diarrhea
- Heartburn, nausea or stomach discomfort
- Headache and smelly sweat

In STRENGTH, gastrointestinal side effects were more common at 4 g a day (24.7% vs 14.7%) [27]. In 10 trials of 994 adults aged 60 years or more, with daily doses from 0.03 g to 1.86 g of EPA and/or DHA, no serious adverse events were reported [94].

### Upper Limit

The IOM did not set an upper limit for omega-3s [1]. The European Food Safety Authority judged up to about 5 g a day of EPA and DHA safe long term, and the FDA concluded supplements giving no more than 5 g a day are safe; the FDA also specifies that supplement labels should not recommend a daily intake of EPA and DHA higher than 2 g [1]. Two large trials later found that 4 g a day for several years slightly raised atrial fibrillation [1].

Higher doses may suppress the immune system. The IOM noted that 900 mg/day of EPA plus 600 mg/day of DHA or more for several weeks might reduce immune function [1]. A review of animal studies found that EPA and DHA intake dampened immunity and could lower survival from infections [95].

### Atrial Fibrillation

Fish oil supplements may raise the risk of atrial fibrillation [93]:

- In randomized trials of cardiovascular outcomes, omega-3 supplements were linked to a 25% higher risk of atrial fibrillation overall; the hazard ratio was 1.49 in trials testing more than 1 g a day and 1.12 in trials testing 1 g a day or less [93]
- VITAL on its own found no significant rise at 1 g a day (3.7% vs 3.4%; hazard ratio 1.09) [96]
- In a UK Biobank cohort of people free of cardiovascular disease, regular fish oil use was associated with a 13% higher risk of new atrial fibrillation and a 5% higher risk of stroke over a median 11.9 years [97]
- REDUCE-IT: hospital admission for atrial fibrillation or flutter in 3.1% vs 2.1% with placebo [2]
- STRENGTH, like REDUCE-IT, found a slight increase in atrial fibrillation risk at 4 g a day [1]
- In older people after a heart attack, 1.8 g of omega-3 (930 mg EPA and 660 mg DHA) showed a trend toward more atrial fibrillation (7.2% vs 4.0%; P=0.06) [39]
- In a Japanese trial, 1,800 mg a day of icosapent ethyl doubled new-onset atrial fibrillation (3.1% vs 1.6%) [33]
- In a VITAL substudy, 1 g a day for 2 years lengthened the PR interval and P-wave duration on the ECG [98]

### Bleeding Risk

Fish oil may have a blood-thinning effect at high doses: 3.24 g EPA and 2.16 g DHA per day slightly prolonged bleeding time and modestly reduced platelet aggregation [99]. The IOM noted that doses of 2 to 15 g/day of EPA and/or DHA might increase bleeding time [1]. A review found that clinical trials have not shown more bleeding with omega-3s, even alongside aspirin or warfarin [100].

In REDUCE-IT, serious bleeding occurred in 2.7% on 4 g a day of icosapent ethyl and 2.1% on placebo (P=0.06) [2]. In an analysis of US adverse event reports, icosapent ethyl alone may not increase the risk of bleeding unless combined with antithrombotic drugs such as blood thinners [101].

Fish oil might prolong clotting time (a raised INR) when taken with warfarin, and the prescribing information for prescription omega-3s advises periodic INR checks for people on anticoagulants [1]. In a small trial, 3 to 6 g a day did not change the INR in people on warfarin [102]. One case report describes an older patient on warfarin and fish oil whose blood thinning could not be reversed after a head injury, with a fatal outcome [103].

A systematic review of 52 studies found fish oil did not increase bleeding during or after surgery [104], and in a trial in 1,516 heart surgery patients, 8 to 10 g of fish oil before surgery did not increase major bleeding [105].

A case report suggests high-dose fish oil may be linked to bleeding at the back of the eye. A 32-year-old man with myopia had two macular hemorrhages while taking a high dose of fish oil; after he stopped, the second hemorrhage resolved over 8 weeks [106].

### Pregnancy Concerns

In a large Swedish hospital register study, omega-3 use in early pregnancy was associated with 25% higher odds of postpartum hemorrhage and more than double the odds of massive postpartum hemorrhage. The authors say this gives some support to stopping omega-3 in late pregnancy [8].

### LDL Cholesterol

High-dose fish oil may increase LDL cholesterol in some people: in very high triglycerides, 4 g/day of prescription EPA+DHA raised LDL cholesterol, whereas EPA-only did not [11]. DHA may be more likely to raise LDL than EPA. A meta-analysis found that algal DHA raised LDL cholesterol as well as HDL cholesterol [107]. In a trial giving 4 g of purified EPA, DHA or olive oil daily, DHA raised LDL by 8%, while EPA did not [108].

### Krill Oil and Insulin Sensitivity

In overweight men, five 1 g capsules a day of a krill and salmon oil blend for 8 weeks lowered insulin sensitivity by 14% compared with canola oil (27% after accounting for the rise in blood EPA and DHA), and the authors say krill oil could worsen diabetes and cardiovascular risk in overweight adults [87].

### Fish Allergy

Fish, krill and calamari contain proteins that some people are allergic to. A small study of six fish-allergic patients found no reaction to fish oil supplements [6], though at least one case report describes recurrent anaphylaxis after taking fish oil capsules [7]. If you are allergic to fish or shellfish, check with your doctor before taking fish or krill oil.

### Contaminants

Omega-3 supplements have not been found to contain mercury, because it is removed in processing [1]. In a 2003 analysis of 5 fish oil brands, mercury ranged from undetectable to negligible [109]. Purification also reduces other toxins found in fish, such as PCBs and dioxins [100], and PCB intakes from 37 Japanese fish oil supplements were far below the WHO tolerable daily intake [110]. Fish oil supplement use was not associated with higher blood PFAS levels in a US national survey [111].

Cod liver oil supplements also provide vitamin A and vitamin D [1]. Too much vitamin A may harm the liver [12], so if you take cod liver oil with other supplements that contain vitamin A, check the total with your doctor or pharmacist.

### Hair Loss

A study in mice fed a high-fat diet based on fish oil found hair loss; it does not show the same happens at normal supplement doses in people [112]. In a Japanese study of people with alopecia areata, higher retinol (vitamin A) intake predicted more severe hair loss [113].

### Practical Notes on Dietary Omega-3s

- **Farmed fish** usually have higher EPA+DHA content than wild-caught fish, but it depends on the food they are fed [1]
- **Grass-fed beef** contains somewhat more omega-3s (mainly ALA) than grain-fed beef, but amounts are very low [1]
- **DHA-fortified foods**, such as some brands of eggs, yogurt, juices and milk, contain added DHA and other omega-3s [1]
- **Mercury concern:** Seafood contains varying levels of methylmercury, and people who are or may become pregnant are advised to choose lower-mercury fish such as salmon, sardines, oysters and trout [1]
- **AHA recommendation:** 1 to 2 seafood meals a week to reduce the risk of heart failure, coronary heart disease, ischemic stroke and sudden cardiac death [1]
