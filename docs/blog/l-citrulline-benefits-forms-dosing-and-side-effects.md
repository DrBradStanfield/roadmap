## Current view (reviewed 9 November 2026)

Brad does not recommend L-citrulline, or any other nitric oxide supplement, and does not sell one; when people ask, his answer is dietary: nitrate-rich vegetables such as rocket (arugula) (Brad's current guidance, 10 Aug 2026). He notes that L-citrulline is converted to L-arginine after you swallow it and raises blood levels of L-arginine more effectively than L-arginine itself ([Brad, 18 Mar 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-2-food-beats-every-nitric-oxide-supplement)). For exercise, he calls the research underwhelming: a recent study in healthy young adults found that L-citrulline did not increase time to exhaustion ([Brad, 18 Mar 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-2-food-beats-every-nitric-oxide-supplement)). A 2019 meta-analysis found that L-citrulline seems to slightly reduce blood pressure when the dose was at least 6 g a day, which Brad describes as not really much to get excited about ([Brad, 18 Mar 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-2-food-beats-every-nitric-oxide-supplement)).

## Key safety points

- L-citrulline may lower blood pressure. Ask your doctor or pharmacist first if you take blood pressure medicines, nitrates such as nitroglycerin, or erectile dysfunction drugs such as sildenafil or tadalafil; no trial has tested these combinations [6][18].
- If you have low blood pressure, or surgery planned, talk to your doctor first for the same reason [6][18].
- Trials in pulmonary hypertension and heart failure were small and short, and the kidney does most of the conversion to arginine [3][25][26][33]. Ask your doctor first if you have one of these conditions or kidney disease, or are pregnant, breastfeeding or buying for a child.
- High blood citrulline is one of the tests used to diagnose citrullinemia type I, so a supplement may confuse testing for urea cycle disorders [34].
- Single doses up to 15 g caused no side effects in a small study, and trials have used 10 g a day for 4 to 8 weeks [3][22][23].
- Stomach discomfort is the main reported side effect, in 14.63% of men in one trial of 8 g citrulline malate [16].

## 1. Overview

L-citrulline is a non-protein amino acid. It is an intermediate of the urea cycle, and the body recycles it into L-arginine, the substrate for making nitric oxide [1]. Watermelon (*Citrullus vulgaris*) is a rich natural source [35].

Oral L-arginine is largely removed by the gut and liver before it reaches the blood. L-citrulline is not, so supplementing it raises blood arginine more effectively [1][4]. A 2019 review estimated that citrulline is about twice as potent as arginine at raising plasma arginine, most likely because less of it is lost to first-pass metabolism [5]. The kidneys make most of the arginine derived from citrulline, and at the highest doses this step appears to saturate [2][3].

Nitric oxide widens blood vessels. That is the reason L-citrulline has been tested for blood pressure, exercise and erectile dysfunction [1][4].

Raising nitric oxide markers does not always change outcomes. In a 1-week crossover trial in 20 healthy volunteers, 3 g twice a day raised urinary nitrate and cGMP, but no dose improved flow-mediated dilation of the brachial artery [4].

Overall, human trials show little benefit for exercise, a small and inconsistent effect on blood pressure, and one small, non-randomized trial in mild erectile dysfunction. The sections below give the detail.

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### L-Citrulline vs. L-Citrulline Malate

L-citrulline is sold on its own or bound to malic acid as citrulline malate. Part of each gram of citrulline malate is malate, so it supplies less citrulline per gram than pure L-citrulline. In one recent trial, the citrulline malate dose held 5.3 g of citrulline and 2.7 g of malate [10]. The blood pressure, vascular and erectile dysfunction trials below mostly used pure L-citrulline [18][22][27].

Adding malate does not seem to help. In a crossover trial of 43 trained young adults (21 women), 5.3 g of citrulline taken 45 minutes before training, with or without 2.7 g of malate, improved neither strength-endurance nor repetitions compared with placebo [10]. A 2021 critical review judged the benefit of citrulline malate for exercise ambiguous [12].

### "2:1" Labeling Confusion

Products sold as "2:1" citrulline malate do not always hold what the label implies. A 2018 trial used a product that analysis showed had a 1.11:1 ratio, and its authors warned of a gap between their chemical analyses of the products they reviewed and the manufacturers' claims [8]. A 2021 review also noted quality control problems with some manufacturers' stated citrulline:malate ratios [12]. Choose a product that an independent lab has tested.

### Bioavailability and Pharmacokinetics

Citrulline is efficiently absorbed by transporters in the small intestine, and the gut and liver do not take it up [2]. In 8 healthy men given single doses of 2 to 15 g, plasma arginine peaked 1.17 to 2.29 hours after the dose, and urinary loss of citrulline stayed below 5% [3]. At the highest doses citrulline built up in the blood while arginine rose less than expected, which may reflect saturation of the kidney's conversion step [3].

In 20 healthy volunteers, a week of citrulline raised plasma arginine more effectively than arginine did [4]. In a trial of 10 men, 6 g a day of either citrulline or arginine for 7 days raised plasma arginine by a similar amount [6].

### Summary of Form Selection

| Form | What it is | Evidence |
|------|------------|----------|
| Pure L-citrulline | Citrulline alone | Used in most blood pressure, vascular and erectile dysfunction trials [18][22][27] |
| Citrulline malate | Citrulline bound to malic acid | Mostly tested as a single dose before exercise; malate added nothing in a head-to-head trial [10][12] |
| "2:1" citrulline malate | Ratio varies by product | Lab analysis has found products that differ from label claims [8] |

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Exercise Endurance and Performance

L-citrulline has been tested for exercise on the idea that more nitric oxide improves blood flow to working muscle [12]. Results are mixed, and most trials are small.

**Multi-day supplementation:**

In a crossover trial of 10 healthy men, 6 g a day of citrulline for 7 days improved tolerance to severe-intensity cycling (661 vs 589 seconds with placebo) and slightly increased total work in a performance test (125 vs 123 kJ). The same dose of arginine did not [6].

**Single-dose supplementation:**

- In 12 recreationally trained adults (mean age 24), a single 8 g dose of citrulline malate did not change peak torque, peak power or rate of fatigue during high-intensity exercise [7].
- In a double-blind trial in recreationally active men and women, 8 g of citrulline malate did not increase repetitions over 10 sets of 10 (90.9 vs 94.0 with placebo), and total muscle soreness over 72 hours was higher than with placebo [8].
- In 30 young male lifters, a drink with 3 g of arginine and 8 g of citrulline malate did not change biceps perfusion, size or muscle pump compared with placebo. This was an exploratory feasibility trial [9].
- In 43 trained young adults, 5.3 g of citrulline with or without malate did not improve maximal strength, power or repetitions [10].
- In 21 recreationally active CrossFit participants, 4.4 g of citrulline malate taken 60 minutes before the "Cindy" workout did not change the number of rounds completed (13.5 vs 13.8 with placebo) [17].
- An older trial in 41 men found the opposite: a single 8 g dose of citrulline malate increased bench-press repetitions to fatigue at 80% of one-repetition maximum and cut muscle soreness by 40% at 24 and 48 hours [16].

**Older adults:**

In 44 adults aged 60 to 73 on an exercise programme, 6 weeks of citrulline improved walking speed compared with placebo. Strength and endurance tended to rise, with no significant difference from placebo [11].

**Meta-analyses and systematic reviews:**

A 2019 meta-analysis of 12 studies (198 people) found a small benefit of a single citrulline dose for strength and power, with a standardized mean difference of 0.20; the confidence interval of every individual study crossed the line of no effect [13]. A 2026 meta-analysis of 30 randomized trials (644 people) found a small improvement with citrulline malate (g = 0.16), but prediction intervals were wide and GRADE certainty was low to very low [14]. A 2021 critical review concluded that the effect of citrulline malate on performance remains ambiguous [12].

**Muscle soreness and perceived exertion:**

A 2020 meta-analysis of 13 trials (206 people) found that citrulline taken up to 2 hours before exercise reduced ratings of perceived exertion and muscle soreness at 24 hours. It did not significantly reduce soreness at 48 or 72 hours, or blood lactate [15]. The most common dose in these trials was 8 g of citrulline malate [15]. One trial above found more soreness with citrulline malate [8], so the soreness evidence conflicts.

**Synthesis:** One small trial found that 6 g a day for a week helped exercise tolerance [6]. Single doses before exercise mostly failed, malate added nothing, and meta-analyses find at most a small effect with low certainty [7][8][10][13][14].

### Blood Pressure and Cardiovascular Health

Because citrulline raises arginine and nitric oxide markers, it has been tested for blood pressure. The meta-analyses disagree.

**Meta-analyses:**

- A 2019 meta-analysis of 8 trials (10 data sets of 12 to 34 people, 3 to 9 g a day for 1 to 17 weeks) found that systolic blood pressure fell by 4.10 mmHg. Diastolic pressure fell significantly only in trials that used 6 g a day or more, by 2.75 mmHg [18].
- A 2018 meta-analysis of 5 interventions found no effect on brachial or aortic blood pressure [19].
- A 2025 meta-analysis of 15 randomized trials in 415 middle-aged and older adults found that citrulline or watermelon lowered systolic pressure by 4.02 mmHg and diastolic pressure by 2.54 mmHg. It pooled citrulline with watermelon [20].
- A 2019 review put the likely effect of citrulline at 4.1/2.08 to 7.54/3.77 mmHg, and called its blood-pressure-lowering properties more questionable than those of arginine [5].
- In 10 healthy men, a week of 6 g a day lowered mean arterial pressure [6].

**Postmenopausal hypertension:**

In 25 postmenopausal women with hypertension, 10 g a day for 4 weeks raised serum arginine and brachial flow-mediated dilation compared with placebo. Resting aortic diastolic pressure and mean arterial pressure fell by 2 mmHg, against a 2 mmHg rise with placebo. Aortic stiffness fell by about 0.66 m/s, which was not statistically significant [22].

**Arterial stiffness:**

In 16 overweight young men, 6 g a day for 14 days had no effect at rest but blunted the rise in aortic blood pressure and arterial stiffness during handgrip exercise and cold exposure [24]. A 2025 meta-analysis of 8 trials in 176 middle-aged and older adults found that citrulline improved flow-mediated dilation (mean difference 1.81) but did not significantly change overall pulse wave velocity; a subgroup showed lower brachial-ankle pulse wave velocity [21]. In hypertensive postmenopausal women, 10 g a day for 4 weeks improved leg artery flow-mediated dilation, and adding slow resistance training for another 4 weeks increased leg lean mass (0.49 vs 0.07 kg) and leg curl strength more than training with placebo [23].

**Diastolic heart failure:**

In a trial in heart failure with preserved ejection fraction, 15 patients took 3 g a day of citrulline malate and 15 took 8 g a day of arginine for 2 months; there was no placebo group. Pulmonary artery pressure fell from 56.67 to 47.67 mmHg with citrulline. Treadmill time and right ventricular ejection fraction after exercise rose in both groups, and blood pressure fell [25].

**Systolic heart failure:**

In 35 outpatients with systolic heart failure, 3 g a day of citrulline for 4 months raised left ventricular ejection fraction by 20.3% at rest and 12.7% with stress, and right ventricular ejection fraction by 15.10% at rest, compared with an untreated control group. The trial had no placebo [26].

**Heart failure and atrial fibrillation risk:**

No trial in the public sources reviewed here tested whether citrulline prevents heart failure or atrial fibrillation.

**Combination therapies:**

In the 2025 meta-analysis, trials that combined citrulline with arginine showed larger falls (10.44 mmHg systolic, 4.86 mmHg diastolic) than trials of citrulline or watermelon alone [20].

**Use in older adults:**

In 21 men aged 65 to 80, 10 g of citrulline added to 15 g of whey protein did not increase leg blood flow or muscle microvascular perfusion after exercise [32]. The two 2025 meta-analyses in middle-aged and older adults are described above [20][21].

**Synthesis:** The meta-analyses disagree. Two found a fall in systolic pressure of about 4 mmHg [18][20], and one found none [19]. The trials are small and short, and the heart failure trials had no placebo [25][26].

### Erectile Dysfunction

The evidence rests on one small trial.

**Key clinical trial:**

In a single-blind trial in Italy, 24 men with mild erectile dysfunction (mean age 56.5) took placebo for 1 month and then 1.5 g a day of L-citrulline for 1 month. The order was fixed, not randomized. Erection hardness improved from a score of 3 to 4 in 12 men (50%) on citrulline and in 2 men (8.3%) on placebo, and intercourse rose from 1.37 to 2.3 times a month. No adverse events were reported [27].

**Mechanism:**

The rationale is that citrulline escapes presystemic metabolism and is converted to arginine, which feeds the nitric oxide pathway of erection [27].

**Comparison with PDE5 inhibitors:**

The trial authors judged citrulline less effective than PDE5 inhibitor drugs, at least in the short term [27]. No head-to-head trial has tested this.

**Comparison with L-arginine:**

No trial has compared citrulline with arginine for erectile dysfunction. The case for citrulline rests on its larger effect on blood arginine [4][5].

**Libido:**

There is no good public evidence on this yet.

**Limitations:**

The trial lasted 1 month, so long-term benefit and safety are unknown [27].

**Synthesis:** One small, non-randomized trial found that 1.5 g a day improved erection hardness in half of men with mild erectile dysfunction [27]. Larger randomized trials are needed.

### Brain Function and Neuroprotection

**Animal evidence:**

In mice given L-citrulline (50 to 100 mg/kg) for 10 days after blood flow to the brain was blocked, it prevented neuron death and capillary loss in the hippocampus and improved memory [28].

**Depression and anxiety:**

Serum citrulline was lower in 35 unmedicated people with major depression than in 36 healthy controls (31.58 vs 35.19 µmol/L) [29]. This is an association; no trial has tested citrulline for depression or anxiety.

**Synthesis:** The evidence comes from one mouse study and one observational study. There is no trial evidence of brain benefits in humans.

### Fatty Liver Disease

**Animal evidence:**

In rats fed a fructose diet for 4 weeks, citrulline prevented high blood triglycerides and reduced liver fat [30].

**Synthesis:** The evidence is from animals only. No human trial of citrulline for fatty liver was found in the public sources reviewed here.

### Ammonia Clearance and Urea Cycle Support

Citrulline is an intermediate of the urea cycle [1]. In citrullinemia type I, an inherited urea cycle disorder, plasma ammonia is high and plasma citrulline is usually above 500 µmol/L; daily treatment includes protein restriction, nitrogen scavenger medicines and arginine supplementation [34]. A 2021 review lists better ammonia handling as one possible way citrulline malate could aid exercise, but this is unproven [12].

### Muscle Protein Synthesis

A 2013 review describes citrulline as stimulating muscle protein synthesis through the mTOR pathway [2]. In 8 healthy adults on a 3-day low-protein diet, citrulline raised mixed muscle protein synthesis compared with a non-essential amino acid mix (fractional synthesis rate 0.060 vs 0.049) [31]. But in 21 men aged 65 to 80, 10 g of citrulline added to a low dose of whey protein was no better than non-essential amino acids after resistance exercise, and a high dose of whey alone worked better [32]. The evidence is small and conflicting.

### COVID-19 and Vascular Health

There is no good public evidence on this yet.

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### By Indication

These are the doses trials used, not recommendations.

| Purpose | Dose used in trials | Duration | Result |
|---------|---------------------|----------|--------|
| Blood pressure | 3 to 9 g a day of L-citrulline | 1 to 17 weeks | Systolic fall of about 4 mmHg in two meta-analyses, none in a third [18][19][20] |
| Vascular function in hypertensive postmenopausal women | 10 g a day of L-citrulline | 4 to 8 weeks | Better flow-mediated dilation [22][23] |
| Exercise, multi-day | 6 g a day of L-citrulline | 7 days | Better exercise tolerance in one trial of 10 men [6] |
| Exercise, single dose | 8 g of citrulline malate, or 5.3 g of citrulline | 45 to 60 minutes before | Mostly no benefit [7][8][10] |
| Mild erectile dysfunction | 1.5 g a day of L-citrulline | 1 month | Better hardness in 12 of 24 men [27] |
| Heart failure (no placebo) | 3 g a day | 2 to 4 months | Better ventricular function than no treatment [25][26] |

### Timing and Administration

- Plasma arginine peaked 1.17 to 2.29 hours after single doses [3].
- Single doses before exercise mostly failed in trials; the one positive multi-day trial gave citrulline for 7 days [6][7][8][10].
- In a 1-week trial, 3 g twice a day was the dose that raised trough arginine levels [4].

### How to Read a Supplement Label

- Check whether the label gives the amount of L-citrulline itself or the weight of citrulline malate.
- In one trial, citrulline malate that supplied 5.3 g of citrulline also carried 2.7 g of malate [10].
- Lab analysis of "2:1" products has not always matched the label [8].
- Choose a product that an independent lab has tested.

### Storage

There is no good public evidence on this yet.

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Safety Profile

In 8 healthy men, single doses of 2 to 15 g caused no side effects, and the authors judged short-term use safe and well tolerated [3]. Trials have given 10 g a day for 4 to 8 weeks [22][23], and a review describes tolerance of up to 15 g a day in healthy volunteers [1]. Trials in the 2019 blood pressure meta-analysis lasted 1 to 17 weeks [18], so long-term safety is not known.

### Common Side Effects

Stomach discomfort is the side effect most often reported: 14.63% of men had it after 8 g of citrulline malate in one trial [16]. A review notes the lack of gastrointestinal distress with citrulline compared with arginine, which can cause significant gut problems at high doses (about 13 g) [1]. Several trials reported no adverse events [9][27]. If you get heartburn or reflux after taking it, stop and ask your doctor or pharmacist.

### Specific Populations

**People with pulmonary arterial hypertension:** In 25 people with pulmonary arterial hypertension, 1 g of citrulline malate three times a day for 2 weeks increased 6-minute walk distance by about 44 m in an uncontrolled before-and-after study [33]. The study was small and short. If you have pulmonary hypertension, ask your doctor before trying it.

**People with low blood pressure:** Citrulline lowered mean arterial pressure in healthy men [6] and systolic pressure in a meta-analysis [18]. If you have low blood pressure, ask your doctor first.

**Urea cycle disorders:** Diagnosis of citrullinemia type I relies partly on high plasma citrulline, usually above 500 µmol/L [34]. A supplement may affect such tests, so people with a urea cycle disorder, or being tested for one, should talk to their specialist first.

**Pregnancy and pediatric use:** There is little public safety data on citrulline in pregnancy, breastfeeding or children. Ask your doctor first.

**Kidney disease:** The kidney does most of the conversion of citrulline to arginine [3]. If you have kidney disease, ask your doctor first.

**Surgery:** If you have surgery planned, tell your surgeon or anaesthetist that you take it, because it may lower blood pressure [6][18].

### Bleeding Risk

There is no good public evidence on this yet.

### Alcohol Interactions

There is no good public evidence on this yet.

## 6. Drug Interactions

No trial has tested L-citrulline with blood pressure medicines, nitrates or PDE5 inhibitors. Because citrulline may lower blood pressure [6][18][20], the effects could add up.

| Medicine | Examples | Possible concern |
|----------|----------|------------------|
| Blood pressure medicines | ACE inhibitors, ARBs, calcium channel blockers, beta-blockers | Extra fall in blood pressure [18][20] |
| Nitrates | Nitroglycerin, isosorbide | Extra fall in blood pressure [18][20] |
| PDE5 inhibitors | Sildenafil, tadalafil | Extra fall in blood pressure [18][20] |

### Key Points on Drug Interactions

- These cautions rest on the blood pressure effect of citrulline, not on interaction studies [6][18].
- If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

## 7. Dietary Sources

Watermelon is the best-known food source of citrulline [1][35].

### Top Food Sources

| Food | Citrulline | Source |
|------|------------|--------|
| Watermelon flesh | 1.9 mg/g fresh weight (16.7 mg/g dry weight) | [35] |
| Watermelon rind | 1.3 mg/g fresh weight (24.7 mg/g dry weight) | [35] |

### Practical Notes

- Across watermelon varieties, citrulline ranged from 3.9 to 28.5 mg/g dry weight, and yellow and orange flesh held more than red [35].
- On a fresh-weight basis the rind holds a little less citrulline than the flesh, though more on a dry-weight basis [35].
- Reaching the several grams a day used in trials from watermelon alone would take kilograms of fruit [35].
- In a 2025 meta-analysis, watermelon did not significantly improve flow-mediated dilation or pulse wave velocity in middle-aged and older adults, while citrulline supplements improved flow-mediated dilation [21].

### Endogenous Production

The body makes its own citrulline: it is formed by enzymes in the mitochondria [1]. Arginine is then made from citrulline mainly in the kidneys, and also in endothelial and immune cells [2].
