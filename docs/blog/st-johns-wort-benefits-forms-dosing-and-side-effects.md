## 1. Overview

St. John's wort (*Hypericum perforatum*) is a flowering plant that has been used medicinally for centuries across Greek, Islamic, and Chinese traditional medicine systems [1][2]. The plant's name refers to John the Baptist, as it blooms around the time of his feast day in late June [2]. Also known as hypericum, Klamath weed, and goatweed, St. John's wort is one of the most widely used herbal remedies worldwide, primarily for the treatment of mild to moderate depression [1][2][3].

The medicinal parts of the plant are the aerial portions -- the flowers, leaves, and stems (but not the roots) [1]. St. John's wort contains at least seven groups of biologically active constituents, including naphthodianthrons (e.g., hypericin), flavonoids (e.g., quercetin), bioflavonoids (e.g., biapigenin), xanthones, and phloroglucinol derivatives (e.g., hyperforin) [1][4]. Of these, hypericin and hyperforin have received the most research attention as potential active ingredients, though current evidence suggests the therapeutic effect arises from synergistic interactions among multiple compounds rather than the pharmacological activity of any single constituent [5][6][7].

The vast majority of double-blind, placebo-controlled studies have found St. John's wort extracts to be as effective as standard antidepressant medications -- including selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) such as fluoxetine (Prozac) -- for treating major depression of mild to moderate severity, and with significantly fewer side effects [8][9]. However, for severe major depression (often requiring hospitalization), standard antidepressants are considered more effective [1][3].

Despite its efficacy for mild-to-moderate depression, St. John's wort carries significant safety risks, particularly drug interactions. It can reduce the blood levels and effectiveness of a wide range of medications, including oral contraceptives, HIV antiretrovirals, immunosuppressants, chemotherapy agents, blood thinners, and statins [1][2][10][11]. Because of these interactions, anyone taking medications should consult a healthcare provider before using St. John's wort.

St. John's wort does not cause euphoria and, like conventional antidepressants, is unlikely to elevate mood in people who are not truly depressed [1]. As with standard antidepressants, it takes several weeks to achieve full therapeutic effects [1].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Extract vs. Whole Herb

St. John's wort products come in two primary forms: standardized extracts and whole herb (dried, powdered plant material). Virtually all clinical trials demonstrating efficacy have used standardized extracts rather than whole herb preparations [1][12].

| Form | Standardization | Typical Dose | Notes |
|------|----------------|:---:|-------|
| Standardized Extract (0.3% hypericin) | Contains approximately 0.3% hypericin | 300 mg three times daily (900 mg/day total) | Most studied form. The majority of positive clinical trials used this preparation [1][12]. |
| Standardized Extract (0.3% hypericin + 1-3% hyperforin) | Contains both hypericin and hyperforin standards | 300 mg three times daily (900 mg/day total) | Additionally standardized to hyperforin, which has been identified as a major antidepressant component [13]. Some trials used this dual-standardized preparation with positive results [1]. |
| Whole Herb (dried, powdered) | Contains 0.1-0.15% hypericin | 2-4 grams per day | Less studied. Products containing whole herb tend to have higher concentrations of heavy metals than extracts, as some contaminants may be removed during the extraction process [1]. |
| Liquid Extract / Tincture | Variable standardization | Per manufacturer directions | Less clinical data than solid extracts. Standardization varies widely between products [1]. |
| Topical Preparations (Oil/Cream) | Variable | Applied to skin | Promoted for wound healing and skin conditions. Limited clinical evidence for topical use [2][14]. |

### Active Constituents

**Hypericin** is the primary marker compound used for standardization in most commercial products and clinical trials. Products standardized to 0.3% hypericin content have consistently proven effective in human studies [1][12]. However, hypericin may not be the most pharmacologically important constituent [1][13].

**Hyperforin** has been identified as a major antidepressant component of St. John's wort [13]. It inhibits the reuptake of serotonin, norepinephrine, dopamine, GABA, and L-glutamate -- a broader mechanism than SSRIs, which primarily affect serotonin alone [5][13]. Some products are additionally standardized to 1-3% hyperforin content [1].

**Synergistic mechanism:** Newer research suggests that the benefit of St. John's wort is based on synergistic interactions of multiple compounds present in the plant rather than the pharmacological activity of any single compound [5][6][7]. Butterweck (2007) demonstrated that the extract's antidepressant properties emerge from the combined action of hypericin, hyperforin, flavonoids, and other constituents [5]. Reichling (2003) reached similar conclusions regarding the importance of the whole phytochemical profile [7].

### Bioavailability Considerations

When selecting a St. John's wort supplement, key factors include:

- **Standardization:** Look for products standardized to 0.3% hypericin as a minimum quality marker. Dual standardization to hyperforin (1-3%) provides additional assurance of active ingredient content [1].
- **Plant parts used:** Labels should indicate flowers and leaves (or "aerial portions"), not roots [1].
- **Extract vs. whole herb:** Standardized extracts are preferred over whole herb preparations based on the clinical trial evidence base and lower heavy metal risk [1].
- **Onset of action:** Like conventional antidepressants, St. John's wort takes several weeks of consistent use to achieve full therapeutic effects [1][2].

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Major Depression (Mild to Moderate)

The evidence for St. John's wort in mild to moderate major depression is substantial and represents the best-studied indication for this herb. Major depression of mild to moderate severity is a clinical diagnosis -- it refers to a more serious level of depression than mere "blues" or "moodiness" [1].

**Cochrane systematic review (2008):** Linde and colleagues conducted a comprehensive Cochrane review examining the totality of evidence for St. John's wort in major depression. The review concluded that St. John's wort extracts are superior to placebo in patients with major depression, that they are similarly effective as standard antidepressants, and that they have fewer side effects than standard antidepressants [8].

**Meta-analysis of efficacy vs. SSRIs (2023):** Zhao et al. conducted a meta-analysis of randomized clinical trials directly comparing St. John's wort extract with SSRIs in adults with depression. The analysis found that St. John's wort was comparable in efficacy to SSRIs for treating depression, with a significantly lower rate of adverse events [15]. This is consistent with earlier meta-analyses reaching similar conclusions [8][9].

**Meta-analysis (2017):** Ng, Venkatanarayanan, and Ho published a meta-analysis in the *Journal of Affective Disorders* examining the clinical use of St. John's wort in depression. They confirmed that St. John's wort demonstrated significant antidepressant effects compared to placebo and was non-inferior to standard antidepressants [16].

**Systematic review (2016):** Apaydin et al. conducted a systematic review for the *Evidence-based Practice Centers* and found consistent evidence supporting the efficacy of St. John's wort for major depressive disorder of mild to moderate severity [17].

**Head-to-head trial vs. paroxetine (2005):** Szegedi et al. published a landmark randomized controlled trial in the *BMJ* comparing St. John's wort extract (900 mg/day, escalating to 1,800 mg/day if needed) to the SSRI paroxetine (20 mg/day, escalating to 40 mg/day). In 251 adults with moderate to severe depression, St. John's wort was at least as effective as paroxetine, with the hyperforin-standardized extract showing a trend toward superiority. The remission rate was higher with St. John's wort (hyperforin group) than with paroxetine [9].

#### Negative Trials and Severe Depression

Three well-publicized studies found no benefit with St. John's wort, though two of these studies also found that the active comparator antidepressant was ineffective:

**Hypericum Depression Trial Study Group (2002):** This large, NIH-funded, multicenter trial compared St. John's wort to sertraline (Zoloft) and placebo in patients with moderate to severe major depression. Neither St. John's wort nor sertraline was significantly more effective than placebo, suggesting the study population or design may have been the issue rather than St. John's wort specifically [18].

**Shelton et al. (2001):** Published in *JAMA*, this trial found no benefit for St. John's wort compared to placebo in a sample of patients with major depression. However, the study has been criticized for potentially including patients with more severe depression than St. John's wort is indicated for [19].

These seemingly paradoxical clinical results may have been due to factors such as the specific products tested, the outcomes being measured, the severity of depression in the study populations, and the number of participants involved [1].

**Synthesis on severity:** The overall evidence pattern is clear -- St. John's wort is effective for mild to moderate major depression but should not be relied upon for severe major depression, where standard antidepressants are considered more effective [1][2][3]. The NCCIH notes that it is uncertain whether St. John's wort is effective for severe depression or for treatment periods longer than 12 weeks [2].

### Menopause Symptoms and Depression

**Combination with black cohosh (2006):** Uebelhack et al. published a study in *Obstetrics and Gynecology* examining the combination of St. John's wort and black cohosh extracts in menopausal women with depression. The combination significantly improved both menopause symptoms (including hot flashes) and mood compared to placebo [20].

**Postmenopausal depression (2019):** Eatemadnia et al. conducted an RCT examining *Hypericum perforatum* for postmenopausal symptoms and depression. The results demonstrated that St. John's wort effectively reduced depressive symptoms and improved quality of life in postmenopausal women [21].

**Meta-analysis of menopause symptoms (2014):** Liu et al. conducted a meta-analysis of *Hypericum perforatum* preparations for menopause, examining both efficacy and safety. The analysis found evidence supporting the herb's usefulness for menopausal complaints, particularly when combined with other herbal therapies [22].

**NCCIH assessment:** The NCCIH notes that small numbers of studies suggest St. John's wort may be helpful for hot flashes associated with menopause, though the overall evidence base remains limited [2].

### Somatic Symptom Disorder

The NCCIH reports that small numbers of studies suggest St. John's wort may be helpful for somatic symptom disorder, a condition involving intense anxiety about physical symptoms [2]. However, the evidence is considered preliminary and insufficient for definitive conclusions.

### Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)

The evidence for St. John's wort in ADHD is mixed and overall negative:

**Small open-label study in teens (2010):** Niederhofer published a study in *Natural Product Research* examining three male teens with ADHD. St. John's wort appeared to improve hyperactivity, inattention, and immaturity in this very small, open-label sample [23].

**Randomized controlled trial in children and teens (2008):** Weber et al. published a more rigorous RCT in *JAMA* examining 54 children and teens with ADHD. Taking 300 mg of St. John's wort (standardized to 0.3% hypericin) three times daily for 8 weeks was not beneficial compared to placebo [24].

Furthermore, St. John's wort may diminish the effectiveness of methylphenidate (Ritalin), a standard ADHD medication, creating a potential interaction concern for ADHD patients (Niederhofer, *Med Hypotheses*, 2007) [25]. The evidence does not support using St. John's wort for ADHD.

### Anxiety and Social Phobia

St. John's wort is sometimes tried for anxiety-related conditions given its mechanisms overlap with those of SSRIs, which are also used for anxiety disorders. However, there is no reliable evidence that it is effective for generalized anxiety, social phobia, or obsessive-compulsive disorder [1][2]. The NCCIH has reviewed the limited available studies and found the evidence insufficient to draw conclusions for these conditions [2].

**Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD):** Abrishami et al. (2022) reviewed the clinical evidence for herbal medicines in OCD treatment, including St. John's wort, but the evidence remained insufficient to support its use for this indication [26].

### Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD)

St. John's wort is sometimes recommended for seasonal affective disorder given its antidepressant properties. While the mechanistic rationale exists (SAD is a form of depression), no well-designed RCTs have specifically confirmed efficacy for this indication [1].

### Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS)

There is limited evidence exploring St. John's wort for PMS symptoms. While the antidepressant and mood-stabilizing properties suggest potential benefit for the emotional symptoms of PMS, the clinical evidence is insufficient to recommend it for this purpose [1][2].

### Smoking Cessation

**RCT (2009):** Parsons et al. conducted a proof-of-concept randomized placebo-controlled factorial trial examining St. John's wort for smoking cessation and chromium to prevent weight gain after quitting smoking. The results did not demonstrate significant benefit for smoking cessation [27].

**RCT (2010):** Sood et al. published a randomized clinical trial in the *Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine* examining St. John's wort for smoking cessation. The trial did not find significant improvement in quit rates compared to placebo [28].

The NCCIH notes that the evidence has not clearly shown St. John's wort to be helpful for smoking cessation [2].

### Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

Only a small amount of research has been done on St. John's wort for IBS. The NCCIH states that the evidence is insufficient to draw conclusions about its usefulness for this condition [2].

### Wound Healing (Topical Use)

Topical application of St. John's wort is promoted for wounds and other skin conditions [2].

**Review (2024):** Farasati Far et al. published a comprehensive literature review in *Phytotherapy Research* examining the potential role of *Hypericum perforatum* in wound healing. The review covered the phytochemical composition, pharmacological approaches, and mechanistic perspectives. While preclinical evidence is promising -- with anti-inflammatory and antimicrobial properties documented -- human clinical evidence remains limited [14].

The NCCIH notes that only a small amount of research has been done on topical St. John's wort for wound healing, and the evidence has not clearly shown it to be helpful [2].

### HIV Infection

St. John's wort was once popular as a treatment for HIV infection. However, current evidence demonstrates that not only does it not help, but the herb can seriously impair the effect of standard HIV treatments by reducing blood levels of protease inhibitors and non-nucleoside reverse transcriptase inhibitors [1][2]. St. John's wort should not be used by individuals taking HIV antiretroviral medications (see Drug Interactions section).

### Chronic Pain and Neuropathic Pain

St. John's wort is sometimes tried for chronic pain conditions, including neuropathic pain, given that standard antidepressants (SNRIs, tricyclics) are used for these conditions. However, there is no reliable clinical evidence supporting its use for pain management [1].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Standard Antidepressant Dosing

Based on the clinical trial evidence, the following dosing regimen has the strongest support:

| Preparation | Daily Dose | Frequency | Duration |
|------------|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| Standardized extract (0.3% hypericin) | 900 mg/day total | 300 mg three times daily | Minimum 4-6 weeks for initial effect; 8-12 weeks for full assessment [1][8][9] |
| Standardized extract (0.3% hypericin + 1-3% hyperforin) | 900 mg/day total | 300 mg three times daily | Same timeline as above [1][9] |
| Whole herb (dried, powdered) | 2-4 grams/day | Divided doses | Less clinical support than standardized extracts [1] |

### Important Dosing Notes

- **Time to effect:** Like conventional antidepressants, St. John's wort requires several weeks of consistent use to achieve full therapeutic effects. Patients should not expect immediate relief [1][2].
- **Dose escalation:** In the Szegedi et al. (2005) trial, the dose was escalated from 900 mg/day to 1,800 mg/day if needed after an initial assessment period [9]. Dose increases should only be made under medical supervision.
- **Do not self-treat serious depression:** Depression can be a serious illness. The NCCIH emphasizes that anyone who may have depression should consult a healthcare provider [2]. St. John's wort should not be used as a substitute for professional mental health care, particularly for moderate to severe depression.
- **Abrupt discontinuation:** Avoid abruptly stopping St. John's wort, which can lead to withdrawal symptoms including headache, nausea, insomnia, and fatigue [1]. Gradual tapering is recommended, similar to the approach used with conventional antidepressants.

### Dosing for Menopause

Limited data suggest the standard antidepressant dose (900 mg/day of standardized extract in divided doses) may also be appropriate for menopause-related symptoms, often in combination with black cohosh [20][22].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Common Side Effects

St. John's wort is generally safe when taken in appropriate amounts, with side effects typically being mild and infrequent [1][2]. The most commonly reported side effects include:

- Mild digestive distress (nausea, diarrhea)
- Headache
- Fatigue
- Anxiety
- Insomnia
- Skin rashes
- Dizziness

**Comparison with SSRIs:** An analysis of adverse drug reactions reported in Australia from 2000 to 2013 found that reactions for St. John's wort were similar in type to those for fluoxetine (Prozac). Most reactions were mild to moderate, including nausea, diarrhea, headache, fatigue, ear pain, and chest pain. Anxiety, panic attacks, dizziness, vomiting, amnesia, and aggression were also reported. The total number of reported events was greater for fluoxetine than for St. John's wort (447 vs. 84), although fluoxetine use was estimated to be approximately double that of St. John's wort. The researchers noted that adverse reactions are more likely to be reported for prescription drugs than for dietary supplements (Hoban et al., *Clin Exp Pharmacol Physiol*, 2015) [29].

### Serious Side Effects

**Serotonin syndrome:** When combined with serotonergic medications (SSRIs, SNRIs, tramadol, triptans), St. John's wort can cause life-threatening serotonin syndrome. The Australian adverse event analysis documented a case of life-threatening serotonin syndrome when St. John's wort was taken with sertraline (Zoloft) [29]. Symptoms include nausea, anxiety, mental confusion, fever, tremor, seizures, altered heart rate, unstable blood pressure, and potentially death [1]. This is the most dangerous acute risk associated with St. John's wort use.

**Mania/bipolar episodes:** Like all antidepressants, St. John's wort can trigger manic episodes in people with bipolar disorder [1]. Individuals with known or suspected bipolar disorder should not use St. John's wort.

**Anorgasmia:** A long-term study by Sarris (*Pharmacopsychiatry*, 2012) found that St. John's wort use was associated with an increase in anorgasmia (inability to have an orgasm), a side effect also seen with conventional antidepressants [30].

**Hair loss:** Use of St. John's wort has been linked to hair loss in one case report, similar to reports with conventional antidepressants [1].

**Cognitive impairment at excessive doses:** There is limited evidence that excessive doses of St. John's wort might slightly impair mental function, and one case report describes apparent severe worsening of symptoms of Alzheimer's disease [1].

### Photosensitivity

St. John's wort can increase sensitivity to ultraviolet light. A few case studies in immunocompromised patients suggest that high doses may increase sunburn risk [1][31].

**In healthy subjects at standard doses:** Studies conducted with healthy subjects at standard doses did not show clinically significant photosensitivity (Hammerness et al., *Psychosomatics*, 2003) [31]. However, some individuals may be unusually sensitive, and individual reports exist of unexpected sunburn in people receiving medical UV treatments (such as light therapy) who were taking St. John's wort at normal doses [1].

**Cataract risk:** An anecdotal, self-reported population survey found an association between St. John's wort use and cataract development, possibly related to the herb's UV-sensitizing effects (Booth et al., *Curr Eye Res*, 2009) [32].

**Recommendations:** It is advisable to avoid prolonged or intense sun exposure while using St. John's wort. This caution is especially important for those taking other photosensitizing drugs such as sulfa antibiotics or the anti-inflammatory piroxicam, as the effects may be additive [1].

### Withdrawal Symptoms

Abruptly stopping St. John's wort can lead to unpleasant withdrawal symptoms, similar to SSRI discontinuation syndrome. Reported symptoms include [1]:

- Headache
- Nausea
- Insomnia
- Fatigue
- Irritability

Gradual dose reduction over 1-2 weeks is recommended when discontinuing St. John's wort.

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy:** The safety of St. John's wort has not been well evaluated during pregnancy. The NCCIH states that it may be unsafe to use during pregnancy because it may increase the risk of birth defects [2]. St. John's wort is not recommended for women who are or may become pregnant [1][2].

**Breastfeeding:** Breastfeeding infants of mothers who take St. John's wort can experience colic, drowsiness, and lethargy [2]. The herb is not recommended during breastfeeding.

**Children:** Safety and efficacy have not been well established in children. The RCT by Weber et al. (2008) in 54 children and teens with ADHD found no benefit compared to placebo, and the safety profile in pediatric populations is not well characterized [24].

**Duration of use:** For most adults who are not taking any kind of medicine, St. John's wort appears safe when used for up to 12 weeks, and some studies indicate it can be used safely for a year or more [2].

## 6. Drug Interactions

Drug interactions are the most significant safety concern with St. John's wort. The herb induces cytochrome P450 enzymes (particularly CYP3A4) and P-glycoprotein, which accelerates the metabolism of many medications and reduces their blood levels and effectiveness [10][11][33]. This interaction mechanism may affect as many as half of all drugs in use today [1].

### Medications with Reduced Effectiveness

The following table lists medications for which St. John's wort has been shown or strongly suspected to reduce blood levels and therapeutic effect:

| Medication/Class | Clinical Consequence | Severity |
|-----------------|---------------------|:---:|
| **Cyclosporine, tacrolimus** (transplant immunosuppressants) | Risk of organ transplant rejection | Life-threatening |
| **HIV protease inhibitors and NNRTIs** (e.g., indinavir, nevirapine) | Worsening of HIV symptoms, viral resistance | Life-threatening |
| **Hepatitis C drugs** (sofosbuvir/Sovaldi, ledipasvir/Harvoni) | Decreased plasma concentration, reduced therapeutic effect, potential viral resistance | Life-threatening |
| **Warfarin (Coumadin)** | Failure of anticoagulation protection, risk of blood clots | Life-threatening |
| **Cancer chemotherapy drugs** (irinotecan, imatinib, docetaxel) | Loss of anti-cancer efficacy | Life-threatening |
| **Paxlovid** (COVID-19 antiviral) | Decreased plasma concentration, loss of virologic response, possible viral resistance | Serious |
| **Oral contraceptives / estrogen replacement** | Unintended pregnancy; decreased hormone replacement drug levels | Serious |
| **Digoxin** (heart disease treatment) | Increased heart disease symptoms | Serious |
| **Statin drugs** (e.g., simvastatin) | Higher cholesterol levels, loss of cardiovascular protection | Serious |
| **Anti-psychotic drugs** (olanzapine, clozapine) | Increased symptoms of psychosis | Serious |
| **Omeprazole (Prilosec)** and related proton pump inhibitors | Increased ulcer or esophageal reflux symptoms | Moderate |
| **Methylphenidate (Ritalin)** | Diminished efficacy of ADHD medication [25] | Moderate |
| **Anti-seizure drugs** (phenytoin, carbamazepine) | Reduced seizure control | Serious |
| **Heart medications** (ivabradine) | Reduced cardiac drug effectiveness | Serious |
| **Erectile dysfunction drugs** (sildenafil/Viagra, tadalafil/Cialis) | Reduced effectiveness (Henderson, *Br J Clin Pharmacol*, 2002; Markowitz, *JAMA*, 2003) [34][35] | Moderate |

Sources: ConsumerLab [1], NCCIH [2], Nicolussi et al. (2020) [10], Soleymani et al. (2017) [11], Czigle et al. (2023) [33], Chan et al. (2023) [36].

### Medications with Other Interaction Risks

Beyond reducing blood levels, St. John's wort can cause other types of dangerous interactions:

| Medication/Class | Interaction Risk | Mechanism |
|-----------------|-----------------|-----------|
| **SSRIs** (fluoxetine/Prozac, sertraline/Zoloft, citalopram/Celexa) | Serotonin syndrome: nausea, anxiety, confusion, fever, tremor, seizures, altered heart rate, unstable blood pressure, potentially death | Additive serotonergic effects |
| **Other antidepressants** (SNRIs, tricyclics including amitriptyline, bupropion, MAOIs) | Serotonin syndrome or MAOI interaction (rapid heart rate, high blood pressure, delirium) | Additive serotonergic or MAOI-like effects |
| **Anti-migraine triptans** (sumatriptan) | Serotonin syndrome | Additive serotonergic effects |
| **Tramadol** (painkiller) | Serotonin syndrome | Additive serotonergic effects |
| **Anesthetics** | Delayed emergence from anesthesia after surgery | Potentiation of anesthetic effects |
| **Photosensitizing drugs** (piroxicam, sulfa antibiotics) | Increased sun sensitivity | Additive photosensitization |
| **MAO inhibitors, stimulant drugs** | MAOI interaction: rapid heart rate, high blood pressure, delirium | MAOI-like interaction |
| **Tyramine-containing foods** (aged cheese, wine, fermented soy products, cured meats, beer) when combined with St. John's wort | MAOI interaction symptoms | Theoretical MAOI-like activity |

### The "Reverse Risk" of Stopping St. John's Wort

A critical but often overlooked safety concern: if a patient is taking St. John's wort alongside a medication, and their physician has adjusted the medication dosage to achieve proper blood levels in the presence of the enzyme-inducing herb, suddenly stopping St. John's wort can cause blood levels of the medication to rise rapidly, potentially causing toxic side effects [1]. This is because the CYP3A4 enzyme induction reverses when the herb is discontinued, and the medication is no longer being cleared as quickly.

This reverse risk applies to all medications affected by St. John's wort's enzyme-inducing properties. Patients should always inform their healthcare provider before starting OR stopping St. John's wort.

### Clinical Relevance Reviews

Nicolussi et al. (2020) published a comprehensive reassessment of St. John's wort drug interactions in the *British Journal of Pharmacology*, examining the clinical relevance of reported interactions. The review confirmed that while many interactions are pharmacokinetically documented, the clinical significance varies by medication and patient factors [10].

Soleymani et al. (2017) reviewed the clinical risks of St. John's wort co-administration in *Expert Opinion on Drug Metabolism & Toxicology*, providing a systematic overview of interaction mechanisms and risk stratification [11].

Czigle et al. (2023) examined pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic herb-drug interactions for herbal medicines of the central nervous system, including St. John's wort, and noted that the CYP3A4 induction is the primary mechanism underlying most interactions [33].

Nobakht et al. (2022) reviewed traditional uses, clinical trials, and drug interactions of *Hypericum perforatum* in the *Iranian Journal of Basic Medical Sciences*, providing additional context for the extensive interaction profile [37].

Chan et al. (2023) specifically examined interactions between natural products and cancer treatments, highlighting the importance of the interaction between St. John's wort and chemotherapy agents, which can lead to loss of anti-cancer efficacy [36].

### A Note on Context

While the extensive drug interaction list may make St. John's wort appear unusually dangerous, it is more accurate to say that it is an especially well-studied herb whose risks are better characterized than most [1]. As a comparison, grapefruit juice causes many of the same CYP3A4-mediated drug interactions and is not ordinarily considered a dangerous substance [1].

### Interactions with Lab Tests

St. John's wort may interfere with the 24-hour urine 5-HIAA test, which is used to help diagnose carcinoid syndrome. Patients should inform their physician and laboratory if they take St. John's wort and have been advised to take this test. Patients are typically also advised to avoid tryptophan and serotonin-rich foods such as pineapple, banana, kiwi, plum, tomato, avocado, walnut, hickory nut, and eggplant one to three days before this test (Burks, *AACE Clin Case Rep*, 2016) [38].

## 7. Dietary Sources

St. John's wort is not a nutrient found in food and has no dietary sources. It is a herbal medicine derived exclusively from the *Hypericum perforatum* plant. The relevant plant parts are the aerial portions (flowers, leaves, and stems), which are harvested, dried, and processed into various supplement forms [1][2].

### Plant Identification

*Hypericum perforatum* is a perennial plant native to Europe, North Africa, and western Asia, now naturalized across much of North America, Australia, and other temperate regions [2]. It is characterized by:

- Yellow, five-petaled flowers
- Opposite leaves with tiny translucent dots visible when held to light (the "perforations" referenced in the species name *perforatum*)
- Blooming period around late June (St. John's Day)
- Found in meadows, fields, roadsides, and disturbed areas

### Harvesting and Processing

The flowers and upper leaves are typically harvested at peak bloom. Commercial preparations are made by extracting the dried plant material with solvents (usually ethanol or methanol-water mixtures) to produce standardized extracts. The extraction process concentrates the active constituents (hypericin, hyperforin, flavonoids) while removing some plant fiber, chlorophyll, and contaminants [1][4].

Whole herb preparations use the dried, ground aerial parts without the extraction step. These tend to have lower concentrations of active compounds per gram and, as noted earlier, may contain higher levels of heavy metals than extracted preparations [1].
