NIH's complementary health center (NCCIH) concludes that St. John's wort appears more effective than placebo and as effective as standard antidepressants for mild or moderate depression, but whether this holds for severe depression or for longer than 12 weeks is uncertain [2]. Its main risk is drug interactions: it can weaken many important medicines, including birth control pills, warfarin, the transplant drug ciclosporin, digoxin and some HIV, cancer, seizure and cholesterol drugs [2][10][34]. Taken with antidepressants or other drugs that affect serotonin, it may cause serotonin syndrome, which can be deadly; if someone develops shaking, muscle jerks, a racing heart, a high temperature, agitation or confusion, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department [2][44][46]. Anyone who takes a prescription medicine, is pregnant or breastfeeding, or may have depression should talk to a doctor before using it [2].

## Key safety points

- It can weaken many medicines, sometimes dangerously: birth control pills, warfarin, ciclosporin (heart transplant rejection has occurred), digoxin, and some HIV, cancer, seizure, heart and cholesterol drugs [2][10][39]. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.
- With antidepressants or other serotonin drugs it may cause serotonin syndrome, which can be deadly. For shaking, muscle jerks, a racing heart, a high temperature, agitation or confusion, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department [2][44][46].
- It may be unsafe in pregnancy because it may raise the risk of birth defects, and breastfed babies have had colic, drowsiness and lethargy [2].
- Depression can be serious, so see a doctor rather than treating it yourself [2]. A case report describes mania after high doses [49].
- Large doses by mouth, or use on the skin, might cause severe skin reactions after sun exposure [2].
- Other side effects can include diarrhea, dizziness, trouble sleeping, restlessness and skin tingling [2].

## 1. Overview

St. John's wort (*Hypericum perforatum*) is a yellow-flowered plant used in Greek, Islamic and Chinese traditional medicine [2][37]. Its name probably refers to John the Baptist, because the plant blooms around his feast day in late June [2]. Other names include hypericum, Klamath weed and goatweed, and today it is promoted mainly for symptoms of depression [2].

The dried herb (*Hyperici herba*) contains naphthodianthrones such as hypericin, flavonol derivatives, biflavones, proanthocyanidins, xanthones, and phloroglucinols such as hyperforin [4][5]. Researchers now regard the total extract as the active substance, with several groups of compounds adding to its antidepressant effect [5].

Reviews of randomized trials suggest that St. John's wort extracts work better than placebo for mild to moderate major depression and about as well as standard antidepressants, including SSRIs, with fewer side effects [2][8][17]. Whether this holds for severe depression or for use longer than 12 weeks is uncertain [2].

Its main risk is drug interactions. It can weaken many medicines, including birth control pills, transplant drugs, some HIV and cancer drugs, warfarin and some statins, and NCCIH describes some of these interactions as life-threatening [2][10][11]. Anyone who takes a medicine should check with their doctor first [2].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Extract vs. Whole Herb

Most products are either standardized extracts or dried, powdered whole herb. The trials in the major reviews tested extracts, and the Cochrane authors warn that products on the market may vary considerably in quality, so their results apply only to the products tested [8][12].

| Form | Standardization | Doses Used in Trials | Notes |
|------|----------------|:---:|-------|
| Standardized extract (0.3% hypericin) | Measured for hypericin | 300 mg, 3 times daily | Used in an ADHD trial in children [24]. |
| Extract with hypericin and hyperforin (0.3% hypericin, 1-4% hyperforin) | Measured for both | Varies by trial | Eight of the 35 trials in one systematic review used this type [17]. |
| WS 5570 extract | Specific branded extract | 900 mg/day in three doses, raised to 1,800 mg/day in non-responders | At least as effective as paroxetine in a six-week trial [9]. |
| Whole herb (dried, powdered) | Not standardized | Not established | Less studied than extracts. |
| Liquid extract / tincture | Variable | Per manufacturer directions | Little trial data. |
| Topical preparations (oil/cream) | Variable | Applied to skin | Promoted for wounds and skin conditions; evidence is limited [2][14]. |

### Active Constituents

**Hypericin**, with pseudohypericin, is among the constituents that have drawn the most research interest, and trials such as the ADHD study standardized their extract to it [5][24]. It is not thought to work alone [5].

Zanoli describes **hyperforin** as a major antidepressant component in the extract, and clinical studies tied the effect of extracts to how much hyperforin they contain, in a dose-dependent way [6]. Like standard antidepressants, it blocks the reuptake of serotonin, norepinephrine and dopamine; it also blocks the uptake of GABA and L-glutamate [6]. Hyperforin also appears to drive the liver enzyme and P-glycoprotein induction behind most drug interactions [6][10].

**Synergistic mechanism:** Current reviews treat the total extract as the active substance, with several groups of compounds contributing to its antidepressant effect [5].

### Bioavailability Considerations

When choosing a product, points to check include:

- **Standardization:** Trials used extracts standardized to hypericin, hyperforin or both [17][24].
- **Extract vs. whole herb:** The trial evidence comes from extracts, not whole herb [8][12].
- **Trial length:** Trials comparing St. John's wort with SSRIs lasted 4 to 12 weeks [16].

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Major Depression (Mild to Moderate)

This is the best-studied use. NCCIH concludes that St. John's wort appears more effective than placebo and as effective as standard antidepressants for mild or moderate depression [2].

**Cochrane systematic review (2008):** Linde and colleagues pooled 29 trials with 5,489 patients with major depression. Extracts beat placebo (response rate ratio 1.28, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.49, in nine larger trials) and matched SSRIs (RR 1.00, 95% CI 0.90 to 1.11; 12 trials), and fewer people stopped because of side effects than on SSRIs (OR 0.53, 95% CI 0.34 to 0.83) [8]. Trials from German-speaking countries reported more favourable results, which complicates interpretation [8].

**Meta-analysis of efficacy vs. SSRIs (2023):** Zhao and colleagues pooled 14 trials with 2,270 patients. They concluded that St. John's wort reduced depression scores with fewer risks and side effects than conventional drugs, though the pooled data were heterogeneous (I2 77%) [15].

**Meta-analysis (2017):** Ng, Venkatanarayanan and Ho reviewed 27 trials with 3,808 patients comparing St. John's wort with SSRIs. Response and remission rates were similar, and fewer people dropped out on St. John's wort (pooled OR 0.587, 95% CI 0.478 to 0.697) [16]. All trials lasted 4 to 12 weeks, and benefit in severe depression or high suicide risk is unclear [16].

**Systematic review (2016):** Apaydin and colleagues included 35 trials with 6,993 patients. In mild and moderate depression, St. John's wort produced more responders than placebo (RR 1.53, 95% CI 1.19 to 1.97) and matched antidepressants (RR 1.01, 95% CI 0.90 to 1.14), with fewer adverse events than antidepressants (OR 0.67, 95% CI 0.56 to 0.81) [17]. Heterogeneity and little research in severe depression lowered the quality of the evidence [17].

**Head-to-head trial vs. paroxetine (2005):** Szegedi and colleagues randomized 251 adults with moderate to severe major depression to the extract WS 5570 (900 mg/day in three doses) or paroxetine (20 mg/day) for six weeks, raising doses to 1,800 mg/day or 40 mg/day in early non-responders. Depression scores fell by 56.6% with the extract and 44.8% with paroxetine; the extract was non-inferior and statistically superior, with fewer adverse events [9].

#### Negative Trials and Severe Depression

Two large US trials in major depression (Hamilton scores of at least 20) found no benefit on their main outcomes:

**Hypericum Depression Trial Study Group (2002):** This trial randomized 340 adults with major depression (Hamilton score of at least 20) to St. John's wort (daily dose 900 to 1,500 mg), sertraline or placebo for 8 weeks. Neither St. John's wort nor sertraline beat placebo on the two main outcomes, and the authors noted the trial may have had low assay sensitivity [18]. Continuation data from a 26-week trial of the same extract (LI-160) against sertraline and placebo found no difference between groups at week 26, with a strong placebo effect [30].

**Shelton et al. (2001):** In 200 adult outpatients with major depression (Hamilton score of at least 20), an extract at 900 to 1,200 mg/day did not beat placebo on the main outcome during 8 weeks of treatment. Remission was more common on the extract (14.3% vs 4.9%), but rates were very low [19].

**Synthesis on severity:** The reviews support use in mild to moderate depression, but research in severe depression is thin [16][17]. One trial in moderate to severe depression was positive [9], while the two trials above were negative [18][19]. The NCCIH notes that it is uncertain whether St. John's wort is effective for severe depression or for treatment periods longer than 12 weeks [2].

### Menopause Symptoms and Depression

**Combination with black cohosh (2006):** In 301 women with menopausal complaints and marked psychological symptoms, a fixed combination of black cohosh and St. John's wort extracts beat placebo after 16 weeks of treatment on both menopause and depression scores [20]. Because it was a combination, the result cannot be credited to St. John's wort alone.

**Postmenopausal depression (2019):** In a two-month trial in 80 postmenopausal women in Iran, St. John's wort reduced hot flashes, menopausal symptom scores and depression compared with placebo [21].

**Meta-analysis of menopause symptoms (2014):** Pooled trials of St. John's wort, alone or with other herbs, found it more effective than placebo for menopausal symptoms, with adverse events in 17.4% of people on St. John's wort and 15.4% on placebo [22].

**NCCIH assessment:** The NCCIH notes that small numbers of studies suggest St. John's wort may be helpful for hot flashes associated with menopause, though the overall evidence base remains limited [2].

### Somatic Symptom Disorder

The NCCIH reports that small numbers of studies suggest St. John's wort may be helpful for somatic symptom disorder, a condition involving intense anxiety about physical symptoms [2]. However, the evidence is considered preliminary and insufficient for definitive conclusions.

### Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)

The evidence for St. John's wort in ADHD is mixed and overall negative:

**Small open-label study in teens (2010):** Niederhofer published a study in *Natural Product Research* examining three male teens with ADHD. St. John's wort appeared to improve hyperactivity, inattention, and immaturity in this very small, open-label sample [23].

**Randomized controlled trial in children and teens (2008):** In a stronger double-blind trial published in *JAMA*, Weber et al. gave 54 young people aged 6 to 17 years with ADHD either an extract standardized to 0.3% hypericin (300 mg, 3 times daily) or placebo for 8 weeks. Inattention and hyperactivity scores improved no more with the extract than with placebo [24].

A hypothesis paper by Niederhofer (2007) proposed that the herb may weaken the effect of methylphenidate (Ritalin), a standard ADHD medicine [25]. The trial evidence does not support it for ADHD.

### Anxiety and Social Phobia

St. John's wort is sometimes tried for anxiety because it acts on the same brain chemicals as SSRIs, which also treat anxiety disorders. Good trial evidence for generalized anxiety or social phobia is lacking. NCCIH lists obsessive-compulsive disorder among the conditions where little research exists and benefit has not been clearly shown [2].

**Obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD):** Abrishami et al. (2022) reviewed randomized trials of herbal medicines for OCD and found that every herb tested appeared effective except St. John's wort [26].

### Seasonal Affective Disorder (SAD)

St. John's wort is sometimes suggested for seasonal affective disorder, a form of depression. The case for it rests on depression trials in general rather than on trials in seasonal affective disorder.

### Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS)

NCCIH lists premenstrual syndrome among the conditions where only a little research exists and the evidence has not clearly shown St. John's wort to help [2].

### Smoking Cessation

**RCT (2009):** Parsons et al. conducted a proof-of-concept randomized placebo-controlled factorial trial in smokers trying to quit. At 4 weeks, 6 of 71 people on St. John's wort (900 mg) and 9 of 72 on placebo had stopped smoking, and the authors concluded it is ineffective for smoking cessation [27].

**RCT (2010):** Sood et al. randomized 118 smokers to a 300 mg or 600 mg tablet 3 times a day, or a placebo tablet, for 12 weeks alongside behavioural support. Neither dose raised quit rates [28].

The NCCIH notes that the evidence has not clearly shown St. John's wort to be helpful for smoking cessation [2].

### Irritable Bowel Syndrome (IBS)

Only a small amount of research has been done on St. John's wort for IBS. The NCCIH states that the evidence is insufficient to draw conclusions about its usefulness for this condition [2].

### Wound Healing (Topical Use)

Topical application of St. John's wort is promoted for wounds and other skin conditions [2].

**Review (2024):** Farasati Far et al. published a literature review in *Phytotherapy Research* on *Hypericum perforatum* in wound healing. It summarizes mechanistic research suggesting effects on each phase of healing, including less inflammation and more collagen deposition [14].

The NCCIH notes that only a small amount of research has been done on topical St. John's wort for wound healing, and the evidence has not clearly shown it to be helpful [2].

### HIV Infection

St. John's wort can undermine HIV treatment. In healthy volunteers it cut exposure to the protease inhibitor indinavir by a mean of 57%, a drop the authors warned could lead to drug resistance and treatment failure [40]. NCCIH lists indinavir and nevirapine among the HIV drugs it can weaken [2]. People taking HIV medicines should not use St. John's wort (see Drug Interactions section).

### Chronic Pain and Neuropathic Pain

St. John's wort is sometimes tried for chronic and nerve pain, because some antidepressants (SNRIs and tricyclics) treat these conditions. Clinical trial evidence for this use is lacking.

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Standard Antidepressant Dosing

Doses used in the main depression trials:

| Preparation | Daily Dose | Frequency | Duration |
|------------|:---:|:---:|:---:|
| WS 5570 extract | 900 mg/day, raised to 1,800 mg/day in non-responders | Three times daily | 6 weeks [9] |
| LI-160 extract | 900 to 1,500 mg | Not stated | 8 weeks; responders continued to week 26 [18][30] |
| Standardized extract (Shelton trial) | 900 mg/day, raised to 1,200 mg/day if needed | Not stated | 8 weeks [19] |

### Important Dosing Notes

- **Time to effect:** The main trials judged response after 6 to 8 weeks [9][18].
- **Dose escalation:** In the Szegedi et al. (2005) trial, the dose was raised from 900 mg/day to 1,800 mg/day after two weeks in people who had not responded [9]. Dose increases should only be made under medical supervision.
- **Do not self-treat serious depression:** Depression can be a serious illness. The NCCIH emphasizes that anyone who may have depression should consult a healthcare provider [2]. St. John's wort should not be used as a substitute for professional mental health care, particularly for moderate to severe depression.
- **Stopping:** Stopping suddenly may cause withdrawal-type symptoms, so ask your doctor how to taper. If you take a medicine that St. John's wort affects, do not stop without your doctor's advice (see Drug Interactions).

### Dosing for Menopause

No dose is established for menopause symptoms. The trials used different products, including a fixed combination with black cohosh [20][21][22].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Common Side Effects

A systematic review of adverse effects found it generally well tolerated in clinical use [31]. In trials for depression, adverse events were similar to placebo and fewer than with antidepressants, though rare events were not well assessed [17]. NCCIH lists these side effects [2]:

- Diarrhea
- Dizziness
- Trouble sleeping
- Restlessness
- Skin tingling

Headache was more common than with placebo in one trial (41% vs 25%) [19].

**Comparison with SSRIs:** An analysis of adverse reaction reports to Australia's medicines regulator from 2000 to 2013 (84 reports about St. John's wort and 447 about fluoxetine) found a similar pattern for both, with most reports involving the central nervous system [29]. The authors concluded that herbal products can cause adverse reactions like those of prescription drugs [29].

### Serious Side Effects

**Serotonin syndrome:** Taken with certain antidepressants or other drugs that affect serotonin, St. John's wort may cause serious serotonin-related side effects [2]. A case series described serotonin syndrome in five older adults who combined prescription antidepressants with it, and European regulators identified interactions with SSRIs and triptans [34][44]. Serotonin syndrome can be deadly; signs include shaking, muscle jerks and overactive reflexes, a racing heart, high or low blood pressure, a high temperature, agitation, delirium and coma [46]. If these appear, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department.

**Mania/bipolar episodes:** A case report describes mania in a 28-year-old woman with depression and no past mania who took high doses and no antidepressants [49]. People with bipolar disorder should not use it without their psychiatrist's advice.

**Anorgasmia:** Difficulty reaching orgasm may occur, as it can with standard antidepressants.

**Hair loss:** A report of two cases suggested the herb may be associated with hair loss [45].

### Photosensitivity

Taken by mouth in large doses or applied to the skin, St. John's wort might cause severe skin reactions after sun exposure [2].

**Cataract risk:** In a laboratory study, hypericin exposed to light damaged a key lens protein; the authors suggested this could lead to cataracts and advised protecting the eyes from intense sunlight [50]. Whether this happens in people is unknown.

**Recommendations:** Limit long or intense sun exposure while you take the herb. Other medicines that increase sun sensitivity may add to the effect.

### Withdrawal Symptoms

Stopping the herb abruptly may cause withdrawal-type symptoms. Ask your doctor about tapering, especially if you take a medicine it interacts with (see the reverse risk below).

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy:** The NCCIH states that it may be unsafe to use during pregnancy because it may increase the risk of birth defects [2]. Avoid it if you are pregnant or trying to conceive [2].

**Breastfeeding:** Breastfeeding infants of mothers who take St. John's wort can experience colic, drowsiness, and lethargy [2]. The herb is not recommended during breastfeeding.

**Children:** Safety and benefit in children are not well established. A randomized trial in 54 young people aged 6 to 17 years with ADHD found no benefit over placebo, and safety in children is not well characterized [24].

**Duration of use:** For most adults who are not taking any kind of medicine, St. John's wort appears safe when used for up to 12 weeks, and some studies indicate it can be used safely for a year or more [2].

## 6. Drug Interactions

Drug interactions are the most significant safety concern with St. John's wort. It activates the pregnane X receptor, which induces liver enzymes (most importantly CYP3A4) and the drug pump P-glycoprotein, so many medicines are cleared faster and their levels and effects fall [10][11]. In 12 healthy volunteers, 14 days of one 300 mg tablet 3 times per day produced a 2-fold decrease in exposure to alprazolam, a drug used to measure CYP3A4 activity [35].

### Medications with Reduced Effectiveness

The following table lists medications whose levels or effects St. John's wort has been shown to reduce, or that NCCIH lists as affected:

| Medication/Class | Clinical Consequence | Evidence |
|-----------------|---------------------|:---:|
| **Ciclosporin, tacrolimus** (transplant drugs) | Acute rejection in two heart transplant patients taking ciclosporin; lower drug levels | Case reports, drug-level studies [2][10][39] |
| **HIV protease inhibitors and NNRTIs** (e.g., indinavir, nevirapine) | Indinavir exposure fell by a mean of 57%, risking drug resistance and treatment failure | Volunteer study, NCCIH [2][40] |
| **Warfarin** | Faster warfarin clearance and a weaker blood-thinning effect | Volunteer study, NCCIH [2][43] |
| **Cancer drugs** (irinotecan, imatinib, docetaxel) | Possible loss of anticancer effect | NCCIH, review [2][36] |
| **Birth control pills** | Breakthrough bleeding in 7 of 12 women vs 2 of 12 without the herb; weaker effect | Volunteer study, NCCIH [2][42] |
| **Digoxin** (heart disease treatment) | Digoxin exposure fell by 25% after 10 days | Volunteer study, NCCIH [2][41] |
| **Statins** (e.g., simvastatin) | Lower statin levels | NCCIH, review [2][10] |
| **Omeprazole** (Prilosec) | Omeprazole exposure fell by 37.9% to 43.9% | Volunteer study [47] |
| **Anti-seizure drugs** (phenytoin, carbamazepine) | Weaker effect | NCCIH [2] |
| **Heart medications** (ivabradine) | Weaker effect | NCCIH [2] |
| **Some antidepressants** (amitriptyline, bupropion) | Weaker effect | NCCIH [2] |
| **Methylphenidate (Ritalin)** | Possibly weaker effect | Hypothesis paper [25] |

Sources: NCCIH [2], Nicolussi et al. (2020) [10], Soleymani et al. (2017) [11], Henderson et al. (2002) [34], Chan et al. (2023) [36].

Other drugs cleared by the same enzymes may also be affected, possibly including some hepatitis C antivirals (sofosbuvir, ledipasvir), the COVID-19 antiviral Paxlovid, estrogen replacement, the antipsychotics olanzapine and clozapine, and the erectile dysfunction drugs sildenafil and tadalafil. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

### Medications with Other Interaction Risks

Some interactions work in other ways than lowering drug levels:

| Medication/Class | Interaction Risk | Mechanism |
|-----------------|-----------------|-----------|
| **Antidepressants** (SSRIs such as fluoxetine, sertraline and citalopram, and others) | Serotonin syndrome (see Serious Side Effects) [2][34][44] | Additive serotonin effects |
| **Anti-migraine triptans** (sumatriptan) | Serotonin-related reactions [34] | Additive serotonin effects |
| **Other drugs that affect serotonin** (such as tramadol) | Serious serotonin-related side effects [2] | Additive serotonin effects |
| **Anesthetics** | A case report raised long-term use as a possible cause of cardiovascular collapse during anesthesia [48] | Not established |
| **Photosensitizing drugs** | Possibly greater sun sensitivity | Additive photosensitization |

### The "Reverse Risk" of Stopping St. John's Wort

Enzyme induction wears off after St. John's wort is stopped. If a doctor has raised a medicine's dose to make up for the herb, stopping the herb can let that medicine's level rise, possibly to toxic levels.

This applies to every medicine whose levels St. John's wort lowers. Tell your doctor before you start or stop St. John's wort.

### Clinical Relevance Reviews

Nicolussi et al. (2020) reviewed two decades of St. John's wort drug interactions in the *British Journal of Pharmacology*. They traced the interactions to activation of the pregnane X receptor and reported that the degree of CYP3A4 induction tracks the hyperforin content of the product [10].

Soleymani et al. (2017) reviewed human studies in *Expert Opinion on Drug Metabolism & Toxicology* and found clinically important interactions with immunosuppressants, cancer drugs, heart drugs, oral contraceptives and lipid-lowering drugs, some of which caused life-threatening events [11]. They suggested that hyperforin-free products might in future reduce these interactions [11].

Czigle et al. (2023) reviewed herb-drug interactions for herbal medicines that act on the brain, including St. John's wort, and noted that such interactions can cause treatment failure or side effects, sometimes fatal [33].

Nobakht et al. (2022) reviewed clinical trials and drug interactions of *Hypericum perforatum* in the *Iranian Journal of Basic Medical Sciences* and advised caution in its use [37].

Chan et al. (2023) found clinical studies showing that St. John's wort taken with common cancer treatments may lead to worse treatment outcomes [36].

### A Note on Context

The authors of the digoxin study compared the interaction with those of grapefruit juice, a food, and said doctors should be aware of herb-drug interactions too [41]. Because the strength of enzyme induction varies with a product's hyperforin content and quality, products can differ in how strongly they interact [10][34].

### Interactions with Lab Tests

If you have a blood or urine test planned, tell your doctor and the laboratory about every supplement you take, including St. John's wort.

## 7. Dietary Sources

St. John's wort is not a nutrient and has no dietary sources. It is a herbal product made from the *Hypericum perforatum* plant [2].

### Plant Identification

*Hypericum perforatum* is a plant with yellow flowers [2]. It is characterized by:

- Yellow, five-petaled flowers
- Opposite leaves with tiny translucent dots visible when held to light (the "perforations" referenced in the species name *perforatum*)
- Blooming period around late June (St. John's Day)
- Found in meadows, fields, roadsides, and disturbed areas

### Harvesting and Processing

Extracts are made by extracting the dried herb with solvents, and the Cochrane authors note that products on the market may vary considerably in quality [12].

Whole herb preparations use the dried, ground plant without the extraction step and are less studied than extracts.
