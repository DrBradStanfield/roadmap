## Current evidence position

Brad has not made a video on this topic; this is a summary of the evidence, not his view. Three small trials from one research group in Cameroon reported weight loss and lower cholesterol with African mango seed, its extract IGOB131 (150 mg twice daily before meals), or IGOB131 combined with *Cissus quadrangularis*, but a systematic review found reporting flaws in all three and judged the effect on weight unproven [7][8][9][10]. A 2020 meta-analysis of five trials found pooled falls in body weight, body fat and waist size, yet four trials had a high risk of bias and the one low-risk trial found no significant difference [16]. Side effects in the trials were minor, such as headache and sleep difficulty [10][16] and gas [7][8], with similar rates on placebo [7], and no trial ran long enough to judge long-term safety [10][16].

## Key safety points

- Folk medicine uses the seeds as a blood thinner [14], and no study has tested African mango with warfarin or other anticoagulants. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.
- Trials reported lower blood glucose with African mango [7][18], so it could add to the effect of diabetes medicines. If you take them, ask your doctor first and watch for signs of low blood sugar.
- A 2014 case report on African mango is indexed on PubMed under drug-induced liver injury [22]. Stop taking it and see your doctor the same day if you notice yellow skin or eyes or dark urine.
- Side effects reported in trials were minor, including headache and sleep difficulty [10][16] and gas [7][8], at similar rates on placebo [7]. The trials were short, so long-term safety is unknown.

## 1. Overview

African mango (*Irvingia gabonensis*, also called bush mango, wild mango or dika nut) is a fruit tree native to tropical Africa, including Nigeria, Cameroon, Gabon and Equatorial Guinea [2][14]. Its fruit looks like the common mango (*Mangifera indica*), but the two are unrelated: African mango belongs to the family Irvingiaceae, common mango to Anacardiaceae.

Both the fruit flesh and the seed (kernel) are eaten. The pulp is eaten fresh or dried, or made into juice and wine [13][14]. The seeds, known as dika nuts or ogbono, are widely traded and used in traditional dishes, where their mucilage thickens soups and sauces [14][20]. The kernel is rich in oil (63% to 69% crude fat), mainly myristic and lauric acids [13].

Extracts of the seed are among the popular herbal weight-loss supplements in the United States [23]. Folk healers already used the seeds to help people lose weight [14], but concentrated, standardised extracts are a newer product.

The human evidence is thin. The early evidence was one cell study and three small trials, all from one Cameroonian research team [5][7][8][9]. Later trials by other groups include a trial in 24 patients with metabolic syndrome [18] and a trial lasting 12 weeks that found no difference from placebo in metabolism [19]. A 2013 systematic review concluded that African mango cannot be recommended as a weight loss aid [10].

### Traditional Uses

In traditional West and Central African medicine and cooking, different parts of the tree have different uses [14]:

- **Seeds (dika nuts):** Ground and added to traditional dishes as a thickener [14][21]. Folk medicine uses them for weight loss, as blood thinners and for diabetes [14].
- **Fruit pulp:** Eaten fresh or made into juice and wine [14]. It is rich in vitamin C [13][14].
- **Bark:** Used for dysentery, colic, scabies, toothache and skin conditions, and in Senegal for gonorrhea, liver disorders and gut complaints [14]. These uses have not been confirmed in human trials.

The seed fat is mostly saturated, mainly lauric and myristic acids [13][15], and is solid at room temperature [13].

### Proposed Mechanisms of Action

Several mechanisms have been proposed, mostly from cell studies rather than human trials [5][7]:

- **Adipogenesis inhibition:** In mouse 3T3-L1 fat cells, the extract IGOB131 inhibited fat cell development [5]. It lowered expression of PPAR-gamma (a gene that drives fat cell formation) and leptin, and raised adiponectin [5].
- **Leptin and adiponectin:** In the 2009 IGOB131 trial, leptin and adiponectin levels improved in the extract group compared with placebo [7].
- **Soluble fiber effects:** The seed contains mucilage [20], a soluble fiber. Soluble fibers lower total and LDL cholesterol in trials [12], likely by binding bile acids in the gut.
- **Glycerol-3-phosphate dehydrogenase inhibition:** The 2009 trial authors describe an earlier cell study as showing effects on this enzyme pathway, which is involved in fat storage [7].

Mechanisms seen in cell studies often fail to translate into clinical effects in people, so these remain speculative until rigorous human trials confirm them.

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

African mango supplements are made from the seed (kernel), not the fruit pulp [7][23].

### Available Supplement Forms

| Form | Description | Study Dose | Notes |
|------|-------------|:-:|-------|
| Seed preparation | *Irvingia gabonensis* seed, as used in the first trial | 1.05 g three times a day | Used in the 2005 trial [9]. |
| IGOB131 | A specific seed extract | 150 mg twice daily before meals | Used in the 2009 trial [7]. |
| Other seed extracts | Seed or kernel extracts not named as IGOB131 | 150 mg twice a day, or 300 mg as one tablet a day | Used in the 2018 and 2022 trials [18][19]. |
| Combination products | African mango extract combined with other weight loss ingredients | Varies | The 2008 trial combined IGOB131 with veldt-grape (*Cissus quadrangularis*), so effects cannot be attributed to African mango alone [8]. |
| Seed powder (whole ground seed) | Dried, ground African mango seeds | Not studied in clinical trials | Sold as a food ingredient (ogbono) for soups [21]. |

### IGOB131: The Most-Studied Extract

IGOB131 is a seed extract of *Irvingia gabonensis*. It was the extract tested in the 2009 trial, given as 150 mg 30 to 60 minutes before lunch and dinner [7], and in the 2008 combination trial [8]. A 90-day rat study and genetic toxicity tests of IGOB131 found no harm (see Safety) [20].

### Bioavailability Considerations

African mango extracts are still poorly characterised chemically [14]. Ellagic acid and its derivatives are the main compounds found in the seeds, extracts and supplements [13][23], but which compounds matter for any effect, and how well they are absorbed, is unknown.

The soluble fiber (mucilage) in the seed probably acts inside the gut rather than being absorbed.

### How African Mango Differs from Common Mango

African mango (*Irvingia gabonensis*) and common mango (*Mangifera indica*) are different species. African mango supplements use the seed and are sold mainly for weight loss. Common mango leaf and fruit extracts contain different compounds, and the two should not be treated as interchangeable.

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Critical Assessment of the Evidence Base

The research base has serious limits:

1. **The first three human trials came from a single research group** in Cameroon (Oben, Ngondi and colleagues) [7][8][9]. The 2008 and 2009 trials used extracts supplied by Gateway Health Alliances, which also part-funded the 2009 trial [7][8].
2. **The trials were short:** those described here lasted from one month [9] to 90 days [18].
3. **Sample sizes were small:** 40, 72 and 102 participants in the first three trials [7][8][9], and 24 in the 2018 trial [18].
4. **Reporting was poor.** A 2013 review found flaws in how all three original trials reported their methods [10]. A 2020 meta-analysis rated four of five trials at high risk of bias [16].
5. **Later trials by other groups were mixed.** The only low-risk trial in the 2020 meta-analysis found no significant difference [16], and a 2022 trial found no difference from placebo in metabolism [19].

The evidence is preliminary at best. Both systematic reviews called for larger, more rigorous trials [10][16].

### Weight Loss

#### Study 1: Crude Seed Extract (Ngondi et al., 2005)

The first trial tested *Irvingia gabonensis* seed in obese adults in Cameroon [9].

- **Design:** Double-blind, randomised, placebo-controlled trial
- **Participants:** 40 adults (mean age 42.4 years); 28 received African mango, 12 received placebo
- **Intervention:** 1.05 g three times a day
- **Duration:** One month
- **Diet:** All participants ate a normocaloric diet, checked weekly with a diet record book
- **Results:**
  - Body weight fell by 5.26 +/- 2.37% in the African mango group and by 1.32 +/- 0.41% in the placebo group; the difference was significant [9]
  - The African mango group also had lower total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol and triglycerides, and higher HDL cholesterol [9]
- **Limitations:** Small sample, especially the placebo group of 12; unequal groups (28 vs 12); short duration [9]

#### Study 2: IGOB131 Extract (Ngondi et al., 2009)

The second trial gave the IGOB131 extract to 102 overweight or obese volunteers [7].

- **Design:** Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial
- **Participants:** 102 healthy overweight or obese adults (BMI > 25 kg/m2), randomly divided into two groups
- **Intervention:** IGOB131 150 mg twice daily, taken 30-60 minutes before lunch and dinner
- **Duration:** 10 weeks
- **Results:**
  - Body weight, body fat and waist circumference improved in the IGOB131 group compared with placebo [7]
  - So did total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, blood glucose, C-reactive protein, adiponectin and leptin [7]
- **Limitations:** Same research group as Study 1, and reporting flaws noted by a later review [10]

#### Study 3: IGOB131 + Cissus quadrangularis Combination (Oben et al., 2008)

The third trial tested a combination of IGOB131 and veldt-grape (*Cissus quadrangularis*) [8].

- **Design:** Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial
- **Participants:** 72 overweight or obese adults aged 21-44, in three equal groups of 24: placebo, *Cissus quadrangularis* alone, and *Cissus quadrangularis* with *Irvingia gabonensis*
- **Intervention:** Capsules twice daily before meals; no diet or exercise changes were suggested
- **Duration:** 10 weeks
- **Results:**
  - Both active groups differed significantly from placebo on body weight, body fat, waist size, total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol and fasting blood glucose by week 10 [8]
  - The combination gave larger reductions than *Cissus quadrangularis* alone [8]
- **Limitations:** Because the trial used a combination, the effects cannot be attributed to African mango alone. *Cissus quadrangularis* alone also beat placebo on every measure [8].

#### Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses

A 2013 systematic review by Onakpoya and colleagues found the same three trials [10]:

- All three reported significant falls in body weight and waist circumference with African mango compared with placebo
- All three had flaws in reporting their methods
- The reviewers concluded that the effect on body weight is unproven and that African mango cannot be recommended as a weight loss aid [10]

A 2020 meta-analysis by Lee and colleagues pooled five trials [16]. Pooled results showed falls in body weight, body fat and waist circumference, but four trials had a high risk of bias, and the only low-risk trial found no significant difference [16]. The authors called the evidence limited by poor quality and reporting.

A 2020 review of 54 trials of herbal weight-loss medicines found statistically and clinically significant weight loss reported for African mango, but in 3 or fewer trials, generally of poor quality, and concluded there is not enough evidence to recommend any of the herbs reviewed [17].

### Cholesterol and Lipid Profiles

All three original trials reported better lipid levels with African mango than with placebo [7][8][9]:

#### Study 1 (Ngondi et al., 2005) — Lipid Effects:
- Lower total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol and triglycerides
- Higher HDL cholesterol
- No change in the placebo group [9]

#### Study 2 (Ngondi et al., 2009) — Lipid Effects:
- Lower total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol [7]

#### Study 3 (Oben et al., 2008) — Lipid Effects:
- Lower total cholesterol and LDL cholesterol
- These effects cannot be separated from the *Cissus quadrangularis* component [8]

The 2020 meta-analysis found pooled benefits on total cholesterol, LDL cholesterol, HDL cholesterol and triglycerides. In the one low-risk trial, HDL and triglycerides only trended in a good direction (HDL net change 11.61%, 95% CI -6.12% to 29.34%; triglycerides -29%, 95% CI -76% to 19%) [16].

#### Context: Soluble Fiber and Cholesterol

Any cholesterol effect of the whole seed may come from its soluble fiber (mucilage) [20] rather than a unique drug-like property. Soluble fibers are thought to bind bile acids in the gut, so the liver uses more cholesterol to make new bile acids.

Other fibers show the size of this effect:
- A meta-analysis of 67 trials found that 2-10 g/d of soluble fiber caused small but significant falls in total and LDL cholesterol; oat, psyllium and pectin worked about equally [12]
- A Cochrane review found more fiber lowered total cholesterol (17 trials; mean difference -0.23 mmol/L, 95% CI -0.40 to -0.06) and LDL cholesterol (mean difference -0.14 mmol/L, 95% CI -0.22 to -0.06) [6]

If African mango lowers cholesterol mainly through fiber, established fiber supplements have far more evidence behind them.

### Blood Glucose

The 2009 IGOB131 trial and the 2008 combination trial both reported lower blood glucose than placebo [7][8]. In a 2018 trial of 24 people with metabolic syndrome, 150 mg of *Irvingia gabonensis* twice a day for 90 days lowered blood glucose in the second hour of a glucose tolerance test compared with baseline, and 58.3% of treated patients no longer met the criteria for metabolic syndrome, against 16.7% on placebo [18].

Read these results with caution:
- The same limits apply as for the weight and cholesterol data: small samples, short trials and poor reporting [10][16]
- Weight loss itself improves blood glucose, so any effect may follow from weight loss rather than act directly
- No trial has tested African mango in people with diagnosed diabetes or compared it with established glucose-lowering treatment

### Fat Cell Development (In Vitro)

In a single cell study, IGOB131 inhibited fat cell development in mouse 3T3-L1 cells, lowering PPAR-gamma and leptin and raising adiponectin [5].

This finding has not been confirmed in people and only generates hypotheses. Compounds that block fat cell development in a dish often do nothing useful in the body.

### Summary of Evidence Quality

| Outcome | Number of Trials | Conclusion |
|---------|:-:|----------|
| Weight loss | 5 in the 2020 meta-analysis [16] | Four at high risk of bias; the low-risk trial found no significant difference |
| Cholesterol reduction | Pooled in [16] | Pooled benefit; may be explained by soluble fiber content |
| Blood glucose reduction | 3 [7][8][18] | May follow weight loss; not studied in diabetes |
| Fat cell inhibition | 1 (in vitro) [5] | Cell culture data only; not shown in humans |

## 4. Recommended Dosing

No established recommended dose exists for African mango, and no government health agency or medical body has issued one.

### Doses Used in Clinical Research

| Form | Daily Dose | Schedule | Duration | Study |
|------|:-:|:-:|:-:|------|
| Seed preparation | 1.05 g three times a day | Three times a day | One month | Ngondi et al., 2005 [9] |
| IGOB131 extract | 150 mg, twice a day | 30-60 min before lunch and dinner | 10 weeks | Ngondi et al., 2009 [7] |
| IGOB131 + Cissus | Capsules twice daily | Before meals | 10 weeks | Oben et al., 2008 [8] |
| Seed extract | 150 mg twice a day | Twice a day | 90 days | Méndez-Del Villar et al., 2018 [18] |
| Kernel extract | 300 mg | One tablet a day | 12 weeks | Nonsa-Ard et al., 2022 [19] |

### Important Dosing Caveats

- No dose-finding studies have been done, and no minimum effective dose is known
- No maximum safe dose has been set for people; in rats, IGOB131 caused no harm for 90 days even at the highest dose tested, 2500 mg/kg body weight a day [20]
- The longest trial described here lasted 90 days [18]; effects beyond that are unknown
- Taking it 30-60 minutes before meals was used in the 2009 trial [7] but has not been compared with other timings

### Given the Limited Evidence

There is not enough data to recommend a dose for any use. Reviews have concluded African mango cannot be recommended for weight loss [10][17]. If you are considering it, discuss the limited evidence with your doctor first.

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Clinical Trial Safety Data

The human safety data come from small, short trials that were not designed to study safety:

- Adverse events in the first three trials included headache and sleep difficulty [10]; the 2008 and 2009 trials also reported gas [7][8], and in the 2009 trial side effects were similar on placebo [7]
- Across five trials, the reported adverse events were minor [16]
- The trials were small and short (the longest described here lasted 90 days [18]), too short to detect rare or delayed harms

In rats given IGOB131 for 90 days, even at the highest dose tested (2500 mg/kg body weight a day), there were no treatment-related changes in blood tests, organs or behaviour, and IGOB131 showed no genetic toxicity in laboratory tests [20]. Animal results do not guarantee safety in people.

### Historical Food Safety

The fruit and seeds of *Irvingia gabonensis* have long been eaten in West and Central Africa [13][14], which offers some reassurance about the whole food. Food use and supplement use differ:

- **Traditional food use** means ground whole seeds cooked in soups as part of a mixed diet [14][21]
- **Supplement use** means concentrated extracts such as IGOB131, a form not eaten traditionally
- A history of safe food use does not guarantee that a concentrated extract is safe
- Many plant compounds that are safe in food amounts can cause harm at supplement doses

### Potential Safety Concerns

The following concerns come from the limited data and from how the supplement may act:

**Gastrointestinal effects:** The seed contains mucilage [20], a soluble fiber. Fiber supplements mostly cause mild to moderate gut side effects [6]. Whether a 150 mg extract carries enough fiber to do this is unknown.

**Hypoglycemia risk:** Trials reported lower blood glucose with African mango [7][8][18]. That could raise the risk of low blood sugar in:
- People taking diabetes medicines (insulin, sulfonylureas or other glucose-lowering drugs)
- People with reactive hypoglycemia
- People who are fasting or on very low-calorie diets

**Allergic reactions:** As with any plant product, allergic reactions are possible. People allergic to *Irvingia* should avoid it.

**Liver injury (one case report):** Some weight-loss supplements have been linked to liver injury. A 2014 case report on African mango, titled "innocent magic formula?", is indexed on PubMed under drug-induced liver injury [22]. A single case cannot show that African mango caused the injury. Stop taking it and see your doctor the same day if you notice yellow skin or eyes or dark urine.

**Quality and contamination:** Bush mango seeds sold as a soup thickener in Nigeria carried aflatoxins (mould toxins): every sample exceeded the 2 μg/kg limit for aflatoxin B1, and 55% exceeded the 4 μg/kg EU limit for total aflatoxins [21]. Discoloured seeds had higher levels of most mycotoxins [21]. Supplement extracts were not tested in that study. Independent testing services such as consumerlab.com can help you check product quality.

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** No safety data exist for African mango supplements in pregnancy or breastfeeding, so it is safest to avoid them.

**Children:** No studies have been done in children, so it is safest to avoid them.

**Liver or kidney disease:** No studies have been done in people with liver or kidney disease. Talk to your doctor first.

**Pre-surgical:** Because African mango may lower blood glucose, tell your surgeon and anaesthetist if you take it.

## 6. Drug Interactions

No drug interaction studies have been published for African mango. The interactions below are theoretical, based on effects seen in trials:

### Potential Interactions Based on Observed Effects

| Drug Class | Examples | Potential Interaction |
|-----------|---------|----------|
| Diabetes medications | Metformin, insulin, sulfonylureas, SGLT2 inhibitors, GLP-1 agonists | Additive blood glucose lowering; possible low blood sugar |
| Cholesterol-lowering drugs | Statins, ezetimibe, bile acid sequestrants, PCSK9 inhibitors | Additive cholesterol lowering; fiber in the whole seed might reduce absorption if taken together |
| Blood thinners | Warfarin, aspirin, clopidogrel | Folk medicine uses the seeds as a blood thinner [14]; not studied with these drugs |

### Fiber-Related Drug Absorption Interactions

The seed contains mucilage [20]. Soluble fibers can form a gel in the gut that may slow or reduce absorption of some medicines. Whether a 150 mg extract carries enough fiber to matter is unknown. Spacing supplements and medicines apart may help; ask your pharmacist how far apart.

### No Confirmed Interactions

No interaction has been confirmed for African mango; the ones above are theoretical. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

## 7. Dietary Sources

### The African Mango Fruit

The fruit pulp of *Irvingia gabonensis*:

- Is eaten fresh or dried, made into juice and wine, or used as a flavouring [13]
- Contains more vitamin C than some vitamin C-rich fruits [13]
- Also provides carbohydrates and protein [14]

### The Seeds (Dika Nuts / Ogbono)

In traditional use the seeds matter more than the pulp, both as food and as trade [14]. They are:

- Rich in oil, 63% to 69% crude fat [13]
- Ground and used to thicken soups [14][21]
- Known as dika nuts or ogbono [14]

### Nutritional Composition of African Mango Seeds

The kernel is mostly fat (63% to 69% crude fat), mainly myristic and lauric acids [13]. Defatted seed extracts contain flavonoid glycosides and ellagic acid derivatives [14].

### Fatty Acid Profile of Seed Oil (Dika Fat)

One analysis of the kernel oil found [15]:

| Fatty Acid | % of Fatty Acids | Type |
|-----------|:-:|:-:|
| Lauric acid (C12:0) | 39.35 ± 0.01% | Saturated |
| Myristic acid (C14:0) | 20.54 ± 0.01% | Saturated |
| Linolenic acid | 6.44 ± 0.02% | Polyunsaturated |

The seed fat is mostly saturated [13][15], and saturated fat tends to raise LDL cholesterol in other diets. Whether supplement extracts keep much of this fat is unknown.

### Traditional Food Preparations

**Ogbono soup (Nigeria):** Ground dika nuts thicken soups and sauces; the seed mucilage gives them their texture [20][21].

**Dika fat:** The fat is solid at room temperature, and a 2020 review saw its melting behaviour as suiting a range of food uses [13].

### Availability Outside West Africa

Outside West and Central Africa:

- The fresh fruit is rarely sold
- Dried ogbono seed powder is sold in African food stores and online, mainly for soup
- The most common form is the seed extract supplement sold for weight loss [23]

### Compared to Common Mango

The common mango (*Mangifera indica*) sold in grocery stores is a different species from African mango (*Irvingia gabonensis*):

| Characteristic | Common Mango (*Mangifera indica*) | African Mango (*Irvingia gabonensis*) |
|---------------|----------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| Family | Anacardiaceae | Irvingiaceae |
| Primary edible part | Fruit pulp | Fruit pulp and seed (kernel) [13] |
| Supplement use | Leaf extract (mangiferin) | Seed extract (IGOB131 and others) [7][23] |
