## Current view (reviewed 10 November 2026)

Brad thinks most adults probably eat too little choline and that a supplement to fill the gap is a reasonable option, but he does not condone megadosing: aim for around the recommended amount, not several times it ([Brad, 6 Nov 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/mothers-took-choline-their-kids-scored-8-iq-points-higher)). For people who are pregnant or planning to be, Brad encourages eggs and says a supplement to make sure you reach 450 mg a day is probably a reasonable option, staying far below the upper limit of 3,500 mg a day ([Brad, 6 Nov 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/mothers-took-choline-their-kids-scored-8-iq-points-higher)). The form studied in pregnancy is phosphatidylcholine, which has the most trial data and did not raise TMAO, so Brad thinks it is probably best to stick with it ([Brad, 6 Nov 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/mothers-took-choline-their-kids-scored-8-iq-points-higher)). Check your prenatal vitamin first, because many contain little or no choline, and talk to your midwife or obstetrician before you start ([Brad, 6 Nov 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/mothers-took-choline-their-kids-scored-8-iq-points-higher)). Brad calls the 2026 Colorado trial, in which children of mothers who took phosphatidylcholine scored about 8 points higher on an IQ test at age four, an exciting result that needs repeating, because half the children are missing from the IQ result, the autism finding is exploratory, and both positive phosphatidylcholine trials come from the same group ([Brad, 6 Nov 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/mothers-took-choline-their-kids-scored-8-iq-points-higher)). Brad reads the link between TMAO, a compound gut bacteria make from choline, and heart disease as an association that has not been shown to be the cause ([Brad, 6 Nov 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/mothers-took-choline-their-kids-scored-8-iq-points-higher)).

## Key safety points

- Choline precursors taken with Alzheimer's medicines such as donepezil, rivastigmine or galantamine are said to raise acetylcholine more than either alone [55]. If you take one of these medicines, check with your doctor before adding a choline supplement.
- If you are pregnant or planning to be, talk to your doctor or midwife before starting a choline supplement; the form studied in pregnancy is phosphatidylcholine [96][97].
- Alpha-GPC prescriptions were linked to a higher 10-year stroke risk in a large South Korean cohort; the study shows an association, not proof of cause [22]. Risk rose with longer use; if you plan to take it long term, or have cardiovascular risk, talk to your doctor first.
- Choline bitartrate raised TMAO and platelet reactivity in healthy adults, and alpha-GPC raised TMAO, in short studies [18][106]. Higher TMAO has been linked to heart attack, stroke and death [77], but a genetic study suggests the link with heart disease and stroke may not be causal [104]. If you have heart disease, talk to your doctor before taking high doses.
- The upper limit for adults is 3,500 mg of choline a day, lower for children; the limits are based on low blood pressure and fishy body odor [93].
- High intakes are also associated with vomiting, heavy sweating and salivation, and liver toxicity [93].
- People with trimethylaminuria (fish odor syndrome) should avoid supplements high in choline or lecithin [114].

## 1. Overview

Choline is an essential nutrient needed for cell membranes, the neurotransmitter acetylcholine, methyl group metabolism and lipid transport. The body uses it to make phosphatidylcholine and sphingomyelin, two phospholipids that every plant and animal cell needs for its membranes [93]. Acetylcholine matters for memory, mood, muscle control and other brain and nerve functions [93]. Choline also helps regulate gene expression, membrane signaling, lipid transport and early brain development [93].

The liver makes some choline, mostly as phosphatidylcholine, but not enough to meet the body's needs, so some must come from food [93]. The Institute of Medicine set the first dietary recommendations for choline in 1998 [2].

In a 1991 study, healthy men in hospital who ate no choline for 3 weeks had their plasma choline fall by an average of 30%, and a liver enzyme (ALT) rose steadily [110].

Most people in the United States eat less than the Adequate Intake (AI). In the 2013-2014 NHANES survey, average daily intake from foods and beverages was 402 mg in men and 278 mg in women [93]. Supplements add very little to total intake [93].

Several groups are at higher risk of low choline status:

- **Postmenopausal women:** Estrogen switches on the gene that makes choline, so premenopausal women may need less from food [93]. In a feeding study, a low-choline diet caused fatty liver or muscle damage in 80% of postmenopausal women but only 44% of premenopausal women [7].
- **Pregnant and breastfeeding women:** About 90% to 95% of pregnant women eat less than the AI [93]. The fetus receives a large supply of choline during pregnancy, and breast milk contains large amounts [10].
- **People with certain genetic variants:** Common variants in genes for choline, folate and methionine metabolism can raise the amount of choline a person needs from food. One common variant in the PEMT gene reduces the extra choline synthesis that estrogen normally triggers in women [93][35].
- **Patients receiving long-term total parenteral nutrition (TPN):** Choline is not routinely added to commercial intravenous feeding solutions, and people on long-term TPN have low plasma choline, which can cause liver problems including fatty liver [93]. The American Society for Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition recommends adding choline to adult and pediatric formulations [13].
- **People with low folate intake:** When the diet lacks folate, choline becomes the main methyl donor and the need for it rises [93].

Choline deficiency can cause muscle damage, liver damage and nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) [93]. Frank deficiency in healthy men and nonpregnant women is very rare, possibly because the body makes some choline itself [93].

Choline status is not routinely measured in healthy people. In healthy adults, plasma choline ranges from 7 to 20 micromol/L, and one study found 7-9.3 micromol/L in fasting adults [93][16]. Plasma choline does not fall below 50% of normal even after more than a week without food [93].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

The form of choline affects how well it is absorbed and how much reaches gut bacteria that turn it into TMAO (see Safety).

### Choline Forms at a Glance

| Form | Key Notes |
|------|------|
| Lecithin | A substance rich in phosphatidylcholine, made during commercial purification of phospholipids and used as a food emulsifier [93]. |
| Phosphatidylcholine | The main form of choline in food; about half the choline in the US diet comes this way [93]. Did not raise fasting TMAO in a 4-week trial [18]. The form used in the pregnancy trials that found benefits [96][98]. |
| CDP-choline (citicoline) | Breaks down quickly to cytidine and choline, which enter the brain separately [72]. A review proposed that it is less prone to conversion to TMA than other forms [20]. |
| Alpha-GPC (choline alfoscerate) | Raised TMAO after a single dose [106]. Prescribed in South Korea, where a large cohort linked it to higher stroke risk [22]. |
| Choline bitartrate | Raised fasting TMAO and platelet reactivity in a 4-week trial [18]; absorbed less well than choline from egg yolk phospholipids [25]. |
| Choline chloride | Used in parenteral nutrition trials and older memory studies [34][40]. |

### Absorption and TMAO Considerations

The NIH fact sheet notes that no studies had compared absorption of the different supplement forms [93]. A 2019 crossover trial in 18 adults then compared a drink with 3 g of phospholipid-bound choline from egg yolk against a control drink with 3 g of choline supplied as choline bitartrate (9.3 g of the compound). The rise in plasma choline was four times higher after the egg yolk drink [25]. Intestinal bacteria convert some dietary choline to trimethylamine (TMA), which is absorbed and turned into TMAO by the liver (see TMAO and Cardiovascular Risk) [93].

The egg yolk study was funded by a company that employs two of its authors [25]. In a crossover study of 6 healthy men, single doses of 550 mg of choline as choline chloride, choline bitartrate, alpha-GPC or egg phosphatidylcholine raised plasma choline to a similar extent [106].

**Phosphatidylcholine and lecithin** are partly absorbed intact, packed into chylomicrons and carried in the lymph to tissues, including the brain and placenta [93].

**CDP-choline (citicoline)** is significantly less toxic than choline on a molar basis, according to a 2019 review, which also proposed that its choline is less prone to conversion to TMA [20]. A 1985 rat study still found more urinary radioactivity linked to TMA after oral than after intravenous dosing, which suggests some citicoline is broken down before it is absorbed [26].

**Choline bitartrate** raised TMAO in a 2021 trial in healthy volunteers with normal kidney function. Plasma TMAO rose in all three groups taking choline bitartrate supplements, and so did platelet reactivity. Four large eggs a day or phosphatidylcholine supplements raised neither [18]. In a crossover study of 37 healthy men, a meal containing 600 mg choline as choline bitartrate gave three times the plasma TMAO response of a meal with phosphatidylcholine or no added choline [27].

**Alpha-GPC** quickly raised TMAO in the six-man crossover study, as choline chloride and choline bitartrate did, while egg phosphatidylcholine did not [106]. It was also linked to higher stroke risk in a large observational study (see Safety) [22].

### Key Principles for Form Selection

**For general supplementation to meet AI:** NIH lists choline bitartrate, phosphatidylcholine and lecithin as the usual supplement forms [93]. In trials, phosphatidylcholine did not raise fasting TMAO, while choline bitartrate did [18][27].

**For cognitive support:** Most memory and attention trials used citicoline or alpha-GPC (see Memory and Cognition) [37][39][52]. Alpha-GPC also carries the stroke signal from one large cohort [22].

**For pregnancy:** The Colorado trials that found benefits used phosphatidylcholine [96][98], and Brad suggests sticking with that form in pregnancy [97]. The main maternal feeding trial compared total intakes of 480 mg or 930 mg choline a day in the third trimester [58]. Eggs and other foods rich in phosphatidylcholine are another way to raise intake.

**For minimizing TMAO risk:** Phosphatidylcholine and eggs did not raise fasting TMAO in the 2021 trial [18], and egg phosphatidylcholine did not raise TMAO after a single dose [106]. If you have heart disease or high cardiovascular risk, talk to your doctor before taking high doses of choline bitartrate.

Dr Brad Stanfield's [MicroVitamin](https://drstanfield.com/products/microvitamin) capsules and MicroVitamin+ Powder each contain 500 mg of CDP-choline (citicoline) (US, UK and EU v9; not for use in pregnancy or breastfeeding). Researchers have suggested that citicoline may be less likely to raise TMAO than other choline sources, though this has not been tested in a human trial [20]. Citicoline is only part choline by weight, so it adds a modest amount of choline, and foods like eggs remain the main way to reach the Adequate Intake.

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Stroke

A 2002 pooled analysis of four trials of oral citicoline (500, 1,000 or 2,000 mg) given within 24 hours of a moderate to severe ischemic stroke included 1,372 patients. Recovery at 3 months was 25.2% with citicoline and 20.2% with placebo (OR 1.33, 95% CI 1.10 to 1.62); the 2,000 mg dose showed the largest difference [36]. A 2014 review later noted that two large pivotal trials found no benefit of citicoline in ischemic stroke or traumatic brain injury [72]. A supplement is no substitute for emergency care. For signs of a stroke (face drooping, arm or leg weakness or numbness, trouble speaking, vision loss, even if it passes, or a sudden severe headache), call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).

Those two trials were ICTUS and COBRIT. ICTUS randomized 2,298 patients with moderate to severe acute ischemic stroke to citicoline or placebo within 24 h; it was stopped for futility, and global recovery was similar in both groups (odds ratio 1.03, 95% CI 0.86 to 1.25) [107]. ICTUS was funded by Ferrer Grupo [107]. In COBRIT, 1,213 patients with traumatic brain injury took citicoline (2,000 mg) or placebo daily for 90 days, and function and thinking did not improve [108].

### Alzheimer's Disease and Dementia

#### Lecithin for Alzheimer's Disease

High-dose lecithin did not help in early trials. In 11 outpatients, lecithin 10 g three times daily for 3 months tripled plasma choline but did not change test scores [49]. In 51 people, 20 to 25 g a day of purified soy lecithin for six months did no better than placebo [50]. A 2003 Cochrane Review of 12 randomized trials in 265 patients with Alzheimer's disease, 21 with Parkinsonian dementia, and 90 with self-identified memory problems found no clear clinical benefits of lecithin supplementation for treating Alzheimer's disease or Parkinsonian dementia [51].

#### Alpha-GPC for Alzheimer's Disease

In 261 people aged 60 to 80 years with mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease, alpha-GPC (choline alfoscerate) 400 mg three times daily for 180 days improved cognitive and global scores compared with placebo [52].

In the ASCOMALVA trial, 113 people with mild to moderate Alzheimer's disease took donepezil 10 mg a day with either alpha-GPC 1,200 mg a day or placebo for 24 months. The combination lowered the severity of behavioral symptoms and caregiver distress; depression, anxiety and apathy fell with the combination and worsened with donepezil alone [53].

#### CDP-Choline with Cholinesterase Inhibitors

In a retrospective study of 448 Italian patients aged 65 or older with Alzheimer's disease, 251 took citicoline 1,000 mg a day with a cholinesterase inhibitor and 197 took the inhibitor alone. Mini-Mental State Examination scores in the combination group were higher at 9 months than at baseline [55]. The design was not randomized.

#### Egg Consumption and Alzheimer's Risk

In the Rush Memory and Aging Project, 1,024 older adults with a mean age of about 81 were followed for a mean of 6.7 years. Eating more than one egg a week was linked to a lower risk of Alzheimer's dementia (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.34 to 0.83), and the authors estimated that 39% of this link ran through dietary choline [48]. The study is observational and cannot show that eggs prevent dementia.

### Pregnancy and Lactation

Choline matters for fetal development, especially of the brain, and the developing nervous system is sensitive to choline supply, including neural tube closure [10]. Prenatal supplements typically contain little if any choline [93].

#### Maternal Supplementation and Infant Brain Development

In a controlled feeding study, pregnant women in their third trimester were randomized to either 480 mg or 930 mg choline a day until delivery, 13 in each group. Infants of mothers in the 930 mg group had faster average reaction times when tested repeatedly over their first 13 months [58].

The feeding study was funded in part by the Egg Nutrition Center and the Beef Checkoff, which had no role in the trial [58].

In a North Carolina trial, 140 pregnant women took 750 mg of choline a day, as about 5 g of phosphatidylcholine, or a corn oil placebo from 18 weeks of pregnancy to 90 days after birth. Their infants did no better on memory, language or overall development at 10 and 12 months [100].

A 2025 systematic review of four randomized trials and five observational studies judged the evidence insufficient to support or refute the idea that more choline in pregnancy improves child development [101]. It came out before the 2026 Colorado trial below.

#### Phosphatidylcholine Trials in Colorado

In a trial of 100 healthy pregnant women, phosphatidylcholine was given from the second trimester at twice normal dietary levels, and the babies were also supplemented to the third month after birth. At the fifth postnatal week, more infants in the choline group showed mature filtering of a repeated sound (76% versus 43%), with no difference at the 13th week; no adverse effects were seen in the mothers or babies [98]. At 40 months, parents rated the children in the phosphatidylcholine group (N=23) as having fewer attention problems and less social withdrawal than the placebo group (N=26) [99].

The larger 2026 trial randomized 206 women to 9,000 mg phosphatidylcholine or placebo from 16 weeks' gestation through delivery [96]. At 4 years, fewer children in the phosphatidylcholine group had multiple or pervasive social withdrawal problems, and Full-Scale IQ was higher (mean difference 7.78, 95% CI 1.88 to 13.7) [96]. Attention problems, the other main outcome, did not fall significantly [96]. In an exploratory analysis, autism-range scores with language problems on a parent questionnaire were seen in 10 of 71 children whose mothers had placebo and 1 of 71 whose mothers had phosphatidylcholine [96]. The questionnaire flags children for assessment; it does not diagnose autism [97]. Nausea, vomiting, dizziness and high blood pressure were not more common in the mothers [96]. The authors list limits: parents rated social behavior, and one-third of women were lost between enrollment and the 4-year assessments [96]. Brad notes that the trial was funded by the US National Institutes of Health and two foundations, not a supplement company [97]. The authors now suggest 6,300 mg phosphatidylcholine or 900 mg choline, beginning as early as possible in pregnancy [96]; Brad suggests a smaller amount, enough to reach the AI (see Practical Guidance for Pregnancy) [97].

#### Long-Term Effects on Attention

At 7 years of age, 20 children from the Cornell feeding trial [58] were tested for sustained attention. Children in the higher-intake group scored better on the main attention measure than children in the lower-intake group [59].

#### Neural Tube Defect Risk

Studies disagree. A 2022 meta-analysis of case-control studies, with 1,131 mothers of newborns with neural tube defects and 4,439 control mothers, linked low maternal choline intake or blood levels to higher odds of a neural tube defect (pooled estimate 1.36, 95% CI 1.11 to 1.67) [103]. Another study found no difference in plasma choline between pregnancies with and without a neural tube defect, and its authors concluded that adding choline to folic acid may not further lower the risk [62].

#### Side Effects in Pregnancy Trials

In a South African trial, 70 heavy drinkers recruited in mid-pregnancy took a daily oral dose of 2 g of choline or a placebo until delivery. The only side effect was a small increase in nausea or indigestion, with no effect on diarrhea, vomiting, blood pressure or body odor [102]. The paper reported feasibility and side effects, not the babies' outcomes [102].

#### Practical Guidance for Pregnancy

The AI for choline is 450 mg a day during pregnancy and 550 mg during breastfeeding, and most pregnant women fall short [93]. Talk to your doctor or midwife about how to reach it from food and, if needed, a supplement.

Brad's advice is to eat eggs, check your prenatal vitamin, and consider a supplement to make sure you are reaching 450 mg a day, preferably as phosphatidylcholine [97].

### Memory and Cognition

Results are mixed. In two trials in healthy middle-aged and older adults, citicoline improved some memory scores [37][39], and in one trial in adolescent boys it improved attention [44]. Choline chloride did not improve memory in older people [40], and choline bitartrate did not improve memory in students [42].

#### Middle-Aged and Older Adults

**CDP-choline for memory (2021).** In 100 healthy adults aged 50 to 85 with age-associated memory impairment, citicoline (Cognizin) 500 mg a day for 12 weeks improved episodic memory and composite memory more than placebo [39]. Both were secondary outcomes.

Brad notes that this citicoline trial was run by the company that makes the ingredient [97].

**CDP-choline for verbal memory (1996).** In 47 women and 48 men aged 50 to 85 years, citicoline 1,000 mg a day for 3 months improved delayed recall only in those with relatively poor memories. In a crossover study of 32 of these people, 2,000 mg a day for 2 months improved immediate and delayed recall [37].

**Choline chloride for memory in elderly (1980).** In 10 people over age 60, choline chloride 2 g four times a day, taken for 21 days, did not improve memory [40].

#### Attention in Adolescents

**CDP-choline in male adolescents (2019).** In 75 healthy adolescent males, citicoline 250 mg or 500 mg for 28 days improved attention and psychomotor speed compared with placebo, and a higher weight-adjusted dose predicted less impulsivity [44].

#### Younger People

**Egg-based foods in preadolescents (2022).** In 122 children aged 9 to 13, foods made with whole egg powder did not improve executive function during the 9-month intervention compared with placebo; foods with milk powder improved one attention measure [43].

**Choline bitartrate in young adults (2016).** In crossover experiments in students, 2.0 to 2.5 g of choline bitartrate did not improve memory about an hour later [42].

#### Observational Data on Choline and Cognition

Higher choline intakes and plasma levels have been linked to better cognitive performance in several observational studies [93]:

- In 2,195 adults aged 70-74 in Norway, those with plasma free choline concentrations below 8.4 micromol/L (20th percentile) had poorer sensorimotor speed, perceptual speed, executive function, and global cognition compared to those above this threshold [45].
- In 1,391 adults aged 36-83 from the Framingham Offspring study, higher choline intakes were associated with better verbal memory and visual memory. Higher choline intakes during the earlier assessment period were also associated with smaller white matter hyperintensity volume, a sign of small-vessel disease in the brain [46].
- In 2,497 Finnish men followed for 21.9 years, those in the highest quartile of phosphatidylcholine intake had a 28% lower risk of dementia than those in the lowest; total choline intake had no association with dementia [109].

However, a 2015 systematic review of 13 studies on choline levels and neurological outcomes in adults found that choline supplements did not result in clear improvements in cognition in healthy adults [93]. The review concluded that high-quality intervention studies are lacking [47].

### Fatty Liver Disease (NAFLD)

Choline, especially as phosphatidylcholine, is needed to move fat out of the liver. When choline is lacking, fat builds up in the liver and NAFLD can result [93].

**Choline deficiency causes NAFLD directly.** In a study of 57 adults fed less than 50 mg choline per 70 kg body weight per day (less than 10% of the AI) for up to 42 days, 37 developed liver dysfunction [93]. Liver function returned to normal in 29 participants after they ate a diet containing 25% to 75% of the choline AI [93].

**Observational evidence in Chinese adults.** A cross-sectional study of 56,195 Chinese adults aged 40 to 75 linked higher choline intake with lower risk of NAFLD [93]. The risk of NAFLD was 32% lower in women in the highest quintile of choline intake (412 mg/day) compared to the lowest (179 mg/day) and 25% lower in men in the highest (452 mg/day) quintile compared to those in the lowest (199 mg/day). In women, the link held only in those of normal weight [93].

**Postmenopausal women with nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH).** In a cross-sectional study of 664 adults and children with biopsy-proven NAFLD, postmenopausal women whose choline intake was under 50% of the AI had worse fibrosis [33].

**Total parenteral nutrition.** In a placebo-controlled trial, 15 patients on long-term TPN received their usual solution or one with 2 g of choline chloride added for 24 weeks. Liver scans improved in the choline group, and fatty liver returned 10 weeks after choline stopped [34].

**Genetic susceptibility.** Variants in genes of choline and folate metabolism, and estrogen status, change how much choline a person needs to protect the liver [8]. At least 40% of women of childbearing age carry a variant that makes the gene insensitive to estrogen, so adequate dietary choline matters more for them [93].

**Limitation.** Only limited data exist on using choline to treat NAFLD, and more research is needed [93].

### Ulcerative Colitis

Colonic mucus in people with ulcerative colitis lacks phosphatidylcholine, and delayed-release phosphatidylcholine aims to replace it [71].

**Mixed results from clinical trials.** In a multicenter trial of 156 patients whose ulcerative colitis had not responded to mesalazine, the highest dose (3.2 g) of a delayed-release phosphatidylcholine formula lowered disease activity by 51.7% versus 33.3% with placebo; remission rates (31.4% versus 15%) did not differ significantly [69].

**Earlier positive results.** A meta-analysis of three single-center trials of a delayed-release lecithin containing about 30% phosphatidylcholine, with 160 patients, found higher remission rates than placebo (OR 9.68). A trial of a lecithin with more than 94% phosphatidylcholine failed [71].

### Asthma

In an Indian trial, choline 500 mg or 1,000 mg three times daily or placebo was given for four months. Symptom scores fell and symptom-free days rose in all three groups, but the changes were significant only in the choline groups, and only the higher dose significantly reduced the need for asthma medicine [66].

In a 6-month trial in 76 people with asthma, a choline supplement of 1,500 mg twice daily lowered symptom and drug scores and markers of airway inflammation in an unblinded comparison with standard treatment [67].

### Dry Eye

In 31 adults with dry eye and meibomian gland dysfunction, sunflower lecithin 4,800 mg a day for 90 days did not reduce symptoms more than an olive oil placebo. Both groups improved [73].

### Physical Performance

In 13 college-aged men, lower body force rose more after 6 days of alpha-GPC 600 mg a day than after placebo; the upper body change was not significant [63]. In 13 healthy men, one dose of choline at 50 mg/kg did not change physical or mental test results after an exhausting load-carriage session [64].

### Breast Cancer

In a population-based study of 1,508 women with breast cancer and 1,556 controls, the highest quintile of choline intake was linked to lower risk (OR 0.76, 95% CI 0.58 to 1.00). Women with the PEMT CC genotype had higher risk (OR 1.30, 95% CI 1.01 to 1.67) [65].

### Glaucoma

A 2014 review judged that citicoline seems to help some slowly progressing neurodegenerative conditions, such as glaucoma [72].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Adequate Intakes (AI)

The Food and Nutrition Board set AIs for choline in 1998 to prevent liver damage, judged by serum alanine aminotransferase levels [2][93]:

| Age / Group | AI (mg/day) |
|-------------|:-:|
| Infants 0-6 months | 125 |
| Infants 7-12 months | 150 |
| Children 1-3 years | 200 |
| Children 4-8 years | 250 |
| Children 9-13 years | 375 |
| Males 14+ years | 550 |
| Females 14-18 years | 400 |
| Females 19+ years | 425 |
| Pregnant women | 450 |
| Lactating women | 550 |

How much choline a person needs depends on dietary methionine, betaine and folate (also methyl donors), sex, pregnancy, breastfeeding, stage of development, how much choline the body makes, and genetic variants [93].

### Tolerable Upper Intake Levels (UL)

The Food and Nutrition Board based the ULs for choline from food and supplements on the amounts linked to low blood pressure and fishy body odor [2][93]:

| Age | UL (mg/day) |
|-----|:-:|
| Children 1-8 years | 1,000 |
| Children 9-13 years | 2,000 |
| Adolescents 14-18 years | 3,000 |
| Adults 19+ years | 3,500 |

No UL was set for infants; breast milk, formula and food should be their only sources [93].

### Dosing by Condition

These are the doses trials used, not recommendations:

**For stroke.** Oral citicoline 500, 1,000 or 2,000 mg, started in the first 24 hours after a stroke; larger later trials found no benefit [36][72].

**For Alzheimer's disease.** Alpha-GPC 400 mg three times daily [52]; alpha-GPC 1,200 mg a day with donepezil [53]; citicoline 1,000 mg a day with a cholinesterase inhibitor [55].

**For pregnancy.** The AI is 450 mg a day [93]. The feeding trial compared total intakes of 480 mg or 930 mg choline a day [58].

The 2026 Colorado trial used 9,000 mg phosphatidylcholine [96]. Brad suggests enough to reach 450 mg a day, preferably as phosphatidylcholine (see Current view) [97].

**For memory and cognition.** Citicoline 500 mg a day for 12 weeks in healthy older adults [39]; 1,000 to 2,000 mg a day in older adults with relatively poor memory [37].

**For attention in adolescents.** Citicoline 250 mg or 500 mg a day for 28 days [44].

**For asthma.** Choline 500 mg or 1,000 mg three times daily [66]; 1,500 mg twice daily [67].

### How to Read a Supplement Label

The weight of a choline compound is not its choline content. A capsule of choline bitartrate, citicoline or lecithin supplies less choline than the compound weight, so look for a label that states the choline content.

By Brad's calculation from molecular weight, choline makes up about 14% of phosphatidylcholine, about 41% of choline bitartrate, about 75% of choline chloride, about 21% of citicoline and about 40% of alpha-GPC [97].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Common Side Effects

The UL for adults is 3,500 mg of choline a day [93]. High intakes are associated with fishy body odor, vomiting, excessive sweating and salivation, low blood pressure, and liver toxicity [93].

**Fishy body odor.** People with trimethylaminuria (fish malodor syndrome) have abnormal amounts of trimethylamine in their urine, sweat and breath, which gives a strong fishy odor [75]. Because gut bacteria make trimethylamine from choline, high choline intake can cause a fishy body odor [93]. A clinical review of primary trimethylaminuria lists food supplements high in choline or lecithin among the things to avoid, while the diet is planned so that choline still meets the recommended intake and is not restricted in pregnancy or breastfeeding [114]. If you have the condition, avoid choline supplements and ask your doctor about your diet.

### TMAO and Cardiovascular Risk

Gut bacteria convert some choline to TMA, which the liver turns into TMAO. Choline intake raises TMAO in a dose-dependent way in adults, and TMAO has been linked to higher cardiovascular risk [93]. In mice, extra dietary choline or TMAO promoted atherosclerosis, and suppressing gut bacteria blocked the effect of dietary choline [111]. In mice and in the Framingham Heart Study, TMAO did not track the extent of atherosclerosis; in a mouse model of unstable plaque, it tracked markers of plaque instability [76].

**TMAO and cardiovascular events.** In 4,007 patients having elective coronary angiography, those in the highest quartile of fasting TMAO had more major adverse cardiovascular events (death, heart attack or stroke) during 3 years of follow-up than those in the lowest (HR 2.54, 95% CI 1.96 to 3.28) [77].

**Is TMAO a cause?** A 2019 Mendelian randomization study, which uses genes as a natural experiment, found that genetically higher TMAO was not linked to coronary artery disease, heart attack or stroke, while type 2 diabetes and kidney disease raised TMAO. The authors concluded that the observational link may be due to confounding or reverse causation [104]. Mice fed extra phosphatidylcholine had 2-fold higher plasma TMAO yet less atherosclerosis [105]. Brad reads the TMAO link with heart disease as an association that has not been shown to be the cause, and notes that, as far as he can find, no trial has yet lowered TMAO in people and then counted heart attacks [97].

**Choline bitartrate raises TMAO.** In the 2021 trial, plasma TMAO and platelet reactivity rose in every group taking choline bitartrate supplements [18]. In 37 healthy men, a 600 mg choline meal as choline bitartrate gave three times the plasma TMAO response and 2.5 times the urinary TMAO change of meals with phosphatidylcholine or no added choline [27]. One of the 2021 trial's authors reports patents and industry ties related to cardiovascular diagnostics [18].

In a 2-month study, choline bisulfate supplying about 450 mg of choline a day raised fasting TMAO more than 10-fold in adults without heart disease, diabetes or high blood pressure, and appeared to make their platelets clump more readily, though the study measured blood tests, not heart attacks or strokes [113].

**Phosphatidylcholine supplements did not raise fasting TMAO.** In the same 2021 trial, phosphatidylcholine supplements did not raise fasting TMAO or platelet reactivity [18], and in the meal study phosphatidylcholine performed like the no-choline meal [27]. That finding is about fasting levels and short trials: labeled phosphatidylcholine eaten in eggs raised TMAO over the following hours [77], and mice fed extra phosphatidylcholine had higher TMAO [105].

**Eggs and TMAO.** Dietary phosphatidylcholine comes mainly from eggs, red meat and fish [94]. Eating four large eggs daily for 4 weeks left fasting TMAO unchanged [18]. After two hard-boiled eggs with labeled phosphatidylcholine, TMAO rose over time and fell when gut bacteria were suppressed with antibiotics [77].

### Alpha-GPC and Stroke Risk

A South Korean cohort study followed about 12 million adults aged 50 or older. People prescribed alpha-GPC during 2006 to 2008 were more likely to have a stroke from 2009 to 2018 than matched nonusers (adjusted HR 1.43, 95% CI 1.41 to 1.46), and risk rose with greater use in a dose-response pattern [22]. The study shows an association, not cause and effect. TMAO was not measured. In atherosclerosis-prone mice, alpha-GPC promoted atherosclerosis [112]. Higher TMAO was linked to first stroke in a separate study of patients with high blood pressure [79]. Risk rose with longer use, so the caution grows with how long you plan to take it: if you have had a stroke, have high cardiovascular risk, or plan to take alpha-GPC long term, talk to your doctor first [22].

### Choline and Mortality

In 80,978 women and 39,434 men followed for mortality, higher phosphatidylcholine intake was linked to higher all-cause mortality (HR 1.11, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.17) and cardiovascular mortality (HR 1.26, 95% CI 1.15 to 1.39), top versus bottom quintile [94]. The authors suggest TMAO as a cause but did not measure it, and the link is observational [93]. In US national survey data, adults in the highest quartile of choline intake had a greater risk of total (23%), cardiovascular (33%) and stroke (30%) mortality than those in the lowest. In the same paper's meta-analysis of prospective studies, choline intake was linked to overall (RR 1.12, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.17) and cardiovascular (RR 1.28, 95% CI 1.17 to 1.39) mortality, but not significantly to stroke mortality, an observational finding that cannot show cause [80].

Several large observational studies have found no significant associations between choline intakes and cardiovascular disease risk. An analysis of 72,348 women and 44,504 men showed no association between choline intake and risk of peripheral artery disease [93]. A prospective study of 14,430 middle-aged adults found that over 14 years, risk of coronary heart disease was not significantly different across choline intake quartiles [82]. Choline intakes also had no association with cardiovascular disease risk in 16,165 women in the European Prospective Investigation into Cancer and Nutrition [93].

### Eggs and Cardiovascular Risk

In 96,831 postmenopausal women in the Women's Health Initiative, eating one or more eggs a day, compared with less than one a week, was linked to modestly higher risk of cardiovascular disease (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.25) and death from any cause (HR 1.14, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.22) [84]. In 3,975 US adults with existing coronary heart disease or stroke, eating eggs at 50 to 100 g a day (HR 1.281, 95% CI 1.004 to 1.635) or more than 100 g a day (HR 1.312, 95% CI 1.036 to 1.661) was linked to higher all-cause mortality than eating no eggs [85].

### Kidney Stones

Rare kidney stones made of calcium tartrate have been reported in people who regularly took an energy supplement [24]; in one case report, the supplement held choline bitartrate and L-carnitine tartrate [95]. The link to the supplement is unproven [95].

### Cancer Risk

**Colorectal adenoma.** In 39,246 women in the Nurses' Health Study, the highest quintile of choline intake was linked to a higher risk of colorectal adenoma (RR 1.45, 95% CI 1.27 to 1.67); it was not clear whether choline or other parts of the same foods explain this [86].

**Prostate cancer.** Evidence is conflicting. In 47,896 men in the Health Professionals Follow-Up Study, the highest quintile of choline intake was linked to lethal prostate cancer (HR 1.70, 95% CI 1.18 to 2.45) [87]. In 6,528 men in the ARIC study, choline intake was not linked to total or lethal prostate cancer [88].

## 6. Drug Interactions

Choline is not known to have any clinically relevant interactions with medications according to the NIH Office of Dietary Supplements [93]. One combination deserves attention:

- **Cholinesterase inhibitors** (donepezil, rivastigmine, galantamine): Taken with choline precursors such as citicoline, these drugs are said to raise acetylcholine in the synapse more than either alone [55]. Trials of these combinations in Alzheimer's disease are described above [53][55]. If you take one of these medicines, check with your doctor before adding a choline supplement.

If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

## 7. Dietary Sources

### Food Sources

Many foods contain choline. The main dietary sources in the United States are animal foods that are rich in choline: meat, poultry, fish, dairy products and eggs [93]. Cruciferous vegetables and certain beans are also rich in choline, and other sources include nuts, seeds, and whole grains [93].

About half the dietary choline consumed in the United States is in the form of phosphatidylcholine [93].

### Practical Notes on Dietary Choline

- **Eggs are a rich common source.** Eggs are among the top sources in the NIH table [93], and egg yolks are rich in choline [18].
- **Organ meats are exceptionally rich.** Beef liver provides approximately 356 mg per 3-ounce serving, the highest in the NIH table [93].
- **Choline from egg yolk phospholipids is better absorbed than choline bitartrate.** A study showed a fourfold higher plasma choline response [25].
- **Choline and folate share methyl-donor functions.** When dietary folate is deficient, choline becomes the primary methyl donor, increasing the need for dietary choline [93].
