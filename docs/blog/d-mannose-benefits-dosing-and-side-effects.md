## Current evidence position

Brad has not made a video on this topic; this is a summary of the evidence, not his view. The largest trial randomised 598 women with recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs) to 2 g a day of D-mannose powder or a placebo powder for 6 months; D-mannose did not reduce the share who went on to see a doctor or clinic with a suspected UTI, and the authors concluded it should not be recommended for prevention in this group [8]. An earlier trial without a placebo gave 308 women 2 g of D-mannose powder a day, nitrofurantoin 50 mg a day or no prophylaxis for 6 months, and repeat UTIs occurred in 15 of 103, 21 of 103 and 62 of 102 women [11]. A 2022 Cochrane review of seven trials (719 participants) found little to no evidence to support or refute D-mannose for preventing or treating UTIs, and side effects were few, none serious, and mostly diarrhoea [16]. With no good evidence that it treats an active infection, it is not a substitute for seeing a doctor about urinary symptoms [16].

## Key safety points

- D-mannose has not been shown to treat an active UTI [16]. If you have urinary symptoms with fever, back or side pain, or vomiting, or any UTI symptoms while pregnant, see a doctor the same day.
- One case report describes a serum sickness-like reaction (rash, headaches, joint pain and swelling) in a 16-year-old taking a D-mannose supplement; it came back when she restarted the product [14]. If this happens, stop the supplement and see a doctor.
- A review of mannose metabolism notes that mannose could cause diabetic complications [3], though a week of mannose did not worsen glucose tolerance or insulin levels in people [15]. If you have diabetes, ask your doctor before starting.
- Side effects in trials were few and mild, mostly diarrhoea and vaginal burning [13][16].

## 1. Overview

D-mannose is a naturally occurring simple sugar (monosaccharide) closely related to glucose. It is the C-2 epimer of glucose: the two differ only in the arrangement at the second carbon atom [2]. The idea behind its use is that D-mannose taken by mouth reaches the urine, where it may stop bacteria sticking to the bladder wall [16].

D-mannose is a building block of polysaccharides and glycoproteins [2]. Mannose is a critical component of N-linked glycosylation, a post-translational modification required for proper protein folding, cell signaling, and immune recognition [2][4]. Mannose-binding lectin (MBL), a key component of the innate immune system, depends on mannose residues for pathogen recognition and complement activation [2].

D-mannose has gained significant consumer interest primarily as a natural approach to preventing recurrent urinary tract infections (UTIs). UTIs are among the most common bacterial infections, accounting for 0.9% of all ambulatory visits in the United States [6]. They affect 50% of women at least once in their lifetime [16]. Guidance on recurrent UTIs aims to limit repeated antibiotic use, because of antibiotic resistance and side effects [5].

Uropathogenic *Escherichia coli* (UPEC) attach to bladder cells through the FimH adhesin on their type 1 pili, which binds mannose on the cell surface [7]. D-mannose in the urine may block this binding, so the bacteria cannot attach and are carried out in the urine [16].

The anti-adhesion effect of D-mannose is well established in the laboratory [16]. However, as detailed in the Evidence section below, clinical trial results in humans have been mixed, with the largest and highest-quality placebo-controlled study showing no benefit [8]. The overall evidence base remains inconclusive, and D-mannose should not be considered a proven treatment or preventive for UTIs.

D-mannose is available as a dietary supplement in powder and capsule forms. It is classified as a food supplement in most jurisdictions and is not FDA-approved for the treatment or prevention of any disease.

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Available Forms

D-mannose supplements are available in two primary delivery formats:

**Powder:** The two largest prevention trials used D-mannose powder at 2 g a day [8][11].

**Capsules/Tablets:** Early pilot studies used tablets or sachets, at doses from 200 mg up to 2 to 3 g [16].

**Combination products:** Some products add other ingredients to D-mannose. The efficacy of these combinations is difficult to evaluate because individual ingredient contributions cannot be isolated. One placebo-controlled trial tested a supplement of D-mannose, citric acid, prebiotic fibres, astragalus and dandelion, so its result cannot be credited to D-mannose alone [9].

### Bioavailability and Pharmacokinetics

Mannose can help or harm depending on its steady-state level and how fast cells use it [3]. Trials have given D-mannose every day, once or twice a day [8][11][12].

### Sourcing and Manufacturing

D-mannose is extracted from plants and microorganisms, or made from fructose or glucose by chemical synthesis or with enzymes such as D-mannose isomerases [2].

### Storage

Store it sealed, somewhere cool and dry.

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Prevention of Urinary Tract Infections (UTIs)

UTI prevention is the primary proposed use for D-mannose supplementation. The evidence base includes several clinical trials of varying quality, a meta-analysis, and extensive preclinical research. Importantly, the results are mixed, with the largest and most rigorous study showing no benefit.

#### The Definitive Trial: Hayward et al. (2024)

The largest placebo-controlled prevention trial took place in 99 primary care centres in the UK [8].

**Design:** Randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial.

**Participants:** 598 women (mean age 58 years) whose primary care records showed consultations for at least 2 UTIs in the previous 6 months or 3 UTIs in 12 months [8].

**Intervention:** 2 g a day of D-mannose powder, or the same volume of placebo powder, for 6 months [8].

**Results:**
- 150 of 294 women (51.0%) taking D-mannose and 161 of 289 (55.7%) taking placebo contacted a doctor or clinic with a suspected UTI [8].
- There were no statistically significant differences in any secondary outcome, which included symptom duration, antibiotic use and UTI-related hospital admissions [8].
- Estimates were similar in per-protocol analyses and in preplanned subgroups [8].

**Significance:** The authors concluded that daily D-mannose did not reduce repeat UTIs in women seen in primary care and should not be recommended for prevention in this group [8].

#### Earlier Positive Studies

Several earlier, smaller studies, mostly without placebo controls, did suggest benefit for UTI prevention. These should be interpreted cautiously in light of the Hayward trial.

**Kranjčec et al. (2014):** After antibiotic treatment of cystitis, 308 women with recurrent UTIs were randomised to 2 g of D-mannose powder in 200 ml of water daily, nitrofurantoin 50 mg daily, or no prophylaxis, for 6 months [11]. Results [11]:
- Repeat UTIs occurred in 15 of 103 women taking D-mannose, 21 of 103 taking nitrofurantoin and 62 of 102 with no prophylaxis [11].
- Compared with no prophylaxis, the risk of a repeat UTI was lower with D-mannose (RR 0.239) and with nitrofurantoin (RR 0.335) [11].
- In the two active groups, 17.9% of women reported side effects, all mild; side effects were less likely with D-mannose than with nitrofurantoin (RR 0.276), though nitrofurantoin was well tolerated [11].

Key limitations: This was not placebo-controlled. The no-treatment arm provides some comparison, but with no placebo, placebo effects cannot be ruled out. The authors called for more studies to validate the results [11].

**Domenici et al. (2016):** In this pilot study, women first took a D-mannose compound for an acute UTI and were then randomised to prophylaxis or none for the next 6 months; recurrences were 4.5% with prophylaxis and 33.3% without [12]. With no placebo group, firm conclusions are not possible.

#### Meta-Analysis: Lenger et al. (2020)

A 2020 meta-analysis of three studies found a pooled relative risk of UTI recurrence of 0.23 (95% CI 0.14 to 0.37) for D-mannose compared with placebo, and 0.39 (95% CI 0.12 to 1.25) compared with preventive antibiotics [13].

The authors cautioned that the result rests on limited data [13]:
- Just 3 of 8 eligible studies could be pooled
- The studies varied in design and quality
- The overall sample size was small

Critically, this meta-analysis was published before the much larger Hayward et al. (2024) trial, which showed no benefit [8]. The 2022 Cochrane review found that no two trials were alike enough to pool, and rated the evidence as very low certainty [16].

#### Summary of UTI Prevention Evidence

| Study | Year | N | Design | Dose | Duration | Result |
|-------|:----:|:---:|--------|------|----------|--------|
| Kranjčec et al. [11] | 2014 | 308 | D-mannose vs. nitrofurantoin vs. no prophylaxis (no placebo) | 2 g daily | 6 months | Fewer repeat UTIs than no prophylaxis; no different from nitrofurantoin |
| Domenici et al. [12] | 2016 | Not reported | Prophylaxis vs. none after treatment (no placebo) | Not reported | 6 months | 4.5% vs. 33.3% recurrences |
| Lenger et al. (meta-analysis) [13] | 2020 | 3 studies | Pooled | Various | Various | RR 0.23 vs. placebo |
| Cooper et al. (Cochrane review) [16] | 2022 | 719 | 7 RCTs, not pooled | Various | 15 days to 6 months | Little to no evidence either way |
| **Hayward et al. [8]** | **2024** | **598** | **RCT, placebo-controlled** | **2 g daily** | **6 months** | **No benefit vs. placebo** |

The evidence trajectory for D-mannose in UTI prevention follows a familiar pattern in supplement research: early, small, often uncontrolled studies show promising results, but when tested rigorously in a large, properly controlled trial, the benefit disappears. The Hayward trial remains the definitive study, and its findings should carry the most weight in clinical decision-making.

### Treatment of Active UTIs

Evidence for D-mannose as a treatment for active UTIs is more limited and preliminary.

**Domenici et al. (2016):** In this pilot study, women with acute cystitis took a D-mannose compound twice a day for 3 days, then once a day for 10 days, and scores for most symptoms improved from baseline [12]. The absence of a placebo control is particularly problematic for UTI treatment studies because UTI symptoms often resolve spontaneously, and the placebo effect for pain and urgency can be substantial.

**Salvatore et al. (2023):** A placebo-controlled trial in 70 nonmenopausal women with an acute uncomplicated *E. coli* UTI tested a supplement of D-mannose, citric acid, prebiotic fibres, astragalus and dandelion, taken twice a day for 5 days, with phenazopyridine and alkalising agents given to both groups [9]. At day 35, clinical resolution was 88.6% with the supplement and 20% with placebo, and bacteriological resolution was 100% and 40% [9].

Key limitations: It is impossible to determine what role D-mannose specifically played in the results, given the multiple active ingredients. It was also a single-centre trial [9].

**Clinical position:** D-mannose should NOT be used as a substitute for antibiotic treatment of confirmed UTIs. Untreated UTIs can spread to the kidneys and the bloodstream. Any woman experiencing UTI symptoms should consult a healthcare provider for appropriate diagnosis and treatment. See a doctor the same day if you have urinary symptoms with fever, back or side pain, or vomiting, or if you are pregnant.

### Treatment of MPI-Congenital Disorder of Glycosylation

Mannose supplements are one of the few potentially curative treatments for mannose phosphate isomerase congenital disorder of glycosylation (MPI-CDG), a rare inherited disorder; liver transplant is another [4]. MPI-CDG is caused by mutations in the MPI gene, leading to impaired conversion of fructose-6-phosphate to mannose-6-phosphate. Oral D-mannose supplementation bypasses this enzymatic block by providing mannose directly for glycoprotein synthesis [4].

This is a niche clinical application with a strong mechanistic rationale and is managed under specialist metabolic physician supervision. The specialist team sets the dose.

### Preclinical Research: Anti-Adhesion Mechanism

The scientific rationale for D-mannose in UTI prevention is primarily based on extensive in vitro and animal research demonstrating the anti-adhesion mechanism [2][7]:

- **FimH binding:** Uropathogenic *E. coli* (UPEC) express type 1 fimbriae with FimH adhesin at the tip. FimH binds to mannose residues on uroplakins (glycoproteins covering the bladder epithelium). D-mannose acts as a competitive inhibitor, binding to FimH and blocking bacterial attachment [2][7].
- **In vitro studies:** Cell culture experiments consistently demonstrate that D-mannose inhibits UPEC adhesion to uroepithelial cells in a dose-dependent manner [2][7].
- **Animal models:** Mouse studies have shown that D-mannose administration reduces bacterial colonization of the bladder following UPEC inoculation [2][7].
- **Structural biology:** X-ray crystallography of FimH has revealed the molecular details of mannose binding, enabling rational drug design of high-affinity FimH antagonists (mannosides) that are more potent than D-mannose itself [7]. These synthetic mannosides are in early-stage clinical development.

The gap between laboratory and trial results may have several explanations:
- Urinary D-mannose concentrations achieved after oral dosing may be insufficient to outcompete the high-density mannose residues on the bladder surface
- The pharmacokinetics of oral D-mannose (rapid absorption and excretion) may not maintain adequate urinary concentrations throughout the day
- Not all UTIs are caused by type 1 fimbriated *E. coli*: other uropathogens (Klebsiella, Proteus, Enterococcus, Staphylococcus) use different adhesion mechanisms
- Women with recurrent UTIs may have risk factors beyond bacterial adhesion that D-mannose cannot address

### Immune Function and Glycosylation

Beyond urinary tract health, D-mannose plays fundamental roles in human biology through its involvement in glycoprotein synthesis [2][4]:

- **N-linked glycosylation:** D-mannose is a critical sugar component of N-linked glycans, which are attached to proteins in the endoplasmic reticulum. These glycans are essential for proper protein folding, stability, trafficking, and function [2][4].
- **Mannose-binding lectin (MBL):** This pattern recognition molecule of the innate immune system recognizes mannose residues on pathogen surfaces, triggering the lectin complement pathway. Adequate mannose availability supports MBL-mediated immune surveillance [2].
- **Immune cell function:** Laboratory studies suggest mannose can increase regulatory T cells, but a week of mannose in people did not change their number [15].

These roles are important for basic physiology but do not necessarily translate to clinical benefits from supplementation, as the body synthesizes adequate D-mannose from glucose under normal metabolic conditions [2].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Doses Used in Clinical Trials

There is no Recommended Dietary Allowance (RDA) or Adequate Intake (AI) for D-mannose, because it is not an essential nutrient.

Doses in the clinical trials [8][9][11][12]:

| Protocol | Dose | Frequency | Duration | Study |
|----------|------|-----------|----------|-------|
| UTI prevention | 2 g a day of powder | Daily | 6 months | Hayward et al. 2024 (negative) [8] |
| UTI prevention | 2 g powder | Daily | 6 months | Kranjčec et al. 2014 [11] |
| Acute UTI treatment | D-mannose compound | Twice daily, then once daily | 3 days, then 10 days | Domenici et al. 2016 [12] |
| Acute UTI (combination product) | D-mannose plus other ingredients | Twice daily | 5 days | Salvatore et al. 2023 [9] |

### Practical Dosing Considerations

**Product label doses:** The two largest prevention trials both used 2 g of powder a day: the placebo-controlled trial found no benefit, while the trial without a placebo found fewer repeat UTIs than no prophylaxis [8][11].

**Preparation:** In one trial the powder was taken in 200 ml of water daily [11].

### Important Caveats

Given that the largest and most rigorous clinical trial (Hayward et al., 2024) showed no benefit for D-mannose in preventing UTIs [8], supplementation cannot be broadly recommended for this indication. Individuals who choose to try D-mannose should:
- Not use it as a substitute for prescribed antibiotic treatment of active UTIs
- Inform their healthcare provider about supplementation
- Seek medical attention promptly if UTI symptoms develop (painful urination, frequency, urgency, fever, flank pain)
- Not delay evidence-based preventive strategies (e.g., vaginal estrogen for postmenopausal women, which has stronger evidence) in favor of D-mannose

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Tolerability

Trials lasting 15 days to 6 months reported few side effects [16]. Diarrhoea is the one reported most often, in a small share of users [13]. In the Cochrane review, adverse events were few, none serious, and mostly diarrhoea and vaginal burning [16].

In Kranjčec et al. (2014), side effects were less likely with D-mannose than with nitrofurantoin (RR 0.276), though nitrofurantoin was also well tolerated [11]. This favorable side effect profile relative to antibiotics is one of the main reasons D-mannose has attracted interest as an alternative preventive approach.

Trials have lasted 6 months at most, so long-term safety is unknown [16].

### Allergic Reactions

**Corn allergy:** If you have a corn allergy, you may want to ask the maker what plant source its D-mannose comes from.

**Serum sickness-like reaction (SSLR):** One case report describes a 16-year-old who developed a widespread hive-like rash, headaches, painful joints and swelling while taking UQora, a supplement whose active ingredient is D-mannose [14]. Her symptoms resolved after all her medicines were stopped, came back when she restarted UQora, and did not return once it was stopped for good [14]. SSLR is most often blamed on anti-inflammatory drugs, anticonvulsants or antibiotics [14].

It is a single case, but it is worth knowing about if you have had an immune-mediated hypersensitivity reaction before. If a rash with joint pain or swelling develops, stop the supplement and see a doctor.

### Blood Sugar and Diabetes

As a sugar, D-mannose could in theory affect blood glucose. A review of mannose metabolism notes that mannose could cause diabetic complications, depending on its levels in the body [3]. Evidence in people is limited:

- A week of mannose, 0.1 g per kg of body weight twice a day, did not worsen meal glucose tolerance, markers of inflammation or insulin levels [15].
- Higher blood mannose levels are linked to later type 2 diabetes, but the same study suggests mannose is a marker of insulin resistance rather than a cause [15].
- Diabetes trials of D-mannose supplements are lacking. If you have diabetes, ask your doctor before starting and watch your blood glucose.

### Gastrointestinal Effects

Diarrhoea is the main digestive side effect reported in trials [13][16].

### Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

There is insufficient safety data on D-mannose supplementation during pregnancy and breastfeeding. Pregnant and breastfeeding women who experience recurrent UTIs should discuss evidence-based prevention strategies with their healthcare provider rather than self-treating with D-mannose. A UTI in pregnancy needs a doctor the same day.

### Children

The Cochrane review found trials only in adults [16]. In children, D-mannose is mainly a specialist treatment for MPI-CDG [4]. Routine supplementation in children is not recommended.

## 6. Drug Interactions

Little is known about drug interactions with D-mannose. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting. Several theoretical points apply:

### Diabetes Medications

As a sugar, D-mannose could in theory affect blood glucose in people on insulin or other glucose-lowering medicines; no trial has looked at this. A week of mannose did not worsen glucose tolerance or insulin levels [15]. If you take diabetes medicines, ask your doctor first and check your glucose more often at the start.

### Anticoagulants

No interactions between D-mannose and blood thinners are known. Combination products may contain other ingredients that can interact with blood thinners, so check the full ingredient list with your pharmacist.

### Antibiotics

D-mannose is not known to interfere with antibiotic efficacy. Trials have compared D-mannose with preventive antibiotics such as nitrofurantoin [11][13]. However, D-mannose should not be used as a substitute for antibiotics when antibiotic treatment is indicated.

### Laboratory Tests

Tell your doctor you take D-mannose before a urine test.

## 7. Dietary Sources

D-mannose comes from plants and microorganisms, and it is part of many polysaccharides and glycoproteins [2].

### Food Sources of D-Mannose

Food amounts vary, and the trials used supplement powder rather than food [8][11].

### Practical Notes on Dietary D-Mannose

The body also synthesizes D-mannose endogenously from glucose through the action of phosphomannose isomerase and other enzymes in the mannose metabolic pathway [2][4]. This endogenous production is sufficient for normal glycoprotein synthesis and immune function, meaning D-mannose is not a dietary essential under normal physiological conditions.
