## 1. Overview

Menopause is the end of menstruation and of a woman's reproductive years, and it typically happens at about 51 years of age [15]. Hot flashes and night sweats are common in most societies, though how often women report them varies widely between regions and cultures [1].

Falling estrogen affects many body systems, and menopausal symptoms can include changes in the brain, metabolism, weight, heart, muscles and bones, urogenital and skin atrophy, and sexual problems [4]. In the Framingham study of women less than 55 years old, there were 20 cardiovascular events before menopause and 70 after it over similar follow-up time, and the usual risk factors did not explain the difference [5].

Menopausal hormone therapy (MHT) is the most effective treatment for hot flashes [6][7]. For women younger than 60 years or within 10 years of menopause onset who have no contraindications, its benefits for bothersome hot flashes and bone loss outweigh its risks [6]. Other options exist for women who prefer not to use MHT or who should not use it [7]. Between 40% and 50% of women in Western countries use complementary therapies for menopausal symptoms [22].

Phytoestrogens are plant compounds, found most notably in soy, that act as weak estrogen agonists or antagonists [8]. Some, such as genistein, bind more strongly to estrogen receptor beta than to estrogen receptor alpha [9]. The three main phytoestrogen products used for menopausal symptoms are:

- **Soy isoflavones**, from soybeans; the most studied phytoestrogens for menopause
- **Red clover isoflavones**, from the aerial parts of Trifolium pratense
- **Black cohosh**, from the root and rhizome of Actaea racemosa (Cimicifuga racemosa); its active compounds and mechanism are unknown and may not be hormonal [15][19]

**Progesterone cream**, applied to the skin, is sometimes used as well, though it is a hormone rather than a phytoestrogen.

This article reviews the clinical evidence for each of these supplements, covering efficacy for vasomotor symptoms, bone health, cardiovascular effects, and safety considerations.

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Soy Isoflavones

In food, isoflavones are bound to sugars as glycosides, and intestinal bacteria convert them into hormone-like compounds [25]. Soy supplements deliver three isoflavones: genistein, daidzein and glycitein [29].

Some, but not all, adults carry gut bacteria that turn daidzein into equol, an isoflavone with stronger estrogenic activity [11][12]. The S-equol made by these bacteria binds selectively to estrogen receptor beta [12]. People who make equol may respond better to soy, and failing to separate producers from non-producers could explain why trial results vary [11].

### Red Clover Isoflavones

Red clover (Trifolium pratense) contains the same main isoflavones as soy, genistein and daidzein, plus two others:
- **Biochanin A**
- **Formononetin**

Both are weaker estrogens than genistein in laboratory tests [9]. Extracts are standardized to total isoflavones; one 12-month trial used 398 mg a day of extract standardized to 120 mg isoflavones [15].

Red clover extracts in trials are made from the aerial parts of the plant [15], while soy isoflavones come from the bean.

### Black Cohosh

Black cohosh (Actaea racemosa, formerly Cimicifuga racemosa) is a perennial plant native to North America, belonging to the buttercup family [15]. Preparations are made from the roots and rhizomes and are available as powdered whole herb, liquid extracts, and dried extracts in capsule or tablet form [15].

Substances in black cohosh that may account for its activity include [15]:
- **Triterpene glycosides**, such as actein, 23-epi-26-deoxyactein, and cimicifugoside
- **Resins**, such as cimicifugin
- **Aromatic acid derivatives**, including caffeic, isoferulic, and fukinolic acids

The compounds responsible for any relief of menopausal symptoms are not known, so products vary considerably in their chemistry [15].

Remifemin, a commercial extract used in several trials in the 2012 Cochrane review, is standardized to be equivalent to 40 mg of black cohosh root and rhizome (extracted with isopropyl alcohol) per daily dose of two tablets; it is not standardized to triterpene glycosides [15].

How black cohosh works is unclear. Studies disagree on whether it raises estrogen or changes luteinizing hormone or follicle-stimulating hormone, and whether it affects vaginal and uterine tissue is unclear [15]. Animal and laboratory studies point to an action in the brain rather than a hormonal effect, and some researchers suggest serotonergic, antioxidant, anti-inflammatory or selective estrogen receptor modulator activity [15][19].

### Progesterone Cream

Progesterone cream delivers the hormone through the skin. When women using estrogen applied creams supplying 16, 32 or 64 mg of progesterone a day, their progesterone levels were too low to cause any detectable change in the endometrium [56].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Hot Flashes and Vasomotor Symptoms

#### Soy Isoflavones

Soy isoflavones are the most studied phytoestrogen for hot flashes, and the results are mixed [20][22].

**Meta-analyses and systematic reviews:**

A 2006 meta-analysis of randomized trials in JAMA (Nelson et al.) found mixed results for soy isoflavone extracts and no drop in hot flash frequency with red clover extracts [20]. A 2006 review of eleven well-characterized studies (Williamson-Hughes et al.) found that all five studies giving more than 15 mg of genistein (as aglycone equivalents) per treatment reported a significant drop in hot flashes, against one of the six studies below that level [21].

A 2016 meta-analysis in JAMA (Franco et al.) of 62 studies including 6653 women found that phytoestrogens were linked to fewer daily hot flashes (pooled mean difference -1.31, 95% CI -2.02 to -0.61) and less vaginal dryness, but not to fewer night sweats [22]. Soy isoflavones on their own were linked to a smaller drop in daily hot flashes (-0.79, 95% CI -1.35 to -0.23), and the authors called the reductions modest because most trials were of poor quality [22].

**Positive trials:**

A 2008 trial (Khaodhiar et al.) randomized 190 menopausal women to placebo or 40 or 60 mg a day of daidzein-rich isoflavone aglycones for 12 weeks [23]. Together, the two doses reduced hot flash frequency by 52% at 12 weeks (P = 0.048 against placebo), though neither dose alone differed significantly from placebo [23].

**Equol production as a modifier:**

Women whose gut bacteria make equol may get more benefit from soy [11]. The North American Menopause Society's 2023 position statement does not recommend soy foods, soy extracts or the soy metabolite equol for hot flashes, rating the evidence for them as limited or inconsistent [61].

**Epidemiological context:**

Hot flash rates vary widely between regions, and climate, diet, lifestyle, women's roles and attitudes to aging may all play a part [1]. Lower rates in countries that eat more soy therefore do not show that soy is the cause.

#### Red Clover

The evidence for red clover isoflavones in treating vasomotor symptoms is weaker than for soy isoflavones.

**RCT evidence:**

A 2009 trial (Geller et al.) assigned 88 perimenopausal and postmenopausal women (mean age 53 years; 55% from underrepresented minority groups) with at least 35 hot flashes and night sweats a week to 128 mg/day black cohosh, 398 mg/day red clover extract (standardized to 120 mg isoflavones), hormone therapy, or placebo [15]. After 12 months, vasomotor symptoms had fallen by 34% with black cohosh, 57% with red clover, 63% with placebo and 94% with hormone therapy (conjugated equine estrogens plus medroxyprogesterone acetate); only hormone therapy differed significantly from placebo [14].

**Clinical guideline position:**

A literature review found no clinical evidence that red clover isoflavone extracts relieve hot flashes [13]. The North American Menopause Society does not recommend over-the-counter supplements and herbal therapies as proven treatments for hot flashes [18].

#### Black Cohosh

Studies since the 1950s have tested black cohosh for menopausal symptoms, but high-quality evidence of benefit is lacking [15].

**High-quality negative trials:**

Newton et al. (2006) conducted a rigorous RCT assigning 351 women aged 45-55 years experiencing daytime hot flashes and night sweats to one of five groups [26]:
1. 160 mg/day black cohosh (70% ethanolic extract standardized to 2.5% triterpene glycosides)
2. A multibotanical preparation containing 200 mg black cohosh plus Siberian ginseng, dong quai, and other ingredients
3. The same multibotanical preparation plus two daily servings of soy foods (providing 12-20 g soy protein)
4. Hormone therapy (estrogen with or without progesterone)
5. Placebo

After 3, 6, and 12 months, the number and intensity of hot flashes and night sweats did not differ between the herbal-intervention groups and placebo, with one exception: at 12 months, participants consuming the multibotanical preparation plus soy foods had significantly worse symptom intensity than those consuming placebo.

Geller et al. (2009) [14], described above, similarly found no statistically significant differences between black cohosh and placebo for vasomotor symptom frequency. The black cohosh group showed worse symptom intensity at 6 and 9 months [15]. Somatic symptoms, mood and sexual function (including vaginal dryness) mostly showed no differences between groups [15].

**Small positive trial:**

A 2013 Iranian trial (Mohammad-Alizadeh-Charandabi et al.) gave 84 early postmenopausal women 6.5 mg of dried black cohosh root extract or placebo daily for 8 weeks [57]. Menopausal symptom scores fell more with black cohosh (adjusted mean difference -12.9 at week 8), and no side effects were reported [57].

**Cochrane Review (2012):**

A 2012 Cochrane review (Leach and Moore) included 16 randomized trials with a total of 2,027 women, who took 8 to 160 mg/day of black cohosh extract for 8 to 54 weeks [15]. It found no significant difference from placebo in hot flush frequency (mean difference 0.07 flushes per day; 95% CI -0.43 to 0.56) or menopausal symptom scores, while hormone therapy reduced symptoms more than black cohosh [3]. The authors concluded that there is "insufficient evidence to support the use of black cohosh for menopausal symptoms" [3]. The studies were highly heterogeneous with respect to design, duration, type and amount of black cohosh used, and main findings.

**Systematic review and meta-analysis (2016):**

Franco et al. (2016) examined four black cohosh trials (three from the Cochrane Review above) randomizing a total of 511 women to 6.5-160 mg/day black cohosh extract or placebo [15]. There were no significant associations between black cohosh supplementation and reduction in vasomotor symptom frequency. Nor was black cohosh linked to relief of menopausal symptoms on self-reported rating scales.

**Updated meta-analysis (2023):**

A 2023 meta-analysis (Sadahiro et al.) pooled 22 trials with 2,310 menopausal women taking black cohosh extracts, alone or combined with other ingredients, and found possible benefit over placebo for hot flashes (Hedges' g 0.315, 95% CI 0.107 to 0.524) but not for anxiety or depressive symptoms [62].

**Clinical guideline positions:**

- The American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), in its 2014 clinical guidelines for managing menopausal symptoms, concluded that "data do not show that" herbal dietary supplements like black cohosh "are efficacious for the treatment of vasomotor symptoms" [15][27].
- The North American Menopause Society (NAMS) advises clinicians against recommending herbal therapies such as black cohosh because "they are unlikely to be beneficial" (italics in original) in alleviating vasomotor symptoms [18].

#### Progesterone Cream

The evidence for progesterone cream is mixed. In a 1999 trial (Leonetti et al.) of 102 women within 5 years of menopause, a cream containing 20 mg of progesterone, applied to the skin each day, improved or resolved hot flashes in 83% of treated women with symptoms, against 19% on placebo [54]. A 2009 placebo-controlled trial (Benster et al.) of daily creams with 60, 40, 20 or 5 mg of progesterone found them no more effective than placebo for menopausal symptoms after six months [55].

### Bone Density and Osteoporosis

#### Soy Isoflavones

Trials of soy isoflavones for bone in postmenopausal women show mixed results [28][29][30].

**Whole-body versus regional bone:**

A 24-month trial (Wong et al., 2009) gave 403 postmenopausal women 80 or 120 mg a day of soy hypocotyl aglycone isoflavones, plus calcium and vitamin D, or placebo [28]. The 120 mg dose slowed whole-body bone loss compared with placebo at 1 and 2 years, but it did not slow bone loss at common fracture sites [28].

**Bone calcium retention:**

In a crossover trial in 24 postmenopausal women (Pawlowski et al., 2015), soy isoflavone supplements of 52 to 220 mg of total isoflavones a day increased bone calcium retention by between 3.4% and 7.6%, a moderate effect next to the bisphosphonate risedronate at 15.3% [29]. The most effective supplement delivered 105.23 mg of mixed isoflavones a day in their natural ratios, and mixed isoflavones worked better than enriched genistein [29].

**Genistein-specific evidence:**

In a 24-month trial (Marini et al., 2007) in 389 osteopenic postmenopausal women, 54 mg of genistein a day, with calcium and vitamin D, raised bone density at the lumbar spine and femoral neck while it fell on placebo [30]. The trial did not measure fractures [30].

#### Black Cohosh

The 2012 Cochrane review found too few data to assess black cohosh for bone health [3].

### Cholesterol and Cardiovascular Health

#### Soy Isoflavones

Soy protein eaten in place of other proteins may lower cholesterol slightly, but soy isoflavones may not have the same effect [60]. A review of red clover studies found that clinical evidence is lacking that its isoflavone extracts lower LDL [13].

Cardiovascular disease is more common after menopause, and in the Framingham study the gap was not explained by the usual risk factors [5]. Cholesterol rose somewhat more steeply in women going through menopause [5].

### Cognitive and Mood Effects

Estrogen decline during menopause is linked to cognitive changes. In a cohort of 15,754 women (Gilsanz et al., 2019), natural menopause at an age less than 47.4 (the mean) was linked to a higher risk of dementia (HR 1.19, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.31) [31].

Whether soy improves cognitive function is uncertain, as studies have given conflicting results [60]. In the Geller trial, mood outcomes such as depression and anxiety mostly did not differ between black cohosh, red clover and placebo [15].

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) has stronger trial support. In the MENOS 2 trial of 140 women, group and self-help CBT both made hot flushes and night sweats less of a problem at 6 and 26 weeks, and improved mood and quality of life at 6 weeks [32].

### Sleep Disturbances

Sleep disruption is common during menopause, often secondary to night sweats, hormonal changes, and mood disturbances.

Neither soy isoflavones, red clover, nor black cohosh have demonstrated consistent improvements in sleep quality in randomized controlled trials. In the Geller trial, somatic symptoms such as insomnia mostly did not differ between groups [15].

Melatonin shortens the time to fall asleep and lengthens total sleep in insomnia, though whether the gains are clinically meaningful is debated [33]. Trials of magnesium for sleep have given contradictory results [34].

### Genitourinary Symptoms

Vaginal dryness and painful sex are part of the genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM) [6]. In the Geller trial, sexual function outcomes such as vaginal dryness mostly did not differ between black cohosh and placebo [15]. Phytoestrogens as a group were linked to a small improvement in vaginal dryness scores in the 2016 meta-analysis [22].

For bothersome GSM symptoms that over-the-counter products do not relieve, low-dose vaginal estrogen or other therapies such as vaginal DHEA or oral ospemifene are recommended [6]. In a 12-week trial of 605 women, ospemifene 60 mg a day reduced painful sex more than placebo [35]. Vaginal moisturizers and lubricants are an option for women who do not choose hormonal therapy [7].

### Weight and Body Composition

Menopause is associated with increased visceral adiposity and loss of lean muscle mass. In the 12-week WAVS trial of 38 postmenopausal women, a low-fat vegan diet with half a cup (86 g) of cooked soybeans a day cut moderate-to-severe hot flashes by 84%, against 42% in the control group [36]. The diet changed along with the soy, so the result cannot be credited to soy alone.

In a 6-month trial of 338 women who were overweight or obese, an intensive behavioural weight-loss program improved bothersome hot flushes more than a health education program (OR 2.25, 95% CI 1.20 to 4.21) [37].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Soy Isoflavones

No dose has been firmly established. Doses used in trials:

**For hot flash reduction:**
- **Genistein:** more than 15 mg per treatment (as aglycone equivalents) in the studies that found a benefit [21]
- **Daidzein-rich isoflavone aglycones:** 40 or 60 mg a day for 12 weeks [23]

**For bone density preservation:**
- 120 mg of soy hypocotyl isoflavones, taken daily, slowed whole-body bone loss, but not bone loss at common fracture sites [28]
- 105.23 mg of mixed isoflavones a day gave the largest gain in bone calcium retention [29]
- Genistein at 54 mg, taken daily, raised bone density at the lumbar spine and femoral neck after 24 months [30]

### Red Clover

- A 12-month trial used 398 mg a day of extract standardized to 120 mg isoflavones, without benefit over placebo [15]
- The evidence base for any specific dose is weak

### Black Cohosh

- Trials in the 2012 Cochrane review used a median of 40 mg a day of extract for a mean of 23 weeks [3]
- Remifemin provides the equivalent of 40 mg of black cohosh root and rhizome per daily dose of two tablets [15]
- One 8-week trial of 84 women reported benefit with 6.5 mg of dried root extract daily [57]
- Most studies lasted 6 months or less, so long-term safety is unknown [15]

### Progesterone Cream

- 20 mg of progesterone a day in cream helped hot flashes in one trial [54], but creams with 60, 40, 20 or 5 mg of progesterone did not beat placebo in another [55]

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Soy Isoflavones

**Gastrointestinal effects:** In a 24-month trial, more women taking 54 mg of genistein a day had gastrointestinal side effects than women on placebo (19% vs 8%) [30].

**Uterine stimulation:** In a 5-year trial (Unfer et al., 2004), postmenopausal women taking 150 mg a day of soy isoflavones had more endometrial hyperplasia than those on placebo (3.37% vs 0%), and no cancers were found [39]. The journal has since published an expression of concern about this paper [39]. In the genistein bone trial, endometrial thickness did not change [30].

**Breast cancer:** A 2013 systematic review found no clear evidence that soy harms women with breast cancer, but said better evidence of safety is needed before high-dose isoflavones (100 mg or more) can be recommended for them [63].

**Thyroid effects:** A review of 14 trials found little evidence that soy foods or isoflavones harm thyroid function in healthy people who get enough iodine, but soy foods may raise the dose of thyroid hormone that people with hypothyroidism need by reducing its absorption [40]. In a crossover trial in people with subclinical hypothyroidism, 66 mg of soy phytoestrogens a day for 8 weeks did not change thyroid function tests, though two people progressed to overt hypothyroidism after the high dose [41].

**Soy allergy:** Some people are allergic to soy [60].

**Fertility:** In women attending a US fertility center (median age 35.0 years), soy food intake was not linked to antral follicle count or FSH, though the highest-intake group had lower AMH; overall, the authors found no strong link between soy or isoflavone intake and ovarian reserve [42].

**Pregnancy and lactation:** Soy in amounts greater than those in foods may be unsafe during pregnancy, and little is known about its safety in such amounts while breastfeeding [60].

### Red Clover

Red clover contains the same main isoflavones as soy, so similar cautions may apply. The safety of red clover isoflavone supplements in women with breast or endometrial cancer has not been established [13]. In the Geller trial, a standardized red clover extract was safe during daily use for 12 months [14].

### Black Cohosh

**Common side effects:** Trials show a low rate of side effects; the most common are stomach upset and rash, both mild and short-lived [15]. Breast pain or enlargement, infection, vaginal bleeding or spotting, and musculoskeletal complaints occurred about as often with black cohosh as with placebo [15].

**Hepatotoxicity (liver damage):**

Liver injury is the most serious concern. Worldwide, at least 83 cases of liver damage, including hepatitis, liver failure and raised liver enzymes, have been linked to black cohosh, but a causal link has not been shown [15]. Some cases may have come from impurities, adulterants or the wrong Actaea species, though no one analyzed the products to check [15]. A US Pharmacopeia review of the case reports rated every case as possible, none as probable or certain, and recommended a cautionary label [47]. A later analysis found no causal role for black cohosh in any published case but noted quality problems in a few products [44], and a 2008 systematic review judged that the safety concerns need urgent investigation [43]. If you take black cohosh, stop it and see your doctor if you get abdominal pain, dark urine, tiredness or yellow skin or eyes [47][59]. If yellow skin or eyes come with confusion, unusual drowsiness, or easy bleeding or bruising, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department.

Regulatory responses to these reports include:
- **Australia (2007):** Required the label statement: "Warning: Black cohosh may harm the liver in some individuals. Use under the supervision of a healthcare professional" [15]
- **US Pharmacopeia (2008):** Recommended labeling black cohosh products with: "Discontinue use and consult a healthcare practitioner if you have a liver disorder or develop symptoms of liver trouble, such as abdominal pain, dark urine, or jaundice" [47]
- The US FDA does not currently require a warning on black cohosh product labels [15]

**Cardiac effects:**

A case report described repeated fainting in a 76-year-old woman taking black cohosh for years, with her heart rate dropping to 30 beats per minute; the slow heart rate gradually resolved after she stopped black cohosh [48]. If you faint, or feel faint with a very slow pulse, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).

**Breast cancer:** Whether black cohosh is safe for women who have had hormone-sensitive conditions such as breast or uterine cancer is uncertain [59].

**Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** The American Herbal Products Association advises pregnant women not to take black cohosh except under the supervision of their health care provider, because its use in pregnancy has not been rigorously studied [15]. Black cohosh may not be safe during pregnancy or while breastfeeding [59].

**Confusion with blue cohosh:** Black cohosh should not be confused with blue cohosh (Caulophyllum thalictroides), a different plant. In one case report, a newborn whose mother took blue cohosh to bring on contractions had a heart attack with severe heart failure and shock [58].

**Long-term safety unknown:** Most studies have examined black cohosh use for 6 months or less. No published studies have assessed the long-term safety of black cohosh in humans [15].

### Progesterone Cream

- Headache was more common with progesterone cream than with placebo in one trial [55]
- In women also using estrogen, creams giving 16 to 64 mg of progesterone a day did not change the endometrium, so cream may not protect the uterus from estrogen [56]

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Soy Isoflavones

**Simvastatin:** In a crossover study of 18 healthy Chinese men, taking about 80 mg of soy isoflavones a day for 14 days, then one 20 mg dose of simvastatin, lowered exposure to simvastatin acid, with the geometric mean AUC falling from 16.1 to 12.1 [51]. The drop was significant only in people with the SLCO1B1 521TT genotype [51]. In a separate cohort study with no soy involved, simvastatin users with the 521 CC genotype were more likely than those with the TT genotype to need a dose decrease or switch, a sign of side effects (HR 1.74, 95% CI 1.05 to 2.88) [52].

**Thyroid medications:** If you take levothyroxine or another thyroid hormone, soy may change the dose you need [40].

### Black Cohosh

**Prescription medicines:** If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

**Overall assessment:** The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements notes that black cohosh "is not known to have any clinically relevant interactions with medications, although this has not been systematically studied" [15]. The lack of known interactions reflects limited study rather than confirmed safety.

### Red Clover

No well-established drug interactions have been identified for red clover specifically, though the same theoretical concerns apply as for soy isoflavones given the shared isoflavone content.

### Progesterone Cream

If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

## 7. Dietary Sources

### Soy Isoflavone Content in Foods

Soy is the best-known food source of phytoestrogens [8]. Soy products, whole-grain cereals, seeds and berries contain the precursors of the isoflavones and lignans found in people [25].

### Achieving Therapeutic Doses from Diet

No dietary amount of soy has been shown to relieve hot flashes on its own. The WAVS trial used half a cup (86 g) of cooked soybeans a day as part of a low-fat vegan diet [36]. Among women attending a US fertility center, median isoflavone intake was 1.78 mg a day [42].

### Red Clover Dietary Sources

Red clover is not commonly consumed as a food. It is primarily available as:
- Standardized extracts in capsule or tablet form
- Red clover tea (isoflavone content is highly variable and typically much lower than standardized extracts)
- Dried herb preparations

### Other Phytoestrogen-Rich Foods

Beyond soy and red clover, other foods contain phytoestrogens in varying amounts:
- **Flaxseeds** and other seeds contain lignans, a different class of phytoestrogen [25]
- **Legumes** (chickpeas, lentils) contain small amounts of isoflavones
- **Whole grains** (oats, barley) contain lignans [25]
- **Fruits and vegetables** contain various polyphenols with weak estrogenic activity

### Nutritional Strategies for Menopause

The Endocrine Society advises all postmenopausal women to adopt appropriate lifestyle measures [7]. For women who are overweight, losing weight may also ease hot flushes [37].
