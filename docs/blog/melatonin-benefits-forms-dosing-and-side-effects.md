## Key safety points

- If you take a blood thinner such as warfarin, or have epilepsy, NIH advises medical supervision [1] and the MSD Manual advises against melatonin [2]. It may increase warfarin's effect and the risk of bleeding [2][3].
- Fluvoxamine raised exposure to a melatonin dose 17-fold [4], and melatonin worsened zolpidem's effects on driving tests [5]. Ask your doctor or pharmacist first if you take antidepressants, sedatives, or blood pressure or diabetes medicines.
- In type 2 diabetes, melatonin increased blood sugar swings [6]. In carriers of a common diabetes-risk gene variant, a 5 mg dose worsened glucose tolerance [7].
- Do not use it if pregnant or nursing; safety there has not been studied [1]. The MSD Manual also advises against it when trying to conceive, and in dementia or autoimmune disease [2].
- Talk to a doctor before giving melatonin to a child, and store it out of reach: US poison centre reports in people 19 and younger rose 530% from 2012 to 2021 [1][8].
- Labels are unreliable: 22 of 25 US gummies were mislabelled, and most held more melatonin than stated [1].
- Melatonin may stay active longer in older people and cause daytime drowsiness; in older adults, a 3 mg dose affected balance an hour later [1][9].
- Side effects are usually mild, such as headache, dizziness, nausea and sleepiness. Long-term safety is unknown [1].

## 1. Overview

Melatonin is a hormone that the brain makes in response to darkness, and it helps time the body's 24-hour clock and sleep [1]. The pineal gland releases it at night, and it acts on two receptors, MT1 and MT2, that shape sleep and the body clock [10]. It also synchronises body clocks in organs such as the liver, gut and heart [95]. Several tissues besides the pineal gland make it, the heart and gut among them [11][96], and beyond the sleep-wake cycle it acts as an antioxidant that neutralises free radicals [11][94][95][96].

Melatonin also acts on the immune system. A review describes it as a stimulant when immune activity is low and as an anti-inflammatory when immune responses run high [12].

Light at night lowers melatonin [1]. In healthy young adults, room light before bedtime suppressed melatonin and shortened its nightly duration by about 90 min [13]. Beta-blockers suppress the body's night-time melatonin [14].

Most melatonin supplements are made synthetically, though some come from animals or microorganisms [1]. In the United States melatonin is sold as a dietary supplement and regulated less strictly than a drug; in several other countries it is a prescription medicine [1].

Supplements seem to leave natural secretion intact. In night shift workers, 0.5 mg at bedtime for seven days left the amplitude of their own melatonin rhythm unchanged [15]. In an open-label study, 244 adults with primary insomnia took a 2 mg prolonged-release tablet nightly for 6 to 12 months; stopping brought no rebound insomnia or withdrawal symptoms, and their pineal output was not suppressed [16].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Oral Formulations

Melatonin is sold as tablets, capsules, liquids and gummies [1]. Surge-sustained products combine an immediate-release dose with a controlled-release dose to mimic the body's own overnight profile [17]. In a small study in which two authors worked for the ingredient's maker, a 5 mg sustained-release capsule gave half the peak level of immediate-release melatonin and a half-life about five times longer (5.10 versus 1.01 hours) [18].

### Absorption and First-Pass Metabolism

The liver breaks melatonin down almost entirely through the enzyme CYP1A2 [19]. In healthy volunteers, about 15% of 2 mg and 4 mg oral tablets reached the bloodstream [20]; in another small study of a 10 mg oral dose, median bioavailability was only 2.5% [21]. A systematic review of 22 studies found oral bioavailability ranged from 9 to 33% [22]. Age, caffeine, smoking, oral contraceptives, food and fluvoxamine all changed how the body handled melatonin [22].

### Synthetic vs. Plant-Derived Melatonin

Most supplements are made synthetically [1]. There is no good public evidence yet that plant-derived melatonin works differently in people.

Plant-based melatonin (phytomelatonin) products are extracted from sources such as alfalfa, chlorella and rice [99]. In laboratory tests, a phytomelatonin complex inhibited the inflammatory enzyme COX-2 about 6.5 times more strongly than synthetic melatonin, which the authors put down to other compounds in the plant extract [99]. These were enzyme and cell tests, not studies in people.

### Topical Melatonin

In a crossover trial in 63 undergraduates, a melatonin sleep lotion raised salivary melatonin up to about 1,000-fold compared with a placebo lotion. Sleep quality improved in a subgroup of 18 poor sleepers but not in the group as a whole [23]. The authors warn that the undisclosed dose in such lotions can be high and well absorbed through the skin [23].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Insomnia and General Sleep Improvement in Adults

The evidence for melatonin in chronic insomnia is modest. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine suggests clinicians not use melatonin for trouble falling or staying asleep, a weak recommendation [24], and the American College of Physicians strongly recommends cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) as the first treatment [1]. The 2023 European insomnia guideline does not recommend fast-release melatonin, but says prolonged-release melatonin can be used for up to 3 months in people aged 55 and over [25].

An umbrella review found that, across twelve papers on melatonin, it gave statistically significant improvements in time to fall asleep and total sleep time, but reviewers disagreed on whether these gains are clinically meaningful, and the papers ranged from moderate to critically low in quality [26].

A 2013 meta-analysis of 19 studies (1,683 people) with primary sleep disorders found melatonin shortened time to fall asleep by 7.06 minutes and lengthened total sleep by 8.25 minutes. The effects were modest but did not appear to fade with continued use [27].

An earlier meta-analysis of 17 studies (284 people) found melatonin shortened time to fall asleep by 4.0 minutes and added 12.8 minutes of sleep [28].

A 2024 dose-response meta-analysis of 26 trials found the benefit for falling asleep and total sleep rose with dose up to a peak at 4 mg a day. A longer gap between the dose and bedtime predicted faster sleep onset, and the authors suggest taking it about 3 hours before bed [29].

Across 23 trials that used a standard sleep-quality questionnaire, melatonin improved scores in adults with various conditions, though results varied widely between studies [30].

Trials in specific groups give mixed results:

**Older adults:** In 354 adults aged 55 to 80 with insomnia, 2 mg of prolonged-release melatonin 2 hours before bedtime for 3 weeks gave more responders than placebo (26% versus 15%) and shortened time to fall asleep by 24.3 minutes, against 12.9 minutes with placebo [31].

**Tinnitus:** In a trial of 120 people, tinnitus perception fell by 40% with 3 mg of melatonin daily for 1 month, by 81% with melatonin plus the drug sulpiride, and by 22% with placebo [35]. A US clinical practice guideline recommends against melatonin and other dietary supplements for persistent, bothersome tinnitus [36].

**Primary insomnia:** In 97 middle-aged adults with primary insomnia, 3 mg of fast-release melatonin 1 hour before bedtime for 4 weeks cut early-morning waking by 30.63 min and lowered the share of N2 sleep, measured overnight. It did not change time to fall asleep, sleep efficiency, waking during the night or self-rated sleep [32].

**Glaucoma:** In a crossover trial, 64 adults with advanced glaucoma took 5 mg a day or placebo for 30 days each; neither sleep questionnaires nor wrist-monitor sleep measures differed between melatonin and placebo [101].

**Parkinson's disease:** In a trial of 18 people with Parkinson's disease, 3 mg of melatonin 1 hour before bedtime for four weeks improved self-rated sleep quality, but overnight sleep recordings and motor symptoms did not change [33]. In 34 people with Parkinson's disease and poor sleep, 2 mg of prolonged-release melatonin for 4 weeks improved self-rated sleep quality but not motor scores [34].

A review suggests melatonin may also help non-motor symptoms such as sleep in Parkinson's disease [96].

**REM sleep behavior disorder:** In a 4-week pilot trial of 30 people, 2 mg or 6 mg a day of prolonged-release melatonin, taken 30 min before bedtime, did not improve symptoms or sleep quality compared with placebo [100].

**Melatonin tolerance:** In three patients with intellectual disability whose good response faded within weeks, melatonin levels stayed high 2 and 4 hours after a dose. Sleep problems went away once the dose was cut considerably. The authors linked this to slow breakdown by CYP1A2 [19].

### Delayed Sleep Phase Syndrome (DSPS)

People with DSPS (also called delayed sleep-wake phase disorder) find it hard to get to sleep at usual times or to wake in the morning, often not sleeping before 2 to 6 a.m. [1]. In 2015, the American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommended melatonin for it, though the recommendation was weak and it was uncertain whether benefits outweigh harms [1][37].

In a trial of 116 people with DSPS, 0.5 mg of fast-release melatonin 1 hour before the desired bedtime for 4 weeks, combined with a set bedtime in both groups, brought sleep onset 34 min earlier than placebo. Sleep disturbance, insomnia severity and functional disability also fell. In a subset, the timing of the body's own melatonin did not differ between groups after treatment [38].

A 2016 review of 2 studies (52 people) found melatonin cut the time to fall asleep by about 22 minutes in DSPS [1].

In 13 people with DSPS, 0.3 mg and 3 mg both moved the body clock earlier, and the earlier in the evening the dose was taken, the larger the shift [39].

### Jet Lag

A Cochrane review found that in 9 of 10 trials, melatonin taken close to bedtime at the destination reduced jet lag after flights across five or more time zones. Doses of 0.5 to 5 mg worked about equally well, though people fell asleep faster after 5 mg; 2 mg of slow-release melatonin worked less well. Taken at the wrong time, early in the day, it can cause sleepiness and delay adaptation [40].

A 2021 meta-analysis found melatonin reduced self-rated jet lag after both eastbound (5 trials) and westbound (4 trials) flights [41]. NIH summarises four studies of 142 travelers showing less jet lag after eastward flights and two studies of 90 travelers showing benefit after westward flights [1].

In 320 travelers flying east across 6 to 8 time zones, fast-release melatonin at bedtime worked better than 2 mg of controlled-release; 0.5 mg of fast-release was almost as effective as 5 mg [42]. In a New Zealand trial on flights to London and back, 5 mg lowered jet-lag ratings (2.15 versus 3.4) [43].

Timing matters. In 52 international cabin crew, starting 5 mg 3 days before arrival led to a worse recovery than placebo, while starting on arrival gave less jet lag [44].

### Shift Work

Evidence is small and inconclusive [1]. A Cochrane review found low-quality evidence that 1 to 10 mg of melatonin after a night shift may lengthen daytime sleep by about 24 minutes (seven trials, 263 participants), with no change in time to fall asleep [45]. Another review of 8 studies (300 participants) was inconclusive and made no recommendations [1].

In 27 overweight female nurses, 3 mg of melatonin on the nights they slept, for 12 weeks, reduced circadian misalignment by about 20% and, in those with the most misalignment (early chronotypes), lowered body weight, BMI, and waist and hip circumference [103]. In a pilot crossover trial in 25 doctors and nurses, 6 mg of slow-release melatonin before sleep after each night shift improved a concentration task, with negligible side effects, though melatonin levels stayed raised for longer [102].

### Sleep in Children and Adolescents

NIH advises parents to speak with a health care provider before giving a child melatonin, because much is unknown about the right dose, long-term effects and whether benefits outweigh risks [1].

A 2019 review of 18 studies (n=1,021 children) found melatonin was better than placebo for time to fall asleep and total sleep. Results by condition [1]:
- **Autism spectrum disorder (ASD):** Fell asleep 37 minutes earlier, slept 48 minutes longer
- **ADHD:** Fell asleep 20 minutes earlier, slept 33 minutes longer
- **Atopic dermatitis:** Fell asleep 6.8 minutes earlier, slept 35 minutes longer
- **Chronic sleep-onset insomnia:** Fell asleep 24 minutes earlier, slept 25 minutes longer

A review of 19 trials found melatonin helped children fall asleep (median 28 minutes sooner) and sleep longer (median 33 minutes), with the largest gains in autism and other neurodevelopmental disorders [53].

**Children with ASD:** In children with ASD given 1 mg (n = 65), 4 mg (n = 65) or placebo (n = 66) before bedtime, sleep onset latency shortened by 22.0, 28.0 and 5.0 min respectively [51].

In a 13-week trial of 125 children and adolescents with ASD or Smith-Magenis syndrome, prolonged-release melatonin (2 mg, raised to 5 mg) lengthened sleep by 32.43 minutes and shortened sleep latency by 25.30 minutes compared with placebo; somnolence was more common with melatonin [49]. In a follow-up of 80 participants given 2 mg, 5 mg or 10 mg nightly for up to 104 weeks, fatigue (6.3%), somnolence (6.3%) and mood swings (4.2%) led the list of treatment-related adverse events, and growth and puberty stayed within normal ranges [50].

In 62 children with ASD, melatonin, cycling and the two combined each improved sleep efficiency, time to fall asleep and sleep duration, with no difference between them; the placebo group did not improve [104].

**Children with ADHD:** In 105 children with ADHD and chronic sleep onset insomnia, 3 or 6 mg of melatonin for 4 weeks brought sleep onset forward by 26.9 min (placebo delayed it by 10.5 min) and lengthened total sleep, but did not change behavior, cognition or quality of life [52].

**Children with late sleep onset:** In 84 children (mean age 10.0 years) whose own melatonin rose late in the evening, a 3- to 4-week course shortened sleep latency and lengthened sleep, but waking after sleep onset increased [46].

**Dose in children:** In 72 children aged 6 to 12 with chronic sleep-onset insomnia, 0.05, 0.1 and 0.15 mg per kg worked equally well, bringing sleep onset about 1 hour earlier; earlier dosing gave larger shifts [47]. In 40 children with the same problem, 5 mg at 6 pm for 4 weeks brought sleep onset 63 minutes earlier and added 41 minutes of sleep; one child developed mild generalized epilepsy 4 months after the trial began [48].

### Beta-Blocker-Induced Sleep Disturbance

Beta-blockers suppress the body's night-time melatonin. In 16 people taking atenolol or metoprolol for high blood pressure, 2.5 mg nightly for three weeks added 36 min of sleep and cut the time to reach stage 2 sleep by 14 min compared with placebo, with no rebound on stopping [14].

### Perioperative Use (Anxiety, Sedation, and Recovery)

**Preoperative anxiety:** A Cochrane review of 27 trials (2,319 participants) found melatonin probably reduces anxiety before surgery compared with placebo, and may work about as well as benzodiazepines. Some studies reported nausea, sleepiness, dizziness and headache, with no serious adverse events [54]. An NIH-cited 2015 review of 12 studies (n=774) found strong evidence that melatonin reduces anxiety before surgery, possibly as well as midazolam [1].

**Post-operative delirium:** In 210 adults having heart surgery, a 7-day course of 3 mg nightly, begun 2 days beforehand, left delirium unchanged, at 21.4% versus 20.2% with placebo, and did not shorten it or make it milder [56].

**ICU sleep quality:** In 203 ICU patients, 10 mg on up to seven nights in a row raised sleep quality scores. Very good sleep was reported by 45.8% versus 34.4% with placebo (risk ratio 1.33, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.89), and very poor sleep by 3.1% versus 14.6% (risk ratio 0.21, 95% CI 0.06 to 0.71). Melatonin did not reduce sedative or pain medicine use, pain, anxiety or delirium [55].

**After surgery:** In 80 patients after rotator cuff repair, 5 mg of melatonin 1 hour before bed for 6 weeks improved sleep quality at 6 weeks compared with sleep-hygiene advice alone; there was no placebo group, and one shoulder score (SANE) was worse at 6 weeks [105]. Shoulder function was better and narcotic use lower months later [105].

**Post-stroke delirium:** In a propensity-matched (non-randomised) comparison, 2 mg of melatonin at night started within 24 hours of an ischemic stroke was linked to less delirium: 25.6% versus 36.6% (odds ratio 0.597, 95% CI 0.372 to 0.958) [106].

### Heart and Metabolic Health

A meta-analysis of 63 randomized trials published up to October 2025 found melatonin lowered systolic blood pressure (by 2.34 mmHg), fasting blood glucose (by 11.63 mg/dL), LDL cholesterol (by 6.28 mg/dL), total cholesterol, C-reactive protein, interleukin-6 and hip circumference, and raised HDL cholesterol [58]. It found no significant effect on diastolic blood pressure, waist circumference, body weight, HbA1c or triglycerides [58]. Brad notes that most of these trials were short, many lasting only weeks, so the long-term effects are unsettled [11].

An earlier meta-analysis of 8 trials found melatonin lowered triglycerides and total cholesterol but not LDL or HDL cholesterol [59].

In people who already have heart failure, a 2025 meta-analysis of 4 randomized trials found melatonin improved quality of life, while the change in the heart's pumping strength (ejection fraction) was not significant [60].

### Headache and Migraine

**Migraine:** In a placebo-controlled trial of 178 people, 3 mg of melatonin for 3 months cut migraine days by 2.7 a month, against 2.2 with 25 mg of amitriptyline and 1.1 with placebo [57]. Melatonin was better tolerated than amitriptyline, and people taking it lost weight while amitriptyline users gained [57].

### Cognitive Function and Dementia

In healthy adults aged 55 and older, a 2 mg prolonged-release dose did not impair thinking, memory or driving tests [5]. In a meta-analysis including 9 Alzheimer's trials, those with the disease treated for more than 12 weeks improved their MMSE scores (mean difference 1.82), including in mild disease, while daytime melatonin lowered accuracy on some tasks in healthy people [67]. The American Academy of Sleep Medicine recommends against melatonin for people with dementia [1][37], and the MSD Manual advises people with dementia not to take it [2].

### Gastrointestinal Conditions

**GERD:** The case rests on two trials, one of melatonin added to omeprazole and one of a combined supplement, so melatonin may help reflux symptoms but the evidence is thin. In 72 people who completed a trial, 3 mg of sublingual melatonin plus omeprazole reduced heartburn, epigastric pain and symptom scores more than omeprazole plus placebo [66].

In a single-blind trial, all 176 people given a combined supplement (melatonin plus L-tryptophan, vitamin B6, folic acid, vitamin B12, methionine and betaine) reported their symptoms gone after 40 days, against 65.7% of those taking omeprazole; the result cannot be credited to melatonin alone [118].

**Irritable bowel syndrome (IBS):** In three small trials, melatonin may have eased IBS pain or overall symptoms; two of them found no change in sleep. In 40 patients with sleep problems, melatonin lowered mean abdominal pain score more than placebo over two weeks (2.35 v 0.70), while sleep did not change [65]. In 18 patients, 3 mg at bedtime for 8 weeks improved overall IBS scores by 45% versus 16.66% with placebo, and quality of life by 43.63% versus 14.64% [116]. In a crossover trial in 17 women with IBS, symptom scores improved further on melatonin than on placebo, 88% versus 47% responded, and sleep scores did not change [64].

**Gastric ulcers:** In people with ulcers and H. pylori infection, all ulcers had healed by day 21 when 5 mg of melatonin twice daily was added to omeprazole, against 3 of 7 with omeprazole plus placebo in each ulcer group [117].

### Menopause and Women's Health

**Bone density in postmenopausal women:** In 81 postmenopausal women with osteopenia, a year of nightly melatonin (1 mg or 3 mg) raised femoral neck bone density by 2.3% at 3 mg compared with placebo. Across both melatonin doses, trabecular thickness in the tibia rose by 2.2% and 24-hour urinary calcium fell by 12.2%; at 3 mg, volumetric spine density rose by 3.6% [63]. An observational study has linked prescribed melatonin with fractures (see Safety section).

**Postmenopause, symptom relief:** In 60 postmenopausal women aged 51 to 64, a year of melatonin, 3 mg each morning plus 5 mg at night, lowered the Kupperman Index of climacteric symptoms from 29.1 to 19.7 points, against 28.4 to 25.6 with placebo [61].

**Nocturia:** In 60 women aged over 55 years with nocturia, 2 mg a day of melatonin for 2 weeks reduced night-time urination by a median of 1.0 episode a night (0.0 with placebo) and lengthened the first stretch of unbroken sleep by a median of 1.0 h [107].

**Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS):** In 56 women with PCOS, 5 mg twice a day for 12 weeks lowered hirsutism scores, total testosterone, hs-CRP and malondialdehyde compared with placebo [108].

**Climacteric, sleep quality at low dose:** In 46 nurses working fixed shifts, 0.3 mg of melatonin on nights off reduced climacteric symptoms by 15.8% compared with placebo and improved sleep quality on days off by 35.33%. It did not change total sleep time or reproductive hormones [62].

**Endometriosis pain:** Results are mixed. In 40 women with endometriosis, 10 mg of melatonin daily for 8 weeks cut daily pain scores by 39.80% and menstrual pain by 38.01% compared with placebo [109]. In a later trial, 40 women with endometriosis took 20 mg at bedtime or placebo for two menstrual cycles, and pain did not differ, statistically or clinically [110].

### Skin Conditions

**Atopic dermatitis:** In children with atopic dermatitis, a 2019 review found melatonin helped them fall asleep 6.8 minutes earlier and sleep 35 minutes longer [1]. In a crossover trial in 48 children and adolescents, 3 mg a day of melatonin for 4 weeks lowered the SCORAD severity index by 9.1 points and shortened sleep-onset latency by 21.4 minutes compared with placebo, with no adverse events reported [119].

### Multiple Sclerosis

Results are mixed. In a placebo-controlled trial of 27 people with MS, 3 mg a night for 12 weeks improved sleep quality and total sleep time, as well as heart rate variability and oxidative stress markers [114]. In a pilot study of 14 adults with relapsing-remitting MS, a single 6 mg night-time dose improved next-morning balance and raised sleep questionnaire scores by 32.21% compared with placebo [115]. In 30 people with relapsing MS who took either 3 mg or 5 mg a day for 12 months (no placebo group), fatigue, MS impact and sleep quality scores did not change [113].

### Cancer Treatment Support

Studies of melatonin for cancer symptoms or treatment side effects have been small, with mixed results, and NIH advises people with cancer to talk with their care team before using it [1].

**Breast cancer:** In 54 women randomised after breast cancer surgery, 6 mg of melatonin for 3 months raised sleep efficiency by 4.28% and total sleep period by 37.0 min compared with placebo, without affecting cognitive function; 1 woman on melatonin and 10 on placebo dropped out [111]. In 79 women with early breast cancer, 20 mg of melatonin during radiotherapy did not prevent fatigue or improve other symptoms, and the trial stopped early [112].

### Pain Conditions

**Neuropathic pain:** In a crossover trial of 30 adults with nerve pain, melatonin at the highest tolerated dose (mean 11.9 mg a day) for 4 weeks left mean daily pain no lower than on placebo (4.1 versus 4.2) [120].

### Age-Related Macular Degeneration (AMD)

In US health records of 121,523 people aged 50 or older, melatonin use was linked to a lower risk of developing AMD (RR 0.42, 95% CI 0.28 to 0.62) and, in people who already had dry AMD, of progression to wet AMD (RR 0.44, 95% CI 0.34 to 0.56) [68]. This is an observational association and does not prove causation.

In a study without a placebo group, 55 people with AMD given 3 mg nightly were followed for more than 6 months, and visual acuity stayed stable [121].

### COVID-19

NIH considers it too soon to say whether melatonin helps prevent or treat COVID-19 [1].

In a pilot trial of 98 adults with mild to moderate COVID-19, 10 mg of melatonin daily for 14 days led to faster symptom resolution and quality-of-life recovery than placebo, though the authors were unsure the benefit was clinically meaningful [122].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### General Principles

Especially at doses above what the body makes, there is too little information to judge melatonin's overall safety, and long-term data are lacking [1]. One study that tracked melatonin in the blood found the body released about 36 micrograms a night in men and 22 micrograms in women [69]. In older adults, even a 0.4 mg surge-sustained dose produced peak levels well above normal levels for their age [17]. In adults over 50, 0.3 mg restored normal night-time melatonin levels, while 3 mg kept levels raised into the daylight hours and lowered body temperature [70].

Brad points out that the body makes about 10 to 80 micrograms a night and only about 15% of an oral dose is absorbed, so 5 mg delivers 10 times what the body makes or more; he would not take more than 1 mg a night [11]. The 2024 dose-response meta-analysis, by contrast, found the benefit for sleep onset kept rising up to 4 mg a day [29].

### Dosing by Indication

| Indication | Dose used in trials | Timing | Duration |
|-----------|------|--------|----------|
| Insomnia, adults aged 55 to 80 | 2 mg prolonged-release [31] | 2 hours before bedtime | 3 weeks |
| Primary insomnia, middle-aged adults | 3 mg fast-release [32] | 1 hour before bedtime | 4 weeks |
| Delayed sleep phase syndrome | 0.5 mg fast-release [38] | 1 hour before desired bedtime | 4 weeks |
| Jet lag | 0.5 to 5 mg [40] | Close to bedtime at the destination | Varied by trial |
| Shift work | 1 to 10 mg [45] | After the night shift | Varied by trial |
| Children with ASD | 2 mg prolonged-release, raised to 5 mg [49] | Under medical supervision | 13 weeks |
| Children with ADHD and sleep-onset insomnia | 3 or 6 mg [52] | Under medical supervision | 4 weeks |
| Migraine prevention | 3 mg [57] | Daily | 3 months |
| IBS symptoms | 3 mg [65] | Bedtime | 2 weeks |
| Beta-blocker sleep disturbance | 2.5 mg [14] | Nightly | 3 weeks |

### How and When to Take

Brad suggests taking melatonin 3 to 4 hours before you want to fall asleep (Brad's current guidance, 26 Oct 2026). In March 2026 he said around 2 to 4 hours, because it is a hormone that shifts the body clock over hours rather than a sedative [11]. In young adults, 0.3 mg or 1 mg taken 2 to 4 hours before their usual bedtime shortened time to fall asleep, with no hangover the next morning [71]; that study did not compare timings. In the dose-response meta-analysis, a longer gap before bed predicted faster sleep onset [29].

Timing differs by use. In the delayed sleep phase trial, people took it 1 hour before their desired bedtime [38]. For jet lag, trials gave it close to bedtime at the destination [40].

Glycine lowered core body temperature in rats, which researchers suggest may be one way it helps sleep.

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### Common Side Effects

Short-term use appears safe for most people, but long-term safety is unclear [1]. A 2015 review found only mild side effects in short-term studies of adults, surgical patients and critically ill patients, including headache, dizziness, nausea and sleepiness [1]. Across 17 trials (651 people), melatonin taken for 3 months or less showed no evidence of adverse effects [72].

A systematic review of 37 trials (doses 0.15 to 12 mg) found daytime sleepiness (1.66%) and headache (0.74%) were the most often reported side effects; others included agitation, fatigue, mood swings and nightmares. Most went away within days. The authors say the lack of long-term trials limits what can be said about continuous use [73].

In children, reported side effects have usually been mild: drowsiness, more bedwetting or evening urination, headache, dizziness and agitation [1]. There may be a risk of allergic reactions [1]. A single case report linked melatonin to Crohn's disease symptoms [74].

### Cardiovascular Concerns

An analysis of 5 years of health records from 130,828 adults with insomnia, presented at the American Heart Association meeting in 2025, linked a year or more of prescribed melatonin to a higher risk of heart failure (hazard ratio 1.89, 95% CI 1.78 to 2.00), of heart failure hospitalisation (hazard ratio 3.44, 95% CI 3.32 to 3.56) and of death from any cause (hazard ratio 2.09, 95% CI 1.99 to 2.18) [77]. Over the 5 years, heart failure was diagnosed in 4.6% of melatonin users and 2.7% of matched controls [77]. The findings are preliminary: it was a conference abstract, not a peer-reviewed paper, and as an observational study it shows an association, not that melatonin caused the harm [77]. Because the database includes countries where melatonin needs a prescription, people taking it over the counter in the US would have been in the comparison group [77]. Brad also notes that insomnia itself is linked to heart disease [11]. A 2026 letter from sleep researchers argues that such safety reports need to be read against the effects of insomnia itself [78]. Until full results are published, talk to your doctor if you have heart disease and plan to use melatonin long term.

For randomized trial evidence on heart and metabolic markers, see Heart and Metabolic Health above.

A report of two patients with structurally normal hearts described frequent premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) while on melatonin that stopped after melatonin was discontinued [79]. A systematic review of 37 trials listed palpitations among the few side effects judged clinically significant [73]. If you have a heart rhythm disorder, or notice palpitations after starting melatonin, stop it and talk to your doctor.

### Blood Pressure Effects

In healthy men, 1 mg of melatonin lowered blood pressure within 90 minutes [80]. In people with type 2 diabetes, 10 mg nightly for 3 months did not significantly change blood pressure [81]. In 47 people whose high blood pressure was controlled with nifedipine GITS, a 5 mg evening dose for 4 weeks pushed 24-hour systolic pressure up 6.5 mmHg and diastolic up 4.9 mmHg [82]. If you take blood pressure medicine, check with your doctor first.

### Blood Sugar and Diabetes

Blood sugar control may suffer. In a crossover trial, 30 patients with type 2 diabetes took 3 mg nightly for 7 days; glucose swings around breakfast grew compared with placebo, and the authors advise caution [6]. In 21 healthy women, a 5 mg dose before a glucose drink worsened glucose tolerance, mainly through lower insulin release at 9 AM and lower insulin sensitivity at 9 PM [83].

A 2026 trial in 21 healthy adults found the effect depends on genes. In carriers of a common variant of the melatonin receptor gene MTNR1B, which raises type 2 diabetes risk, 5 mg worsened glucose tolerance by 11.7% and cut the first-phase insulin response by 40%; it did not do so in non-carriers [7]. In contrast, the 63-trial meta-analysis found lower fasting glucose over weeks of use [58]. If you have diabetes or take diabetes medicines, talk to your doctor first.

### Interactions with Sleep Medications

In healthy adults aged 55 and older, a 2 mg prolonged-release dose taken with 10 mg of zolpidem (Ambien) worsened zolpidem's impairment of thinking and driving tests 1 and 4 hours later; the authors point to an interaction between melatonin and drugs of this class (GABA-A modulators) [5]. Melatonin may add to the sedative effects of benzodiazepines [2]. In a trial of 34 people taking benzodiazepines, 14 of 18 given 2 mg of controlled-release melatonin stopped their benzodiazepine, against 4 of 16 given placebo [75].

### Balance and Driving

Melatonin may stay active longer in older people and cause daytime drowsiness [1]. In 34 older adults, a single 3 mg dose increased body sway one hour later, though it did not affect a thinking task [9]. Until you know how it affects you, avoid driving or using machinery soon after taking it.

Higher-dose sustained products may keep melatonin raised beyond the usual sleep period. In older adults, a 4.0 mg surge-sustained dose kept levels above 50 pg/mL for an average of 10 hours [17].

### Pregnancy and Lactation

Safety in pregnant or breastfeeding women has not been studied [1], so do not use melatonin if you are pregnant or nursing; the MSD Manual also advises women trying to become pregnant not to take it [2]. In a trial of 189 women having labor induced, 10 mg of melatonin every 6 hours (up to four doses) did not reduce cesarean births, and more babies in the melatonin group needed special care nursery admission, mainly to monitor for low blood sugar (18.5% vs. 8.1%) [90].

### Children -- Poison Control Concerns

Reports to US poison control centers of melatonin ingestion by people 19 and younger rose 530% from 2012 to 2021; five children needed mechanical ventilation and two died [8]. Most calls (94.3%) were for children 5 and younger who took it by accident, most of those who took melatonin (82.8%) had no symptoms, and the data did not show whether the two deaths were caused by melatonin [1]. The CDC estimated about 11,000 emergency department visits from 2019 to 2022 for unsupervised melatonin ingestion by children 5 and younger, many involving gummies [1]. NIH advises keeping melatonin out of children's reach and sight [1].

### Label Accuracy Concerns

A 2023 study found 22 of 25 melatonin gummy products were inaccurately labeled, with melatonin ranging from 74% to 347% of the labeled amount; most held 1.2 to 1.7 times more than stated [1]. In a Canadian study of 31 supplements, melatonin content ran from 83% below to 478% above the label, more than 71% missed the label by over 10%, and 26% contained serotonin, a hormone that can have harmful effects at relatively low levels [91][1]. In 110 US products marketed to children, melatonin content ranged from 0% to 667% of the label [92]. Choose a product that an independent lab has tested.

### Seizures

Evidence is limited. A 1998 report described pro-convulsant effects of oral melatonin in children with neurological disabilities [88]. In people with epilepsy, melatonin may increase seizures and may weaken antiseizure medicines [2]. NIH advises people with epilepsy to take melatonin only under medical supervision [1]; the MSD Manual advises them not to take it [2].

### Mental Health

Melatonin may cause short-term low mood and may make existing depression worse [2]. In a 1976 crossover study in six people with moderate to severe depression and two with Huntington's chorea, melatonin given in varying doses worsened low mood and caused loss of sleep and weight [87].

### Hormonal Effects

Because melatonin is a hormone, it is possible that supplements could affect hormonal development in children, including puberty, menstrual cycles and prolactin, but this is uncertain [1]. Melatonin may decrease fertility, and women trying to become pregnant should not take it [2]. In 29 people given 6 mg of melatonin, blood levels were 4- to 5-fold higher in oral contraceptive users, because the pill slows melatonin's breakdown by CYP1A2 [84].

### Autoimmune Disease

Melatonin may make autoimmune diseases worse, and people with an autoimmune disease should not take it [2].

### Fracture Risk

In UK primary care records, people aged 45 and older prescribed melatonin had a higher fracture risk than matched controls (hazard ratio 1.90, 95% CI 1.41 to 2.57), still raised after adjustment (hazard ratio 1.44, 95% CI 1.01 to 2.04). Only those with three or more prescriptions had raised risk [76]. This is an observational association.

### Restless Legs Syndrome

Leg movements may get worse. In 8 people with RLS, melatonin increased periodic leg movements on a standard immobilization test [85].

### Asthma

In 22 women with mild to moderate asthma, a 4-week course of 3 mg improved sleep quality without changing asthma symptoms, reliever use or peak flow; the authors say longer studies are needed before melatonin can be recommended in asthma [89].

### Aggression

In 63 healthy men, those given melatonin chose high punishments for an opponent more often than those given placebo in a laboratory aggression task [86].

## 7. Dietary Sources

Melatonin occurs naturally in many foods [124]. In 8,320 university graduates, mean dietary melatonin intake was about 26 micrograms (25,554.7 ng) a day [123].

### Food Sources

Among animal foods, eggs and fish contain more melatonin; among plant foods, nuts contain the most, and some mushrooms, cereals and germinated legumes or seeds are also sources [124]. Blood melatonin rises after eating melatonin-containing food [124].
