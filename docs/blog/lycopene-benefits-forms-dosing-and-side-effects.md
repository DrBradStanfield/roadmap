## 1. Overview

Lycopene is a fat-soluble red pigment and carotenoid antioxidant with the molecular formula C40H56 and a molecular weight of 536.9 g/mol. It is an acyclic isoprenoid consisting of eight isoprene units and 11 conjugated double bonds, and is one of the most potent biological antioxidants identified — with a singlet oxygen quenching capacity approximately twice that of beta-carotene and ten times greater than alpha-tocopherol (vitamin E) [1][2]. Unlike provitamin A carotenoids such as beta-carotene, lycopene is a non-provitamin A carotenoid: it cannot be converted to vitamin A in the human body because it lacks the cyclic beta-ionone rings required for retinal conversion [2][3].

Lycopene is responsible for the red and pink pigmentation in tomatoes, watermelon, pink grapefruit, guava, and apricots. Tomatoes and tomato-based products account for approximately 80-90% of lycopene intake in Western diets [2][4][5]. Average daily intake in the United States and Europe ranges from about 5 to 10 mg per day, with intake reaching up to 20 mg per day in Mediterranean populations that consume more tomato products [2][6]. In Europe, average daily intake is highest in Italy (7.4 mg) and lowest in the UK (1.1 mg) [6].

The compound's biological activities extend well beyond simple antioxidant scavenging. Lycopene inhibits inflammation, modulates gene expression through the Nrf2 pathway (which upregulates detoxifying enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase and superoxide dismutase), inhibits the NF-kB inflammatory pathway, and modulates insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) signaling [2][3][7]. It also inhibits HMG-CoA reductase, the same enzyme targeted by statin drugs for cholesterol reduction [8]. Lycopene enhances endothelial function by promoting nitric oxide bioavailability and reducing adhesion molecule expression on vascular walls [9].

A notable analysis of data from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), among approximately 30,000 adults in the U.S., found that lycopene supplementation was associated with an 18% lower risk of all-cause mortality and a 34% lower risk of cancer-related death over the study period. No other dietary supplement examined in the analysis was associated with a reduced risk of death [10].

Lycopene is classified as Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA), with multiple GRAS notices issued for synthetic lycopene, tomato-derived extracts, and lycopene from fungal sources such as Blakeslea trispora [11][12]. No established upper intake limit exists for lycopene, though clinical studies have demonstrated tolerability of supplemental doses up to 75 mg per day without significant adverse outcomes [13].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Isomer Forms

Lycopene predominantly exists in the all-trans configuration in natural food sources, where all 13 double bonds adopt a trans geometry, resulting in a straight, rod-like molecular shape [2][14]. However, various cis (or Z) isomers also occur, with common forms including 5-cis, 9-cis, 13-cis, and 15-cis lycopene. These cis isomers introduce bends in the polyene chain, altering their solubility and biological interactions compared to the all-trans form.

Bioavailability differs substantially between isomers, and this has significant practical implications. The predominant all-trans form found in raw foods has an estimated absorption rate of only 10-30%, while cis-isomers exhibit higher absorption rates of 40-50%, owing to their greater solubility in bile acid micelles and reduced tendency to crystallize [15]. Cooking and food processing enhance bioavailability specifically by inducing isomerization from the less absorbable all-trans form to more bioavailable cis-isomers, without substantially reducing total lycopene content [16][17]. This is why processed tomato products consistently outperform raw tomatoes for lycopene delivery.

Lycopene is susceptible to degradation and isomerization under exposure to light, oxygen, and elevated temperatures. Temperatures of 60-80 degrees C promote conversion from all-trans to cis forms, with more pronounced effects during prolonged heating or in the presence of pro-oxidants [14][18]. Moderate cooking is beneficial for bioavailability, but extreme or prolonged processing can lead to loss of total lycopene content and reduced bioactivity.

### Supplement Forms

Lycopene supplements are available in several forms:

- **Tomato-derived oleoresin extracts** — The most common supplement form. Lyc-O-Mato (also written Lycomato, from Lycored) is the most widely studied standardized tomato oleoresin extract. It has a greasy consistency, which is why it is best formulated as softgels. Only a small percentage of Lycomato is actually lycopene, but this is already factored into the lycopene amounts listed on Supplement Facts panels. Lycomato claims to be non-GMO and allergen-free [19]. This form has been used in the majority of positive clinical trials for blood pressure, prostate cancer, and skin protection.

- **Whole tomato food supplements** — Contain lycopene along with other tomato phytonutrients. Used in some clinical studies, such as the BPH study by Quiros-Roldan et al. (2021), which used a supplement providing 23.75 mg of lycopene per serving [20].

- **Synthetic lycopene** — Produced via chemical synthesis or microbial fermentation. Available for individuals allergic to tomatoes. Synthetic lycopene appears to be as well-absorbed as natural-source lycopene [19]. One branded synthetic form is LycoVit (from BASF), used in the BPH study by Schwarz et al. (2008) [21]. Synthetic lycopene has received GRAS status from the FDA [11]. Modern microbial production in engineered E. coli has achieved titers over 1.2 g/L, enabling sustainable industrial production [2].

- **LactoLycopene** — A proprietary formulation (from Cambridge Nutraceuticals Ltd) claimed to enhance lycopene absorption. However, a study found that its bioavailability is only equivalent to that of lycopene from tomato paste, not superior [22][23].

- **Tomato nutrient complex** — Products like Lycored's Lycopene-Rich Tomato Nutrient Complex and Lycoderm (combined with rosemary extract) have been used in skin protection and wrinkle reduction studies [24][25].

### Key Factors Affecting Absorption

Lycopene absorption occurs primarily in the small intestine through passive diffusion, facilitated by incorporation into mixed micelles formed with dietary fats and bile salts [15]. Once absorbed, lycopene is packaged into chylomicrons and transported via the lymphatic system before entering the bloodstream. It accumulates selectively in various tissues, with the highest concentrations observed in the testes, adrenal glands, and liver, where levels can reach up to 10 times higher than in other organs [26]. Following absorption, lycopene undergoes metabolism primarily through enzymatic cleavage by beta-carotene oxygenase 1 (BCO1), producing apo-lycopenals such as apo-10'-lycopenal, which are further oxidized to apo-lycopenoic acids — bioactive metabolites that may contribute to lycopene's health effects [27].

**Fat co-consumption is critical.** Whether obtained from food or supplements, lycopene must be consumed with a meal containing fats or oils for adequate absorption. A 2005 human study demonstrated that adding 150 g of avocado (approximately 24 g of lipids, primarily monounsaturated fatty acids) to tomato salsa increased lycopene absorption by 4.4-fold (measured by plasma area under the curve) compared to salsa consumed alone [28].

**The type of fat matters.** A study conducted in Singapore found that lycopene absorption from a salad was 45.8% higher when consumed with olive oil compared to coconut oil. Interestingly, this effect was specific to lycopene — there was no difference in the absorption of lutein or zeaxanthin between oil types, which the researchers attributed to the more hydrophobic nature of lycopene compared to these other carotenoids [29].

**High-dose calcium supplements dramatically reduce lycopene absorption.** A clinical study found that 500 mg of calcium from calcium carbonate taken with a meal containing 19 mg of lycopene (from tomato paste) reduced the bioavailability of lycopene by 83%, compared to the same meal consumed without the supplement [30]. This effect is likely due to a reaction between carotenoids and the divalent ions of minerals (calcium, magnesium, certain forms of iron, zinc) that makes carotenoids less bioavailable [31][32]. Lycopene or any carotenoid supplement should be taken at a different time of day than supplements or meals containing large amounts (hundreds of milligrams) of minerals.

**Genetic variation affects absorption efficiency.** Polymorphisms in the SCARB1 gene, which encodes the scavenger receptor class B type 1 involved in carotenoid transport, can significantly affect absorption, with certain variants associated with up to 24% higher serum lycopene concentrations [33]. This genetic variability may partially explain why some individuals respond more robustly to lycopene supplementation than others.

**Processing substantially enhances bioavailability from food.** Lycopene from cooked or processed tomato products (paste, juice, sauce, ketchup) is significantly more bioavailable than from raw tomatoes. This occurs because heat breaks down plant cell walls (releasing lycopene from the food matrix), converts all-trans lycopene to more absorbable cis-isomers, and the addition of lipids during cooking promotes micelle formation [2][16][17]. Lycopene in supplements appears to have bioavailability similar to that of lycopene in cooked foods and juice [19].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Cancer Prevention

#### Overview of Observational Evidence

There is observational evidence that higher intakes of lycopene-containing foods, particularly tomatoes, may reduce the risk of certain types of cancer, though this evidence is indirect and far from definitive. The FDA concluded in 2005 that while there was "very limited evidence" to support an association between tomato consumption and reduced risks of various cancers, there was "no credible evidence" of such an association for lycopene itself [34]. Despite this conservative assessment, subsequent research has continued to demonstrate associations between lycopene intake and reduced cancer risk.

A large observational study of elderly Americans found that those who consumed the most tomatoes had a 50% lower risk of death from cancer compared to those who consumed the least [35]. Higher tomato consumption has been associated with a lower risk of stomach cancer, and some (but not all) studies have found associations with reduced risk of colorectal cancer. There is also evidence that dietary lycopene intake is associated with decreased risk of pancreatic cancer, lung cancer, and ovarian cancer in premenopausal women [36].

The NHANES analysis among approximately 30,000 U.S. adults found that lycopene supplementation was associated with an 18% lower risk of all-cause mortality and a 34% lower risk of cancer-related death — with no other dietary supplement examined showing a similar association with reduced mortality [10].

The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) issued a 2011 scientific opinion evaluating but rejecting a proposed health claim for lycopene from tomatoes contributing to the protection of DNA, proteins, and lipids from oxidative damage, citing insufficient evidence from human intervention studies [37].

#### Prostate Cancer

Prostate cancer has been the most extensively studied cancer type in relation to lycopene. The weight of observational evidence suggests a modest protective effect, though the magnitude and certainty vary across studies.

A large observational study in the U.S. found that a high intake of lycopene reduced the risk of prostate cancer by 21%, and a high intake of tomatoes and tomato products was associated with a 35% lower risk of total prostate cancer and a 53% lower risk of advanced prostate cancer. Tomato sauce (2 to 4 servings per week) had the strongest inverse association with prostate cancer risk, while weaker associations were found with tomatoes and pizza, but not tomato juice [38].

However, a more recent systematic review and meta-analysis of available studies (including the study above) concluded that, overall, "tomato intake may have a weak protective effect against prostate cancer" [39]. A dose-response meta-analysis of observational studies found that each 5 mg/day increase in lycopene intake was associated with a 2.1% reduction in prostate cancer risk (RR 0.979, 95% CI 0.962-0.996), with higher intakes (>6 mg/day) linked to modest overall risk reductions of 10-20% in some cohorts [40]. A 2025 meta-analysis reported an odds ratio of 0.86 (95% CI 0.76-0.98) for advanced prostate cancer with lycopene intakes exceeding 15 mg/day, primarily from dietary sources [41].

Lycopene's proposed anti-cancer mechanisms in prostate cancer involve multiple pathways: interference with cell cycle progression, induction of apoptosis (programmed cell death), and downregulation of androgen receptor activity in hormone-sensitive malignancies. Lycopene also modulates IGF-1 signaling by decreasing its expression and receptor activation, curbing cell proliferation and survival signals implicated in prostate cancer development [42][43].

#### Supplementation Studies in Prostate Cancer

A few small but notable interventional studies have examined lycopene supplementation directly in men with prostate conditions:

A preliminary study in men with high-grade prostate intraepithelial neoplasia (precancerous changes) found that 4 mg of lycopene (Lyc-O-Mato) taken twice daily (8 mg total) for one year appeared to help prevent precancerous cells in the prostate from turning cancerous compared to no lycopene supplementation. In the lycopene group, none of the patients progressed to cancer, while some in the control group did progress [44].

A study using a higher dose found that 15 mg of the same lycopene supplement (Lyc-O-Mato) taken twice daily (30 mg total) with meals for three weeks prior to surgical removal of the prostate reduced tumor growth compared to a control group that did not receive lycopene before surgery. The lycopene group also showed smaller tumors and lower PSA levels at the time of surgery [45].

These studies, while promising, were small and require confirmation in larger, well-controlled trials. The evidence supports a preventive rather than therapeutic role for lycopene, with no robust evidence from large-scale RCTs demonstrating efficacy in treating established prostate cancer [2].

#### Oral Leukoplakia

In a study among 58 middle-aged men and women with leukoplakia (a precancerous condition of the mouth and mucous membranes), 8 mg of lycopene taken daily as a supplement (LycoRed) for three months significantly reduced the size and severity of oral lesions compared to placebo. A daily dose of 4 mg also reduced the size and severity of lesions compared to placebo, although the effect was less pronounced than with the higher dose, demonstrating a dose-response relationship [46].

#### Lung and Breast Cancer

For lung and breast cancers, lycopene's modulation of estrogen metabolism and inhibition of carcinogen-induced DNA damage have been proposed as protective factors, with higher intake levels correlating to reduced tumor growth in animal models [42][47]. In human observational data, the associations are weaker and less consistent than those observed for prostate cancer. Lycopene's inhibition of the NF-kB pathway — which reduces transcription of genes involved in inflammation and cell survival — may limit disease progression across multiple cancer types [48].

### Cardiovascular Health

#### Cholesterol Reduction

Lycopene inhibits HMG-CoA reductase, the same enzyme involved in cholesterol production that is targeted by statin drugs [8]. This mechanism has been validated in both laboratory and clinical settings.

A systematic review and meta-analysis of 12 clinical trials lasting between two weeks and six months investigated the effects of lycopene from tomatoes, tomato-based foods (sauce, paste, soup), and/or lycopene supplements in middle-aged and older adults. The review concluded that daily doses of 25 mg or more of lycopene lowered average total cholesterol levels by approximately 7 mg/dL and LDL cholesterol levels by approximately 10 mg/dL. Importantly, lycopene intakes below 25 mg per day did not significantly lower cholesterol, establishing a clear dose threshold [49]. Lycopene does not appear to increase HDL ("good") cholesterol levels [8].

The cholesterol-lowering effect of lycopene, while modest compared to statin therapy, may be clinically relevant as an adjunctive dietary strategy, particularly for individuals with borderline elevations who are not yet on pharmacological therapy.

#### Blood Pressure

There is evidence that lycopene supplements may help lower blood pressure in people with hypertension. In a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study of 31 men and women with stage 1 hypertension, a capsule containing 15 mg of lycopene (from Lyc-O-Mato) taken daily with a meal for two months reduced average systolic blood pressure by 9 mmHg and diastolic blood pressure by 4 mmHg compared to placebo [50]. A reduction of this magnitude is clinically meaningful — a systolic decrease of 9 mmHg is comparable to the effect achieved by some antihypertensive medications. Supplementation at this dose did not decrease total or LDL cholesterol levels, consistent with the meta-analysis finding that higher doses (>25 mg per day) are needed for cholesterol lowering.

A systematic review of broader supplementation and blood pressure data indicates that results are mixed, with inconsistent effects on blood pressure across studies, likely due to variability in doses, formulations, and study populations [51]. Lycopene may exert its antihypertensive effects by attenuating vascular stiffness and reducing inflammatory cytokine production [9].

#### Cardiovascular Risk Reduction from Dietary Sources

A large observational study among middle-aged and elderly women found that those who consumed 10 servings of tomato-based products per week had a 29% lower risk of cardiovascular disease and a 57% lower risk of heart attack compared to those who consumed fewer than 1.5 servings per week [52].

Preclinical studies indicate that lycopene reduces the oxidation of low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol — a key initiating step in the development of atherosclerosis — by scavenging reactive oxygen species and stabilizing lipid membranes [53]. Lycopene enhances endothelial function by promoting nitric oxide bioavailability and reducing expression of adhesion molecules on vascular walls, which may contribute to improved blood flow and vessel relaxation [9].

#### Inflammation and Heart Failure

A pilot RCT in patients with heart failure showed that 29.4 mg/day of lycopene supplementation for 30 days reduced C-reactive protein (CRP) levels in women, an inflammatory marker strongly associated with cardiovascular risk [54]. Broader meta-analyses indicate that the anti-inflammatory effects of lycopene supplementation on cardiovascular endpoints beyond CRP are inconsistent and require larger, longer-duration trials to confirm [51].

Lycopene may also have blood-thinning (antiplatelet) effects that could be beneficial for cardiovascular health. In laboratory studies, lycopene has been shown to inhibit platelet aggregation and to increase the antiplatelet effects of aspirin [8]. However, clinical data on bleeding risk are lacking.

### Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH)

Lycopene supplements are sometimes promoted for decreasing symptoms of enlarged prostate (benign prostatic hyperplasia or BPH), but research to date has yielded mixed results with no firm evidence of efficacy.

A six-month randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled study in Germany among 37 older men with BPH found that while prostate growth tended to be slower in those who took 15 mg of lycopene daily (LycoVit, a synthetic form from BASF), the difference was not statistically significant compared to placebo. Similarly, there was no significant change in PSA levels compared to placebo. The supplement was well-tolerated with no adverse effects. Men in both groups consumed an average of 4 mg of lycopene per day through their diet [21].

A study in Italy among 31 HIV-positive men (average age 66) with BPH found that taking 5 grams of a whole-tomato food supplement containing 23.75 mg of lycopene once daily for 12 weeks reduced prostate symptoms across multiple domains of the International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS): incomplete bladder emptying was reduced by approximately 28%, urinary frequency by approximately 20%, intermittency by approximately 25%, urgency by approximately 20%, weak stream by approximately 35%, and nocturia (the need to urinate during the night) by approximately 40% compared to baseline. The placebo group showed improvement in only intermittency. However, a critical limitation is that no direct statistical comparison between the lycopene and placebo groups was performed, so it is unclear whether the between-group difference was significant. There was no improvement in total or free PSA levels [20].

### Male Infertility

Lycopene has been shown to reduce oxidative damage in sperm, which is a significant contributor to male infertility. A comprehensive review of six small clinical studies found that lycopene supplementation at doses of 4 to 8 mg daily for 3 to 12 months was beneficial in the treatment of male infertility, improving measures of sperm health and increasing pregnancy rates. One of the reviewed studies reported a 70% increase in sperm count and a 54% increase in sperm motility in infertile men taking 8 mg of lycopene daily [55].

A placebo-controlled study in England among 56 healthy young men found that taking 7 mg of lycopene twice daily (14 mg total, as LactoLycopene) for three months did not increase the overall concentration of motile ("moving") sperm but did produce two significant improvements: the percentage of motile sperm that were healthy-shaped (normal morphology) nearly doubled, increasing from 7.5% to 13.5%, and the percentage of fast-moving sperm traveling in a forward direction increased from 10.6% to 14.76% [23]. These improvements in sperm quality parameters, even in healthy (not infertile) men, suggest that lycopene may enhance sperm function independently of total count.

### Skin Protection

#### UV Damage Prevention

Carotenoids such as beta-carotene, astaxanthin, and lycopene from foods and supplements are absorbed into the skin, where they may help protect against oxidative damage and ultraviolet (UV) damage from the sun [56].

A randomized controlled study among women in England found that consuming 55 grams of tomato paste in olive oil (providing 16 mg of lycopene) daily for three months significantly decreased measures of tissue damage and redness (erythema) from exposure to an artificial source of ultraviolet radiation compared to consuming olive oil alone. The protective effect was attributed to lycopene's ability to quench free radicals generated by UV exposure [57].

A company-funded, double-blind, placebo-controlled study in Germany among men and women found that two capsules of a "tomato nutrient complex" (Lycopene-Rich Tomato Nutrient Complex, Lycored) taken twice daily (providing a total of 20 mg of lycopene) for three months prevented genetic damage in skin cells of the upper buttock when exposed to ultraviolet radiation compared to placebo. The study demonstrated that lycopene supplementation reduced DNA strand breaks and other markers of UV-induced genotoxicity in the skin [24].

Additional RCTs have demonstrated reduced UVB-induced erythema (skin reddening from sunburn) after 12 weeks of supplementation with 10-20 mg/day of lycopene [58]. Lycopene's skin protection is attributed to its free radical quenching capacity in the dermis and epidermis, reducing the oxidative cascade that leads to collagen degradation and photoaging.

#### Wrinkle Reduction

A study among 60 women (average age 48) found that those who took one capsule of tomato extract combined with rosemary extract standardized for 2 mg carnosic acid (Lycoderm, Lycored — which funded the study) twice daily with a meal (providing a total of 15 mg of lycopene) for four months had very modest but measurable improvements in wrinkle depth and skin hydration. The lycopene group showed an average 5.6% reduction in wrinkle depth severity in lines around the eyes ("crow's feet") as measured by photo analysis, and 90.3% of participants reported an improvement in skin hydration. In the placebo group, there was no improvement in wrinkle depth and only 58.6% reported improved skin hydration. Participants agreed to avoid extensive sun exposure during the study and a month prior, and were provided with a cleanser (Cetaphil) and sunscreen (Neutrogena SPF 30) for use during the study [25].

### Metabolic Health

Lycopene's anti-inflammatory properties show promise for metabolic disorders including diabetes and obesity. In preclinical models, lycopene decreases adipocyte hypertrophy (fat cell enlargement) and insulin resistance by suppressing pro-inflammatory adipokine release [59]. These findings align with lycopene's established ability to inhibit the NF-kB pathway and reduce inflammatory signaling.

Reviews of the clinical evidence affirm the anti-inflammatory potential of lycopene for metabolic diseases but emphasize the need for more long-term, well-powered RCTs to address inconsistencies in human outcomes related to insulin sensitivity and lipid metabolism [60]. The evidence is currently insufficient to recommend lycopene specifically for diabetes or obesity management.

### Antioxidant Mechanisms

Lycopene exerts its antioxidant effects through multiple distinct pathways:

- **Singlet oxygen quenching:** Lycopene's extended conjugated pi-electron system (11 conjugated double bonds) enables extremely efficient physical quenching of singlet oxygen without chemical alteration of the carotenoid molecule. The rate constant is approximately 3.1 x 10^10 per molar per second in ethanol, making it more than twice as effective as beta-carotene (1.4 x 10^10) and approximately ten times more effective than alpha-tocopherol (vitamin E) [1][2].

- **Free radical scavenging:** Lycopene donates electrons to peroxyl radicals, forming a resonance-stabilized lycopene radical cation that does not propagate chain reactions in lipid peroxidation. The resulting radical is delocalized across the conjugated system, reducing its reactivity and allowing it to be repaired by other antioxidants [2][61].

- **Nrf2 pathway activation:** Lycopene activates the Nrf2 (nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2) signaling pathway, a master regulator of cellular redox homeostasis. Upon activation, Nrf2 translocates to the nucleus and binds to antioxidant response elements, upregulating genes encoding protective enzymes such as glutathione peroxidase and superoxide dismutase. This indirect mechanism enhances the cell's endogenous antioxidant capacity [7][62].

- **NF-kB pathway inhibition:** Lycopene suppresses the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-kB) inflammatory pathway, reducing transcription of genes involved in inflammation, cell survival, and tumor promotion [48].

- **IGF-1 modulation:** Lycopene decreases insulin-like growth factor-1 (IGF-1) expression and receptor activation, which curbs cell proliferation and survival signals implicated in cancer development and metabolic dysregulation [43].

### Other Potential Uses

Other potential uses for lycopene that have been investigated include the prevention of preeclampsia in pregnancy and the treatment of gingivitis [19]. Limited evidence suggests lycopene may protect against toxin-induced thyroid damage rather than interfere with thyroid function [63]. Lycopene may also support neuroprotective effects, though evidence for supplements is limited and inconsistent compared to food sources [3]. In the context of skin health beyond UV protection, lycopene's presence in the dermis correlates significantly with skin roughness and texture measures, suggesting a role in overall skin quality [56].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

An optimal dosage for lycopene supplementation has not been established. Research studies have demonstrated potential benefits from amounts ranging from 4 mg to 25 mg or more per day, depending on the health outcome being targeted. The wide range reflects the different conditions studied, with higher doses generally needed for cardiovascular effects and lower doses showing some benefit for cancer chemoprevention.

### Practical Dosing Guidance

- **Always take with dietary fat.** Whether from food or supplements, lycopene requires dietary fat for adequate absorption. Consume with a meal containing oils or fat-rich foods such as avocado, olive oil, nuts, or cheese [28][29].
- **Olive oil is the preferred fat source.** Lycopene absorption was 45.8% higher with olive oil compared to coconut oil in a controlled study [29].
- **Separate from mineral supplements.** High-dose calcium, magnesium, iron, or zinc supplements can reduce lycopene absorption by up to 83% when taken together. Take lycopene at a different time of day — ideally at least 2 hours apart from mineral supplements [30][31][32].
- **Dietary sources may be sufficient for general health.** Lycopene from processed tomato products (paste, sauce, juice) is readily absorbed and provides a matrix of other beneficial compounds. For general health maintenance, 2-4 servings per week of tomato sauce or other cooked tomato products may provide meaningful benefits based on the epidemiological data [38].
- **Consider splitting higher doses.** Absorption efficiency declines at higher single doses. If targeting 20+ mg/day for cardiovascular benefits, consider splitting into two doses with fat-containing meals.
- **Supplements have comparable bioavailability to cooked foods.** Lycopene in supplement form (particularly oleoresin softgels) appears to have similar bioavailability to lycopene from cooked foods and juice [19].

### How to Read a Supplement Label

Most lycopene supplements contain standardized tomato extracts. The Supplement Facts panel should list the amount of lycopene per serving, not the weight of the total tomato extract. For example, a Lyc-O-Mato softgel may contain 500 mg of tomato oleoresin but is standardized to provide only 15 mg of lycopene — the 15 mg figure is the clinically relevant dose.

For synthetic lycopene supplements, the listed amount typically reflects pure lycopene content directly. Check whether the dose is listed per capsule or per serving (which may be multiple capsules).

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Tolerability

Lycopene supplements are generally well-tolerated across the range of doses used in clinical studies. Average daily adult intake of lycopene from foods in the U.S. ranges from about 5 to 10 mg per day [6]. Clinical studies have demonstrated tolerability of supplemental doses up to 75 mg per day without significant adverse outcomes [13].

Lycopene is classified as GRAS by the FDA for use as a food ingredient, with levels up to 575 ppm approved in foods and supplements. Recent GRAS notices (e.g., GRN 1253 in 2025) have confirmed safety for broader applications including synthetic, tomato-derived, and fungal-source lycopene [11][12].

Toxicological evaluations confirm lycopene's low acute toxicity, with an oral LD50 exceeding 5,000 mg/kg body weight in rats, indicating practical non-toxicity. Subchronic and chronic studies in rodents show no evidence of genotoxicity, developmental toxicity, or carcinogenicity at doses up to 3 g/kg per day [64][65].

### Gastrointestinal Symptoms

Mild gastrointestinal symptoms have been reported in some clinical studies of lycopene supplementation, including stomach upset, gas and bloating, diarrhea, and vomiting [66][67]. These effects are uncommon at typical dietary intake levels of 8-21 mg per day and appear to be more common with higher supplemental doses [13].

### Allergic Reactions

Rarely, allergic skin reactions and rashes have been reported in clinical studies of lycopene supplementation [68][69]. Although reports of allergic reactions to tomatoes and tomato-based foods are rare, reactions including rash, hives, throat swelling, and anaphylaxis can occur in sensitive individuals [70]. Individuals with known tomato allergy may consider synthetic lycopene supplements as an alternative, as these avoid tomato-derived proteins [19].

### Lycopenodermia (Skin Discoloration)

Excessive intake of lycopene from foods can cause lycopenodermia (a form of carotenoderma), a benign and reversible condition marked by yellow-orange coloration of the skin due to high levels of carotenoids in the dermal and subcutaneous tissues. This condition typically occurs with sustained daily consumption exceeding approximately 30 mg, often from high-dose supplements or concentrated tomato products [71][72].

A well-documented case report described a 26-year-old woman who presented to the hospital with yellow coloration on her fingers and palms. Approximately two months before the discoloration appeared, she had begun consuming about 20 to 30 cherry tomatoes daily (providing approximately 8.7 to 13 mg of lycopene), in addition to her regular dietary intake of 180 mL (about 6 fluid ounces) of tomato juice daily (providing approximately 17 mg of lycopene) and eating other carotenoid-rich foods including pumpkins, carrots, blueberries, and green vegetable juice. Blood tests revealed lycopene at 272.2 mcg/dL compared to normal levels of 44.3 mcg/dL or lower. Even after reducing dietary lycopene intake for 6 months, her skin discoloration persisted and the yellowish areas slightly thickened, despite blood lycopene levels decreasing to 31.5 mcg/dL [73]. This case illustrates that while lycopenodermia is medically benign, the cosmetic effects can be slow to resolve completely.

### Long-Term Safety

The long-term safety of taking high amounts of lycopene (15 mg or more per day) as supplements has not been extensively evaluated in studies lasting more than 6-12 months [6]. While the GRAS status and toxicological data are reassuring, individuals considering long-term high-dose supplementation should be aware that this represents use beyond the duration of most clinical trials. There is no established tolerable upper intake level for lycopene from any regulatory authority.

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Blood-Thinning Medications

In laboratory studies, lycopene has been shown to have antiplatelet effects and to increase the antiplatelet effects of aspirin [8]. Although there do not appear to be published clinical reports of increased bleeding in people taking lycopene supplements, the in vitro evidence warrants caution when combining lycopene supplementation with blood-thinning medications, including:

- Aspirin
- Warfarin (Coumadin)
- Clopidogrel (Plavix)
- Heparin and low-molecular-weight heparins
- Other anticoagulant and antiplatelet agents
- NSAIDs with significant antiplatelet activity (ibuprofen, naproxen)

Patients on anticoagulant or antiplatelet therapy should inform their healthcare provider before starting lycopene supplementation and monitor for any signs of increased bruising or bleeding.

### Statin Drugs and Lipid-Lowering Therapy

Lycopene inhibits HMG-CoA reductase, the same enzyme targeted by statin drugs (atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, simvastatin, etc.) [8]. This shared mechanism of action may produce additive cholesterol-lowering effects, which could potentially benefit patients. However, monitoring is warranted in those already on lipid-lowering therapy to avoid excessive cholesterol reduction. There are no published reports of clinically significant adverse interactions between lycopene supplements and statins, but the theoretical concern is noted.

### Calcium Supplements

As detailed in the Forms and Bioavailability section, high-dose calcium supplements (500 mg or more from calcium carbonate) can reduce lycopene absorption by up to 83% when taken concurrently with a lycopene-rich meal [30]. This is a pharmacokinetic interaction affecting absorption rather than a pharmacodynamic drug interaction. The practical recommendation is to separate lycopene supplementation from calcium supplements by at least 2 hours.

### Other Mineral Supplements

Divalent mineral ions from supplements containing calcium, magnesium, certain forms of iron (ferrous), and zinc can bind to carotenoids including lycopene, beta-carotene, and astaxanthin, reducing their bioavailability [31][32]. When taking high-dose mineral supplements, separate from lycopene or other carotenoid supplements by at least 2 hours. Note that food-source minerals at typical dietary levels are unlikely to cause clinically significant interactions.

### Thyroid Medications

No significant adverse interactions between lycopene and thyroid medications have been identified. Studies indicate that lycopene may actually protect against toxin-induced thyroid damage rather than interfere with thyroid function or thyroid hormone medications [63].

## 7. Dietary Sources

Lycopene is found in various red and pink fruits and vegetables. Tomatoes and their processed derivatives are by far the richest and most commonly consumed sources, accounting for 80-90% of dietary lycopene intake in Western diets.

### Tips for Maximizing Dietary Lycopene Absorption

- **Cook your tomatoes.** Processed tomato products (paste, sauce, canned, sun-dried) contain more bioavailable lycopene than raw tomatoes because heat breaks down plant cell walls and converts the all-trans isomer to more absorbable cis forms [16][17]. Cooking can increase bioavailability up to several-fold compared to raw tomatoes [2].

- **Add olive oil.** Consuming lycopene-rich foods with olive oil increases absorption by approximately 46% compared to coconut oil [29]. The traditional Mediterranean practice of cooking tomato sauces in olive oil is an ideal strategy for maximizing lycopene uptake.

- **Tomato paste is the most concentrated source.** Tomato paste can contain 25-150 mg of lycopene per 100 g, making it by far the most efficient dietary source [4][5]. A single tablespoon of tomato paste provides approximately 3-5 mg of lycopene.

- **Avocado dramatically enhances absorption.** Adding 150 g of avocado to tomato-based dishes increased lycopene absorption by 4.4 times in one controlled study [28]. Guacamole with salsa, avocado added to tomato-based soups, or avocado on tomato-topped toast are all practical combinations.

- **Do not take calcium supplements with lycopene-rich meals.** 500 mg of calcium carbonate reduced lycopene absorption from tomato paste by 83% [30]. If taking both, separate them by at least 2 hours.

- **Vine-ripened, in-season tomatoes contain more lycopene.** Lycopene biosynthesis in tomatoes is strongly reduced below 12 degrees C and halted above 32 degrees C, reflecting why vine-ripened summer tomatoes in temperate climates tend to be the richest in lycopene. Cultivar and growing conditions also significantly influence lycopene content [74].

- **Variety matters.** Choose deep-red tomato varieties over paler ones. Lycopene content correlates directly with the depth of red color in tomatoes, with some specialty cultivars bred for enhanced carotenoid content. In general, the redder the tomato, the more lycopene it contains [4].

### Regional Intake Patterns

In Western diets, tomatoes and tomato-based products account for 80-90% of total lycopene intake [2][4]. Average daily intake ranges from about 1.1 mg in the UK to 7.4 mg in Italy, with intake reaching up to 20 mg/day in populations following traditional Mediterranean diets rich in tomato sauces, soups, and salads dressed with olive oil [5][6]. Given the strong epidemiological associations between Mediterranean dietary patterns and reduced cardiovascular and cancer risk, the higher lycopene intake characteristic of these diets may contribute to their protective effects.
