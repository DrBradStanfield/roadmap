## Current evidence position

Brad has not made a video on this topic; this is a summary of the evidence, not his view. Taking echinacea may slightly reduce your chance of catching a cold, but it is still unclear whether it shortens a cold [3]. A 2014 Cochrane review of 24 trials found no clear benefit for treating colds; post hoc pooling of prevention trials suggested a relative risk reduction of 10% to 20%, of questionable clinical relevance [43]. Products differ widely in species, plant part and extraction method, so a result from one product may not apply to another [43]. Some people have allergic reactions to echinacea, which may be severe, and there are theoretical reasons it might interact with immunosuppressants or caffeine [3].

## Key safety points

- Echinacea might interact with immunosuppressants, and case reports link immune-stimulating supplements, including echinacea, to autoimmune flares [3][46]. If you take immunosuppressants or have an autoimmune disease, talk to your doctor first.
- Echinacea root slowed caffeine clearance in one small study [35]; in another, echinacea lowered S-warfarin levels without a clinically significant INR change [39]. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.
- Echinacea is related to ragweed; allergic reactions may be severe [3]. Allergic reactions, including anaphylaxis, have been reported, some after a first dose and more than half in people with known allergies [44][45].
- If hives, swelling of the face or throat, wheezing or trouble breathing start after a dose, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department [44].
- In children aged 2 to 11 years, rash occurred during 7.1% of colds treated with echinacea and 2.7% with placebo [20]. Talk to your child's doctor first [3].
- Some studies suggest use for up to 7 days in the first trimester of pregnancy is possibly safe; little is known about breastfeeding. If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, talk to your health care provider first [3].
- Rare cholestatic hepatitis cases have been reported [32][33]. The most common side effects are digestive: abdominal pain, nausea or stomach pain [3].

## 1. Overview

Echinacea (purple coneflower) covers several perennial plants closely related to sunflowers, daisies and ragweed. Supplements use *Echinacea purpurea*, *E. angustifolia* and *E. pallida* [3]. Native Americans used *E. purpurea* for respiratory tract infections, tooth pain and snakebite [3]. Today echinacea is sold mainly for the common cold and other respiratory infections, on the idea that some species may stimulate the immune system [3].

Trial results for colds have been inconsistent. A Cochrane review found no clear benefit for treating colds and possibly a weak benefit from some products for prevention [43].

One reason is that "echinacea" covers very different products. They differ in plant material, extraction method and added ingredients [43]. Growing conditions matter too: in one study, soil richer in organic matter made *E. purpurea* root extracts 4.2-fold more active at stimulating immune cells in the laboratory [5].

The National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) says that echinacea may slightly reduce your chance of catching a cold, but it is unclear whether it shortens a cold [3]. NCCIH-sponsored research also suggests that soil conditions affecting the plant's bacteria change how active the extracts are [3][5].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Active Compounds

Echinacea extracts contain many plant compounds. Laboratory work suggests their antiviral and immune effects come from several components acting together rather than from any single compound [6].

### Supplement Forms

Trials have used several forms. The largest prevention trial used liquid drops of an alcohol extract of freshly harvested *E. purpurea* (95% herb, 5% root) [8]. Other trials used tablets made from dried echinacea root [19], tablets of *E. purpurea* extract for children [17], and lozenges, sprays, tablets and drops in a trial comparing doses [25]. Results from one form do not automatically apply to another [43].

### Species Differences

- **E. purpurea:** The most studied species. More trials have tested preparations of its aerial (above-ground) parts than any other preparation [9][43].

- **E. angustifolia:** A standardized root extract has been studied for anxiety [10][11][47]. In a 12-week prevention trial, adverse effects were reported by 18 people taking *E. angustifolia* root extract, 10 taking *E. purpurea* root extract and 11 taking placebo [12].

- **E. pallida:** Its root is an ingredient in some tested combination products [22].

### Quality Considerations

Dietary supplements are not approved by the US Food and Drug Administration before they are sold, and the rules for making them are less strict than those for drugs [3]. Independent testing services such as consumerlab.com can help you check product quality.

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Common Cold: Prevention

The evidence for preventing colds is mixed. Any benefit appears small.

**Meta-analysis (2007):** A meta-analysis of 14 studies found that echinacea decreased the odds of developing a cold by 58% (OR 0.42; 95% CI 0.25 to 0.71) and shortened colds by 1.4 days (Shah et al., *Lancet Infect Dis*, 2007) [14]. Later reviews were more cautious [16][43].

**Largest prevention trial (2012):** 755 healthy people took an alcohol extract of freshly harvested *E. purpurea* (95% herb, 5% root) or placebo for 4 months (Jawad et al., *Evid Based Complement Alternat Med*, 2012) [8]. Echinacea had 149 colds and 672 cold days against 188 and 850 with placebo (about 21% fewer); only the difference in cold days was statistically significant [8]. It also reduced colds treated with painkillers and had its largest effect on recurrent infections [8]. Participants took 3 × 0.9 mL a day (2400 mg of extract) for prevention and 5 × 0.9 mL a day (4000 mg) during colds, diluting each dose in water and holding it in the mouth for 10 s [8]. The placebo drops contained the same amount of alcohol [8]. Benefits grew with better adherence, and adverse events were similar to placebo [8].

**Negative prevention trial (2008):** 90 hospital staff were randomized to 3 capsules of *E. purpurea* or placebo twice daily for 8 weeks in winter, and 58 were analysed. The echinacea group reported 9 sick days per person and the placebo group 14, a difference that was not significant (O'Neil et al., *Ann Allergy Asthma Immunol*, 2008) [15].

**E. angustifolia and E. purpurea root comparison:** In a trial of 302 volunteers, alcohol extracts of *E. angustifolia* root or *E. purpurea* root for 12 weeks did not prevent upper respiratory tract infections. Infections occurred in 36.7% of the placebo group, 32.0% of the *E. angustifolia* group and 29.3% of the *E. purpurea* group. People in the echinacea groups thought the medicine helped them more than the placebo group did (Melchart et al., *Arch Fam Med*, 1998) [12].

**Systematic review and meta-analysis (2019):** A review found a risk ratio of 0.78 (95% CI 0.68 to 0.88) for preventing upper respiratory tract infections, but whether this effect is clinically meaningful is debatable (David & Cunningham, *Complement Ther Med*, 2019) [16].

**Children — prevention:** In children aged 4 to 12 years, *E. purpurea* tablets taken three times daily lowered respiratory infection episodes compared with vitamin C (OR 0.52; 95% CI 0.30 to 0.91) and reduced antibiotic use (Ogal et al., *Eur J Med Res*, 2021) [17]. NCCIH notes that overall there is not enough evidence to support a clear connection [3], and a review of trials in children found no clear evidence that echinacea prevents respiratory symptoms [42]. NCCIH advises talking with a child's health care provider before giving echinacea [3].

**Cochrane and Bayesian network analysis:** An overview of Cochrane reviews on preventing influenza rated echinacea as probably beneficial, and said the effect needs to be confirmed with high-quality evidence (Yuan et al., *J Integr Med*, 2021) [18].

### Common Cold: Treatment (Reducing Duration and Severity)

**Large trial — no substantial benefit (2010):** 719 people aged 12 to 80 years with a new cold took echinacea tablets, placebo or no pills. The echinacea dose was the equivalent of 10.2 g of dried echinacea root in the first 24 hours and 5.1 g on each of the next 4 days. Compared with blinded placebo, echinacea gave a nonsignificant 0.53-day benefit (CI, -1.25 to 0.19 days) (Barrett et al., *Ann Intern Med*, 2010) [19].

**Children — no benefit (2003):** In 407 children aged 2 to 11 years, *E. purpurea* started at the first symptoms and taken for up to 10 days did not shorten colds or ease symptoms. Rash occurred during 7.1% of colds treated with echinacea and 2.7% of those treated with placebo (Taylor et al., *JAMA*, 2003) [20].

**Esberitox combination product:** A fixed combination of echinacea root, wild indigo (*Baptisia*) root and thuja (*Thuja occidentalis*) has been tested in colds. In 263 patients, 3 tablets three times daily for 7 to 9 days brought equal levels of improvement three days earlier than placebo, with the clearest effect in people who started early (Henneicke-von Zepelin et al., *Curr Med Res Opin*, 1999) [21]. In 91 adults, the benefit grew with dose: fewer tissues were used as the dose rose (p = 0.0259 for the trend) (Naser et al., *Phytomedicine*, 2005) [22]. Thuja oil contains thujone and has caused convulsions in poisoning cases [23].

**Esberitox in chronic bronchitis exacerbation:** In 53 adults taking an antibiotic for a flare of chronic bronchitis, adding Esberitox N for 28 days gave better lung function at day 10 than placebo, 68.7 versus 59.2 points (Hauke et al., *Chemotherapy*, 2002) [24]. There were no serious adverse events.

**Novel echinacea formulations (2023):** In 246 adults with a respiratory infection, high-dose lozenges or spray (16,800 mg/d of extract on days 1-3) were compared with standard tablets or drops at a lower daily dose of 2,400 mg. Among those with an identified respiratory virus, viral clearance by day 10 was more frequent with the high-dose forms (70 vs. 53%), but faster recovery was not significant by day 10 (Sumer et al., *Front Med*, 2023) [25]. There was no placebo group.

**Cochrane Review (2006):** A Cochrane review found some evidence that preparations based on the aerial parts of *E. purpurea* might help in the early treatment of colds in adults, but results were not fully consistent (Linde et al., *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*, 2006) [9]. Its 2014 update, with 24 trials and 4631 participants, found that only one of seven treatment trials reporting cold duration showed a significant effect (Karsch-Völk et al., *Cochrane Database Syst Rev*, 2014) [43].

**Systematic review of immune supplements (2022-2023):** A systematic review of 39 trials of immune supplements, including echinacea, found that some studies may point to benefit, but gaps prevent firm statements (Crawford et al., *Nutrients*, 2022) [26]. An expert panel then set research priorities for these supplements (Crawford et al., *J Integr Complement Med*, 2023) [27].

### Synthesis: Echinacea for Colds

The best evidence suggests echinacea, particularly *E. purpurea* extracts, may slightly reduce the chance of catching a cold when taken regularly. The effect is modest: a risk ratio of 0.78 in one meta-analysis [16], a possible 10% to 20% relative risk reduction in the Cochrane review [43], and about 21% fewer cold days in the largest trial [8]. For treating colds, the larger trials show no clear benefit [19][20][43]. Differences in species, plant part, preparation and dose likely explain much of the inconsistency [43].

NCCIH reflects this uncertainty: echinacea may slightly reduce your chance of catching a cold, but whether it shortens a cold remains unclear [3].

### Antiviral Effects (Coronavirus and Other Viruses)

**In-vitro antiviral activity:** In laboratory studies, standardized *E. purpurea* preparations show antiviral and antimicrobial activity [6].

**SARS-CoV-2 (COVID-19):** In cell cultures, an *E. purpurea* extract (Echinaforce) inactivated a common cold coronavirus and also inactivated SARS-CoV-2 at 50 μg/ml (Signer et al., *Virol J*, 2020) [28]. This was a laboratory study, not a trial in people [28].

### Anxiety

Echinacea has been proposed as an anti-anxiety remedy, but the trials disagree and are small.

**Early small studies:** In healthy volunteers with high anxiety scores, 2 tablets a day of an *E. angustifolia* extract (20 mg per tablet) for 1 week lowered anxiety scores within 3 days, while 1 tablet a day did not (Haller et al., *Phytother Res*, 2013) [47].

**Seven-day placebo-controlled trial:** In volunteers with high anxiety scores, 40 mg of a standardized *E. angustifolia* root extract twice daily for 7 days lowered state anxiety by about 11 points, against about 3 points with placebo. Changes in trait anxiety were less robust (Haller et al., *Phytother Res*, 2020) [10].

**Six-week anxiety trial:** In 108 adults with mild to moderately severe anxiety, 20 mg or 40 mg of the extract twice daily for 6 weeks did not improve anxiety more than placebo. Mood and emotional wellbeing scores improved more, but only on secondary measures (Lopresti et al., *J Affect Disord*, 2021) [11].

**Synthesis:** The evidence for echinacea as an anti-anxiety agent is insufficient. Short trials from one research group found benefit [10][47], but the largest and longest trial found anxiety fell no more than with placebo [11].

### Cervical Health

One randomized controlled trial in 153 women tested an echinacea supplement (*E. angustifolia* and *E. purpurea*) with vaginal hyaluronic acid for low-grade cervical lesions (L-SILs). At 6 and 12 months, fewer women in the combination group still had the lesions than in the echinacea-only or hyaluronic acid-only groups (Riemma et al., *Medicina (Kaunas)*, 2022) [31]. The authors note the small sample, and the finding needs replication.

### Immune Modulation — Mechanisms

Echinacea is thought to act mainly on the immune system [3][6]. Proposed mechanisms include:

- **Immunostimulation:** In laboratory studies, *E. purpurea* extracts stimulate the phagocytic activity of macrophages [6].
- **Anti-inflammatory effects:** The same extracts suppress pro-inflammatory responses of airway cells to viruses and bacteria [6].
- **Viral replication inhibition (in vitro):** Laboratory studies show activity against respiratory viruses, though the clinical relevance is uncertain [6][28].
- **Soil microbiome influence:** NCCIH-funded research found that raising soil organic matter from 5.6% to 67.4% led to a 4.2-fold increase in the immune-stimulating activity of *E. purpurea* root extracts, linked to changes in the plant's bacteria (Haron et al., *Sci Rep*, 2019) [5]. This may partly explain why products vary.

## 4. Recommended Dosing

There is no single standard dose, because products vary widely in species, plant part, extraction method and strength [43]. The doses below are those used in trials.

### By Preparation Type

| Preparation | Daily Dose in Trials | Clinical Basis |
|---|---|---|
| Alcohol extract of fresh *E. purpurea* (liquid drops) | 3 × 0.9 mL a day (2400 mg of extract) for prevention; 5 × 0.9 mL a day (4000 mg) during a cold | Jawad et al. 2012, largest prevention trial [8] |
| *E. purpurea* extract tablets, children 4 to 12 years | 400 mg three times daily | Ogal et al. 2021 [17] |
| Dried echinacea root tablets (treatment) | Equivalent of 10.2 g of dried root in the first 24 hours, then 5.1 g on each of the next 4 days; no significant benefit | Barrett et al. 2010 [19] |
| *E. angustifolia* root extract (anxiety) | 20 mg or 40 mg twice daily | Haller 2020, Lopresti 2021; modest effects at best [10][11] |
| Combination tablets (echinacea, wild indigo, thuja) | 3 tablets three times daily for 7 to 9 days | Henneicke-von Zepelin 1999 [21] |

### Timing of Use

The evidence suggests timing matters:

- **Prophylactic (preventive) use:** Prevention trials gave echinacea daily for 8 weeks to 4 months [8][12][15]. The largest positive trial ran for 4 months [8].

- **At first symptoms:** In one combination trial, the benefit was clearest in people who started at an early phase of their cold [21].

- **During established cold:** The evidence for treating a cold is weakest; the largest trial found no significant benefit [19].

### Duration of Use

Prevention trials have lasted 12 weeks [12] and 4 months [8]. NCCIH describes echinacea as likely safe for most adults for short periods of time [3].

### Administration Tips

In the largest prevention trial, each dose of liquid extract was diluted in water and held in the mouth for 10 s, a method expected to give the most local antiviral effect [8]. Whether this step adds any benefit is unclear.

### Important Caveats

- Products differ greatly in composition, so trial results apply to the product tested [43].
- Many trials used specific proprietary preparations, and results may not apply to all echinacea products.

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Safety Profile

NCCIH says it is likely safe for most adults to take products with extracts of *E. purpurea*, and some mixtures of *E. purpurea* and *E. angustifolia*, for short periods of time [3]. The most common side effects are digestive symptoms, such as abdominal pain, nausea or stomach pain [3]. In the 4-month prevention trial, side effects were no more common than with placebo [8], and a 2019 meta-analysis found a risk ratio for adverse events of 1.09 (95% CI 0.95 to 1.25) [16].

### Allergic Reactions

Some people have allergic reactions to echinacea, which may be severe [3]. Echinacea is closely related to sunflowers, daisies and ragweed [3]. In Australian reports, reactions included anaphylaxis, acute asthma and hives or swelling; some occurred after a first dose, and more than half of the people affected had known allergies (atopy) [44]. A case report described anaphylaxis in a woman with atopy and warned that people with atopy face a risk of life-threatening reactions [45]. One severe adverse event with a possible hypersensitivity reaction occurred in a trial of a high-dose echinacea spray [25]. If hives, swelling of the face or throat, wheezing or trouble breathing start after a dose, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department.

### Rash in Children

In a large trial in children, rash occurred during 7.1% of colds treated with echinacea and 2.7% of those treated with placebo [20]. NCCIH says it is possibly safe for children to take *E. purpurea* extract for short periods, but there is concern that allergic reactions could be severe in some children [3]. Talk with your child's health care provider before giving echinacea to a child [3].

### Hepatotoxicity

Case reports describe acute cholestatic hepatitis linked to echinacea, one with features of cholestatic autoimmune hepatitis (Kocaman et al., *Eur J Intern Med*, 2008; Gabranis et al., *J Postgrad Med*, 2015) [32][33]. These are rare single cases, but they warrant caution.

### Autoimmune Concerns

There are theoretical reasons to suspect that echinacea might interact with immunosuppressants [3]. In a case series, echinacea and spirulina were implicated in two patients' flares of pemphigus vulgaris, an autoimmune blistering disease; the authors proposed that immune-stimulating supplements may worsen existing autoimmune disease [46]. The evidence is limited to case reports. If you have an autoimmune disease, such as lupus, rheumatoid arthritis or multiple sclerosis, talk to your doctor before taking echinacea.

### Thuja-Containing Combination Products

Some echinacea combination products contain thuja (*Thuja occidentalis*). Commercial thuja oil, which contains thujone, has caused convulsions in poisoning cases (Millet et al., *Clin Toxicol*, 1981) [23]. In animal and laboratory studies, alpha-thujone inhibited GABA type A receptors, which may explain this (Höld et al., *Proc Natl Acad Sci U S A*, 2000) [49]. Check ingredient labels, and talk to your doctor first if you have epilepsy.

### Pregnancy and Lactation

Some studies of *E. purpurea* and *E. angustifolia* extracts suggest echinacea is possibly safe for up to 7 days during the first trimester of pregnancy [3]. A systematic review found good evidence from a prospective cohort study that echinacea taken in the first trimester did not increase the risk of major malformations, but advised caution during breastfeeding until better studies are done (Perri et al., *Can J Clin Pharmacol*, 2006) [34]. Little is known about whether echinacea is safe while breastfeeding [3]. If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, talk to your health care provider first [3].

### Children

Echinacea did not shorten colds in children aged 2 to 11 years, and rash was more common with echinacea [20]. One trial found fewer infections and less antibiotic use in children taking echinacea compared with vitamin C [17], but a review of trials in children could draw no firm conclusion [42]. NCCIH recommends talking with a child's health care provider before giving echinacea to a child [3].

## 6. Drug Interactions

### CYP1A2 Inhibition (Caffeine Interaction)

In 12 healthy people, *E. purpurea* root (400 mg 4 times a day for 8 days) reduced the oral clearance of caffeine from 6.6 to 4.9 L/h (Gorski et al., *Clin Pharmacol Ther*, 2004) [35]. The authors advised caution with drugs that depend on CYP1A2 for their elimination [35].

### CYP3A4 Metabolism

In the same study, echinacea changed how the body handled midazolam, a CYP3A drug: it changed liver and gut availability in opposite directions [35]. The authors advised caution with drugs that depend on CYP3A [35]. In a laboratory test, *E. angustifolia* root extract inhibited CYP3A4 (Budzinski et al., *Phytomedicine*, 2000) [36]. Common CYP3A4 substrates include:

- Lovastatin (Mevacor)
- Atorvastatin (Lipitor)
- Simvastatin (Zocor)
- Clarithromycin (Biaxin)
- Cyclosporine (Neoral, Sandimmune)
- Diltiazem (Cardizem)
- Estrogens
- Triazolam (Halcion)

### Warfarin

In 12 healthy men, 2 weeks of echinacea lowered blood levels of S-warfarin, but this did not lead to a clinically significant change in INR (international normalized ratio) [39]. NCCIH notes conflicting evidence about whether echinacea interacts with some drugs metabolized by the liver [3]. If you take warfarin, tell your doctor before using echinacea.

### Immunosuppressant Drugs

There are theoretical reasons to suspect that echinacea might interact with immunosuppressants [3], such as cyclosporine, tacrolimus, azathioprine and corticosteroids. If you take immunosuppressive therapy, talk to your doctor first.

### General Recommendation

NCCIH advises that anyone taking any type of medicine should talk to their health care provider before using echinacea or other herbal products, because some herbs and medicines interact in harmful ways [3].

## 7. Dietary Sources

Echinacea is not part of the food supply; it is taken as a supplement or herbal preparation. The three medicinal species are perennials native to areas east of the Rocky Mountains in the United States, and they are widely cultivated today [3].

### Combination Products

Echinacea is sometimes combined with other ingredients:

- **Thuja (*Thuja occidentalis*) and wild indigo (*Baptisia tinctoria*):** Fixed combinations of echinacea with thuja and wild indigo have improved cold symptoms in trials [21][22], and one helped lung function alongside antibiotics in chronic bronchitis [24]. Thuja oil has caused convulsions in poisoning cases [23].

- **Other ingredients:** Products also pair echinacea with goldenseal, elderberry, propolis, vitamin C or andrographis. Results from echinacea-only trials do not apply to these combinations. Goldenseal may change the way your body processes many medications. Women who are pregnant or breastfeeding should not use goldenseal, and it should not be given to infants [48].
