## 1. Overview

Glycine is the smallest and simplest amino acid found in the human body [1]. Unlike all other standard amino acids, glycine is the only achiral proteinogenic amino acid — it has no stereogenic center, meaning there are no L- or D- forms. This simplicity belies its remarkable biological importance: glycine plays critical roles in neurotransmission, immune regulation, collagen synthesis, detoxification, and antioxidant defense [1].

The liver and kidneys produce approximately 3 grams of glycine per day from precursors including the amino acids serine and threonine [1].

Since the body can produce it, glycine is classified as a non-essential amino acid. However, some research suggests it should be considered conditionally essential, as endogenous production may not meet the body's demands for collagen synthesis, glutathione production, creatine synthesis, bile acid conjugation, and other metabolic processes [1][4][51]. One analysis estimated that the body makes about 3 g a day and the diet supplies 1.5 to 3.0 g a day, which may fall short of the amount needed for all metabolic uses, including collagen synthesis, by about 10 g a day for a 70 kg adult [4]. This deficit may become clinically relevant in older adults, athletes, pregnant women, and individuals with certain medical conditions where glycine demands are increased [1].

Glycine acts as both an inhibitory neurotransmitter and an excitatory co-agonist in the central nervous system. As an inhibitory neurotransmitter, it helps control motor and sensory functions such as movement, hearing, and vision [1][7]. In the brain, glycine is also a co-agonist of the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor, meaning it helps activate this receptor [1]. This effect on the NMDA receptor has been proposed as the mechanism behind the improvements in both sleep and schizophrenia symptoms seen with supplementation [1].

Glycine is also the main component of collagen, the most abundant protein in the body [1]. It helps regulate immune function, and the body uses it to make other compounds, such as glutathione, a major antioxidant [1]. A review lists glycine as a building block for glutathione, heme, creatine, nucleic acids and uric acid, and as a part of the bile acids that help digest fat [51].

Some experts recommend a daily intake of approximately 1.5 to 3 grams of glycine per day to maintain blood levels between 200–300 μmol/L [1]. This can be met through a protein-rich diet containing fish, chicken, legumes, and seeds. ConsumerLab's view is that unless you need glycine for a specific condition, such as trouble sleeping or overactive bladder, glycine supplements are not necessary for most people [1].

Several small clinical trials, generally supported by Ajinomoto (a major glycine manufacturer), have evaluated glycine supplementation for improving sleep quality, reducing nighttime urinary frequency, and improving symptoms in schizophrenia.

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Supplemental Forms

Glycine supplements are available in several delivery formats:

| Form | Typical Dose per Unit | Notes |
|------|:---:|------|
| Powder (free-form) | 1–3 g per serving | Most cost-effective format. Mildly sweet taste. Dissolves well in water, juice, coffee, or tea. Stable at temperatures above the boiling point of water [1][9]. |
| Capsules (free-form) | 500–1,000 mg per capsule | Convenient but requires multiple capsules to reach clinical doses (3 g = 3–6 capsules). |
| Magnesium Glycinate (Bisglycinate) | Varies by product | Chelated form delivering both magnesium and glycine. Each gram of magnesium glycinate provides approximately 140 mg elemental magnesium plus glycine as the chelating amino acid [10]. |
| Collagen Peptides | Varies | Glycine is the main component of collagen, so collagen supplements provide glycine in peptide-bound form [1]. |
| GlyNAC (Glycine + N-Acetylcysteine) | Varies | Combination supplement providing both glycine and NAC. In a small pilot trial in older adults, it corrected glutathione deficiency [11]. |

### Key Purchasing Considerations

As long as glycine is listed as the ingredient in a supplement, it is in the free form regardless of whether "free form" is indicated on the label [1]. Unlike most amino acids, glycine has no L- or D- chemical form due to its achiral structure, so there is no need to look for a specific stereoisomer or to pay a premium for products claiming to be "biologically active" or "free form" [1].

### Stability and Storage

Glycine is chemically stable, including at temperatures above the boiling point of water, making it suitable for mixing into hot beverages such as coffee and tea [9]. However, decomposition can be accelerated by moisture and heat during long-term storage. Store glycine in a cool, dry place. Do not discard desiccant packs from containers; leave them in to protect glycine from moisture. Refrigeration is not necessary [1].

### Calorie Content

Glycine contains approximately 4 calories per gram, equivalent to table sugar. However, due to a regulatory loophole in calorie labeling, the calorie content may not be listed on supplement labels. The FDA permits manufacturers to calculate calorie content based only on protein, carbohydrate, and fat content — but glycine, as a single amino acid, is classified as none of these [1][12].

### Bioavailability

Free-form glycine is rapidly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract. As the simplest amino acid, it does not require enzymatic digestion before absorption and is transported across intestinal epithelial cells via active amino acid transport systems.

When glycine is chelated to minerals (as in magnesium glycinate), the glycine component is released during digestion and contributes to the body's glycine pool. A standard dose of magnesium glycinate providing 200 mg of elemental magnesium delivers approximately 1.4 g of glycine as well [10].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Sleep and Fatigue

Glycine's effects on sleep are among the best-studied benefits of supplementation. The proposed mechanism involves glycine acting as a co-agonist at NMDA receptors in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), an area in the brain that regulates circadian rhythm. Animal research suggests that by modulating NMDA receptor activity in the SCN, glycine lowers core body temperature through peripheral vasodilation and modulates circadian rhythm, thereby improving sleep quality [13][14].

**Clinical trial 1 — Yamadera et al. (2007):** A study among 11 men and women with self-reported "unsatisfactory sleep" found that 3 grams of glycine granules taken one hour before bedtime significantly improved self-reported sleep quality and shortened the time it took to fall asleep (as measured by polysomnography — brain wave, heart rate, and breathing monitors) compared to placebo. Glycine reduced sleep onset latency and facilitated faster entry into deep (slow-wave) sleep [15].

**Clinical trial 2 — Inagawa et al. (2006):** A study conducted among 15 Ajinomoto employees who reported dissatisfaction with their sleep found that 3 grams of glycine taken one hour before bedtime improved self-reported sleep quality compared to placebo. Participants reported feeling more refreshed upon waking and experienced less daytime fatigue [16].

**Clinical trial 3 — Bannai et al. (2012):** A study among 10 Ajinomoto employees found that 3 grams of glycine taken before bedtime improved subjective sleep quality and decreased next-day fatigue. The study also evaluated cognitive performance and found that glycine improved reaction times and reduced errors on psychomotor vigilance tasks the following day, suggesting improvements in daytime function attributable to better sleep quality [17]. No adverse events were reported in any of these three trials.

**Mechanism — core body temperature:** Kawai et al. (2015) demonstrated in animal models that glycine's sleep-promoting and hypothermic effects are mediated by NMDA receptors in the suprachiasmatic nucleus [13]. The hypothermic response — a lowering of core body temperature — is a critical physiological signal for sleep initiation. This mechanism is distinct from that of sedative drugs, which suppress neural activity rather than facilitating the body's natural thermoregulatory sleep signals. This means glycine promotes sleep through a natural physiological pathway rather than through pharmacological sedation, which is consistent with the clinical finding that glycine does not cause next-morning grogginess [13][14].

All three clinical trials were small (10–15 participants) and short-term; two were conducted among Ajinomoto employees, and the research was generally supported by Ajinomoto [1]. While the consistent direction of results across the three trials is encouraging, larger independent trials are needed to strengthen the evidence base.

### Overactive Bladder and Chronic Prostatitis

Sugaya et al. (2021) conducted a study in Japan among 16 men and 4 women (average age 67) with mild to moderate overactive bladder, chronic prostatitis, and/or bladder pain syndrome/interstitial cystitis [1]. Participants received 3 grams of glycine taken twice daily (6 g/day total) for one month.

The published report describes all participants taking a glucose placebo for the first four weeks and then glycine for the next four weeks, rather than random assignment [18].

Results:
- Glycine modestly reduced nighttime urinary urgency and frequency (but not daytime symptoms)
- Overall overactive bladder symptom scores improved by approximately 1 point on a 15-point scale compared to placebo
- Self-reported "vitality" improved compared to placebo
- Time to fall asleep was slightly reduced and bladder pain was modestly decreased compared to baseline, but these improvements were NOT statistically significant compared to placebo
- Systolic and diastolic blood pressure modestly decreased compared to baseline, but again not statistically significant versus placebo

Despite these possible benefits, the American Urological Society has stated that there is insufficient evidence to support the use of any nutraceutical, vitamin, or supplement to treat the symptoms of overactive bladder [1][19]. The study was small (n=20) and short (1 month), limiting the strength of conclusions that can be drawn.

### Schizophrenia

Research suggests that glycine may benefit some people with schizophrenia by enhancing NMDA receptor function. The NMDA receptor hypofunction hypothesis of schizophrenia proposes that reduced NMDA receptor signaling contributes to the negative symptoms of the disease (emotional withdrawal, reduced motivation, social withdrawal) [20][22].

**Heresco-Levy et al. (2004):** A study among 17 people (average age 45) with schizophrenia being treated with the antipsychotic medications olanzapine or risperidone found that treatment with a daily dose of 0.8 g/kg body weight of glycine (between 40–80 grams per day) for 6 weeks, in addition to regular antipsychotic medication, achieved notable results [1]:
- Negative symptoms reduced by 23% compared to baseline
- 57% of those given glycine plus medication showed greater than 20% improvement in negative symptoms during glycine treatment versus none during placebo plus medication treatment
- Moderate improvements in depression and excitement symptoms were also observed

The published trial reported that glycine was well tolerated, and its authors suggested that olanzapine and risperidone may act on NMDA receptors differently from clozapine [21].

**Heresco-Levy et al. (1999):** An earlier study among 22 people with treatment-resistant schizophrenia found that supplementing with very high doses of glycine (starting at 4 grams daily and increased to 40–80 grams daily over approximately 2 weeks) for a total of 6 weeks slightly improved depression symptoms compared to placebo [1].

The published trial reported a 30% reduction in negative symptoms [22].

A separate study in 12 people found a 34% reduction in negative symptoms, without significant changes in serum antipsychotic levels [8].

**Clozapine interaction — Potkin et al. (1999):** One important study found that glycine may interfere with the efficacy of the atypical antipsychotic clozapine (Clozaril) [23]. This finding means that while glycine may be a useful adjunct to some antipsychotic medications (olanzapine, risperidone), it should NOT be combined with clozapine, as it may worsen outcomes.

**Summary:** High-dose glycine (0.8 g/kg/day, typically 40–80 g/day) may modestly reduce negative symptoms of schizophrenia when used as an adjunct to non-clozapine antipsychotics. The doses required are very large and may cause gastrointestinal side effects. This application should only be pursued under specialist psychiatric supervision.

### Depression

There is speculation that glycine may be beneficial for depression, although this has not been proven in dedicated clinical trials.

**Observational evidence:** Altamura et al. (1995) reported that people with major depressive disorder have lower blood levels of glycine compared to healthy controls [24]. Plasma glycine levels are also lower in people with metabolic syndrome than in healthy people [46].

**Preclinical evidence:** Test-tube and animal studies have demonstrated mechanisms by which glycine could influence mood [1][26]:
- Laboute et al. (2023) reported in *Science* that the receptor GPR158 responds to glycine and regulates the excitability of brain cells, with possible implications for mood and thinking [26]

**Clinical data:** The only clinical evidence for glycine's antidepressant effects comes from the Heresco-Levy et al. (1999) schizophrenia study, which found that very high-dose glycine (40–80 g/day) slightly improved depression symptoms in people with treatment-resistant schizophrenia compared to placebo [1]. There do not appear to be any studies evaluating glycine supplementation in people without schizophrenia who have depression.

**Conclusion:** The mechanistic rationale exists, and the observational association between low glycine levels and depression is consistent, but there is insufficient interventional evidence to recommend glycine supplementation specifically for depression.

### Glutathione and Antioxidant Support

Glycine is needed to make glutathione (GSH), a major antioxidant in the body [1].

**GlyNAC supplementation:** GlyNAC combines glycine with N-acetylcysteine (NAC). In an open-label pilot trial, 8 older adults took GlyNAC for 24 weeks. It corrected red blood cell glutathione deficiency, oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, and improved inflammation, insulin resistance, strength, gait speed and cognition; the benefits declined after 12 weeks off GlyNAC [11]. Research by Kumar et al. at Baylor College of Medicine has shown that GlyNAC supplementation in older adults and in people with HIV corrects age-related glutathione deficits and improves markers of oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, and inflammation.

**Human aging data:** Human evidence for longevity effects is preliminary, deriving mainly from GlyNAC studies that improve GSH and mitochondrial function without yet demonstrating direct longevity outcomes [11].

### Anti-inflammatory Effects

Glycine helps regulate immune function [1]. A review reported that glycine dampens the activity of several white blood cells, including macrophages, neutrophils and lymphocytes, and that dietary glycine protected rats against endotoxaemia and liver injury after interrupted blood flow (ischaemia-reperfusion) [50]. There is limited human clinical evidence for direct anti-inflammatory benefits of glycine supplementation.

### Cardiovascular Health

**Blood pressure:** The Sugaya et al. (2021) overactive bladder trial observed modest reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure with glycine supplementation (6 g/day), though these did not reach statistical significance compared to placebo [1].

**Overall assessment:** The cardiovascular evidence for glycine supplementation is largely preclinical. Blood pressure reduction has been observed but not confirmed in placebo-controlled trials. The strongest cardiovascular rationale may be glycine's role in glutathione synthesis and anti-inflammatory signaling.

### Blood Sugar and Insulin Sensitivity

**Insulin stimulation:** In a small randomised, placebo-controlled trial in 12 healthy first-degree relatives of people with type 2 diabetes, a morning dose of 5 g of glycine increased insulin responses without changing insulin action [44].

**Glucose response:** In a small study in healthy people, taking glycine with glucose cut the blood glucose response by more than 50% compared with glucose alone [45].

**Metabolic syndrome:** A review found that plasma glycine levels are lower in people with metabolic syndrome than in healthy people [46].

**Overall assessment:** Long-term supplementation trials for glucose metabolism are insufficient to support specific recommendations.

### Collagen Synthesis and Joint Health

Glycine is the main component of collagen, the most abundant protein in the body [1].

**Conditional deficiency argument:** Meléndez-Hevia et al. (2009) argued through metabolic flux analysis that the amount of glycine synthesized endogenously is insufficient to meet the body's needs for collagen turnover, glutathione production, and other demands — supporting the concept that glycine is conditionally essential [4]. It estimated that synthesis supplies about 3 g a day and the diet 1.5 to 3.0 g a day, which may fall short of total needs by about 10 g a day for a 70 kg adult [4].

**Practical implications:** Direct human clinical trials of glycine supplementation for joint health are limited.

### Gut Health

This review found no reliable human evidence to cite on glycine and gut health.

### Exercise Performance and Recovery

A 2024 literature review reports some evidence that glycine supplementation may enhance peak power output, reduce lactic acid build-up during high-intensity exercise, and improve sleep quality and recovery [28]. It concluded that more randomized controlled trials in humans are needed before glycine can be recommended for athletic performance.

### Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

Glycine has been investigated as a treatment for OCD because it is an NMDA receptor co-agonist [30].

**Greenberg et al. (2009):** A small double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (n=24) of adjunctive glycine (up to 60 g/day) in adults with stabilized OCD found statistically significant reductions in Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) scores over at least 12 weeks among completers [29]. However, the study was limited by high dropout rates primarily due to poor palatability and nausea at the very high doses required.

**Case report:** A long-term open-label case report described high-dose glycine treatment administered over more than 5 years resulting in robust and sustained reduction of symptoms in a patient with refractory OCD and comorbid body dysmorphic disorder (BDD), with only partial relapses during brief interruptions [30].

These findings expand on glycine's psychiatric applications beyond schizophrenia, though evidence remains preliminary and requires confirmation in larger, better-tolerated controlled trials.

### Tinnitus

There do not appear to be any published clinical trials investigating the effects of glycine supplementation in people with tinnitus (ringing in the ear), although such use has been promoted based on glycine's inhibitory or "calming" effect on nerve activity [1][31]. Glycine's role as an inhibitory neurotransmitter in auditory pathways provides theoretical rationale, but no clinical evidence supports this use.

### Aging and Longevity

**Animal models:** In the Interventions Testing Program, funded by the U.S. National Institute on Aging, glycine supplementation led to a small but statistically significant lifespan increase in mice of about 4–6% on average across three independent testing sites [42][48]. A small earlier study had suggested that glycine could extend lifespan in rats [48]. In a mouse study, mice given GlyNAC lived 24% longer than control mice [47]. In a lab study, treating skin cells (fibroblasts) from elderly people with glycine prevented age-related defects in mitochondrial respiration [49]. Whether these findings apply to humans is unknown.

**Human evidence:** Human evidence is preliminary, deriving mainly from GlyNAC combination studies that improve glutathione levels, mitochondrial function, and oxidative stress markers without yet demonstrating direct longevity outcomes [11]. Larger, longer-duration human trials are needed before making anti-aging recommendations.

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Dosing by Indication

| Indication | Dose used in studies | Timing | Evidence Level |
|-----------|:---:|--------|:-:|
| Sleep quality improvement | 3 g/day | 1 hour before bedtime | 3 small RCTs |
| Total daily intake (food plus any supplement) | 1.5–3 g/day | Usually met by a protein-rich diet | Expert recommendation |
| Overactive bladder symptoms | 3 g twice daily (6 g total) | Morning and evening | 1 small RCT |
| GlyNAC (glycine + NAC) for oxidative stress | Glycine component varies by product | As directed | Emerging clinical evidence |
| Schizophrenia adjunct (specialist use only) | 0.8 g/kg/day (40–80 g/day) | Divided doses | Small RCTs; specialist supervision required |

### Practical Dosing Notes

- **Most studied dose for sleep:** 3 grams taken approximately 1 hour before bedtime. This dose has been consistent across all three sleep trials [15][16][17].
- **Powder versus capsules:** At a dose of 3 g/day, powder is more practical than capsules (3 g = 3–6 capsules depending on capsule size). Glycine powder has a mildly sweet taste and dissolves easily in water, juice, or hot beverages.
- **Duration:** Sleep benefits have been observed within the first few nights in clinical trials. Doses used in clinical studies typically range from 3 to 15 g per day, split into 2 to 4 servings, but have been as high as 80 g per day [1].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Tolerability

Glycine supplements are generally well-tolerated and are generally considered safe, even at high doses [1]. In a schizophrenia trial that added 0.8 g/kg body weight of glycine a day for 6 weeks, glycine was well tolerated [22].

### Reported Side Effects

Side effects are rare at typical supplemental doses. When they occur, they may include [1]:

- **Gastrointestinal effects:** Stomach pain, nausea, diarrhea — reported rarely at standard doses, more common at high doses
- **Nausea and vomiting:** When taken in very high doses (over 40 g/day), glycine may cause nausea and vomiting in some people [1]
- **Drowsiness:** Occasionally noted at higher doses, consistent with glycine's mechanism of action on sleep
- **Headache:** Rarely reported
- **Irritability and insomnia:** Paradoxical effects reported rarely, possibly related to NMDA receptor excitatory co-agonist effects
- **Optical migraines:** One ConsumerLab member reported several optical migraines after supplementing with glycine powder (¼ to ¾ teaspoon daily for three weeks), which resolved after stopping supplementation [1]. There are no published reports of glycine supplementation triggering migraines. Elevated glycine concentrations have been reported in blood cells and saliva of individuals with migraine with and without aura [37][38], although this does not prove causation.

### Glycine Does NOT Cause Morning Grogginess

Unlike high-dose melatonin supplements, glycine does not cause next-morning grogginess. Clinical studies consistently report that glycine improves next-day alertness and reduces daytime fatigue rather than causing sedation [15][16][17]. This may be because glycine appears to promote sleep through thermoregulatory mechanisms (core body temperature reduction, shown in rats) rather than through pharmacological sedation [13][14].

### FDA Classification Note

Interestingly, based on evidence of adverse effects in animals upon exposure to high levels of glycine, glycine is not considered generally recognized as safe (GRAS) for use as a food additive in human food. It may only be used in very small amounts for certain technical effects, including masking the bitter aftertaste of saccharin in beverages (not to exceed 0.2% in the finished beverage) [12][39]. Note: details of the adverse animal effects to which the FDA refers are difficult to find in the literature. While not considered suitable as a food additive, glycine can be legally sold as a dietary supplement [12].

### Special Populations

- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** Glycine supplement use has not been studied in pregnant or breastfeeding women. If pregnant or breastfeeding, avoid glycine supplements unless specifically recommended and monitored by a healthcare provider [1].
- **Children:** Safety data in pediatric populations are insufficient. Glycine supplements are not recommended for children.
- **Non-ketotic hyperglycinaemia (NKH):** In this rare inherited disorder, large amounts of glycine build up in all body tissues, including the brain, and treatment aims to lower plasma glycine [43]. Supplements add glycine, so anyone with NKH should check with their specialist before taking one.
- **Epilepsy:** If you have epilepsy, check with your doctor before taking glycine.

### Laxative Effect Comparison

Diarrhea has been reported rarely with glycine supplements [1].

## 6. Drug Interactions

Glycine may interact with certain medications. Known and theoretical interactions include:

| Drug/Class | Interaction | Clinical Significance |
|-----------|-----------|:-:|
| **Clozapine (Clozaril)** | High-dose glycine may reduce clozapine's antipsychotic efficacy | HIGH — Contraindicated combination [23] |
| **Other atypical antipsychotics** (olanzapine, risperidone) | High-dose glycine may enhance therapeutic effect on negative symptoms | MODERATE — Specialist supervision required [21] |
| **Anticonvulsants** | Glycine may interact with anticonvulsant medications | MODERATE — Consult prescriber [1] |
| **Blood pressure medications** | Glycine may lower blood pressure, potentially adding to antihypertensive effects | LOW-MODERATE — Monitor blood pressure [1] |
| **Sedatives and sleep medications** | Theoretical additive effect due to glycine's sleep-promoting properties | LOW — Monitor for excessive sedation |

### Key Interaction Details

**Clozapine:** The interaction between glycine and clozapine is the most clinically significant. Potkin et al. (1999) found that high-dose glycine appeared to interfere with clozapine's efficacy in treating schizophrenia [23]. While glycine enhances NMDA receptor function (which may benefit negative symptoms), clozapine already has glycine-site NMDA receptor activity, and the combination may paradoxically reduce therapeutic benefit. This combination should be avoided.

**Blood pressure medications:** Glycine has demonstrated hypotensive effects in some populations [1]. If you are taking blood pressure medication, consult your healthcare provider before taking a glycine supplement to avoid excessive blood pressure reduction.

## 7. Dietary Sources

Glycine is concentrated in protein-rich foods, particularly those containing collagen (connective tissue, skin, bone broth). If you eat a well-rounded, protein-rich diet, you will likely get at least 1.5 to 3 grams of glycine per day from food [1][41].
