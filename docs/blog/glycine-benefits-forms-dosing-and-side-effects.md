## Current view (reviewed 12 November 2026)

In three small trials, 3 g of glycine before bed improved how people rated their sleep, helped them fall asleep faster, and reduced next-day tiredness or sleepiness (Brad's current guidance, 3 Oct 2026). In one of these trials, overnight sleep recordings showed people reached slow-wave sleep sooner with no change in sleep architecture, but no trial reported fewer night-time wake-ups (Brad's current guidance, 3 Oct 2026). For Brad, glycine is about sleep, and he calls the effect real, though modest ([Brad, 20 Aug 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/this-amino-acid-was-accidentally-found-to-improve-our-sleep)). He considers glycine generally safe at typical doses of about 3 g a day, though his taking a supplement does not mean you should ([Brad, 27 May 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/doctor-reveals-why-he-takes-glycine-every-day)). He sees GlyNAC, glycine combined with N-acetylcysteine, as a smarter kind of support than a finished antioxidant, because it gives the body the raw materials to make its own, and in older adults it improved a range of aging markers ([Brad, 8 Jul 2026](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/more-vitamins-worse-health-new-trial)). He plans to add 1 g of GlyNAC at age 45, because glutathione declines rapidly after 45, citing a placebo-controlled trial in which GlyNAC corrected glutathione deficiency in older adults and improved hand grip strength (Brad's current guidance, 10 Aug 2026).

## Key safety points

- Do not combine glycine with clozapine. In a small trial, glycine added to clozapine gave no benefit, and the authors suggested it may interfere with clozapine's effect [23]; a second trial found no benefit and no worsening [54].
- High-dose glycine alongside olanzapine or risperidone for schizophrenia is a specialist treatment, to be used only under psychiatric supervision [21].
- If you take blood pressure medicine, check with your doctor first: blood pressure fell while people took glycine in two small studies [18][63].
- If you take an anti-seizure medicine, check with your doctor or pharmacist first. In rats, glycine strengthened the effect of diazepam and valproate [70].
- Anyone with non-ketotic hyperglycinaemia, a rare inherited disorder in which glycine builds up in the body, should ask their specialist first [43].
- Late pregnancy raises the body's need for glycine [71], but no trial has tested glycine supplements in pregnancy: if you are pregnant or breastfeeding, ask your doctor first.
- Very high doses are hard to take. In an OCD trial of glycine titrated to 60 g a day, many participants stopped, mainly because of the taste or nausea [29].
- At the 3 g sleep dose, people were less sleepy the next day in one trial [15], and in another they were less tired and tended to be less sleepy [17]; neither trial found more next-day sleepiness.

## 1. Overview

Glycine is the simplest amino acid, and a major one in humans and other mammals [46][51][52]. It is the only standard amino acid with no L- or D- form. The body uses it to make glutathione, creatine, heme and other compounds, and it has roles in antioxidant defence and nerve signalling [51].

Glycine is made in the body mainly in the liver and kidneys, from serine, threonine and other precursors [51].

Because the body can make it, glycine is classed as non-essential [52]. However, a 2009 analysis and a 2013 review both argue that it is conditionally essential, because the body may not make enough for collagen, glutathione, creatine, bile acids and its other uses [4][51]. One analysis estimated that the body makes about 3 g a day and the diet supplies 1.5 to 3.0 g a day, which may fall short of the amount needed for all metabolic uses, including collagen synthesis, by about 10 g a day for a 70 kg adult [4].

In the nervous system, glycine is an obligatory co-agonist at the N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor, a glutamate receptor: the receptor needs glycine to work [22]. This NMDA action has been proposed to explain glycine's effects on sleep [13] and on schizophrenia symptoms [22].

The body also needs glycine to make collagen [4]. A review lists glycine as a building block for glutathione, heme, creatine, nucleic acids and uric acid, and as a part of the bile acids that help digest fat [51].

Brad takes glycine for one aim, sleep, and says you do not need to take any supplements unless you want to ([Brad, 11 Sep 2025](https://drstanfield.com/blogs/articles/the-diet-my-patients-use-to-crush-visceral-fat)).

Most human trials of glycine are small. Researchers at Ajinomoto, a glycine maker, ran or co-wrote at least two of the three sleep trials [15][17]; other trials tested glycine for bladder symptoms, schizophrenia and metabolic health.

### Key Purchasing Considerations

Every glycine supplement supplies the same molecule, because glycine has no L- or D- version (see Overview), so label wording about its form does not matter. Choose a product that an independent lab has tested.

### Stability and Storage

There is no good public evidence on this yet. Follow the storage advice on the label.

### Calorie Content

Like other amino acids, glycine supplies some energy, but a few grams a day adds few calories.

### Bioavailability

Glycine supplements raise blood glycine levels, with some evidence that larger doses raise them more [69]. At very high doses, oral glycine also raises glycine in the brain [76].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Sleep and Fatigue

Glycine's effects on sleep are among the most-studied uses of supplementation. The proposed mechanism centres on the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), the brain's body clock. Rat studies suggest that glycine acts on NMDA receptors in the SCN, widens blood vessels in the skin and lowers core body temperature, which may help sleep begin [13][14].

**Clinical trial 1, Yamadera et al. (2007):** In volunteers who had been sleeping poorly for some time, 3 g of glycine before bed improved how they rated their sleep and their sleep efficiency. Overnight recordings of brain waves, heart rate and breathing (polysomnography) showed they fell asleep faster and reached deep (slow-wave) sleep sooner, with no change in sleep architecture. They were less sleepy the next day and did better on memory recognition tasks [15].

**Clinical trial 2, Inagawa et al. (2006):** In a small double-blind trial in people who reported trouble sleeping, glycine before bed improved next-morning ratings of liveliness and clear-headedness and reduced fatigue, compared with placebo [16][42]. No public abstract exists, so the number of participants cannot be checked here.

**Clinical trial 3, Bannai et al. (2012):** In healthy volunteers whose sleep was cut by 25% for three nights, 3 g of glycine before bed reduced next-day fatigue and tended to reduce sleepiness compared with placebo. They also did better on a psychomotor vigilance test the next day [17].

**Mechanism, core body temperature:** In rats, the sleep-promoting and temperature-lowering effects of glycine disappeared when the SCN was removed [13]. Oral glycine raised glycine in the blood and spinal fluid of rats and lowered core body temperature, which the authors suggest may be how it helps sleep [14]. Glycine did not change melatonin levels or clock genes in rats [17]. The Yamadera authors concluded that glycine seems to improve sleep in a different way from traditional sleeping pills such as benzodiazepines [15].

**What reviews say:** A 2026 scoping review found only 3 glycine sleep trials; all 3 reported a benefit on at least one sleep outcome [69]. A 2024 systematic review found that glycine improved sleep in healthy people, but the studies were small and at high risk of bias [68].

All three clinical trials were small and short-term, and researchers at Ajinomoto, which makes glycine, ran or co-wrote at least two of them [15][17]. While the consistent direction of results across the three trials is encouraging, larger independent trials are needed to strengthen the evidence base.

The trials enrolled people who slept poorly or whose sleep was cut short, not people diagnosed with chronic insomnia [15][17]. For chronic insomnia, Brad's order is sleep habits first, then cognitive behavioural therapy for insomnia (CBT-I), then a supplement trial (Brad's current guidance, 26 Oct 2026).

### Schizophrenia

Small trials suggest glycine may help some people with schizophrenia, possibly by boosting NMDA receptor signalling, but the largest trial found no benefit. The NMDA receptor hypofunction hypothesis of schizophrenia proposes that reduced NMDA receptor signalling contributes to the negative symptoms of the disease (emotional withdrawal, reduced motivation, social withdrawal) [20][22]. In 144 medication-free people with schizophrenia, plasma glycine was lower than in healthy controls [20].

**Heresco-Levy et al. (2004):** In a double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial in 17 people with schizophrenia taking olanzapine or risperidone, adding 0.8 g/kg of glycine a day for 6 weeks gave these results [21]:
- Negative symptoms fell significantly, by 23% [21]
- Cognitive and positive symptoms also improved significantly [21]

The published trial reported that glycine was well tolerated, and its authors suggested that olanzapine and risperidone may act on NMDA receptors differently from clozapine [21].

**Heresco-Levy et al. (1999):** An earlier double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover trial in 22 treatment-resistant patients added 0.8 g/kg of glycine a day to their medicine for 6 weeks [22].

The published trial reported a 30% reduction in negative symptoms [22].

In a 1996 crossover trial, 11 treatment-resistant patients completed 6 weeks of 0.8 g/kg a day; negative symptoms fell by 36%, and depressive and cognitive symptoms also improved [57]. A separate study in 12 people found a 34% reduction in negative symptoms, without significant changes in serum antipsychotic levels [8].

**CONSIST (2007):** The largest trial randomised 157 people with schizophrenia and moderate to severe negative symptoms to glycine, D-cycloserine or placebo, added to their medicine for 16 weeks. Glycine was no better than placebo for negative symptoms or for thinking skills, and the authors concluded that glycine is not a generally effective treatment for either [53].

**Meta-analyses:** A 2010 meta-analysis of about 800 people in 26 studies found that glycine, D-serine and sarcosine improved several symptom groups; people taking risperidone or olanzapine improved, but people taking clozapine did not [55]. A 2021 meta-analysis of 40 trials in 4,937 people ranked glycine among the better add-ons, effective only with antipsychotics other than clozapine [56].

**People at risk of psychosis:** In two small pilot studies (10 people open-label, 8 randomised), glycine alone at up to 0.8 g/kg a day was linked to fewer symptoms, with no safety concerns [58].

**Clozapine interaction, Potkin et al. (1999):** In a double-blind trial in 19 people on clozapine, 30 g of glycine a day for 12 weeks did not help, while the placebo group had a 35% fall in positive symptoms; the authors suggested glycine may interfere with clozapine's antipsychotic effect [23]. In a second trial in 30 adults on clozapine, 60 g a day for 8 weeks gave no benefit, and no one got clinically worse [54]. Glycine may help as an add-on to some antipsychotic medications (olanzapine, risperidone), but it should not be combined with clozapine, because it may blunt clozapine's effect [23]. One caveat: in the separate study of 12 people above, negative symptoms still fell with glycine although most participants took clozapine or olanzapine [8].

**Summary:** High-dose glycine (0.8 g/kg/day) may modestly reduce negative symptoms of schizophrenia when used as an adjunct to non-clozapine antipsychotics, but the largest trial found no benefit [53]. The doses are very large, and at 60 g a day taste and nausea made many people stop in an OCD trial [29]. This application should only be pursued under specialist psychiatric supervision.

### Depression

Glycine has been proposed as a possible help for depression, but this remains unproven.

**Observational evidence:** Altamura et al. (1995) found significantly lower plasma glycine in 25 unmedicated people with major depression than in 22 healthy controls [24]. Sumiyoshi et al. (2004) found that in 44 medication-free people with major depression, plasma glycine was not different from that of healthy controls, although the ratio of glycine to serine was lower [20]. The two studies disagree.

**Preclinical evidence:** Laboratory studies have found mechanisms by which glycine could influence mood [26]:
- Laboute et al. (2023) reported in *Science* that the receptor GPR158 responds to glycine and regulates the excitability of brain cells, with possible implications for mood and thinking [26]

**Clinical data:** The trial evidence is indirect. In the Heresco-Levy et al. (1999) schizophrenia trial, the improvement in negative symptoms was unrelated to changes in depression symptoms [22]. In the 1996 trial, depressive symptoms improved along with negative symptoms [57].

**Conclusion:** There is a mechanistic rationale, but blood-level studies in depression disagree, and this review found no trial of glycine for depression alone, so there is not enough evidence to recommend glycine supplementation for depression.

### Glutathione and Antioxidant Support

The body uses glycine to make glutathione (GSH), an important antioxidant [51]. Glutathione redox balance in the blood holds steady until about age 45 and then declines rapidly, in a study of 122 healthy people aged 19 to 85 [62].

**GlyNAC supplementation:** GlyNAC combines glycine with N-acetylcysteine (NAC). In an open-label pilot trial, 8 older adults took GlyNAC for 24 weeks. It corrected red blood cell glutathione deficiency, oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, and improved inflammation, insulin resistance, strength, gait speed and cognition; the benefits declined after 12 weeks off GlyNAC [11]. An earlier study in 8 older adults found that 2 weeks of cysteine and glycine raised glutathione by 94.6% and lowered oxidative stress [61]. The same Baylor group reported similar improvements in an open-label trial in 8 people with HIV, which faded after stopping [73].

**Randomised trial, Kumar et al. (2023):** 24 older adults were randomised to GlyNAC or an alanine placebo for 16 weeks, and 12 young adults took GlyNAC for 2 weeks. Compared with the young adults, the older adults started with glutathione deficiency, oxidative stress, poorer physical function and other defects; GlyNAC, and not placebo, improved or corrected these in the older adults, and it was safe and well tolerated [59]. The abstract does not report results for the young adults, who had no placebo group.

**A larger null trial, Lizzo et al. (2022):** In 114 healthy older adults (average age 65), GlyNAC at 2.4, 4.8 or 7.2 g a day for 2 weeks was safe and well tolerated but did not raise glutathione, the trial's main measure [60]. A later analysis found a rise only in a subgroup with high oxidative stress and low glutathione at the start [60]. Most authors worked for Nestlé, which markets glycine and NAC products [60].

**Human aging data:** Human evidence on lifespan is preliminary, deriving mainly from GlyNAC studies that improve GSH and mitochondrial function without yet showing any effect on lifespan [11][59].

### Anti-inflammatory Effects

Glycine may help regulate immune function [50]. A review reported that glycine dampens the activity of several white blood cells, including macrophages, neutrophils and lymphocytes, and that dietary glycine protected rats against endotoxaemia and liver injury after interrupted blood flow (ischaemia-reperfusion) [50]. In 74 people with type 2 diabetes, 5 g of glycine three times a day (15 g a day in all) for 3 months lowered an inflammatory marker (TNF receptor I) compared with placebo [64][68]. There is limited human clinical evidence for direct anti-inflammatory benefits of glycine supplementation.

### Cardiovascular Health

**Blood pressure:** In a small study of glycine for bladder symptoms in 20 urology outpatients (Sugaya et al., 2021), blood pressure fell during the glycine phase (3 g twice a day), but everyone took placebo first and glycine second, without randomisation [18]. In a placebo-controlled trial in 60 people with metabolic syndrome, 15 g of glycine a day for 3 months lowered systolic blood pressure in men [63].

**Observational and genetic data:** In 4,109 people with suspected stable angina followed for a median of 7.4 years, higher plasma glycine was linked to fewer heart attacks (hazard ratio 0.89 per standard deviation) [65]. A genetic study of 80,003 people found that glycine is genetically linked to lower coronary heart disease risk, partly through blood pressure [66].

**Trial outcome:** In a randomised trial of 214 people having coronary angioplasty, oral glycine was safe but did not reduce narrowing of the treated artery at six months [67].

**Assessment:** Blood levels and genetics point to a link between glycine and lower heart risk, and blood pressure fell in two small studies, but no trial in this review has shown that glycine supplements prevent heart disease.

### Blood Sugar and Insulin Sensitivity

**Insulin stimulation:** In a small randomised, placebo-controlled trial in 12 healthy first-degree relatives of people with type 2 diabetes, a morning dose of 5 g of glycine increased insulin responses without changing insulin action [44].

**Glucose response:** In a small study in healthy people, taking glycine with glucose cut the blood glucose response by more than 50% compared with glucose alone [45].

**Type 2 diabetes:** In 74 people with type 2 diabetes, 5 g of glycine three times a day (15 g a day in all) for 3 months gave lower HbA1c than placebo [64][68].

**Metabolic syndrome:** A review found that plasma glycine levels are lower in people with metabolic syndrome than in healthy people [46]. In 60 people with metabolic syndrome, 15 g a day for 3 months cut a marker of fat oxidation by 25% compared with placebo [63].

**Assessment:** These trials are small and short, and they used 5 to 15 g a day rather than the 3 g sleep dose. Long-term supplementation trials for glucose metabolism are insufficient to support specific recommendations.

### Overactive Bladder

Sugaya et al. (2021) studied 20 urology outpatients (16 men and four women) with overactive bladder symptoms [18]. During the glycine phase, each took 3 g twice a day (6 g a day in total) for four weeks [18].

The published report describes all participants taking a glucose placebo for the first four weeks and then glycine for the next four weeks, rather than random assignment [18].

Results:
- Compared with the placebo phase, glycine reduced night-time urination, urgency and urine storage symptom scores [18]
- Sleep latency, bladder pain and blood pressure also improved during the glycine phase [18]
- The placebo phase itself improved some symptoms, including urinary frequency [18]
- There were no adverse events [18]

The study was small (n=20) and short (1 month), and the fixed placebo-first order limits the strength of conclusions that can be drawn. The abstract gives no effect sizes, and it does not say whether the sleep latency, bladder pain and blood pressure changes beat the placebo phase [18].

### Collagen Synthesis and Joint Health

The body needs glycine to make collagen [4].

**Conditional deficiency argument:** Meléndez-Hevia et al. (2009) argued from an analysis of metabolic pathways that the body does not make enough glycine for collagen turnover, glutathione production and its other uses, which supports the idea that glycine is conditionally essential [4]. It estimated that synthesis supplies about 3 g a day and the diet 1.5 to 3.0 g a day, which may fall short of total needs by about 10 g a day for a 70 kg adult [4].

**Practical implications:** Direct human clinical trials of glycine supplementation for joint health are limited.

### Gut Health

This review found no reliable human evidence to cite on glycine and gut health.

### Exercise Performance and Recovery

A 2024 literature review reports some evidence that glycine supplementation may enhance peak power output, reduce lactic acid build-up during high-intensity exercise, and improve sleep quality and recovery [28]. It concluded that more randomized controlled trials in humans are needed before glycine can be recommended for athletic performance.

### Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

Glycine has been investigated as a treatment for OCD because it is an NMDA receptor co-agonist [30].

**Greenberg et al. (2009):** A small double-blind, placebo-controlled trial added glycine, titrated to 60 g a day, for 24 adults with OCD on stable treatment. Many did not keep taking it, mainly because of the taste or nausea, so only 14 could be evaluated. Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) scores fell 6.04 points with glycine (n=5) and 1.00 point with placebo (n=9). The weekly difference just missed statistical significance (p=0.053) [29].

**Case report:** A long-term case report described high-dose glycine over 5 years that led to a large reduction of symptoms in a patient with refractory OCD and body dysmorphic disorder (BDD), with partial relapses when treatment stopped [30].

A 2011 review judged that early clinical evidence only tentatively supports glycine for OCD and called for larger placebo-controlled trials [72]. The evidence remains preliminary and needs confirmation in larger, better-tolerated controlled trials.

### Tinnitus

Glycine is sometimes promoted for tinnitus (ringing in the ears) because it calms nerve activity. Animal studies link tinnitus to changes in glycine signalling in hearing pathways [31]. This review found no human trial of glycine for tinnitus.

### Aging and Lifespan

**Animal models:** In the Interventions Testing Program, funded by the U.S. National Institute on Aging, glycine supplementation led to a small but statistically significant lifespan increase in mice of about 4–6% on average across three independent testing sites [42][48]. A small earlier study had suggested that glycine could extend lifespan in rats [48]. One author of the mouse study declared a link to a company that sells a glycine supplement [48]. In a mouse study, mice given GlyNAC lived 24% longer than control mice [47]. In a lab study, treating skin cells (fibroblasts) from elderly people with glycine prevented age-related defects in mitochondrial respiration [49]. Whether these findings apply to humans is unknown.

**Human evidence:** Human evidence is preliminary, deriving mainly from GlyNAC combination studies that improve glutathione levels, mitochondrial function, and oxidative stress markers without yet showing any effect on lifespan [11][59]. Larger, longer human trials are needed before making recommendations on aging.

- **GlyNAC dose:** Brad plans about 1 g of GlyNAC a day from age 45 [75]; the trial abstracts reviewed here do not report a 1 g dose.
- **Duration:** In the sleep-restriction trial, effects were measured over three nights [17]. Trials outside sleep used far larger doses, such as 0.8 g/kg a day in schizophrenia [21][22].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Tolerability

In trials, glycine has generally been well tolerated, including at high doses [21][22]. In a schizophrenia trial that added 0.8 g/kg body weight of glycine a day for 6 weeks, glycine was well tolerated [22]. In a randomised trial of 214 people after coronary angioplasty, long-term oral glycine had side effects similar to placebo [67]. A meta-analysis of NMDA-enhancing add-ons in schizophrenia noted no significant side effect or safety concern [55]. A 2024 review warns that very high doses (over 500 mg per kg of body weight) could be toxic to cells [28].

### Reported Side Effects

Side effects appear to be uncommon at supplement doses; the bladder study of 3 g twice a day reported no adverse events [18]. Possible side effects include:

- **Gastrointestinal effects:** Stomach upset, nausea or diarrhoea may occur, and are more likely at high doses
- **Nausea at very high doses:** In an OCD trial of glycine titrated to 60 g a day, many participants stopped, mainly because of the taste or nausea [29]
- **Drowsiness:** May occur at higher doses, given glycine's effect on sleep; no public study has measured this
- **Migraine:** Studies have found higher glycine levels in the platelets of people with migraine with aura [37] and in the saliva of people with migraine [38]. These studies measured the body's own glycine, not supplements, so they cannot tell us whether glycine supplements trigger migraine.

### Morning Grogginess in Trials

In Yamadera et al. (2007), 3 g before bed lessened next-day sleepiness, and people did better on memory tasks the next day [15]. In Bannai et al. (2012), the same dose reduced next-day fatigue, and sleepiness tended to fall [17]. Neither trial found more next-day sleepiness. In rats, glycine appears to act through a drop in core body temperature [13][14].

### FDA Classification Note

Under 21 CFR 170.50, the FDA no longer regards glycine and its salts as generally recognized as safe (GRAS) for use in human food, because reports found adverse effects when experimental animals were fed high levels and because people's intake may have been rising [39]. Food makers may add glycine only under a food additive rule, for technical uses such as masking saccharin's bitter aftertaste in drinks, at most 0.2% of the finished beverage [12]. The regulation keeps glycine's GRAS status in animal feed [39]. These rules cover glycine added to food; they do not address dietary supplements.

### Special Populations

- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** No trial has tested glycine supplements in pregnancy. In late pregnancy the body's need for glycine rises [71], but that is not evidence that supplements are safe. If you are pregnant or breastfeeding, check with your doctor before you take glycine.
- **Children:** Safety data in pediatric populations are insufficient. Glycine supplements are not recommended for children.
- **Non-ketotic hyperglycinaemia (NKH):** In this rare inherited disorder, large amounts of glycine build up in all body tissues, including the brain, and treatment aims to lower plasma glycine [43]. Supplements add glycine, so anyone with NKH should check with their specialist before taking one.
- **Epilepsy:** If you have epilepsy, check with your doctor before taking glycine. In rats, glycine strengthened the effect of two anti-seizure medicines [70].

### Laxative Effect Comparison

Glycine may occasionally cause diarrhoea; if it does, lower the dose or stop.

## 6. Drug Interactions

Some medicines may interact with glycine. Known and theoretical interactions include:

| Drug/Class | Interaction | Clinical Significance |
|-----------|-----------|:-:|
| **Clozapine (Clozaril)** | High-dose glycine may reduce clozapine's antipsychotic efficacy | HIGH: do not combine [23][54] |
| **Other atypical antipsychotics** (olanzapine, risperidone) | High-dose glycine may enhance therapeutic effect on negative symptoms | MODERATE: specialist supervision required [21][55] |
| **Anticonvulsants** | In rats, glycine strengthened the effect of diazepam and valproate, but not ethosuximide or phenobarbital [70]. If you take an anti-seizure medicine, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting glycine | Consult prescriber |
| **Blood pressure medications** | Glycine may lower blood pressure, potentially adding to antihypertensive effects | LOW-MODERATE: monitor blood pressure [18][63] |
| **Sedatives and sleep medications** | Theoretical additive effect due to glycine's sleep-promoting properties; no study has tested this | LOW: monitor for excessive sedation |

### Key Interaction Details

**Clozapine:** The interaction between glycine and clozapine is the most clinically significant. In Potkin et al. (1999), people who took glycine with clozapine did not improve, while those on clozapine alone had a 35% fall in positive symptoms, and the authors suggested glycine may interfere with clozapine's effect [23]. Evins et al. (2000) found no benefit and no clinical worsening [54]. Two meta-analyses found glycine-site add-ons helped with other antipsychotics but not with clozapine [55][56]. This combination should be avoided.

**Blood pressure medications:** Blood pressure fell while people took glycine in two small studies [18][63]. If you take blood pressure medicine, check with your doctor before you take glycine, in case your blood pressure drops too far.
