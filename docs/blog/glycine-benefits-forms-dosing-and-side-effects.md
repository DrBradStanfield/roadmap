## 1. Overview

Glycine is the smallest and simplest amino acid found in the human body, with the molecular formula C₂H₅NO₂ and systematic name 2-aminoacetic acid [1][2]. Unlike all other standard amino acids, glycine is the only achiral proteinogenic amino acid — it has no stereogenic center, meaning there are no L- or D- forms. This simplicity belies its remarkable biological importance: glycine plays critical roles in neurotransmission, immune regulation, collagen synthesis, detoxification, and antioxidant defense [1][3][4].

The liver and kidneys produce approximately 3 grams of glycine per day from precursors including the amino acids serine and threonine [1]. Serine hydroxymethyltransferase (SHMT) catalyzes the primary biosynthetic reaction, converting L-serine into glycine while generating 5,10-methylene-tetrahydrofolate, a key one-carbon unit for folate-dependent metabolism [2]. SHMT exists in cytosolic (SHMT1) and mitochondrial (SHMT2) isoforms, with the reaction equilibrium favoring glycine formation under physiological conditions [2]. Alternative biosynthetic routes include cleavage of L-threonine by threonine aldolase (yielding glycine and acetaldehyde) and indirect derivation from choline (via sarcosine dehydrogenase) and hydroxyproline (through oxidative degradation) [2].

Since the body can produce it, glycine is classified as a non-essential amino acid. However, some research suggests it should be considered conditionally essential, as endogenous production may not meet the body's demands for collagen synthesis, glutathione production, creatine synthesis, bile acid conjugation, and other metabolic processes [3][5][6]. The human body requires approximately 10 grams of glycine daily for collagen turnover alone, yet endogenous synthesis provides only about 3 grams and a typical Western diet contributes roughly 2 grams — leaving a potential shortfall of approximately 5 grams per day [5][6]. This deficit may become clinically relevant in older adults, athletes, pregnant women, and individuals with certain medical conditions where glycine demands are increased [7].

Glycine acts as both an inhibitory neurotransmitter and an excitatory co-agonist in the central nervous system. In the spinal cord and brainstem, it mediates fast synaptic inhibition through strychnine-sensitive glycine receptors (GlyRs), pentameric ligand-gated chloride channels that hyperpolarize postsynaptic neurons when activated [2][8]. Throughout the rest of the brain, glycine serves as an obligatory co-agonist at N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptors, binding to the GluN1 subunit to enable glutamate-induced channel opening and calcium influx, thereby contributing to excitatory synaptic transmission, synaptic plasticity, learning, and memory [2][9]. This dual role — inhibitory via GlyRs and facilitatory via NMDA receptors — allows glycine to fine-tune neural circuits and underlies its therapeutic potential in conditions ranging from insomnia to schizophrenia.

Glycine is also the most abundant amino acid in collagen, comprising approximately one-third of all amino acid residues in the repeating Gly-X-Y motif that forms the collagen triple helix [2][10]. Its small size allows the tight packing required for the triple helical structure, making glycine indispensable for connective tissue integrity, skin elasticity, joint health, and bone structure.

Additionally, glycine serves as a direct precursor for several critical metabolic pathways [2][11]:
- **Glutathione synthesis:** Glycine is one of three amino acids (along with cysteine and glutamate) that form glutathione (GSH), the body's primary intracellular antioxidant
- **Heme synthesis:** Glycine condenses with succinyl-CoA to produce δ-aminolevulinic acid (ALA), the rate-limiting step in porphyrin ring assembly for hemoglobin and cytochromes
- **Purine nucleotide synthesis:** The entire glycine molecule contributes carbon atoms 4 and 5 and nitrogen atom 7 to the purine ring
- **Creatine synthesis:** Glycine serves as the acyl acceptor in the first step of creatine biosynthesis, catalyzed by L-arginine:glycine amidinotransferase (AGAT)
- **Bile acid conjugation:** Glycine conjugates with primary bile acids to form glycine-conjugated bile salts (e.g., glycocholic acid), enhancing their solubility and emulsification capacity
- **Xenobiotic detoxification:** Glycine conjugates with benzoic acid and other aromatic compounds to form hippuric acid for urinary excretion, eliminating approximately 400–800 mg of glycine daily through this pathway alone

Some experts recommend a daily intake of approximately 1.5 to 3 grams of glycine per day to maintain blood levels between 200–300 μmol/L [1][3]. This can be met through a protein-rich diet containing fish, chicken, legumes, and seeds. However, those requiring glycine supplementation for specific conditions — particularly sleep difficulties, overactive bladder symptoms, or glutathione support — may benefit from targeted supplemental doses.

Several small clinical trials, generally supported by Ajinomoto (a major glycine manufacturer), have evaluated glycine supplementation for improving sleep quality, reducing nighttime urinary frequency, and improving symptoms in schizophrenia. The evidence base also includes preclinical studies showing effects on glutathione levels, anti-inflammatory pathways, cardiovascular markers, gut barrier function, and aging biomarkers [1][4][12].

### Key Purchasing Considerations

As long as glycine is listed as the ingredient in a supplement, it is in the free form regardless of whether "free form" is indicated on the label [1]. Unlike most amino acids, glycine has no L- or D- chemical form due to its achiral structure, so there is no need to look for a specific stereoisomer or to pay a premium for products claiming to be "biologically active" or "free form" [1][2].

### Stability and Storage

Glycine is chemically stable, including at temperatures above the boiling point of water, making it suitable for mixing into hot beverages such as coffee and tea [13]. However, decomposition can be accelerated by moisture and heat during long-term storage. Store glycine in a cool, dry place. Do not discard desiccant packs from containers; leave them in to protect glycine from moisture. Refrigeration is not necessary [1].

### Calorie Content

Glycine contains approximately 4 calories per gram, equivalent to table sugar. However, due to a regulatory loophole in calorie labeling, the calorie content may not be listed on supplement labels. The FDA permits manufacturers to calculate calorie content based only on protein, carbohydrate, and fat content — but glycine, as a single amino acid, is classified as none of these [1][16].

### Bioavailability

Free-form glycine is rapidly absorbed from the gastrointestinal tract. As the simplest amino acid, it does not require enzymatic digestion before absorption and is transported across intestinal epithelial cells via active amino acid transport systems. Glycine from collagen peptides requires digestion to release the bound amino acid, though bioavailability of collagen-derived amino acids is generally good following hydrolysis [10].

When glycine is chelated to minerals (as in magnesium glycinate), the glycine component is released during digestion and contributes to the body's glycine pool. A standard dose of magnesium glycinate providing 200 mg of elemental magnesium delivers approximately 1.4 g of glycine as well [14].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Sleep and Fatigue

Glycine's effects on sleep are among the best-studied benefits of supplementation. The proposed mechanism involves glycine acting as a co-agonist at NMDA receptors in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN), an area in the brain that regulates circadian rhythm. Animal research suggests that by modulating NMDA receptor activity in the SCN, glycine lowers core body temperature through peripheral vasodilation and modulates circadian rhythm, thereby improving sleep quality [17][18]. A 2025 review of clinical evidence confirmed these effects, attributing them to glycine's promotion of peripheral vasodilation and hypothermic responses without significant next-day sedation [19].

**Clinical trial 1 — Yamadera et al. (2007):** A study among 11 men and women with self-reported "unsatisfactory sleep" found that 3 grams of glycine granules taken one hour before bedtime significantly improved self-reported sleep quality and shortened the time it took to fall asleep (as measured by polysomnography — brain wave, heart rate, and breathing monitors) compared to placebo. Glycine reduced sleep onset latency and facilitated faster entry into deep (slow-wave) sleep [20].

**Clinical trial 2 — Inagawa et al. (2006):** A study conducted among 15 Ajinomoto employees who reported dissatisfaction with their sleep found that 3 grams of glycine taken one hour before bedtime improved self-reported sleep quality compared to placebo. Participants reported feeling more refreshed upon waking and experienced less daytime fatigue [21].

**Clinical trial 3 — Bannai et al. (2012):** A study among 10 Ajinomoto employees found that 3 grams of glycine taken before bedtime improved subjective sleep quality and decreased next-day fatigue. The study also evaluated cognitive performance and found that glycine improved reaction times and reduced errors on psychomotor vigilance tasks the following day, suggesting improvements in daytime function attributable to better sleep quality [22]. No adverse events were reported in any of these three trials.

**Mechanism — core body temperature:** Kawai et al. (2015) demonstrated in animal models that glycine's sleep-promoting and hypothermic effects are mediated by NMDA receptors in the suprachiasmatic nucleus [17]. The hypothermic response — a lowering of core body temperature — is a critical physiological signal for sleep initiation. This mechanism is distinct from that of sedative drugs, which suppress neural activity rather than facilitating the body's natural thermoregulatory sleep signals. This means glycine promotes sleep through a natural physiological pathway rather than through pharmacological sedation, which is consistent with the clinical finding that glycine does not cause next-morning grogginess [17][18].

**Individual variation:** While clinical studies consistently show sleep benefits, individual responses may vary. In a minority of people, glycine may produce paradoxical effects such as increased alertness or vivid dreams. This is thought to occur when glycine's facilitatory action at excitatory NMDA receptors overrides its inhibitory effects via glycine receptors in certain neurochemical contexts, possibly influenced by genetics [4]. Such reports are primarily anecdotal, with clinical studies consistently reporting beneficial and well-tolerated effects on sleep.

It is worth noting that all three clinical trials were small (10–15 participants), short-term, and conducted among Ajinomoto employees with Ajinomoto funding. While the consistent direction of results across three independent trials is encouraging, larger independent trials are needed to strengthen the evidence base.

### Overactive Bladder and Chronic Prostatitis

Sugaya et al. (2021) conducted a study in Japan among 16 men and 4 women (average age 67) with mild to moderate overactive bladder, chronic prostatitis, and/or bladder pain syndrome/interstitial cystitis [23]. Participants received 3 grams of glycine taken twice daily (6 g/day total) for one month.

Results:
- Glycine modestly reduced nighttime urinary urgency and frequency (but not daytime symptoms)
- Overall overactive bladder symptom scores improved by approximately 1 point on a 15-point scale compared to placebo
- Self-reported "vitality" improved compared to placebo
- Time to fall asleep was slightly reduced and bladder pain was modestly decreased compared to baseline, but these improvements were NOT statistically significant compared to placebo
- Systolic and diastolic blood pressure modestly decreased compared to baseline, but again not statistically significant versus placebo

Despite these possible benefits, the American Urological Society has stated that there is insufficient evidence to support the use of any nutraceutical, vitamin, or supplement to treat the symptoms of overactive bladder [24]. The study was small (n=20) and short (1 month), limiting the strength of conclusions that can be drawn.

### Schizophrenia

Research suggests that glycine may benefit some people with schizophrenia by enhancing NMDA receptor function. The NMDA receptor hypofunction hypothesis of schizophrenia proposes that reduced NMDA receptor signaling contributes to the negative symptoms (emotional withdrawal, reduced motivation, social withdrawal) and cognitive deficits characteristic of the disease [9][25].

**Heresco-Levy et al. (2004):** A study among 17 people (average age 45) with schizophrenia being treated with the antipsychotic medications olanzapine or risperidone found that treatment with a daily dose of 0.8 g/kg body weight of glycine (between 40–80 grams per day) for 6 weeks, in addition to regular antipsychotic medication, achieved notable results [26]:
- Negative symptoms reduced by 23% compared to baseline
- 57% of those given glycine plus medication showed greater than 20% improvement in negative symptoms during glycine treatment versus none during placebo plus medication treatment
- Moderate improvements in depression and excitement symptoms were also observed

**Heresco-Levy et al. (1999):** An earlier study among 22 people with treatment-resistant schizophrenia found that supplementing with very high doses of glycine (starting at 4 grams daily and increased to 40–80 grams daily over approximately 2 weeks) for a total of 6 weeks slightly improved depression symptoms compared to placebo [27]. The study reported that negative symptoms improved by approximately 34% without altering serum antipsychotic levels [4].

**Clozapine interaction — Potkin et al. (1999):** One important study found that glycine may interfere with the efficacy of the atypical antipsychotic clozapine (Clozaril) [28]. This finding means that while glycine may be a useful adjunct to some antipsychotic medications (olanzapine, risperidone), it should NOT be combined with clozapine, as it may worsen outcomes. Meta-analyses show that efficacy diminishes or reverses with clozapine specifically, and overall effect sizes across all studies remain small with inconsistent replication [4].

**Summary:** High-dose glycine (0.8 g/kg/day, typically 40–80 g/day) may modestly reduce negative symptoms of schizophrenia when used as an adjunct to non-clozapine antipsychotics. The doses required are very large and may cause gastrointestinal side effects. This application should only be pursued under specialist psychiatric supervision.

### Depression

There is speculation that glycine may be beneficial for depression, although this has not been proven in dedicated clinical trials.

**Observational evidence:** Altamura et al. (1995) reported that people with major depressive disorder have lower blood levels of glycine compared to healthy controls [29]. Serum glycine concentrations also inversely correlate with metabolic syndrome traits, and lower levels are observed in individuals with insulin resistance, obesity, and type 2 diabetes — conditions that frequently co-occur with depression [4].

**Preclinical evidence:** Test-tube and animal studies have demonstrated mechanisms by which glycine could influence mood [30][31]:
- Sutton et al. (2018) identified glycine's effects on neural circuits relevant to depression in animal models [30]
- Laboute et al. (2023) published findings in *Science* demonstrating glycine's ability to modulate neural signaling through novel receptor pathways [31]

**Clinical data:** The only clinical evidence for glycine's antidepressant effects comes from the Heresco-Levy et al. (1999) schizophrenia study, which found that very high-dose glycine (40–80 g/day) slightly improved depression symptoms in people with treatment-resistant schizophrenia compared to placebo [27]. There do not appear to be any studies evaluating glycine supplementation in people without schizophrenia who have depression.

**Conclusion:** The mechanistic rationale exists, and the observational association between low glycine levels and depression is consistent, but there is insufficient interventional evidence to recommend glycine supplementation specifically for depression.

### Glutathione and Antioxidant Support

Glycine is one of three amino acids (alongside cysteine and glutamate) required for glutathione (GSH) synthesis. GSH is the body's primary intracellular antioxidant, neutralizing reactive oxygen species, detoxifying electrophilic xenobiotics, and maintaining cellular redox homeostasis [11][12].

**GlyNAC supplementation:** The combination of glycine with N-acetylcysteine (NAC) — termed GlyNAC — addresses deficiencies in both glycine and cysteine simultaneously, making it potentially more effective at raising glutathione levels than either amino acid alone [15]. Research by Kumar et al. at Baylor College of Medicine has shown that GlyNAC supplementation in older adults and in people with HIV corrects age-related glutathione deficits and improves markers of oxidative stress, mitochondrial function, and inflammation.

**Obesity trial (2025):** A randomized trial in individuals with severe obesity found that 30 grams of glycine daily increased plasma glycine by 35%, boosted GSH availability, reduced body weight and adiposity, and enhanced detoxification pathways [4][12]. These effects parallel those impaired in non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD).

**Preclinical aging data:** In rodent models, glycine supplementation — often combined with NAC — corrected age-related glutathione deficits and enhanced electron transport chain efficiency. In aged mice, this intervention extended median lifespan by approximately 24% and improved mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, reducing reactive oxygen species by 50–70% in liver and muscle tissues [4][32]. In rats, chronic glycine feeding extended lifespan by 4–6%, potentially via methionine restriction mimicry and autophagy induction [4][33]. Human evidence for longevity effects is preliminary, deriving mainly from GlyNAC studies that improve GSH and mitochondrial function without yet demonstrating direct longevity outcomes [15].

**Fibroblast research:** Glycine supplementation reversed age-associated mitochondrial respiration defects in elderly human fibroblast cells, linked to autophagy activation and methionine restriction mimicry [34][35].

### Anti-inflammatory Effects

Glycine exhibits anti-inflammatory properties through multiple mechanisms that have been demonstrated primarily in preclinical models [4][36]:

- **Glycine-gated chloride channels:** Glycine activates GlyRs on immune cells such as macrophages and leukocytes, leading to chloride influx, membrane hyperpolarization, reduced intracellular calcium, and suppressed production of inflammatory mediators including TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β
- **NF-κB inhibition:** Glycine inhibits NF-κB activation through inactivation of IκB and reduced phosphorylation of IKK-α/β, a central inflammatory signaling pathway
- **NMDA receptor modulation:** Through co-activation of NMDA receptors in non-neuronal cells, glycine dampens pro-inflammatory cytokine release in macrophages and endothelial cells. Studies in activated microglia report 40–60% reductions in inflammatory markers at physiological glycine levels (0.1–1 mM)
- **IL-10 upregulation:** Glycine increases the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 while reducing pro-inflammatory cytokines

These mechanisms have been observed in animal models of sepsis, obesity, ischemia-reperfusion injury, endotoxemia, and other inflammatory conditions, where glycine supplementation demonstrated protective benefits including reduced organ damage, attenuated inflammation, and improved survival [4][36]. However, it is important to note that these effects are primarily observed in preclinical settings. There is limited human clinical evidence for direct anti-inflammatory benefits of glycine supplementation.

### Cardiovascular Health

**Blood pressure:** The Sugaya et al. (2021) overactive bladder trial observed modest reductions in systolic and diastolic blood pressure with glycine supplementation (6 g/day), though these did not reach statistical significance compared to placebo [23]. Animal studies by Petersen et al. (1991) demonstrated glycine's hypotensive effects [37].

**Endothelial function:** Glycine supplementation supports cardiovascular health by acting as an antioxidant and anti-inflammatory agent, improving endothelial function, and boosting glutathione production to protect the heart from oxidative stress and inflammation [4][38].

**Methionine-glycine balance:** There is ongoing debate regarding glycine-methionine imbalances in diets high in muscle meat. Excess methionine relative to glycine may elevate homocysteine levels — a cardiovascular risk factor — though human causality has not been definitively established beyond transient elevations [4][39].

**Overall assessment:** The cardiovascular evidence for glycine supplementation is largely preclinical. Blood pressure reduction has been observed but not confirmed in placebo-controlled trials. The strongest cardiovascular rationale may be glycine's role in glutathione synthesis and anti-inflammatory signaling.

### Blood Sugar and Insulin Sensitivity

Serum glycine concentrations inversely correlate with metabolic syndrome traits, with lower levels observed in individuals exhibiting insulin resistance, obesity, and type 2 diabetes [4][40][41]. Higher circulating glycine associates positively with insulin sensitivity as measured by homeostasis model assessment [4][42].

**Insulin stimulation:** In healthy first-degree relatives of individuals with type 2 diabetes, a single oral 5 g dose of glycine increased early, late, and total insulin responses without altering insulin action [43]. In healthy subjects, oral glycine alone slightly increased insulin levels, while co-ingestion with glucose significantly reduced the plasma glucose response by over 50%, likely via stimulation of gut hormones that potentiate insulin's effects on glucose disposal [43][44].

**Mixed results in obesity:** Results vary by context. While some animal models demonstrate improved insulin sensitivity with glycine supplementation, others — particularly in obesity models — show no benefit or worsening of glucose intolerance, such as through enhanced hepatic gluconeogenesis [4][43][44]. This inconsistency limits the strength of recommendations.

**Overall assessment:** The epidemiological association between low glycine and insulin resistance is consistent, and acute studies show glycine can modulate postprandial glucose. However, long-term supplementation trials for glucose metabolism are insufficient to support specific recommendations.

### Collagen Synthesis and Joint Health

Glycine comprises approximately one-third of the amino acid residues in collagen, making it structurally essential for connective tissue integrity [2][10]. In the repeating Gly-X-Y motif of collagen's triple helix, glycine's small size (it is the only amino acid small enough to fit in the interior of the triple helix) allows the tight packing required for structural stability.

**In vitro evidence:** Studies in bovine chondrocytes have demonstrated that elevating glycine concentrations to levels significantly above physiological plasma concentrations (≥1.5 mM) can increase type II collagen synthesis by up to 2.5-fold [10]. Metabolic control analysis indicates that glycine scarcity leads to protein misfolding and substantial waste in the procollagen cycle, where much newly synthesized collagen is degraded rather than incorporated into the extracellular matrix [10].

**Conditional deficiency argument:** Meléndez-Hevia et al. (2009) argued through metabolic flux analysis that the amount of glycine synthesized endogenously is insufficient to meet the body's needs for collagen turnover, glutathione production, and other demands — supporting the concept that glycine is conditionally essential [5]. The estimated total daily glycine requirement is approximately 10 g for collagen alone, versus roughly 3 g from endogenous synthesis and 2 g from a typical diet [5][6].

**Practical implications:** While direct human clinical trials of glycine supplementation for joint health are limited, the mechanistic rationale is strong. Collagen peptide supplements (which provide substantial glycine alongside hydroxyproline and proline) have demonstrated benefits for skin and joint health in clinical trials [10]. Supplementing with glycine, possibly alongside proline and lysine, may enhance collagen synthesis efficiency and support joint maintenance.

### Gut Health

Preclinical studies in animal models indicate that glycine supplementation exerts protective effects on gut health [4][45][46]:

- **Intestinal barrier function:** In mice with high-fat diet-induced obesity, glycine supplementation enhanced intestinal mucosal integrity, improved tight-junction proteins, reduced endoplasmic reticulum stress-related apoptosis, and mitigated inflammation
- **Colitis model:** In a mouse model of acetic acid-induced colitis, dietary glycine ameliorated inflammation by regulating colonic cytokine expressions, particularly IL-10, possibly via effects on gut bacteria
- **Oxidative protection:** Glycine protects intestinal epithelial cells against oxidative stress and damage through the glycine transporter GLYT1
- **NAFLD:** Preclinical data support glycine's role in NAFLD amelioration via stimulated hepatic fatty acid oxidation and GSH synthesis

**Negative human trial:** Despite these promising preclinical findings, a multicenter randomized double-blind crossover trial in chronic hemodialysis patients found that glycine supplementation (7 g twice daily) had no significant impact on gut barrier function, microbiota composition, or systemic inflammation [4]. This discrepancy between animal and human data highlights the need for caution in extrapolating preclinical results.

### Exercise Performance and Recovery

A 2024 systematic review indicates potential benefits of glycine for exercise, including reduced lactic acid accumulation and improved peak power output in high-intensity efforts, alongside faster muscle recovery via collagen synthesis support [47]. However, the evidence is derived from small trials with heterogeneous dosing (3–10 grams), lacking consensus on optimal protocols and insufficient for broad ergogenic recommendations.

**Muscle cell recovery:** Recent investigations highlight glycine's involvement in muscle cell recovery post-stress, maintaining amino acid pools essential for connective tissue repair and reducing catabolic signaling. In vitro models demonstrate 15–30% faster recovery in glycine-replete conditions [4][48].

**Creatine precursor role:** Glycine is the acyl acceptor in the first step of creatine biosynthesis. Enhanced glycine availability supports creatine synthesis and promotes phosphocreatine stores that buffer energy demands, exhibiting anti-catabolic effects relevant to age-related muscle decline (sarcopenia) [2][49].

### Obsessive-Compulsive Disorder (OCD)

Glycine has been investigated as an adjunctive treatment for OCD, leveraging its role as an NMDA receptor co-agonist to potentially address glutamatergic dysfunction implicated in OCD pathophysiology [4].

**Greenberg et al. (2009):** A small double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (n=24) of adjunctive glycine (up to 60 g/day) in adults with stabilized OCD found statistically significant reductions in Yale-Brown Obsessive Compulsive Scale (Y-BOCS) scores over at least 12 weeks among completers [50]. However, the study was limited by high dropout rates primarily due to poor palatability and nausea at the very high doses required.

**Case report:** A long-term open-label case report described high-dose glycine treatment administered over more than 5 years resulting in robust and sustained reduction of symptoms in a patient with refractory OCD and comorbid body dysmorphic disorder (BDD), with only partial relapses during brief interruptions [51].

These findings expand on glycine's psychiatric applications beyond schizophrenia, though evidence remains preliminary and requires confirmation in larger, better-tolerated controlled trials.

### Tinnitus

There do not appear to be any published clinical trials investigating the effects of glycine supplementation in people with tinnitus (ringing in the ear), although such use has been promoted based on glycine's inhibitory or "calming" effect on nerve activity [52][53][54]. Glycine's role as an inhibitory neurotransmitter in auditory pathways provides theoretical rationale, but no clinical evidence supports this use.

### Aging and Longevity

**Animal models:** Rodent studies show lifespan extension with chronic glycine feeding — approximately 4–6% in mice and up to 28% in rats — potentially via methionine restriction mimicry and autophagy induction [4][33]. In aged mice, glycine combined with NAC (GlyNAC) extended median lifespan by approximately 24% and improved mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation, reducing reactive oxygen species by 50–70% in liver and muscle tissues [32].

**Mitochondrial function:** Glycine supplementation has been shown to reverse age-associated mitochondrial respiration defects in elderly human fibroblast cells, linked to enhanced autophagy activation [34][35]. In rodent models, GlyNAC improved mitochondrial function by correcting age-related glutathione deficits and enhancing electron transport chain efficiency [15][32].

**Human evidence:** Human evidence is preliminary, deriving mainly from GlyNAC combination studies that improve glutathione levels, mitochondrial function, and oxidative stress markers without yet demonstrating direct longevity outcomes [15]. Larger, longer-duration human trials are needed before making anti-aging recommendations.

### Practical Dosing Notes

- **Most studied dose for sleep:** 3 grams taken approximately 1 hour before bedtime. This dose has been consistent across all three sleep trials [20][21][22].
- **Powder versus capsules:** At a dose of 3 g/day, powder is more practical than capsules (3 g = 3–6 capsules depending on capsule size). Glycine powder has a mildly sweet taste and dissolves easily in water, juice, or hot beverages.
- **Empty stomach versus with food:** There is no universal consensus on whether to take glycine on an empty stomach or with food. Many sources recommend an empty stomach for sleep benefits (to potentially improve absorption and effectiveness), while others suggest taking it with food to reduce potential gastric discomfort. Clinical studies often administered glycine shortly before bedtime without specifying meal timing [4].
- **Duration:** Sleep benefits have been observed within the first few nights in clinical trials. Long-term safety data exist for doses up to 90 g/day short-term and typical supplemental doses (3–5 g/day) over several weeks to months [4].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Tolerability

Glycine supplements are generally well-tolerated [9][55]. At typical supplemental doses of 3–5 g/day, no serious side effects have been reported in clinical studies. Even at very high doses (up to 90 g/day for several weeks), glycine has been used in schizophrenia studies without serious adverse effects, though gastrointestinal symptoms become more common at higher doses [4][55].

### Reported Side Effects

Side effects are rare at typical supplemental doses. When they occur, they may include [1][55][56]:

- **Gastrointestinal effects:** Stomach pain, nausea, diarrhea — reported rarely at standard doses, more common at high doses
- **Nausea and vomiting:** When taken in very high doses (over 40 g/day), glycine may cause nausea and vomiting in some people [57]
- **Drowsiness:** Occasionally noted at higher doses, consistent with glycine's mechanism of action on sleep
- **Headache:** Rarely reported
- **Irritability and insomnia:** Paradoxical effects reported rarely, possibly related to NMDA receptor excitatory co-agonist effects
- **Optical migraines:** One ConsumerLab member reported several optical migraines after supplementing with glycine powder (¼ to ¾ teaspoon daily for three weeks), which resolved after stopping supplementation [1]. There are no published reports of glycine supplementation triggering migraines. Elevated glycine concentrations have been reported in blood cells and saliva of individuals with migraine with and without aura [58][59], although this does not prove causation.

### Glycine Does NOT Cause Morning Grogginess

Unlike high-dose melatonin supplements, glycine does not cause next-morning grogginess. Clinical studies consistently report that glycine improves next-day alertness and reduces daytime fatigue rather than causing sedation [4][20][21][22]. This is because glycine promotes sleep through natural thermoregulatory mechanisms (core body temperature reduction) rather than through pharmacological sedation.

### FDA Classification Note

Interestingly, based on evidence of adverse effects in animals upon exposure to high levels of glycine, glycine is not considered generally recognized as safe (GRAS) for use as a food additive in human food. It may only be used in very small amounts for certain technical effects, including masking the bitter aftertaste of saccharin in beverages (not to exceed 0.2% in the finished beverage) [16][60]. Note: details of the adverse animal effects to which the FDA refers are difficult to find in the literature. While not considered suitable as a food additive, glycine can be legally sold as a dietary supplement [16].

### Special Populations

- **Pregnancy and breastfeeding:** Glycine supplement use has not been studied in pregnant or breastfeeding women. If pregnant or breastfeeding, avoid glycine supplements unless specifically recommended and monitored by a healthcare provider [61].
- **Children:** Safety data in pediatric populations are insufficient. Glycine supplements are not recommended for children.
- **Non-ketotic hyperglycinemia (NKH):** Individuals with this rare genetic disorder affecting the glycine cleavage system should not take glycine supplements, as they already have pathologically elevated glycine levels [2].
- **Epilepsy:** Excessive glycine concentrations can theoretically potentiate NMDA receptor activity, raising concerns for individuals with epilepsy, though clinical evidence for this risk at supplemental doses is lacking [4].

### Laxative Effect Comparison

Unlike magnesium citrate or magnesium oxide, glycine does NOT have a significant laxative effect [4]. Occasional loose stools may occur at very high doses (9+ grams), but this is mild and dose-dependent rather than a primary laxative action.

## 6. Drug Interactions

Glycine may interact with certain medications. Known and theoretical interactions include:

| Drug/Class | Interaction | Clinical Significance |
|-----------|-----------|:-:|
| **Clozapine (Clozaril)** | High-dose glycine may reduce clozapine's antipsychotic efficacy | HIGH — Contraindicated combination [28] |
| **Other atypical antipsychotics** (olanzapine, risperidone) | High-dose glycine may enhance therapeutic effect on negative symptoms | MODERATE — Specialist supervision required [26] |
| **Anticonvulsants** | Glycine may interact with anticonvulsant medications | MODERATE — Consult prescriber [1] |
| **Blood pressure medications** | Glycine may lower blood pressure, potentially adding to antihypertensive effects | LOW-MODERATE — Monitor blood pressure [37] |
| **Sedatives and sleep medications** | Theoretical additive effect due to glycine's sleep-promoting properties | LOW — Monitor for excessive sedation |

### Key Interaction Details

**Clozapine:** The interaction between glycine and clozapine is the most clinically significant. Potkin et al. (1999) found that high-dose glycine appeared to interfere with clozapine's efficacy in treating schizophrenia [28]. While glycine enhances NMDA receptor function (which may benefit negative symptoms), clozapine already has glycine-site NMDA receptor activity, and the combination may paradoxically reduce therapeutic benefit. This combination should be avoided.

**Blood pressure medications:** Glycine has demonstrated hypotensive effects in some populations [37]. If you are taking blood pressure medication, consult your healthcare provider before taking a glycine supplement to avoid excessive blood pressure reduction.

## 7. Dietary Sources

Glycine is concentrated in protein-rich foods, particularly those containing collagen (connective tissue, skin, bone broth). If you eat a well-rounded, protein-rich diet, you will likely get at least 1.5 to 3 grams of glycine per day from food [1][62].

### Top Food Sources

| Food | Serving | Glycine Content |
|------|---------|:-:|
| Dry gelatin powder | 100 g | ~19 g |
| Bone broth | 100 g | 3–5 g (varies by preparation) |
| Pork skin, chicken skin, organ meats | 100 g protein | >10 g |
| Steak (beef) | 3-ounce serving | 1.43 g |
| Chicken breast | 3-ounce serving | 1.15 g |
| Salmon | 3-ounce serving | 1.04 g |
| Pork | 3-ounce serving | 1.04 g |
| Pumpkin seeds | 2-ounce serving | 1.00 g |
| Eggs | 2 large | 0.92 g |
| Lentils | 1 cup cooked | 0.72 g |

Sources: USDA FoodData Central [62]; ConsumerLab [1].

### Dietary Context

Average daily dietary intake of glycine in adults from mixed Western diets is approximately 2 grams, primarily from animal proteins [4][63]. East Asian diets may derive more from grains and fish. Plant proteins such as those in legumes, grains, and nuts yield lower glycine densities — typically under 5 g per 100 g of protein — making vegan diets more reliant on endogenous synthesis [4][64].

Modern processed diets emphasizing lean muscle meats and refined plant products over connective tissues or slow-cooked bone broths result in reduced glycine intake relative to ancestral dietary patterns [4]. Traditional foods like bone broth, gelatin, and collagen-rich cuts of meat (skin-on, slow-cooked, organ meats) provide substantially more glycine than the lean muscle cuts favored in contemporary diets.

Collagen-rich foods are particularly high in glycine because collagen protein is approximately 33% glycine by amino acid composition [10]. For reference, a 12.5 g serving of collagen peptides provides roughly 4 g of glycine.
