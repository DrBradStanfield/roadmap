## Current evidence position

The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) finds no conclusive evidence that ginkgo helps any health condition [2]. In the GEM trial, 3,069 adults aged 75 or older took a 120 mg ginkgo extract dose twice daily or placebo for a median of 6.1 years, and ginkgo did not lower the rate of dementia (HR 1.12, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.33) [7][8]; the GuidAge trial, using the standardised extract EGb 761 at the same dose, did not reduce new cases of Alzheimer's disease during 5 years of follow-up [9]. Small, old trials hint at help for vertigo, while a Cochrane review found ginkgo may have little to no effect on tinnitus [2][15][17]. Ginkgo may raise bleeding risk in people who take anticoagulants such as warfarin, and it may be unsafe in pregnancy [2].

## Key safety points

- Ginkgo may raise bleeding risk in people who take anticoagulants such as warfarin [2][24]. Case reports include serious bleeds, though a meta-analysis of trials found no rise in bleeding risk on clotting tests [37][38].
- Stop nonessential supplements, ginkgo included, 2 weeks before surgery [24].
- In people with epilepsy, case reports link ginkgo to returning seizures, one of them fatal [39][40]. If a seizure lasts more than 5 minutes, or seizures come back to back, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).
- Ginkgo may interact with other drugs; in a small study it sped up the breakdown of omeprazole [2][42]. If you take any medicine, talk with your doctor or pharmacist first.
- Ginkgo may be unsafe in pregnancy: it might cause early labor or extra bleeding at delivery. Little is known about breastfeeding [2].
- Raw ginkgo seeds are toxic and have caused seizures in children, a separate hazard from leaf extract [2][43]. If anyone has a seizure after eating them, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).
- The most common side effects of leaf extract are dizziness, stomach and bowel symptoms, and headache [2].

## 1. Overview

Ginkgo biloba is an ancient tree, often called a "living fossil", and the last surviving species of a primitive plant family [2]. It has a long history of use in Chinese medicine for cough, diarrhea and other conditions [2]. Today the leaf, usually as an extract, is the most used form, and it is promoted for anxiety, dementia, PMS, tinnitus and many other conditions [2].

Supplements are made from the leaves, whose main active compounds are flavonoids and terpenoids [36]. Medicine-grade leaf extracts keep potentially toxic ginkgolic acids below 5 ppm [32].

The most studied extract is EGb 761, developed by Dr Willmar Schwabe Pharmaceuticals and sold under names such as Tanakan and Tebonin [45]. It contains flavone glycosides, about 24%, mainly quercetin, kaempferol and isorhamnetin, and terpene lactones, about 6%, made up of ginkgolides A, B and C plus bilobalide [45]. Proposed actions include widening blood vessels, lowering blood viscosity, changing neurotransmitter systems and lowering oxygen free radicals [10].

The clinical evidence is mostly disappointing. NCCIH finds no conclusive evidence that ginkgo helps any health condition [2]. In GEM, more than 3,000 people aged 75 or older took ginkgo or placebo for a median of 6 years, and ginkgo did not prevent dementia [2]. A small amount of evidence suggests benefit for vertigo, but the overall evidence is not conclusive [2][15].

This article reviews the evidence by condition, then dosing, safety and drug interactions.

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Standardized Extracts vs. Raw Leaf

Quality varies. A Polish analysis of 11 ginkgo leaf extract products found that the three registered as medicines held their declared amounts of flavonoids and terpene lactones, with ginkgolic acids below 5 ppm [32]. Most of the eight dietary supplements held less of the active compounds, and several had raised ginkgolic acids [32]. Ginkgolic acids and endotoxins are the main toxic compounds found across the leaves, seeds and seed coat [36].

### EGb 761: The Clinical Standard

EGb 761 is the extract used in GuidAge and many smaller trials [9][12]. It is standardised to flavone glycosides (about 24%) and terpene lactones (about 6%); the terpene lactones split into 2.8 to 3.4% ginkgolides A, B and C and 2.6 to 3.2% bilobalide [45].

### Other Studied Extracts

Other standardised extracts appear in smaller trials, such as Seredrin, a ginkgo phytosome tested in Raynaud's disease [20].

### Combination Products

Some products combine ginkgo with other herbs. One 90-day trial in 100 middle-aged and older Chinese adults tested Cistanche tubulosa with ginkgo; two tablets a day gave 72 mg echinacoside and 27 mg flavonol glycosides [6]. A combination's result cannot be credited to ginkgo alone.

### Bioavailability Considerations

Trials have given ginkgo split into two doses (120 mg twice daily in GEM and GuidAge) or as 240 mg once daily [7][8][9][12].

### Summary Table: Ginkgo Supplement Forms

| Form | Clinical Evidence | Notes |
|------|:-:|:-:|
| Standardized leaf extract (such as EGb 761) | Used in the large trials [7][9] | Flavone glycosides about 24%, terpene lactones about 6% [45] |
| Dietary supplement leaf extracts | Rarely tested | Active content and ginkgolic acids vary [32] |
| Ginkgo seeds | None | Toxic when eaten raw [2] |
| Combination products | Single trials | Results cannot be credited to ginkgo alone [6] |

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Alzheimer's Disease and Dementia

Ginkgo has been widely studied for dementia. The two largest trials were both negative [7][9].

**The GEM Study (Ginkgo Evaluation of Memory):** Funded by NCCIH and the National Institute on Aging, GEM enrolled 3,069 adults aged 75 or older at 5 US academic centres: 2,587 with normal cognition and 482 with mild cognitive impairment [2][7][8]. They took a 120 mg ginkgo extract dose twice daily or placebo for a median of 6.1 years. The main outcome was new dementia [7].

Results: The dementia rate was 3.3 per 100 person-years on ginkgo and 2.9 on placebo (HR 1.12, 95% CI 0.94 to 1.33; P = .21), and for Alzheimer's disease the HR was 1.16 (95% CI 0.97 to 1.39) [7]. Ginkgo did not slow progression to dementia in people who started with mild cognitive impairment (HR 1.13, 95% CI 0.85 to 1.50) [7]. A later GEM analysis found no difference in the yearly rate of decline in memory, attention, language or executive function, or on the 3MSE and ADAS-Cog scales (Snitz et al., JAMA, 2009) [8].

**The GuidAge Study:** This French trial enrolled 2,854 adults aged 70 or older with memory complaints they had raised with their family doctor. They took 120 mg of EGb 761 twice daily or placebo for 5 years; the main outcome was probable Alzheimer's disease [9].

Results: By 5 years, 61 people on ginkgo and 73 on placebo had probable Alzheimer's disease (1.2 and 1.4 cases per 100 person-years; HR 0.84, 95% CI 0.60 to 1.18; p=0.306), a difference that could be chance [9]. Deaths, strokes and other bleeding or heart events did not differ between the groups [9].

**Cochrane Review:** A 2007 Cochrane review of randomised, double-blind trials found some benefits on certain measures at 12 or 24 weeks, but judged the evidence that ginkgo gives predictable, clinically meaningful benefit in dementia or cognitive impairment "inconsistent and unconvincing" (Birks, Cochrane Database, 2007) [10]. Many early trials were small or used weak methods, and publication bias could not be ruled out [10].

**Meta-analysis of treatment:** A 2020 meta-analysis of 7 studies (939 patients with Alzheimer's disease), drawing on English and Chinese databases, reported better cognition and global ratings with ginkgo than placebo (Liao et al., Clinical Neuropharmacology, 2020) [11]. NCCIH notes that ginkgo may have a modest benefit for dementia symptoms, particularly at relatively high doses, but the evidence is inconsistent [2].

**Synthesis:** Ginkgo does not prevent dementia: GEM and GuidAge were both negative [7][9]. Results for people who already have dementia are mixed [2][10][11].

### Memory and Cognitive Function in Healthy Adults

For healthy people, the evidence that ginkgo sharpens thinking is weak and inconsistent [2][5].

**Positive evidence (small, single trial):** In a 6-week trial, 188 healthy adults aged 45 to 56 took 240 mg of EGb 761 once daily or placebo. Those on ginkgo recalled more items from a demanding list of appointments, but did no better at recognising a driving route (Kaschel, Phytomedicine, 2011) [12].

**Meta-analysis (negative):** A 2012 meta-analysis of randomised trials in healthy people found effects close to zero for memory (13 trials, 1,132 participants), executive function and attention (Laws et al., Human Psychopharmacology, 2012) [5]. Effects did not vary with age, dose or trial length [5].

**Combination product study:** In a 90-day trial of 100 middle-aged and older adults in China, a Cistanche tubulosa and ginkgo combination raised Mini-Mental State Examination scores from 26.5 to 27.1 and Montreal Cognitive Assessment scores from 23.4 to 25.3, more than placebo, and lowered blood total tau, a cognition-related marker (Chen et al., Phytotherapy Research, 2024) [6]. The product held two herbs, so the result cannot be credited to ginkgo alone.

**Self-reported memory impairment:** In a pilot trial of 61 older adults with subjective memory impairment, EGb 761 at 240 mg daily improved one measure of cognitive flexibility (task switching), with no effect on delayed response or prospective memory (Beck et al., Human Psychopharmacology, 2016) [13].

**Systematic review of cognitive supplements:** A 2021 systematic review of nine supplement ingredients, ginkgo among them, found the studies inconsistent and imprecise, with too little certainty to support recommendations for healthy adults (Crawford et al., Journal of Alternative and Complementary Medicine, 2021) [14]. A 2023 review of herbal and nutritional medicines in older adults, where 8 of 21 trials used a form of ginkgo, rated the overall evidence low quality because of high risk of bias [35].

**NCCIH position:** Ginkgo products are marketed for "brain health," but it is uncertain whether they change thinking in healthy people, and much of the research is low quality [2].

**Synthesis:** Ginkgo is not a reliable cognitive enhancer in healthy adults. The positive findings are narrow, come from short trials, and are not borne out by the meta-analysis [5][12][13].

### Vertigo and Dizziness

Vertigo is one of the few conditions with some, still limited, evidence in favour of ginkgo [2].

**Primary trial:** In a 3-month double-blind trial of 70 people with recent vertigo of unknown cause, 47% of those on ginkgo extract were free of symptoms at the end, against 18% on placebo (Haguenauer, Presse Medicale, 1986) [15].

**Vestibular disorders:** In an open trial of 44 people with vertigo or dizziness from vascular vestibular disorders, EGb 761 80 mg twice daily and the drug betahistine 16 mg twice daily worked about equally over 3 months: symptoms improved in 65% and 64.7% of patients in the first month (Cesarani, Advances in Therapy, 1998) [16]. There was no placebo group.

**NCCIH assessment:** NCCIH says a small amount of evidence suggests ginkgo taken by mouth may help vertigo, but the overall evidence is not conclusive [2].

**Synthesis:** Vertigo is among the more promising uses, but the evidence rests on two small, old trials, one without a placebo group [15][16].

### Tinnitus

Ginkgo is widely promoted for tinnitus (a ringing or buzzing sound in the ears), but the clinical evidence does not support it [2][17].

**Cochrane Review:** A 2022 Cochrane review of 12 trials with 1,915 participants found ginkgo may have little to no effect on tinnitus severity at 3 to 6 months compared with placebo, though the evidence is very uncertain (Sereda et al., Cochrane Database of Systematic Reviews, 2022) [17]. Single trials found little to no difference in loudness or quality of life, and ginkgo did not raise the risk of bleeding, seizures or other side effects [17].

**Post-Cochrane study:** In a trial of 69 people aged 40 to 70 with tinnitus, ginkgo 60 mg twice a day combined with antioxidants improved tinnitus scores more than ginkgo alone or placebo (Chauhan, Journal of Audiology & Otology, 2023) [18]. The benefit was seen with the combination, so it cannot be credited to ginkgo.

**NCCIH position:** NCCIH says research suggests ginkgo is not helpful for relieving tinnitus [2].

**Synthesis:** Despite its popularity for tinnitus, ginkgo has not shown a clear benefit; the Cochrane review found little to no effect, with very uncertain evidence [17].

### Peripheral Vascular Disease and Intermittent Claudication

Intermittent claudication is cramping leg pain on walking, caused by narrowed leg arteries (peripheral arterial disease).

**Clinical evidence:** A 2013 Cochrane review included 14 trials with 739 participants. In 11 placebo-controlled trials, ginkgo increased treadmill walking distance by only 64.5 metres (CI -1.8 to 130.7), and publication bias likely inflated even that [46]. The reviewers found no evidence of a clinically significant benefit for peripheral arterial disease [46].

### Raynaud's Disease

**Clinical trial:** In a small 10-week placebo-controlled trial in people with Raynaud's disease, a standardised ginkgo extract (Seredrin) cut weekly attacks from 13.2 to 5.8, a 56% fall, against a 27% fall on placebo (Muir, Vascular Medicine, 2002) [20]. Only the number of attacks improved significantly [20].

**Synthesis:** One small trial suggests ginkgo may reduce how often Raynaud's attacks happen. It needs repeating in larger studies.

### Diabetic Neuropathy

Ginkgo is not among the drug classes the 2022 American Academy of Neurology guideline summary recommends offering for painful diabetic neuropathy (tricyclic antidepressants, SNRIs, gabapentinoids and sodium channel blockers) [22].

### Anxiety

A 2022 network meta-analysis of 29 trials of 12 herbs for anxiety found ginkgo lowered Hamilton Anxiety Scale scores (MD -4.63, 95% CrI -9.01 to -0.23), but its trials were small and ginkgo ranked worst for tolerability (Zhang et al., Pharmacological Research, 2022) [23]. NCCIH says only a small amount of evidence suggests a benefit for anxiety [2].

### Stroke Rehabilitation

NCCIH says ginkgo might also help in the rehabilitation of people who have had strokes [2].

### Premenstrual Syndrome (PMS)

NCCIH lists PMS among the conditions where a small amount of evidence suggests a benefit from ginkgo by mouth, but the overall evidence is not conclusive [2].

### Glaucoma

In a 2003 crossover trial of 27 people with normal-tension glaucoma, ginkgo extract 40 mg three times daily for 4 weeks appeared to improve existing visual field damage in some patients (Quaranta, Ophthalmology, 2003) [47]. A 2014 crossover trial in 35 Chinese patients, using the same dose, found no effect on visual field or contrast sensitivity (Guo, Investigative Ophthalmology & Visual Science, 2014) [48].

### Conditions Where Ginkgo Is Ineffective

Research suggests ginkgo is not helpful for these conditions:

- **Heart disease prevention:** Research suggests ginkgo is not helpful for preventing heart disease [2].
- **High blood pressure treatment:** Ginkgo is not helpful for treating high blood pressure [2].
- **Multiple sclerosis symptoms:** Research suggests ginkgo is not helpful for relieving symptoms of multiple sclerosis [2].
- **Peripheral arterial disease:** A Cochrane review found no clinically significant benefit [46].

### Summary of Evidence by Condition

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Standard Dosing by Condition

These are the doses used in trials. For most conditions the evidence is weak or negative, so they show what was studied, not what works.

| Condition | Daily Dose | Division | Duration Studied |
|-----------|:-:|:-:|:-:|
| Dementia prevention (GEM, GuidAge) | 120 mg twice daily | Two doses | Median 6.1 years in GEM [7][8]; 5 years in GuidAge [9] |
| Memory function (healthy adults) | 240 mg EGb 761 | Once daily | 6 weeks [12] |
| Vertigo (vestibular disorders) | 80 mg EGb 761 twice daily | Two doses | 3 months [16] |

### Practical Dosing Guidance

**Extract quality matters more than dose.** The large trials used standardised leaf extract such as EGb 761, standardised to flavone glycosides (about 24%) and terpene lactones (about 6%) [9][45]. Supplement quality varies: in one analysis, many dietary supplements held lower levels of the active compounds, and some had more ginkgolic acids [32].

**Splitting the daily dose** is common. GEM and GuidAge gave 120 mg twice daily, while trials in healthy adults often gave 240 mg once daily [7][8][9][12][13].

**Ginkgo leaf extract, not seeds.** The trials tested standardised leaf extracts [7][9][12]. Raw ginkgo seeds are toxic [2].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Safety Profile

For most adults, ginkgo leaf extracts are likely to be safe taken by mouth in moderate amounts, and they have been used safely in studies lasting as long as 6 years [2]. In GEM, side effect profiles were similar on ginkgo and placebo, and the 2007 Cochrane review found no excess side effects compared with placebo [7][10].

The most common side effects are [2]:
- Dizziness
- Gastrointestinal symptoms
- Headache

### Bleeding Risk

Ginkgo may raise bleeding risk in people who take anticoagulant drugs such as warfarin [2]. EGb 761 inhibits platelet aggregation and prolongs bleeding time [45]. The evidence on real bleeding is mixed:

- **Case reports:** A 2005 review of 15 published case reports found a possible causal link between ginkgo and bleeding, including 8 bleeds inside the skull, though 13 of the reports named other bleeding risk factors [38].
- **Clinical trials:** A 2011 meta-analysis of 18 randomised trials (1,985 adults) found no effect on platelet aggregation or standard clotting times, and no sign of a higher bleeding risk with standardised extract [37].
- **Anticoagulants and surgery:** A 2022 review linked ginkgo to bleeding risk in people on anticoagulants, found conflicting evidence on bleeding risk overall, and advised stopping nonessential supplements 2 weeks before surgery (Hatfield et al., Proceedings of Baylor University Medical Center, 2022) [24].
- **Bleeding disorders:** People with known bleeding risks should be told about a possible rise in bleeding risk [38].

Sudden severe headache, new weakness on one side or trouble speaking can mean bleeding in the brain: call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).

### Dermatologic Reactions

Skin reactions to ginkgo are infrequent [27]:

- **Contact dermatitis:** Ginkgo fruit can cause contact dermatitis on the skin [27].
- **Mucosal symptoms:** Eating ginkgo fruit can cause mouth and anal symptoms, and a widespread red rash can follow leaf extract taken by mouth [27].
- **Fixed drug eruption:** One man developed itching and redness on the end of his penis each time he took a supplement containing ginkgo and vinpocetine (Cohen, Journal of Clinical and Aesthetic Dermatology, 2017) [27]. The product also held vinpocetine.
- **Stevens-Johnson syndrome:** A 75-year-old man developed Stevens-Johnson syndrome that progressed to toxic epidermal necrolysis, probably from a ginkgo extract (Yuste, Actas Dermosifiliograficas, 2005) [28]. In these reactions the skin detaches; in toxic epidermal necrolysis more than 30% of the body surface is affected [28]. If you develop blistering or peeling skin, or sores in the mouth or eyes, stop ginkgo and call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK) or go to the emergency department.

### Hyponatremia (Low Sodium)

A 2019 case report links ginkgo to low blood sodium (hyponatraemia) (Hamilton, Internal Medicine Journal, 2019) [29].

### Blood Sugar Effects

In a small study of adults with type 2 diabetes, 3 months of EGb 761 changed insulin responses to a glucose load [41]. The authors suggest ginkgo may speed the clearance of insulin and of diabetes tablets, which could raise blood glucose [41]. If you have diabetes, check with your doctor, and monitor your glucose more closely if you start ginkgo.

### Fertility Concerns

In a laboratory study, high concentrations of ginkgo reduced sperm penetration of hamster eggs [44]. This was a lab study, not a human trial. If you hope to conceive, check with your doctor before taking ginkgo.

### Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

Ginkgo may be unsafe during pregnancy. It might cause early labor or extra bleeding during delivery if used near that time [2]. Little is known about whether it is safe to use ginkgo while breastfeeding [2].

### Cancer Concerns from Animal Studies

In a US National Toxicology Program study, rats and mice were given ginkgo extract by stomach tube 5 days per week for 2 years [31]. Mice given 200 to 2,000 mg per kg of body weight had more liver tumours, including hepatocellular carcinoma and hepatoblastoma, and rats had slightly more thyroid follicular cell adenomas [31].

This was an animal study, and whether it applies to people taking usual doses is unclear.

### Ginkgolic Acids

Ginkgolic acids are among the main toxic compounds in ginkgo [36]. Medicine-grade extracts keep them below 5 ppm, but several dietary supplements tested in Poland had raised levels [32].

### Fresh Ginkgo Seeds: A Distinct Hazard

Fresh ginkgo seeds are toxic when eaten, and serious side effects have also followed roasted seeds or the crude plant [2]. Seed poisoning usually involves children, who can have repeated seizures that may be fatal; the toxin measured is 4-methoxypyridoxine [43]. If anyone has a seizure after eating ginkgo seeds, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).

## 6. Drug Interactions

Ginkgo may interact with other drugs, and NCCIH advises anyone who takes medicine to talk with their health care provider before using it [2]. Reviews of herb-drug interactions include ginkgo among the herbs with clinically relevant interactions [3][34].

### Anticoagulants and Antiplatelet Agents

| Drug | Interaction | Clinical Significance |
|------|:-:|:-:|
| Warfarin and other anticoagulants | Ginkgo may increase bleeding risk | Linked to bleeding in people on anticoagulants [2][24] |
| Aspirin, clopidogrel and other antiplatelet drugs | EGb 761 inhibits platelet aggregation [45], so effects may add up | Ask your doctor before combining |

Trials of clotting tests did not show a higher bleeding risk, but case reports include serious bleeds [37][38]. If you take a blood thinner, check with your doctor or anticoagulation clinic before taking ginkgo.

### Anti-Seizure Medications

Two people with well-controlled epilepsy had seizures again within 2 weeks of starting ginkgo extract, and both were seizure-free months after stopping it [39]. In another case, a man taking Depakote (valproate) and Dilantin died of a breakthrough seizure. Both drugs were at low levels in his blood, and he was taking several supplements, ginkgo prominent among them [40]. The authors proposed that ginkgo speeds the breakdown of these drugs through the CYP2C19 enzyme [40]. If you have epilepsy, check with your doctor before taking ginkgo. If a seizure lasts more than 5 minutes, or seizures come back to back, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).

### Blood Sugar-Lowering Medications

Ginkgo may speed the clearance of diabetes tablets as well as insulin, which could raise blood glucose [41]. If you take glucose-lowering medicine, check with your doctor and monitor your glucose more often.

### Cytochrome P450 Interactions

In 18 healthy Chinese adults, 12 days of ginkgo at 140 mg twice daily lowered blood levels of omeprazole by speeding its breakdown through the CYP2C19 enzyme [42]. The authors warn that ginkgo may weaken omeprazole and other drugs broken down by CYP2C19, though more studies are needed [42].

### Serotonergic Medications

A review of herb-drug interactions in psychiatric and neurological drug treatment lists ginkgo among the herbs with clinically relevant interactions [34]. If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

### Summary: Who Should Avoid Ginkgo or Use with Extreme Caution

- People taking warfarin or other anticoagulants, or antiplatelet drugs [2][24][45]
- People with known bleeding risks [38]
- People with epilepsy [39][40]
- People scheduled for surgery within 2 weeks [24]
- Pregnant or breastfeeding women [2]
- Couples trying to conceive (laboratory data only) [44]
- People with diabetes on glucose-lowering medicines (monitor glucose more often) [41]
- People taking omeprazole or other drugs broken down by CYP2C19 [42]

## 7. Dietary Sources

Ginkgo is not a nutrient, and food does not supply the flavone glycosides and terpene lactones found in leaf extract.

### Traditional Culinary Use of Ginkgo Seeds

Ginkgo seeds are a traditional food and are used in foods and drinks [36][43].

**Important safety warning:** Raw ginkgo seeds are toxic, and poisoning, mostly in children, can cause repeated seizures that may be fatal [2][43]. A 2-year-old girl who ate a large amount of seeds had vomiting and diarrhea, then a seizure; she was treated with pyridoxal phosphate, a form of vitamin B6 [43]. If anyone has a seizure after eating ginkgo seeds, call your local emergency number (111 in NZ, 000 in Australia, 911 in the US, 999 in the UK).

Ginkgo seeds differ chemically from the leaves and are not a substitute for leaf extract [36].

### Ginkgo Leaf Tea

The trials above used standardised leaf extracts, not tea [7][9][12].
