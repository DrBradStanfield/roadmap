## Current evidence position

Brad has not made a video on this topic; this is a summary of the evidence, not his view. The US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health (NCCIH) says there is not enough reliable evidence to tell whether rhodiola helps any health problem, and most research in people is of low to moderate quality [3]. In a 6-week trial in adults with mild to moderate depression, SHR-5 extract at 340 or 680 mg/day eased depression compared with placebo [7], but a 12-week trial in 57 people found no significant difference between rhodiola, sertraline and placebo [8], and a 2022 clinician guideline did not support rhodiola for mood disorders [19]. Fatigue trials conflict, and a systematic review found every included trial at high risk of bias or with reporting flaws [20]. Rhodiola is possibly safe for up to 12 weeks; it may cause dizziness, headache or insomnia, an interaction with the blood pressure drug losartan has been reported, and little is known about its safety in pregnancy or breastfeeding [3].

## Key safety points

- A case report titled "Herbal medicine -- sets the heart racing!" described a potentially dangerous interaction when rhodiola was taken with an SSRI antidepressant [32]. If you take an antidepressant or any other medicine, talk with your doctor before using rhodiola [3].
- In test-tube studies, rhodiola root extracts blocked monoamine oxidase (MAO) [17]. If you take an MAO inhibitor, talk to your doctor before using rhodiola [3][17].
- An interaction with losartan, a blood pressure drug, has been reported [3]. In theory rhodiola could add to blood pressure or diabetes medicines: in the test tube it blocked an enzyme that some blood pressure drugs target [30], and it lowered blood sugar in diabetic mice [31].
- Little is known about whether rhodiola is safe in pregnancy or breastfeeding [3].
- Rhodiola is possibly safe for up to 12 weeks; longer use has not been shown to be safe [3].
- Side effects may include dizziness, headache, insomnia, and dry mouth or excess saliva [3][9].

## 1. Overview

Rhodiola rosea grows in cold regions and at high altitudes in Europe, Asia and North America [3]. It belongs to the Crassulaceae family [29] and is also called arctic root, golden root, rose root, rosenroot or king's crown [3]. Rhodiola has a long history of use in Russia, Scandinavia and other parts of Europe for work performance, endurance, fatigue, depression and altitude sickness [3].

Rhodiola is often called an "adaptogen", a term for herbs said to help the body cope with stress, and Rhodiola species have long been used as adaptogens and anti-stress medicines in Asia and Europe [29]. It is promoted for improving athletic performance, mood and cognition, increasing energy, and reducing stress [3].

The root contains two groups of marker compounds:

- **Rosavins** (rosavin, rosarin and rosin): rosavin is the key marker used to tell *R. rosea* apart from related species [5].
- **Salidroside** (also called rhodioloside): in a rat study, salidroside and its breakdown product tyrosol were the active antidepressant-like compounds, while rosavin, rosarin and rosin on their own were inactive [6].

In the same rat study, a fixed mix of rhodioloside, rosavin, rosarin and rosin was "more active than any of the individual components alone, indicating a synergistic effect" [6]. Most clinical trials use whole-root extracts rather than single compounds.

*R. crenulata*, a Chinese species, is thought to be a common adulterant of *R. rosea* products [5]. Products labeled only "Rhodiola" may contain other species that differ from the extracts tested in trials.

The evidence for rhodiola is preliminary. NCCIH states that "there isn't enough reliable evidence to determine whether rhodiola or its components are useful for any health-related purpose" and that "most of the research in people is of low-to-moderate quality" [3].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Extract vs. Root Powder

The main trials used standardized extracts of *R. rosea* rhizome, not raw root powder [7][11]. An extract holds set amounts of the marker compounds, so doses can be compared across studies.

| Form | Description | Doses in Trials | Notes |
|------|-------------|:--:|-------|
| SHR-5 standardized extract | Rhizome extract | 340 or 680 mg/day | Used in a depression trial [7] and at low doses for fatigue [11][12]. |
| Standardized extract (3% rosavin, 1% salidroside) | Root extract | 200 mg, single dose | Used in an endurance trial [21]. |
| Vitano or Rhodax extracts | Proprietary extracts | Total daily dose of 340 mg (Rhodax); 200 mg twice a day (Vitano) | Used in small anxiety studies [9][13]. |
| Golden root extract | Extract used in short exercise trials | 1,500 mg/day plus 500 mg before testing | 3-day trials [25][26]. |

### Key Standardized Extracts Used in Research

**SHR-5:** A standardized extract of *R. rosea* rhizome, tested in several trials [15]: on depression [7], mental fatigue [11] and exam stress [12].

**Vitano:** A proprietary extract tested at 200 mg twice a day in an anxiety study [13].

**Rhodax:** An extract tested at a total daily dose of 340 mg in an uncontrolled anxiety study [9].

### Identifying Quality Products

When choosing a rhodiola supplement, look for:

- **Species:** *Rhodiola rosea* on the label, not just "Rhodiola". Rosavin is the marker that sets *R. rosea* apart from related species [5].
- **Plant part:** The trials used extracts of the root or rhizome [7][11].

Independent testers such as consumerlab.com and Labdoor publish product test results.

### Adulteration Concerns

A study of about 40 rhodiola products sold to European buyers found that about one fifth of those claiming to be *R. rosea* contained no rosavin [5]. About 80% of the rest had less rosavin than registered products and seemed to contain other Rhodiola species [5].

## 3. Evidence for Benefits

### Depression

Depression is the most studied use, but results are mixed. NCCIH notes that depression can be a serious illness and advises anyone who may have it to consult a health care provider [3].

**Darbinyan et al. (2007) -- Mild to Moderate Depression [7]:** A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in adults aged 18-70 years gave SHR-5 extract at 340 mg/day (31 people) or 680 mg/day (29 people), or placebo (29 people), for six weeks. Both doses improved overall depression, insomnia, emotional instability and somatization, but not self-esteem, while the placebo group did not improve. No serious side effects were reported.

**Mao et al. (2015) -- Comparison with Sertraline [8]:** A phase II randomized trial compared a standardized *R. rosea* extract with sertraline and placebo in 57 people with mild to moderate major depressive disorder over 12 weeks. Key findings:

- Depression scores fell modestly in all three groups, with no significant difference between groups.
- HAM-D scores fell by 8.2 points with sertraline (95% CI -12.7 to -3.6), 5.1 with *R. rosea* (95% CI -8.8 to -1.3) and 4.6 with placebo (95% CI -8.6 to -0.6).
- The odds of improving versus placebo were 1.90 (95% CI 0.44 to 8.20) for sertraline and 1.39 (95% CI 0.38 to 5.04) for *R. rosea*.
- Adverse events were reported by 63.2% on sertraline, 30.0% on *R. rosea* and 16.7% on placebo.
- The researchers concluded that *R. rosea*, although less effective than sertraline, "may possess a more favorable risk to benefit ratio" for mild to moderate depression.

**Gao et al. (2020) -- Combination with Sertraline [14]:** In a randomized, double-blind trial, 100 people with major depressive disorder took sertraline with placebo, or sertraline with rhodiola capsule at 0.3 or 0.6 g/day, for 12 weeks. Depression scores fell most with 0.6 g/day. The result applies to rhodiola added to sertraline, not to rhodiola alone.

**Mechanism:** How rhodiola might affect mood is not understood. A review of lab work points to lower cortisol through the stress hormone system and effects on heat shock proteins and other stress-response proteins [15]. In rats, rhodiola extract raised brain serotonin during nicotine withdrawal [18].

In test-tube studies, methanol and water extracts of *R. rosea* root blocked both MAO A and MAO B, the enzymes that MAO inhibitor antidepressants target [17]. This was a test-tube finding and may not apply when rhodiola is taken by mouth.

**Clinical guidelines:** A 2022 taskforce of the World Federation of Societies of Biological Psychiatry (WFSBP) and the Canadian Network for Mood and Anxiety Treatments (CANMAT) did not support rhodiola for mood disorders [19].

### Anxiety

Evidence for rhodiola in anxiety is very preliminary and comes from small studies without a placebo group.

**Cropley et al. (2015) -- Mild Anxiety [13]:** Eighty mildly anxious people were randomized to Vitano extract (one 200 mg tablet before breakfast and one before lunch) or no treatment for 14 days. The rhodiola group reported less anxiety, stress, anger, confusion and depression, and better overall mood, with no difference in cognitive tests. There was no placebo group and the mood measures were self-reported, which limits the conclusions.

**Bystritsky et al. (2008) -- Generalized Anxiety Disorder [9]:** A pilot study enrolled 10 adults aged 34 to 55 with generalized anxiety disorder (GAD). They took *R. rosea* extract for 10 weeks, at a total daily dose of 340 mg. Findings:

- Scores on the Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale fell significantly.
- Side effects were generally mild or moderate; dizziness and dry mouth were the most common.

With 10 participants and no control group, the results only justify larger trials [9].

### Physical and Mental Fatigue

Rhodiola is widely promoted for fatigue, but small studies give conflicting results.

**Darbinyan et al. (2000) -- Night Shift Physicians [11]:** A double-blind crossover study in 56 young, healthy physicians on night duty tested one tablet a day of SHR-5 extract for two weeks. Tests of associative thinking, short-term memory, calculation, concentration and speed of perception improved significantly during the first two-week period. No side effects were reported. A later systematic review found that every included rhodiola fatigue trial had a high risk of bias or reporting flaws [20].

**Spasov et al. (2000) -- Student Exam Stress [12]:** A double-blind, placebo-controlled pilot study gave a low dose of SHR-5 extract to students for 20 days during exams. Findings:

- Physical fitness, mental fatigue and neuro-motor tests improved most (p <0.01), and self-rated well-being was better (p < 0.05).
- A text-correction test and a tapping test did not improve.
- The authors judged the dose probably too low.

**Punja et al. (2014) -- Nursing Students [10]:** In a randomized, double-blind trial, 48 nursing students aged 18-55 years on shift work took 364 mg of *R. rosea* or placebo on waking, with up to one more capsule in the next four hours, for 42 days. Fatigue worsened with *R. rosea* compared with placebo, on both a vitality scale (-17.3, 95% CI -30.6 to -3.9) and a fatigue scale (1.9, 95% CI 0.4 to 3.5). The authors urged caution in reading the result. The total number of adverse events did not differ between groups [10].

**Systematic Review -- Ishaque et al. (2012) [20]:** Of 11 controlled trials, two of six on physical fatigue and three of five on mental fatigue found a benefit. All had a high risk of bias or reporting flaws, so the evidence is contradictory and a rigorous trial is needed.

**HPA Axis Modulation -- Lopresti et al. (2022) [4]:** A systematic review of randomized human trials of plants and plant compounds, rhodiola among them, found their effect on stress hormones such as cortisol unclear because study designs varied widely.

### Athletic and Exercise Performance

Rhodiola is marketed for exercise performance and endurance. Most studies are very small, and the results are mixed.

**De Bock et al. (2004) -- Endurance [21]:** In a trial, 24 young adults took one 200 mg dose of rhodiola extract (3% rosavin, 1% salidroside) an hour before testing. Results:

- Time to exhaustion in a cycling test was 17.2 minutes, against 16.8 minutes on placebo.
- Peak oxygen uptake (VO2peak) and peak carbon dioxide output (VCO2peak) also rose; muscle strength and reaction time did not change.
- In 12 people who then took 200 mg a day for four weeks, daily use did not change any measure.

A single dose gave a small gain in endurance; four weeks of daily use added nothing.

**Noreen et al. (2013) -- Perceived Exertion [22]:** In a crossover trial, 18 people took 3 mg/kg of body weight of *R. rosea* or placebo one hour before a 6-mile cycling time trial. With rhodiola, heart rate in the warm-up was lower, mean rated exertion was lower (6.0 vs 6.6), and the time trial was faster (25.4 vs 25.8 minutes).

**Duncan et al. (2014) -- Perceived Exertion in Men [23]:** In 10 men taking 3 mg/kg of body weight before 30 minutes of cycling:

- Rated exertion was lower at 30 minutes than with placebo.
- Arousal, pleasure and vigor scores were higher.
- Energy expenditure and fat and carbohydrate use did not change.

Feelings improved without a change in energy use, so the effect may be mainly on perception.

**Walker et al. (2007) -- Muscle Energy Recovery [24]:** In 12 resistance-trained men, 1,500 mg/day of *R. rosea* for 4 days before exhausting forearm exercise made no difference compared with placebo in rated exertion, time to exhaustion or muscle phosphocreatine recovery.

**Ballmann et al. (2019) -- Anaerobic Performance [25]:** In 11 active college-aged women, 1,500 mg/day of golden root extract for 3 days, plus 500 mg 30 minutes before testing, raised mean power, anaerobic capacity and total work over three 15-second sprint tests compared with placebo. The fatigue index did not change.

**Williams et al. (2021) -- Resistance Exercise Performance [26]:** In 10 resistance-trained men on the same dosing, bench press speed rose but the number of repetitions to failure fell compared with placebo. The authors suggest it may help explosive lifting but may impair upper-body strength-endurance.

**Sanz-Barrio et al. (2023) -- Systematic Review [27]:** A review of 13 randomized trials with 263 healthy participants found mixed results. Single doses improved endurance and rated exertion, and longer use improved anaerobic but not endurance performance. Most studies had an unclear or high risk of bias, and the authors call for better trials.

**Summary:** Small trials suggest a single dose may lower perceived effort and slightly improve endurance, and longer use may help sprint (anaerobic) performance [27]. With small trials at risk of bias, rhodiola is not a proven performance aid [27].

### Cognitive Function

Rhodiola is promoted for thinking and memory, but the evidence is limited and comes mostly from the fatigue studies above.

**Crawford et al. (2021) -- Systematic Review of Cognitive Performance [28]:** This review of supplements for thinking in healthy adults included rhodiola and found a low level of certainty in the evidence for the ingredients it assessed.

The fatigue studies found gains in memory, concentration and calculation after night shifts [11] and in neuro-motor tests during exam stress [12]. Any benefit may be limited to people whose performance is impaired by stress or lack of sleep.

### Stress Response and Adaptogenic Effects

The adaptogen idea is central to rhodiola's marketing, though "adaptogen" has no precise drug definition.

**Panossian et al. (2010) [15]:** A review of lab studies listed possible ways rhodiola acts on the stress response:

- Lowering cortisol through the stress hormone (HPA) axis.
- Effects on heat shock proteins such as Hsp70 and other cell defence proteins.
- Effects on the stress-signalling enzyme p-JNK and on nitric oxide.

**Lopresti et al. (2022) [4]:** In randomized human trials, the effect of most plants studied, rhodiola among them, on stress hormones was unclear.

**Tao et al. (2019) [29]:** A review of seven main Rhodiola species covered traditional use, chemistry, pharmacology, toxicity and clinical studies. Lab studies report anti-fatigue, antidepressant, anxiolytic, neuroprotective and cardioprotective effects [15].

Lab findings on stress are interesting, but there is not enough reliable evidence in people to show a real benefit [3].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### General Dosing Ranges

Depression and anxiety trials used 340 or 680 mg/day of standardized extract [7][9], single-dose exercise trials used 200 mg [21], and short exercise trials used 1,500 mg a day [24][25].

### Dosing by Indication

| Indication | Dose Range | Duration Studied | Extract Type | Key Study |
|-----------|:--:|:--:|------|-------|
| Mild to moderate depression | 340 or 680 mg/day | 6 weeks | SHR-5 | Darbinyan 2007 [7] |
| Depression, added to sertraline | 0.3 or 0.6 g/day | 12 weeks | Rhodiola capsule | Gao 2020 [14] |
| Anxiety (preliminary) | Total daily dose of 340 mg; 200 mg twice a day | 2-10 weeks | Vitano or Rhodax | Cropley 2015 [13]; Bystritsky 2008 [9] |
| Mental fatigue (under stress) | One low-dose tablet a day | 2 weeks to 20 days | SHR-5 | Darbinyan 2000 [11]; Spasov 2000 [12] |
| Exercise -- perceived exertion (acute) | 200 mg or 3 mg/kg body weight, single dose | Single dose pre-exercise | Includes 3% rosavin, 1% salidroside | De Bock 2004 [21]; Noreen 2013 [22]; Duncan 2014 [23] |
| Exercise -- sprint and resistance | 1,500 mg/day plus 500 mg before testing | 3 days | Golden root extract | Ballmann 2019 [25]; Williams 2021 [26] |
| Exercise -- muscle recovery | 1,500 mg/day (no benefit observed) | 4 days | *R. rosea* | Walker 2007 [24] |

### Timing and Administration

Trials used these timing patterns:

- For exercise studies, single doses were taken about one hour before exercise [21][22].
- For the anxiety study, one 200 mg tablet was taken before breakfast and one before lunch [13].
- For the depression trial, 340 mg/day was given as two tablets daily and 680 mg/day as two tablets twice a day [7].

### Important Considerations

- Higher doses did not clearly do better for depression alone: at either 340 or 680 mg/day, symptoms improved and self-esteem did not [7]. Added to sertraline, 0.6 g/day did better than 0.3 g/day [14]. In a 12-week comparison with sertraline, a standardized extract did no better than placebo [8].
- In one trial, 364 mg capsules taken for 42 days worsened fatigue compared with placebo [10].
- For exercise, 1,500 mg/day for 4 days did not help muscle recovery [24], while single doses lowered perceived effort in two small trials [22][23].

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Safety Profile

NCCIH states that rhodiola is "possibly safe for up to 12 weeks" [3]. In the anxiety pilot study, side effects were generally mild or moderate [9]. In the sertraline comparison trial (Mao et al., 2015), adverse events were reported by 30.0% of rhodiola users compared with 63.2% of sertraline users and 16.7% of placebo users [8].

### Reported Side Effects

The following side effects have been reported:

| Side Effect | Frequency | Source |
|------------|-----------|----------------|
| Dizziness | Most common in one pilot study | Bystritsky 2008 [9]; NCCIH [3] |
| Dry mouth | Most common in one pilot study | Bystritsky 2008 [9]; NCCIH [3] |
| Headache | Not stated | NCCIH [3] |
| Insomnia | Not stated | NCCIH [3] |
| Excessive saliva production | Not stated | NCCIH [3] |

In one 42-day trial, one rhodiola user each reported nausea, diarrhea with nausea, dark stools or blurred vision; the total number of adverse events matched placebo [10].

### Blood Pressure Effects

In lab tests, *R. rosea* extracts blocked angiotensin-converting enzyme (ACE), the target of some blood pressure drugs [30]. This was a test-tube finding, but in theory rhodiola could lower blood pressure, which matters most if yours is already low or you take blood pressure medicines.

NCCIH notes that interactions between rhodiola and losartan (a blood pressure drug) have been reported [3].

### Blood Sugar Effects

Lab and mouse studies hint that *R. rosea* may reduce blood sugar: extracts blocked the carbohydrate-digesting enzyme alpha-glucosidase in a test tube [30] and lowered blood glucose in diabetic mice [31]. Neither was a human study, but in theory rhodiola could add to the effect of diabetes medicines or lower blood sugar in people prone to low blood sugar.

### Pregnancy and Breastfeeding

Little is known about whether rhodiola is safe during pregnancy or while breastfeeding [3]. Until more is known, it is safest to avoid it while pregnant or breastfeeding.

### Duration of Use

Most clinical trials lasted 2-12 weeks, and NCCIH rates rhodiola possibly safe only for up to 12 weeks [3]. If you plan to use it for longer, talk to your doctor first.

## 6. Drug Interactions

### Antidepressants

**SSRIs (Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors):** A case report titled "Herbal medicine -- sets the heart racing!" described a potentially dangerous interaction when rhodiola was taken with an SSRI antidepressant [32]. If you take an antidepressant, talk with your doctor before using rhodiola [3].

**MAO Inhibitors:** In test-tube studies, *R. rosea* root extracts blocked MAO A and MAO B [17]. This may not happen when rhodiola is taken by mouth. If you take an MAO inhibitor, talk to your doctor before using rhodiola [3][17].

**Sertraline combination:** In one trial, the higher dose of rhodiola capsule added to sertraline lowered depression scores more than sertraline with placebo [14]. Given the case report with an SSRI [32], any combination of rhodiola with an antidepressant should be supervised by a doctor.

### Antihypertensive Medications

NCCIH notes reported interactions with losartan (Cozaar) [3]. Because a test-tube study found rhodiola blocks ACE [30], it could in theory add to the effect of other blood pressure drugs. If you take one, talk with your doctor before using rhodiola [3].

### Blood Sugar-Lowering Medications

Because a test-tube study [30] and a study in diabetic mice [31] suggest rhodiola may lower blood sugar, it could in theory add to the effect of diabetes medicines. If you take one, talk with your doctor before using rhodiola [3].

### Other Potential Interactions

If you take prescription medicines, check with your doctor or pharmacist before starting.

### General Guidance

NCCIH advises: "If you take any type of medicine, talk with your health care provider before using any herbal product; some herbs and medicines interact in harmful ways" [3]. This matters because rhodiola's drug interactions have not been studied systematically.

## 7. Dietary Sources

Rhodiola rosea is not a dietary nutrient and is not found in normal foods. It is taken as a supplement made from the root and rhizome of the *R. rosea* plant.

### Traditional Preparations

Rhodiola has a long history of use in Russia, Scandinavia and other parts of Europe [3]. Rhodiola species have long been used in Asia and Europe as a tonic, antidepressant and anti-stress medicine or functional food [29], and several Chinese species are used in traditional Chinese medicine [5].

### Modern Supplement Forms

Today, rhodiola is sold in several forms:

- **Capsules and tablets:** The most common form, and the form used in most clinical trials.
- **Tinctures and liquid extracts:** Alcohol-based extracts. Rarely studied in trials, so their doses are hard to compare with the research.
- **Powdered root:** Ground dried root, sometimes in capsules or added to teas and smoothies. Less concentrated than standardized extracts.
- **Combination products:** Sometimes combined with other adaptogens such as ashwagandha, ginseng or eleuthero (Siberian ginseng). These products have not been studied as closely as rhodiola alone.

Since rhodiola is not an essential nutrient, there is no RDA (Recommended Dietary Allowance), adequate intake or daily value for it. Dosing is based on the preparations used in clinical trials (see Section 4).
