## 1. Overview

Valerian (*Valeriana officinalis*) is a perennial herb native to Europe and temperate regions of Asia, whose roots and rhizomes have been used as a sedative and anxiolytic for over 2,000 years [1][2]. Hippocrates described its therapeutic properties in the 5th century BCE, Dioscorides recommended it for sleep disturbances in the 1st century CE, and Galen prescribed valerian for insomnia in the 2nd century [3][4][5]. During medieval Europe, valerian earned the name "All-Heal" for its broad application in treating ailments ranging from plague symptoms to nervous disorders [6][7]. In the 16th century, it was used to treat nervousness, trembling, headaches, and heart palpitations [8]. During World War II, it was distributed in England to help civilians cope with the stress of air raids [9].

Today, valerian remains one of the most commonly used herbal supplements for sleep and anxiety. In the United States, it is sold as a dietary supplement and holds Generally Recognized as Safe (GRAS) status from the FDA for use as a flavoring agent and dietary supplement ingredient, but it is not approved as a prescription drug for any medical condition [10][11]. Dietary supplements are regulated as foods, not drugs, so premarket evaluation and approval by the FDA are not required unless specific disease prevention or treatment claims are made [11].

The genus *Valeriana* includes over 250 species, but *V. officinalis* is the species most commonly used in supplements and is the focus of nearly all clinical research [2][11]. Other names for valerian include setwall (English), Valerianae radix (Latin), Baldrianwurzel (German), and phu (Greek) [11]. The plant's therapeutic effects are attributed to a complex mixture of bioactive compounds concentrated in the roots, including valerenic acids (sesquiterpenoids), valepotriates (iridoid esters), volatile oils, alkaloids, and flavonoids. No single active compound has been definitively identified as responsible for valerian's effects; rather, the activity likely results from multiple constituents acting independently or synergistically [12][13][14].

The evidence for valerian's efficacy is mixed. While some clinical trials show modest improvements in subjective sleep quality and sleep latency, systematic reviews and meta-analyses generally conclude that the evidence is inconclusive for objective sleep parameters [15][16][17]. For anxiety, preliminary data suggest a possible benefit, but large-scale trials are lacking. Despite these uncertainties, valerian's favorable safety profile — with few adverse effects reported beyond occasional mild gastrointestinal symptoms — has sustained its popularity as a gentle alternative to pharmaceutical sedatives [10][18].

## 2. Forms and Bioavailability

### Active Compounds

Valerian root contains several classes of bioactive compounds, with varying concentrations depending on plant genetics, growing conditions, harvest timing, and extraction methods [12][19][20]:

**Valerenic acids** are the most pharmacologically studied constituents and include three primary forms:
- **Valerenic acid (VA):** The principal sesquiterpenoid, shown to modulate GABA-A receptors in the brain, producing anxiolytic effects [21][22]. VA acts as a subunit-specific allosteric modulator, potentiating GABA-A receptors containing beta-2 or beta-3 subunits, potentially binding near the loreclezole site [22].
- **Acetoxyvalerenic acid (AVA):** May actually block the anxiolytic effect of VA in some models [23].
- **Hydroxyvalerenic acid (HVA):** Formed by degradation of AVA; may enhance the activity of valerenic acid even though it is a degradation product [23].

Good-quality valerian root powders contain at least 0.1% valerenic acids, while powdered extracts are typically standardized to 0.4-0.8% valerenic acids [10]. The European Pharmacopoeia mandates a minimum of 0.17% sesquiterpenic acids (expressed as valerenic acid) in cut root material, alongside at least 0.5 mL/kg essential oil content [20][24].

**Valepotriates** (valtrate, isovaltrate, and didrovaltrate) constitute 0.1-2% of the dry root weight [19]. These iridoid esters are unstable and degrade rapidly during storage, aqueous extraction, or exposure to heat, light, or acidic conditions, with losses of up to 50% within months [20][24]. Their breakdown product, baldrinal, is a cytotoxic aldehyde [19]. Their instability makes it difficult to assess their contribution to clinical effects; they may not be present in significant amounts in many commercial preparations [12][13].

**Volatile oils** (0.2-1% of root material) contribute to valerian's characteristic strong, musty odor and include sesquiterpenoids such as valeranone (0.5-18% of the oil) and esters like bornyl isovalerate (0.2-2% of the oil) [25][26].

**Other constituents** include alkaloids (e.g., chatinine at trace levels up to 0.05%), flavonoids including hesperidin, and GABA itself — though whether exogenous GABA from valerian extracts crosses the blood-brain barrier to contribute to sedative effects is uncertain [25][27][28]. Glutamine, present in aqueous but not alcohol extracts, may cross the blood-brain barrier and be converted to GABA [28]. Gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS) profiling has identified over 100 compounds in root extracts [29].

### Supplement Forms

| Form | Description | Typical Dose | Valerenic Acid Content |
|------|-------------|:---:|:---:|
| Standardized extract (capsules/tablets) | Concentrated root extract, often ethanol- or water-extracted | 300-900 mg | 0.4-0.8% (standardized) |
| Root powder (capsules) | Dried, ground root in capsules | 2,000-3,000 mg | ~0.1% (variable) |
| Tincture | Alcohol-based extract, typically 1:5 ratio with 60-80% ethanol | 4-5 mL | Variable |
| Tea | Dried root steeped in hot water (2-3 g per cup, 10-15 min) | 2,000-3,000 mg dried root | Variable; often low |
| Combination products | Valerian combined with hops, lemon balm, or other herbs | Varies by product | Varies |

**Standardized extracts** are the most commonly studied form in clinical trials and provide the most consistent valerenic acid content. Many clinical trials have used LI 156 (Sedonium), a standardized commercial preparation of dried valerian root extract [30][31][32][33]. Many extracts are standardized to 0.8% valerenic acids, with common potencies delivering 450 mg of total extract per dose [24].

**Root powder** products require higher doses (2-3 grams) to achieve equivalent valerenic acid levels compared to concentrated extracts. Root powder products may provide greater amounts of contaminants, such as lead [10].

**Teas** are unpredictable in valerenic acid delivery. An analysis of three valerian root teas purchased in Canada found that only one provided more than 2 mg of valerenic acids per cup (made with 2-3 grams of dried root), while the other two provided only 0.14-0.645 mg per cup [34].

**Combination products** containing valerian with hops, lemon balm, or other herbs are widely available. Unless the specific combination has been clinically tested, it is difficult to know whether such products are effective. Theoretically, lower doses of valerian may possibly suffice for products containing other ingredients believed to have tranquilizing or sedative effects, but this remains speculative [10].

### Bioavailability and Quality Considerations

Valerenic acid content varies significantly among plants depending on when they are harvested. Sesquiterpenic acids (including valerenic acid) peak in roots harvested from second-year or older plants, when concentrations can reach up to approximately 1.2% [35]. Valepotriates peak in autumn-harvested roots at the end of the first growing year [35]. Harvesting in late autumn, post-frost, maximizes root biomass and bioactive yields [36].

Post-harvest handling significantly affects quality. Valepotriates are thermolabile and hydrolyze rapidly under heat, light, or acidic conditions [20][24]. Storage at low temperatures (5 degrees C) and controlled humidity preserves integrity [24]. Quality control is further complicated by potential adulteration with related species; HPLC analysis of marker compounds like valerenic acids is the primary method for detecting adulterants and confirming product authenticity [37][38].

Extraction methods also matter. Solvent extraction commonly employs 60-80% ethanol (v/v), with maceration for 2-4 weeks at room temperature to extract valerenic acids and other sesquiterpenoids [20][39]. Supercritical CO2 extraction (10-40 MPa, 40-60 degrees C) produces purer essential oils with up to 95% recovery of target sesquiterpenes while avoiding residual solvents [40]. Ultrasound-assisted extraction can reduce maceration time from weeks to hours while maintaining comparable yields [40].

Valerian extracts and powders typically have a characteristic strong, musty odor. Products commonly have flavors added (spearmint, peppermint, vanilla) to help mask this odor [10]. Tablets may incorporate hydroxypropyl methylcellulose (HPMC) coatings to mask the odor and improve palatability [41].

Because dietary supplements are not always tested for manufacturing consistency, the composition may vary considerably between manufacturing lots [11].

## 3. Evidence for Health Benefits

### Sleep and Insomnia

Valerian is most commonly used as a sleep aid, although the clinical evidence remains mixed. Several systematic reviews have concluded that the overall evidence for valerian's sleep-promoting effects is inconclusive, while individual trials show varying degrees of benefit [15][16][17].

#### Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses

*Taibi et al., 2007:* A systematic review concluded that conventional valerian extracts are "probably not effective for treating insomnia" [15]. This remains one of the most cited assessments of the valerian evidence base.

*Stevinson and Ernst, 2000:* An earlier systematic review evaluated nine randomized, placebo-controlled, double-blind clinical trials and concluded they were not sufficient for determining the effectiveness of valerian for sleep disorders [16]. Reviewers identified fundamental quality issues: none of the studies checked the success of blinding, none calculated the sample size necessary for detecting a statistical effect, only one partially controlled pre-bedtime variables [30], and only one validated outcome measures [42]. Although all nine trials had methodological flaws, three earned the highest quality rating (5 on a scale of 1 to 5) and are described in detail below.

*Bent et al., 2006:* A systematic review and meta-analysis of 16 RCTs found that valerian may modestly improve subjective sleep quality — such as reduced sleep latency and enhanced feelings of refreshment upon waking — though objective measures like polysomnography showed inconsistent results due to methodological limitations [17].

*Shinjyo et al., 2020:* A systematic review indicated that valerian appears more effective for chronic insomnia than acute episodes, with some trials reporting improvements in sleep efficiency among participants with mild sleep disturbances [43].

*2024 umbrella review:* An umbrella review of eight systematic reviews involving over 15,000 participants concluded there was no evidence of benefit for objective sleep parameters such as total sleep time or number of awakenings, while noting a favorable safety profile [44].

#### Individual Clinical Trials

**Leathwood et al., 1982 (n=128, highest-quality trial):** In a repeated-measures design, 128 volunteers were given 400 mg of an aqueous extract of valerian, a commercial preparation containing 60 mg valerian and 30 mg hops, and a placebo [42]. Participants took each of the three preparations three times in random order on nine nonconsecutive nights and filled out a questionnaire the morning after each treatment. Compared with placebo, the valerian extract resulted in a statistically significant subjective improvement in time required to fall asleep, sleep quality, and number of nighttime awakenings. The effect was more pronounced in a subgroup of 61 self-identified poor sleepers. The commercial valerian-hops preparation did not produce statistically significant improvements. Limitations: having insomnia was not required for participation, and the study had a 22.9% participant withdrawal rate.

**Leathwood and Chauffard, 1985 (n=8, highest-quality trial):** Eight volunteers with mild insomnia (usually had problems falling asleep) were evaluated using wrist-worn activity meters and morning questionnaires [45]. Test samples were 450 mg or 900 mg of aqueous valerian extract and placebo. Each volunteer was randomly assigned to receive one test sample each night, Monday through Thursday, for 3 weeks (12 nights total). The 450 mg dose reduced average sleep latency from about 16 to 9 minutes, an effect similar in magnitude to prescription benzodiazepine medication. On a 9-point scale, participants rated sleep latency as 4.3 after the 450 mg sample versus 4.9 after placebo. The 900 mg dose did not further reduce sleep latency and was associated with increased next-morning sleepiness. While statistically significant, the approximately 7-minute reduction in sleep latency and the improvement in subjective sleep rating may not be clinically significant. The very small sample size (n=8) limits generalizability.

**Vorbach et al., 1996 (n=121, highest-quality trial):** A 28-day RCT in which 121 participants with documented nonorganic insomnia received 600 mg of a standardized commercial preparation of dried valerian root (LI 156, Sedonium) or placebo [30]. After 28 days, the valerian group showed decreased insomnia symptoms on all assessment tools compared to placebo. Importantly, the differences in improvement between valerian and placebo increased between the day 14 and day 28 assessments, suggesting valerian's benefits may develop gradually with continued use.

**Dorn, 2000 (n=75):** A randomized, double-blind study compared 600 mg valerian extract (LI 156) with 10 mg oxazepam (a benzodiazepine) over 28 days in 75 participants with nonorganic insomnia [31]. Both groups showed equal improvement in sleep quality, and the valerian group reported fewer side effects than the oxazepam group. However, the study was designed to test superiority (not equivalence), so the results cannot be used to claim the two treatments are equally effective.

**Donath et al., 2000 (n=16, polysomnography):** A randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled crossover study using polysomnographic techniques to objectively measure sleep stages, latency, and total sleep time [33]. Sixteen participants with documented nonorganic insomnia received either a single dose and a 14-day administration of 600 mg valerian extract (LI 156) or placebo. Valerian had no effect on 14 of 15 objective or subjective measurements. The single positive finding was a decrease in slow-wave sleep onset latency (13.5 minutes for valerian versus 21.3 minutes for placebo). Slow-wave sleep is the deepest stage of non-REM sleep, during which arousability, skeletal muscle tone, heart rate, blood pressure, and respiratory frequency decrease. Because only 1 of 15 endpoints was positive, the possibility that this was a chance finding must be considered. The valerian group reported fewer adverse events than the placebo group.

**Shekhar et al., 2023 (n=72):** An RCT among 72 men and women (average age 34) with mild to moderate difficulty sleeping found that taking 200 mg of valerian root powder extract standardized to 2% valerenic acid (4 mg valerenic acid per nightly dose) one hour before bedtime for two months significantly decreased sleep latency by 36 minutes and increased total sleep time by 11 minutes compared to placebo [46]. However, those who took valerian reported only a slight improvement in sleep quality compared to placebo. The study was funded by the extract manufacturer (OmniActive Health Technologies), and the product used was Sleeproot.

**Shekhar et al., 2024 (follow-up analysis):** A subsequent analysis of the same study showed the longer sleep time occurred on the first night, although there were no improvements that night in time to fall asleep or sleep quality [47].

**Bliwise et al., 2007 (Parkinson's disease):** A study among people with Parkinson's disease, who often experience sleep disturbances, failed to show benefit overall. Further analyses suggested a possible benefit in male participants [48]. It is worth noting that many conventional sedative-hypnotic drugs also fail to work in Parkinson's disease patients. ConsumerLab confirmed the quality of the product used in this trial both prior to and at the end of the trial.

**Taavoni et al., 2011 (postmenopausal women, n=not specified):** An RCT evaluated valerian in postmenopausal women aged 50-60 years who were generally healthy but suffered from insomnia [49]. Among those who took 530 mg of valerian root extract (standardized to 0.5-1% valerenic acids) twice daily for 4 weeks, 30% reported improvement in their sleep compared to only 4% in the placebo group. Sleep quality was judged according to sleep onset latency, overnight awakenings, and daytime dysfunction. No adverse events were reported.

**Shekhar et al., 2023 (young adults):** A separate 2023 RCT demonstrated that a standardized valerian extract improved both subjective and objective sleep parameters in young adults with mild insomnia, outperforming placebo in sleep latency reduction, suggesting dry extracts may enhance absorption of active compounds like valerenic acid [50].

#### Synthesis

Valerian may modestly improve subjective sleep quality in some populations, particularly in individuals with mild to moderate chronic insomnia. Effects appear to develop gradually, with greater benefits emerging after 2-4 weeks of regular use. However, objective polysomnographic evidence is weak and inconsistent. The quality of many studies is limited by small sample sizes, high dropout rates, variable sources and amounts of valerian used, different outcomes measured, and industry funding. Valerian is unlikely to be effective for severe insomnia or for acute sleep difficulties. Its effects, if present, appear modest compared to prescription sleep medications.

### Anxiety and Stress

Valerian has been promoted for reducing daytime anxiety and stress, and its mechanism of action through GABA-A receptor modulation provides biological plausibility for anxiolytic effects [22]. However, clinical evidence remains limited, and any potential benefit appears to be modest at best.

**Andreatini et al., 2002 (n=36):** A small randomized, placebo-controlled pilot study evaluated the effect of valepotriates (a valerian component) on generalized anxiety disorder (GAD) [51]. Thirty-six participants received 600 mg/day of valepotriate extract, 10 mg/day of diazepam (Valium), or placebo for four weeks. The study failed to find statistically significant differences in overall anxiety among the three groups. However, it suggested that valerian may have a potential effect specifically in reducing the psychic symptoms of anxiety (mental agitation), warranting further investigation. The valepotriate extract did reduce Hamilton Anxiety Rating Scale scores compared to placebo, suggesting preliminary efficacy.

**Kennedy et al., 2006 (valerian/lemon balm combination):** A study tested the combination of valerian extract and lemon balm extract, administering 120 mg, 240 mg, or 360 mg of valerian extract three times daily [52]. The lowest dose (120 mg three times daily, totaling 360 mg/day) decreased anxiety during laboratory stress tests. However, the highest dose (360 mg three times daily, totaling 1,080 mg/day) paradoxically increased anxiety. Cognitive function decreased with all three dose levels, a concerning finding that complicates the risk-benefit assessment for anxiety.

**Preclinical evidence:** Valerenic acid has been shown to modulate GABA-A receptors in animal models. A 2014 study demonstrated that valerenic acid content correlates with anxiolytic activity in mouse models [53]. A mouse study by Felgentreff et al. (2012) suggested that VA may be most important for reducing anxiety while AVA may block this effect, and HVA may enhance VA activity [23]. Other small studies have found weak evidence that valerian may produce calming effects in stressful situations [10].

#### Synthesis

The evidence for valerian as an anxiolytic is limited to small pilot studies with methodological limitations. The dose-response finding from the Kennedy 2006 study — where higher doses increased anxiety while lower doses reduced it — is particularly notable and underscores the need for careful dose selection. Larger, well-designed RCTs are needed before valerian can be recommended for anxiety management.

### Restless Legs Syndrome

There is no good evidence that valerian decreases the severity of symptoms in people with restless legs syndrome (RLS).

**Cuellar and Ratcliffe, 2009 (n=37):** A small clinical trial involving 37 men and women (average age 49) with generally moderate to severe RLS found that taking two tablets containing a total of 800 mg of valerian root (providing 1.16 mg total valerenic acid) daily did not significantly reduce the severity of RLS compared to placebo [54].

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine 2024 clinical practice guidelines explicitly recommend against the use of valerian for restless legs syndrome due to lack of evidence [55].

### Menopausal Hot Flashes

**Mirabi and Mojab, 2013 (n=100):** A double-blind RCT with 100 menopausal women found that 2.5 mg of valerian extract twice daily for eight weeks significantly reduced the frequency and severity of hot flashes compared to placebo, indicating potential relief for vasomotor symptoms [56]. This is a single trial and the dose used (2.5 mg) is substantially lower than doses used in sleep studies. Replication is needed before this can be considered a standard recommendation.

### Other Traditional Uses

Valerian has historically been used for gastrointestinal spasms and distress, epileptic seizures, and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder (ADHD). However, scientific evidence is not sufficient to support the use of valerian for these conditions [10][11]. In traditional Ayurvedic medicine, a related species known as Tagara (*Valeriana wallichii*) has been employed for centuries to alleviate anxiety, promote mental calmness, and support sleep [57]. In traditional Chinese medicine (TCM), valerian is referred to as Xie Cao and used to relieve digestive spasms and promote Qi flow [58]. Various Native American tribes prepared valerian root teas to ease anxiety and promote sleep [59]. No robust clinical trials support valerian's use for ADHD or pain relief, with reviews highlighting insufficient data and lack of significant outcomes in preliminary studies [10].

## 4. Recommended Dosing

### Dosing by Indication

**For insomnia (primary use):**
- **Standardized extract:** 300-900 mg, taken 30 minutes to 2 hours before bedtime [10][30][46]. The most commonly studied dose is 600 mg of standardized extract. A common daily dose of 600 mg standardized to 0.4-0.8% valerenic acids provides roughly 2-5 mg of valerenic acids.
- **Root powder (non-extract):** 2,000-3,000 mg before bed, also yielding approximately 2-5 mg of valerenic acids since good-quality root powders contain at least 0.1% valerenic acids [10].
- **Duration:** Benefits may take several weeks of nightly use to develop fully. The Vorbach 1996 trial showed continued improvement between day 14 and day 28 [30]. Plan on at least 2-4 weeks of consistent use before assessing efficacy.

**For anxiety (speculative use):**
- 120-240 mg of valerian extract, two to three times daily [52]. The Kennedy 2006 study found that higher doses (360 mg three times daily) paradoxically increased anxiety, so lower doses may be preferable. The anti-anxiety indication is speculative and not well established.

**For menopausal hot flashes:**
- The single positive trial used 2.5 mg of valerian extract twice daily for 8 weeks [56]. This requires replication.

### Valerenic Acid Targets

The clinically studied range for valerenic acid intake is approximately 2-5 mg per day. Different product forms achieve this at different total doses:

| Product Type | Total Dose | Expected Valerenic Acids |
|-------------|:---:|:---:|
| Extract standardized to 0.8% | 450-600 mg | 3.6-4.8 mg |
| Extract standardized to 0.4% | 600-900 mg | 2.4-3.6 mg |
| Root powder (0.1% VA) | 2,000-3,000 mg | 2-3 mg |
| Tea (2-3 g dried root) | 2,000-3,000 mg | 0.14-2+ mg (highly variable) |

### How to Read a Supplement Label

Consumers should verify the following information on valerian product labels, all of which are required by the FDA [10]:

- **Species of valerian:** Look for *Valeriana officinalis* (the claim of "valerian" should denote this species alone)
- **Part of the plant used:** Should be "root" and may also state "rhizome"
- **Form of valerian:** Root powder, extract, or tincture
- **Amount per pill or dose:** In milligrams (mg) or grams (g) — 1 gram = 1,000 milligrams
- **Valerenic acid content** (preferable but not required): Look for standardization claims (e.g., "standardized to 0.8% valerenic acids"); the concentration of total valerenic acids should be indicated for extracts

Many products suggest lower doses of valerian with lower amounts of valerenic acids than those shown to work in clinical trials for insomnia. Such products may be less effective [10].

### Comparison with Evidence-Based Sleep Alternatives

For those seeking evidence-based sleep support, it is worth noting that other compounds have stronger clinical evidence than valerian:

- **Melatonin** (0.3-0.5 mg) has a well-established mechanism for circadian rhythm regulation
- **Magnesium glycinate** (250-300 mg elemental) has been shown to reduce sleep onset latency by approximately 17 minutes in a meta-analysis of RCTs in older adults
- **Glycine** (2,500-3,000 mg) has demonstrated improved subjective sleep quality in three RCTs

## 5. Safety and Side Effects

### General Safety Profile

Single-ingredient valerian products made from *Valeriana officinalis* are generally considered very safe at recommended doses [10][18]. In most clinical studies, few side effects have been observed beyond occasional, minor gastrointestinal symptoms or allergic reactions.

The acute toxicity of valerian is low. Oral LD50 values exceed 15 g/kg in rats for essential oil and over 96 g/kg in mice for aqueous extracts, indicating a wide safety margin for typical human doses [60]. Recent 2024 studies confirm no genotoxicity and high acute toxicity thresholds [61].

### Reported Adverse Effects

**Common (mild):**
- Headache
- Dizziness
- Gastrointestinal disturbances (nausea, stomach discomfort)
- Pruritus (itching)
- Vivid dreams (potentially due to GABA receptor modulation enhancing dream recall during REM sleep) [62]

In clinical trials, the rates of these adverse effects were similar between valerian and placebo groups [33][45].

**Uncommon/Anecdotal:**
- Stimulant effect (paradoxical) — headache, restlessness, or dilation of the pupils. This is poorly documented and based largely on anecdotal evidence [10].

**Next-morning effects:** In one study, 900 mg of valerian produced an increase in sleepiness the next morning [45]. However, a separate study found that 600 mg of valerian (LI 156) did not have a clinically significant effect on reaction time, alertness, or concentration the morning after ingestion [63]. Valerian does not generally cause drowsiness upon waking when used for insomnia at standard doses (approximately 600 mg of extract) [10].

**Mental function:** According to most studies, valerian does not adversely affect mental function at standard doses [10][63]. However, the Kennedy 2006 study found that all three dose levels of a valerian/lemon balm combination decreased cognitive function [52]. One other study found slightly impaired attention immediately after using the herb, which could mean that it is unsafe to drive or operate machinery within six to eight hours after use [10].

### Valepotriate Safety

Valepotriates had cytotoxic activity in vitro but were not carcinogenic in animal studies [64][65][66][67]. They are unstable compounds that break down rapidly during storage and in aqueous environments, so they may not be present in significant amounts in finished commercial products [12][13]. Species of valerian other than *V. officinalis* may contain excessive levels of valtrate, a compound shown to be toxic to cells in laboratory tests [10].

### Hepatotoxicity

Hepatotoxicity is rare and has primarily been associated with combination products rather than single-ingredient valerian. Some valerian products contain other herbs with potential liver toxicity, particularly kava kava (which has shown a worrisome potential to damage the liver) and skullcap [10][68]. Reports of liver toxicity with valerian-skullcap combination products may have been due to inadvertent substitution of the liver-toxic herb germander in place of skullcap [10]. Isolated case reports of elevated liver enzymes associated with valerian use usually resolve after stopping the herb [69].

Patients with pre-existing liver disease should use valerian with caution, given these rare hepatotoxicity reports, particularly with combination products [70].

### Withdrawal and Dependency

Clinical studies have not found withdrawal symptoms upon discontinuation of valerian [10]. However, there is one case report describing cardiac complications and delirium associated with valerian root withdrawal after long-term, high-dose use (Garges et al., JAMA, 1998) [71]. This case report only weakly suggests withdrawal symptoms could occur under some circumstances. Long-term use may carry a mild risk of dependency, with reports of anxiety, insomnia, or restlessness upon abrupt cessation after extended periods [2]. It may be prudent to taper doses gradually after extended use rather than stopping abruptly [10].

### Overdose

Overdose symptoms can include tremors, mydriasis (pupil dilation), fatigue, and abdominal cramps, though these are uncommon and generally self-limiting within 24 hours [72]. One case report described a suicide attempt with a massive valerian overdose; it was not possible to clearly attribute all symptoms to valerian (Willey et al., *Vet Hum Toxicol*, 1995) [73].

### Special Populations

**Pregnancy and nursing:** The safety of valerian has not been well evaluated for pregnant or nursing women. Valerian should be avoided during pregnancy due to potential risks of fetal cytotoxicity and maternal hepatotoxicity, as safety data are insufficient [74][75]. Women who have a chance of becoming pregnant should not use valerian without medical advice [10].

**Children:** Children younger than 3 years old should not take valerian because the possible risks have not been evaluated [74]. Safety data for older children are also limited.

**Pre-surgical patients:** Patients scheduled for surgery should discontinue valerian at least 2 weeks prior to minimize interactions with anesthetics and reduce withdrawal risks from chronic use [70][76].

## 6. Drug Interactions

Valerian may interact with several categories of medications, primarily through pharmacodynamic mechanisms (additive sedative effects) and potentially through pharmacokinetic pathways (cytochrome P450 inhibition).

### Additive Sedation (Primary Concern)

Valerian could have an additive effect when combined with other CNS depressants because it is thought to affect the central nervous system via GABA modulation [10][11][77]. The following combinations should be avoided or used only under medical supervision:

| Drug/Substance | Examples | Risk |
|---------------|---------|------|
| Benzodiazepines | Alprazolam (Xanax), diazepam (Valium), lorazepam (Ativan), triazolam (Halcion) | Additive sedation and drowsiness [11][77] |
| Barbiturates and CNS depressants | Phenobarbital (Luminal), morphine, propofol (Diprivan) | Enhanced CNS depression [11][77] |
| Alcohol | — | Compounded sedation; classified as moderate interaction [10][78] |
| General anesthetics | Various | May prolong emergence from anesthesia or heighten sedative requirements; valerian may interact with GABA receptors targeted by anesthetic agents [70][76] |
| Sedative dietary supplements | St. John's wort, kava, melatonin | Additive therapeutic and adverse sedative effects [77] |

Individuals taking any of these medications or supplements should discuss the use of valerian with their healthcare providers [11].

### Pharmacokinetic Interactions (CYP450)

In vitro evidence suggests valerian extracts can inhibit cytochrome P450 3A4 (CYP3A4)-mediated metabolism, which is involved in processing many drugs including certain statins and immunosuppressants [79][80]. However, in vivo human studies show minimal clinical relevance. A study by Donovan et al. (2004) found that multiple night-time doses of valerian had minimal effects on CYP3A4 activity and no effect on CYP2D6 activity in healthy volunteers [80]. This suggests that while in vitro inhibition exists, it does not translate to clinically significant drug interactions at standard valerian doses.

### Regulatory Guidance

The European Medicines Agency (EMA) emphasizes caution with CNS depressants, noting that valerian's mild sedative action may enhance their effects, though no formal contraindications beyond hypersensitivity are specified in the 2016 herbal monograph [78]. No major interactions with foods have been identified, apart from alcohol, which is classified as a moderate interaction due to compounded sedation [78].

### Laboratory Tests

Although valerian has not been reported to influence laboratory tests, this has not been rigorously studied [3][74][77].

## 7. Dietary Sources

Valerian is not a nutrient obtained from the diet in the way that vitamins and minerals are. It is strictly an herbal supplement derived from the roots and rhizomes of the *Valeriana officinalis* plant.

### Plant Distribution and Habitat

*Valeriana officinalis* is native to Europe and temperate regions of Asia. Its natural distribution spans the British Isles, Scandinavia, Central Europe, and extends eastward to the Caucasus and northwestern Iran. In Asia, it occurs in temperate zones from Siberia to the Himalayas [81][82]. The plant was introduced to North America by European colonists in the 17th-18th centuries for medicinal purposes and has since naturalized across much of the northern United States and Canada, particularly in the Northeast, Midwest, and Pacific Northwest [82][83].

The plant is a perennial that prefers moist, fertile, well-drained soils in damp meadows, woodlands, riverbanks, and stream banks. It thrives in full sun to partial shade, tolerates temperatures down to -34 degrees C (USDA Zone 4), and can grow at altitudes up to approximately 2,000 meters in its native range [82][84].

### Harvesting and Preparation

For medicinal use, the roots and rhizomes are harvested — typically in late autumn from second-year or older plants, when valerenic acid concentrations peak [35][36]. The dried roots are then processed into various supplement forms:

- **Dried root powder** — ground and encapsulated at doses of 300-600 mg per capsule
- **Extracts** — concentrated via ethanol or water-alcohol extraction, typically using 60-80% ethanol
- **Tinctures** — alcohol-based extracts at a 1:5 ratio of plant material to solvent, dosed at 4-5 mL per serving
- **Teas** — dried root steeped in hot water for 10-15 minutes (2-3 grams per cup)

Traditional preparations historically included infusions of 1-2 grams of dried root daily in hot water for internal use, and poultices applied externally for wounds or inflammation [85].

### Traditional Use in Other Systems of Medicine

- **Ayurvedic medicine:** A related species, *Valeriana wallichii* (Tagara), has been used for centuries to alleviate anxiety, promote mental calmness, and support sleep by balancing Vata dosha [57].
- **Traditional Chinese medicine:** Known as Xie Cao, used to relieve digestive spasms, stomach cramps, and promote Qi flow, particularly for conditions involving Liver Qi stagnation [58].
- **Native American medicine:** Various tribes, including the Blackfoot, prepared valerian root teas to ease anxiety and promote restful sleep [59].

## 8. Mechanisms of Action

Valerian's pharmacological effects are thought to arise from multiple mechanisms, none of which is fully understood. It is likely that there is no single active compound and that valerian's effects result from multiple constituents acting independently or synergistically [12][13][14].

### GABA System Modulation

The most studied mechanism involves modulation of the gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA) system. GABA is the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the brain, and increased GABAergic activity produces sedation and anxiolysis.

**Valerenic acid as allosteric modulator:** Valerenic acid acts as a subunit-specific allosteric modulator of GABA-A receptors, potentiating receptors containing beta-2 or beta-3 subunits, potentially binding near the loreclezole site to enhance inhibitory neurotransmission [22]. This mechanism is similar (but not identical) to the mechanism of benzodiazepines, and may contribute to anxiolytic properties without the full sedative side effects seen with benzodiazepines.

**GABA release and reuptake inhibition:** In vitro studies using synaptosomes suggest that valerian extract may cause GABA to be released from brain nerve endings and then block GABA from being taken back into nerve cells, increasing GABA availability in the synaptic cleft [86].

**GABA degradation inhibition:** Valerenic acid inhibits an enzyme that destroys GABA, further increasing GABA levels in the synaptic cleft [87].

**Direct GABA content:** Valerian extracts contain GABA in quantities sufficient to theoretically cause a sedative effect, but whether this GABA can cross the blood-brain barrier to contribute to valerian's sedative effects is not known [28]. Glutamine, present in aqueous but not in alcohol extracts, may cross the blood-brain barrier and be converted to GABA, providing an alternative pathway [28].

### Volatile Oil Sedation

The second major category of proposed active constituents comprises the volatile oils, including valerenic acid and its derivatives. These have demonstrated sedative properties in animal studies [13][21]. However, valerian extracts with very little of these components also have sedative properties, making it probable that other components contribute to or are responsible for these effects [88].

### Valepotriate Activity

Valepotriates and their derivatives are active as sedatives in animal models, but their instability and rapid degradation in storage or aqueous environments make their contribution to clinical effects difficult to assess [12][20][89]. Their practical relevance to commercial preparations is uncertain.

### Levels of Variability

Levels of these bioactive constituents vary significantly among plants depending on when the plants are harvested, resulting in marked variability in the amounts found in valerian preparations [90]. This inherent variability is one reason why clinical trial results have been inconsistent — different studies may effectively be testing different compositions of bioactive compounds.
